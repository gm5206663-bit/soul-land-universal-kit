## Part 1: Ox O'clock

The knock came before the sky, three slow raps, exactly loud enough.

Qiu Yuan surfaced from sleep like a man being raised from the dead and finding it overpriced. "Whatever you are," he told the door, "you have the wrong corpse."

Deng Wu's voice came through the wood, wide awake and deeply offended by the concept of dawn itself. "You said forge-hour was announced. So I'm announced."

"That was for *me*, you ox— it's a *room* rule—"

"I memorized the rule. Item the four-… the two. Announcements." The door opened a crack against Room 105's own injunction, and one round hill-born face assessed the dark. "He said before sunrise. It's before sunrise. Everything legal."

Jin Yang was already up, which was the problem with smiths — the body left the bed when the day did, no vote taken. He moved through the dark like something that had never owned a need for lamps: boots by habit, hammer by hand.

"You don't have to come," he said. "Really, Deng Wu."

The ironhide boy took visible offense. "And let the two of you be the ones who weren't still laying down when the bell went? No. My hills taught me about oxen, weatherman. You feed one one time—" he was doing up his sandals on the corridor stone, with the grief of a man doing arithmetic against his own interests, "—you've got it forever."

"That's… cattle law," said Qiu Yuan, dragging a comb through his hair with his eyes closed, "and I'm certain it's not binding."

"Binding in the hills."

The dawn drills on the frost-cold plaza began that way, three boys in a loose line — and it kept being that way all week. The field staff marking attendance on slate jotted their names together for five straight mornings, a habit the staff started and the boys never quite ratified.

On the fourth morning, warming down by the fountain, they crossed the two boys from the run that mattered — the plain one and the sunrise one, still wearing thirty-jin vests, jogging around the plaza like the vests were part of breakfast. Huo Yuhao raised a hand when he saw them — the lifted-palm motion of people who have shared a water gourd. Wang Dong raised his eyebrows instead, which was somehow warmer.

"The monitor and his monarch train before the bell," Deng Wu observed, awed and aggrieved. He drank the fountain's coldest water and regretted it immediately.

"Monarchs," said Qiu Yuan, "have something to prove."

"No office," said Wang Dong, passing, "in a class of *ninety-one*. Just the legs."

"The monitor," said Huo Yuhao, breathing evenly, not breaking stride — plainly, like the word had always fit.

"Whatever the sun says," said Wang Dong, and was gone around the turn, chasing light.

Deng Wu watched them shrink. "Twelve laps carried, and the man jokes at dawn. There's no justice, brothers. There's just running."

"There is," said Jin Yang, wiping his face, "also breakfast."

"See, that's how I know you're from good stock."

## Part 2: The Cull, Announced (Canon Scene — the season's law)

The teacher had one piece of news for Class 1 in the first theory block, and she delivered it with the chalk.

"Three months," Zhou Yi wrote on the board, three slashes of ribbon-thin chalk. Beneath it she wrote two other numbers, unneedingly, because everyone in the room already knew what they were for.

**NINE HUNDRED + . . . . . HALF**

"Three months from today, this academy holds the freshman assessment," she said, turning to read the result she already knew she'd find in ninety-one faces. "Across all classes: more than nine hundred of you. After the assessment: no more than half that. Nothing about that is new — it is Shrek's oldest arithmetic, older than some of your clans, and it has broken better houses than yours. Half the students in this room will not be students here next term." She set the chalk down, precisely, like a coffin lid on a good fit. "Now you are frightened *formally*. Good. Frightened students pay attention."

The room's fear did come up, then — something like a low tide in the stomach, audible only in the shifting of benches. Deng Wu, on Jin Yang's left, had gone very still in what was becoming a recognizable fashion.

"Team events," the old woman went on. "The assessment is done in teams of three. Teams formed by the teachers from those of you who remain *worth forming*. You have three months to make yourselves into people a good teacher doesn't have to squint at. I will know each of you long before the ropes go tight. And children — " her eyes did that sweep of hers, the ungentle one, settling on nobody and sparing no one — "I do not squint."

Nobody asked questions. They had known since the ninth-page rumor of the first morning; now the cull had a date, and somehow that made it worse than when it had only been a mood.

On the way out, Qiu Yuan's face kept the even, self-possessed cast it wore on public benches, but his voice, down a notch, told the truth: "Three of three can be named already," he said. "The Duke's son, Wang Dong, and the littlest monitor in the school."

"And the fourth through ninth names are not yet written," said Jin Yang. "So the arithmetic isn't the weather, House Qiu. It just does weather's job on the skin."

Deng Wu said nothing at all for about thirty paces, which was itself a contribution. Then: "Oxen go to the butcher in twos and threes." A pause. "That's… that's the last animal thing I say today."

"Probably wise," said Qiu Yuan.

"I have a soul of a bovine, brother — this is *also* weather," and surprisingly, in the stairwell's chill, all three of them laughed, which was itself the day's first piece of good arithmetic.

## Part 3: Two Kinds of Hammer (the soul-tools hall `[design scene, canon-era system]`)

Third morning put them in a room they had not been in — a long hall of the soul-tools division, with bright lamps and iron tables and a teacher iron-gray from the trade.

His name placard said INSTRUCTOR HE; nothing else announced him, and nothing needed to — the hall was the announcement: rows of worktables, presses, soul-lamps, and at the front, two rows of finished blanks under cloth. Freshman theory came here once a week this year, because, said the notice, *the age is one of tools, and ignorance of tools is the one ignorance Shrek does not tolerate.*

"Efficiency," said Instructor He, sweeping the first cloth off in a flat, calloused hand. "This — a spike plate blank — is our line's work once today. Grade-fine, smooth-etched, ten identical pieces." He lifted one; it caught the lamplight cold and regular, shine without character, heart without a face. "Now you tell me the old way, and price it against us."

A courtyard's hands went up. The boy he glanced at — a city-academy candidate with smith-family tattoos — gave the old way in thirty well-schooled words: ore, fold, draw, anneal, refine; a master's hands a full week on one blank; the price that fetched.

"Nineteen days for a full set," said Jin Yang, who did not raise his hand, and had anyway the only voice at his bench to say the words with the weight of experience. "Nineteen hundred-swing heats of bread-price labor, three gold for six, fair-rate per the guild route." He could feel, somewhere distant in his chest, the workshop's sweet old ember-heat darken a shade.

The instructor caught the weight with a craftsman's ear, and his eyes came down rows and found the small country boy in the cheap shirt at mid-bench, and rested there like a hawk on a chimneypot. "You count like you've paid it, student. What's your name?"

"Jin Yang. I was a smith — grade three, White Birch guild."

"Ah." In the hall's humming light, the teacher nodded once, with some iron-rich courtesy given stranger-to-stranger across decade-lines. "Then you know what I'm about to say better than most: the blanks on this table are a *menu*, not an *argument*. The machine makes four this morning — yours the guild priced a week. What the machine will never make is the thing a grade-nine lives by, because the machine can't *listen* — it doesn't know where the metal wants to bend. That hunger to listen, boy, is what we buy at the top of the tower. Come see me when you're hungry to know the presses well enough to out-argue them. Everyone: open your books."

It was a tidy thing, well meant. But it had not touched the plate, which sat in the lamp-light like a very well-dressed stranger who had moved into your childhood home.

Deng Wu leaned over, cheerful and wrong in as only hill boys could be: "Brother, your face looks like a man who saw a grave."

"It looks like a *price*, child," said Qiu Yuan product-reviewingly, on the other side.

"It looks like both," said Jin Yang, and closed his book over the table with the care of a man adjusting a load.

*Third thing I know, brother,* something in his chest said to the plate and the lamplight. *After counting and stance, the third thing: everything the world cannot hear eventually answers to hands that can.* He filed it without heat, because a forge's keeper knows what to do with an ember: bank it, carry it, and don't waste it on the cold.

That night he hammered at the dormitory window-bench until third bell, on a piece of scrap, until the piece of scrap told him the truth it had been shaped around. It wasn't about the blank, exactly. It was about who got to say what a smith was for.

Qiu Yuan let the hammering run, unreported under treaty item the whatever, and judged by that single allowance, everyone stood that little bit more treated.

## Part 4: The Teacher's File (Zhou Yi panel — canon: her first days, the husband; AU: the folder contents `[design]`)

By a window in the teachers' wing, in lamplight a little warmer than the classrooms', the most-feared woman of the freshmen schedule was doing what she did most evenings of opening week: ruining her own good mood with other people's children.

The stew had gone luke at her elbow without her noticing, which was the year's first victory of the dossiers over the crockery.

"Nine already out of one class," said Fan Yu mildly, from the other end of it, toweling clean hands — the soul-tools man home late again, decade-patient, hands ink-blued at the creases and never quite off-duty. "Is the count keeping with your ambitions, Wife, or ahead?"

"I did not ask them to lie, Fan Yu. They *arrived* lying. Cheating is a home-trained skill. I just graded it." She turned a leaf. "The Duke's has a pedigree you'd believe before you met him — cold work, top of intake. The blue-haired one will have every mother's child in this class by the ears before winter, and the plain-eyed one…" The pen stopped, it being the second file she'd stopped the pen at tonight, and the iron in her face unkinked a little, into something that the classrooms never got shown. "Lowest rank in the room. Twelfth-lap heart. He's not a monster, this one — I can't make that kind. It's born in."

"And yet you made him class president — the weakest rank you took."

"He earned the room in front of my eyes, Fan Yu — that's worth more than a rating." She turned the page, and then another, and she stopped a third time, and that pause was a different animal altogether.

The fifth file on the stack, the one her predecessor at the tents had annotated in the margin in a theory-teacher's meticulous hand — *Wang's note: strike two cun into steelface. Purple second. "Priced my life into the hunts. Won't sell the pricing."* — had nothing in it that should have stopped the reader cold. The boy's numbers were open as day, right there on the paper: eleven winters, rank twenty-three, a smith's boy from White Birch with a forehead like a plough horse's patience. No intrigue. Except that the number was wrong. Wrong like a smith's hammer lying on a parlor carpet.

"Wife. What."

She realized Fan Yu had come halfway across the room, reading her face like men with blued diagrams. She closed the file, a little later than natural, as women of very few weaknesses shut one.

"Eleven years old, ring two at the thousand-tier — impossible arithmetic. He's not bragging about it. He runs mid-pack with two friends and the lantern on his desk at night doing *workman's quizzes* in my class. All his exercises in at first bell, all scored low-ordinary, by a hand that cannot be ordinary. I watched him sit down with the fallen in the run — could've finished ahead of the prince. He's *investing* where he spends, Fan Yu. A boy of eleven, from a farm. Your presses are ready to explain away every genius except the ones who hold their fire. And what this class teacher does about such a boy—" She turned the file facedown, and the iron in her went where other people keep smiles. "—she will decide by winter. He is the kind of blade you do not draw until you know the sheath."

"And that's what it is? The genius in a file." Her husband finished drying his hands, unimpressed as only wedding physics gets you to be. "You've found three in twenty years, and two of them tired you. Leave the boy his night-lamp, Wife. Let the blade be still, for once. The year is long." He picked up the cold stew and tasted it without complaint, resolute and forgiving. "Some of these children are homes in themselves. There is time to learn the boy before we teach the boy."

"Fan Yu," said the monster of the freshmen, in the tone of a woman very much used to being right about such things, "this one is a smith that got folded somehow into a hands-and-back strongest-lion shape, and I will find out *where the heat goes*."

Down the hall, the last lamplight clicked out on the folder with the smith's mark on its corner, and in the room the ink dried over: *the workman's bracket* — three names, one written twice — and, for a certain intent, a small inked star, neither favor nor assignment, just a bead dropped in a scale-pan to weigh something later.

## Part 5: Warm Names

Word moved around the freshmen wing by the old methods: over laundry lines and in the queue for hot water, inside shouted hallway jokes and quiet canteen corners. By the end of that first week, Class 1 had its small climate — the Duke's sons upwind of everything, the monitor and the blue-haired dawn in joint flight near the top of the flag, and a few other cold fronts and warm pockets that every new school year accumulates like sediment.

The girls at the next-but-one laundry trough knew *of* the three. "Was it them? The ones latched together crossing on lap hundred?" "The ox, there's the honest one. Cried going over — I heard— " "—did NOT cry—" "The quiet one pushed his bun away for a stranger?" — and the quiet one's roommate from White Birch had to stand for several minutes of acute overhearing with the immobile patience of an eagle being talked about in its own nest, and by the end of it he had been gifted with the one piece of intelligence the day had been put together to yield.

He delivered it over the treaty-page that night with the paper folded twice like a warrant, unreadable in his usual way.

"We have a name, House Qiu," said Jin Yang, at the shutter.

"We have *three* names. Oh, where has your ambition been?" Qiu Yuan flipped open the notebook and wrote, with the full year's ceremony: **weatherman · cavalry · ox**. "The wing is talking about the *three*. The girls call Deng Wu the ox already, and the wing as a whole—" he lowered his voice anatomically, to a register normally reserved for genealogy — "they say we cross the line *latched*. Like mountain-goers."

"My people come from the hills, not the mountains."

"Everyone's people come from better than they presently are — and it is *cattle law* in the hills, the ox told us so himself," and that was so exactly the running room-argument of the whole first week that the lamp had to be pinched before it shook, because the settled treaty's unwritten rule-zero was: *no laughing at the signatory*.

On Saturday, the whole wing had the quiet that came to Shrek's children when the week's boot-tread was let off for half a day — the muscles praising, the tongues conferring. Deng Wu slept against their corridor wall at the second bell with the unsubmission of a man holding a fort. Huo Yuhao and Wang Dong jogged their extra laps under the still sky, how worth establishes itself in persons too young to name it yet. At the fourth bell, a rolled paper clapped down under Room 105's door, standard-issue ink —

**MONDAY, FIRST BELL: COMBAT BASICS. BRING SOUL, LEAVE PRIDE.**

— and pinned under it, in the teacher's own hand, two words: **Zhou Yi**.

"Oh," said Deng Wu, waking.

"Combat," said Qiu Yuan, stricken.

"Good," said Jin Yang, and set the hammer quietly in the corner where the lamplight liked it.

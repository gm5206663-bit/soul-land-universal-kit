## Part 1: Iron (week 3)

The weighted racks appeared on a Monday, no announcement: rows of dull iron plates with straps, stacked at the drill-ground gate like an unasked question.

"Twenty laps," said Zhou Yi. "Armored. Last one past the post runs twenty more tomorrow before dawn." She looked across them all without appetite for anything they were about to feel. "Class 1 gets one soul, children. Here's where you find out you share it."

The class's young philosophy of suffering met iron, and learned it had been suffering decoratively.

Wang Dong, who would sooner have been on fire than last, went out at a pace to die on. Jin Yang ran his own weather: fifth at first, seventh, settling to a sustainable sixth-place metronome — the ox on one flank and Qiu Yuan on the other, because the bracket had signed its paper Friday and discovered, by lap eight, exactly what paper was worth against iron.

What was worth something: shoulders.

Deng Wu's plate came off at lap eleven into Jin Yang's right hand, and the bracket ran lopsided after that, ox crying quietly, Qiu Yuan counting laps aloud through his nose like prayer beads. Ahead, the sunrise boy had found the end of mornings and was still going anyway out of unbriefed pride, wings night-folded, legs writing checks.

But the whole thing — all of it, all term, as Class 1 later remembered itself becoming Class 1 — happened at the back.

Because at the back, a scrawny boy with a fish-stall body was failing arithmetic in public: steps not reaching, breath not coming, plate heavier than he was, twenty laps plainly seven laps more than he had in him. Huo Yuhao was last by half a lap and getting laster. The post was already out of air standing at it. *Who's last* is the only arithmetic Zhou Yi had set, and everyone in the class, running around their own flames, did it once a lap at the back of their heads.

Jin Yang didn't think it in the tallies. What he thought, at lap sixteen, was his grandfather's, out of the dark around a winter fire: *you never let a man go down on the road ahead of you and not go back, boy. That's the only rule that's also chain-mail.*

He dropped back. No words. Natural as evening — the Block, easing off his own weather, settling in half a stride to the boy's left rear shoulder where Zhe Yun's pamphlet would have called it *carry-duty spacing*, all the dignity of a boy choosing to be slower than he was. Twenty minutes' teaching that was just being where a falling boy's shoulder wanted company.

It cannot be said what linked to what, after that. Wang Dong, doubling back belated and horrified and somehow loftily furious, took the other shoulder without asking, as though he had trademarks on the lane. Deng Wu and Xiaolei, sooner or later. Zhu Lu, dry-eyed and sharp. Qiu Yuan, gasping. The class, lap by lap, by ones and pairs, without anyone's speech, piling back into last place until the slowest boy in school history was running inside a moving wall of mostly-strangers who had elected him their pace.

At lap twenty-one Zhou Yi watched the front of the pack come around the post all together, ragged and molten, carrying in their midst a boy who could hardly lift a foot — Huo Yuhao's face running with water he hadn't the air left to be ashamed of — and finish line etiquette had broken down entirely. There was no last.

"Tomorrow," she said, when she could be heard over the panting, with the thinnest line at the corner of her mouth that her class had yet earned, "no armor."

Nobody cheered, for lack of equipment. But Class 1 found, going home under the lamps, strewn across its corridor like the dead who had grown chatty, that it had in fact run together — and run into something, though nobody said what. The way weather meets the ground of a place, and after it rains everything is that place a little more.

## Part 2: Fish, By the Lake (week 5)

Shrek Academy's lake shore, lit with stalls on free evenings, had been going about its own business long before any of them enrolled. The bracket went down to the lake the first time as the poor and the news-hungry go anywhere — Qiu Yuan navigating, Deng Wu sniffing, Jin Yang following with his whole seven-silver week about him and no plans for most of it.

And there, where the smoke smelled like somebody who meant it, was their class's back-of-the-pack boy, barbecuing fish at a little stand with the efficient serenity of a much older soul. Two copper a skewer. *Yuhao's grilled fish*, the wing had already learned to call it, first thing It had learned about him when it found out he was doing it *for rent*.

"Brother," said Deng Wu, moved past protocols. He held up fingers like a man ordering weather. "Six."

They paid from the common purse the treaty still didn't have, because folk are descended from wolves wherever you find them, and stood by the stall's little edge of fire with their sticks and their day, and — because the shore's whole plot was older than their worries — watched everything there was to see.

They weren't the only ones watching. Down the shore a small snow-pale girl was buying skewers with the regularity of custom. They knew her by corridor-sight — Jiang Nannan of the second class. And three older outer-court boys knew who she was, too. They got in the way of her evening at the stall's edge, with the kind of senior swagger that assumes only some people are real.

What happened next was a lesson in what kinds of people are real.

A tall youth came out of the dusk and dropped *into* the swagger like Providence with a broad chest and a genuinely foul mood — Xu Sanshi, they would all know soon, of the fifth year, unfathomable to Children — and the three swaggers were re-sorted into various horizontal attitudes with the brisk efficiency of waste management. Then, because the scuffle had gone through half the stall, he reached into his sash without looking, took out two gold soul coins, and laid them on Huo Yuhao's board like weather clearing.

"For the fish," he said, neither asking pardon nor granting it. "The rest was on the collateral." He reclaimed the girl's company with the profound relief of a man re-entering a temple, and the two of them went up the shore path, snow and stone.

Two gold. More at a stroke than Jin Yang had touched in a month at White Birch market. Yuhao, behind his counter, just went on flipping fish to the next customer as though comets did this nightly — calm already the shape of a town.

Only the stall smoke had opinions, drifting up over the lake. Qiu Yuan, in the tone of a man entering data onto stone: "*Fifth year*." Deng Wu, reverently: "Two *gold*." And Jin Yang, eleven, skewer cooling in his hand, simply fixed the moment away. What he had seen was economics with shoulders — two kinds of strength hurrying past each other on one little plank. The kind of moment you get up tomorrow and forge toward. The running that goes somewhere.

## Part 3: The Works (weeks 6–10)

There came a morning — week six, hall Sundays settled to habit by then — Instructor He took his tea down the steps to the hall doors and stood looking out into the yard for a long minute, because a boy with fish-smoke hands and Class 1 sleeves was being walked in at the superintendent's elbow, and the superintendent was Teacher Zhou's own husband.

Fan Yu of the soul-guidance division had the calm of men who run furnaces and have opinions about everything human passing through them. The yard masters bowed. The works hummed on. Jin Yang, breaking wire at tolerance-station six, was a small enough sight to miss. But Huo Yuhao, coming through the big doors on the first day of the rest of his life, looked around the hall with a stranger's care. He kept looking. At the far bench he found, mildly surprised, the boy who had once dropped back beside him at sixteen laps for no reason anyone had said out loud.

Two beats. No words were available yet at that distance. Huo Yuhao nodded — the nod the whole class had learned to read since the run, the one that meant *counted*.

"Knowledge is counted here," said Instructor He, at Jin Yang's elbow, watching the quiet boy walk the works at the superintendent's elbow. The instructor didn't smile, but he had that settled-around-the-eyes look of a man watching his hall become relevant to canon's own train-track. "Now it matters what you've already been becoming here, boy. The School has begun its real division — watch it. Remember where you stood to see it."

"Yes, teacher."

"And bring your square-foot in Saturday. Your tolerances are ready for coin."

So weeks six through ten, in the ledger of hands, went like this: two mornings of real silver, earned on fitted edges the catalog buys and the press can't hear; five Saturdays without missing bell; one small lime-scorched thumbnail. Zhou Yi's classroom went from gates to grind: stance ladders, three-element drills for the teams, filing still gospel. The bracket came apart and back together daily, as all good instruments do. They found their instinct in October-fine: ox forward in patience, like weather-forecast thunder. Qiu Yuan overhead in the picture's rear-corner, his accuracy up a full letter-grade since he had started calling everything *reports*. And at the block between them, one boy dependable as a sledge on a chisel.

Sundays aside, they saw the fish-stall boy only at speed. Division boys lived in canon's own fast lane. But once in a while, at the yard wall in second bell, somebody would say — *division's littlest one teaches them metal while still chewing rice*, or *they say the superintendent took the quiet one* — and a small warmth would pass along Class 1's bench, irrationally, how houses are warmed by fires burning three rooms over.

## Part 4: The Series (week 11)

Wang Dong took Square Two the eighth time, eighth week of card: 3–0.

The ninth time, at eleven weeks, it went differently, and the whole wing was restructured by the news before twilight.

What changed was eleven weeks' worth of changes, plus one extra gear nobody had filed yet out of the boy who everyone nevertheless filed compulsively for reasons they could no more resist than the road. It happened at two-and-a-half touches, chalk-line balance. Wang Dong glided in for the finish, his octobered speed tuned to the post's now-famous compass. And the Block was gone — not from the square, but from the spot the sunrise's eyes were already deciding was his. He came back in with his shoulder instead of his stance. And touched Wang Dong on the scapula with his knuckles, as politely as knocking at a door.

Three to one. First touch, eight bouts in coming, no crowd.

The square detonated anyway. Girls sat down on the lime. Qiu Yuan, forgetting cavalry, climbed a post. Deng Wu lost the power of sentence in both of his languages. Zhou Yi's chalk, by its register's edge, *did not write the score* for a full second — and then did, even-handedly, with a small renewed interest in her whole life.

Wang Dong did the only rational sunrise thing. He stood there with a hand on the touched spot, staring at nothing, delighted. Then he pointed like a duelist, at close range. Unclean triumph in every syllable —

"**THE WARRANTY IS EXPIRING.**"

"The warranty," Jin Yang agreed, breathing from the real third hour of his life, "is expiring."

## Part 5: Ninety Days Later (week 12, assessment-eve)

The notice went up on the class board on the first morning of the third month, in the hand they had all learned to read faster than they'd learned some people's:

> **THE NEW-STUDENT ASSESSMENT. CLASS 1 SECTION B PUTS OUT AT SIX.**
> **TEAMS SIGNED. SQUARES CIRCUIT, EAST GROUNDS.**
> **NOW I KNOW EACH OF YOU.**
> **Z.**

*Now I know each of you.* Qiu Yuan, arriving first at the board, stood and accounted for winter silently at the bottom of the page — it was the phrase's fourth month in his files, promised long since on a teacher's sill above a plaza he had stood by, and kept.

The bracket stood at their wall before sixth bell like priests at the new year. They hadn't said *assessment* aloud since signing, because Shrek gives children nerves as spring gives streams flood. The purse was 24 gold 1 silver (the dividends of small silver in the works, the common purse still refused). The body of the weatherman's boy went eighteen minutes now in the cold dawn rites where it had once gone twelve; his square series with the sunrise stood at many-to-one, one; the two coins' memory from the lakeside still did arithmetic in the back of his head when things like tridents and fifth-years crossed his mind. The ox fell well now, and started up like morning rain. The treasurer had filed three hundred and eleven reports, all of them eventually read.

Deng Wu straightened, enormity fitting him these days like a doorframe.

"Brothers," he said. "I have only one superstition left."

"Have the others been paid out?"

"Burned, brother. Wages of the square." The ox took a breath, old hills around the shrine of his face. "One superstition. Before a work that bends a family's story, say where it stands when it's bent."

There was a silence the treaty would have knighted.

"Third," said Qiu Yuan, at last, irreversible with quiet. "We're third. Behind the sunrise's three and the cold ones. Nothing else, this month, guesses closer than that, on my books."

"Third it is," Jin Yang said — the smallest agreement of all; a number to walk out onto a field in the morning with in your teeth. And because he was a boy raised in the country of full baskets and plain-speaking men, under the eaves of three months of learning what school is, he said the true thing too, without lighting on it: "Whatever's bent there, when it's bent — all three names still on it."

The bell woke up the grounds. By the east gate the squares were already taking their lime from the morning, four by four, pale in the mist like rules that had decided to be weather. Huo Yuhao's team turned in at the far arch together — division hours on his sleeves; Xiao Xiao spinning a kettle of steady cheer at his elbow; Wang Dong arriving as day does over hills, looking for someone specific, and finding him with a rival's whole month in his eyes.

*Third,* the legs, fine, knew by heart, coming down the row. *Bow to the line; walk in.*

The assessment began as all prophecies do: with chalk, breath, registration paper, and the noise children make when they realize there is now nowhere further to wait.

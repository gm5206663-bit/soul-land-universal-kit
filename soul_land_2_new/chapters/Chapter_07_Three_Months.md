## Part 1: Iron (week 3)

The iron plates arrived on a Monday, without announcement.

They stood stacked beside the drill-ground gate in dull, strap-fitted rows, as plain a question as the School had yet asked its smallest class. Zhou Yi waited beside them with her roster in hand. She read the class's face in one pass through — the parts that asked what the plates weighed, and the parts that already knew they didn't want to find out — and let the silence do the arithmetic a moment before she gave the answer herself.

"Ninety kilograms of good reason," she said, patting the stack once. "Not by the plate. Altogether. Twenty laps of the square, wearing it. The last one past the marker does the same again at dawn and does not need to bother understanding why." Her eyes went over them evenly. "You are one class. One soul, the School keeps telling you. Go find out what sort of soul ninety kilograms teaches you about."

There was no formation to learn. There was only the ground, and the plates, and the sun still deciding whether to be warm.

Jin Yang had grown up with weight that did not ask permission — plough-carried sacks, anvil tongs, sacks that became anvils and then sacks again in the economy of a small forge — and he knew the oldest truth about carrying, which was that it was paid for in breath. He went out at fifth place and settled to sixth in two hundred meters, not because he couldn't have run faster, but because a boy who didn't understand odds like these had no business being near a hammer in the first place.

Wang Dong, ahead of everyone, did not share this opinion, and was showing it.

The sunrise boy had gone out at a pace that suggested he intended to finish before breakfast cooled, carrying his plate as though it had personally offended him. He was still in front at lap three when he started passing people for the second time, trailing the particular cheerfulness of a boy who expects the world to keep up or apologize. He caught Jin Yang's running neighbor at lap four, offered a brief "pardon, industry is coming through," and was gone before the neighbor's reply arrived.

"He talks to people while he runs," Deng Wu observed, working hard even to talk. "Brother, that is not fair."

"Fair," panted Qiu Yuan, three strides past his dignity already, "was not consulted."

Behind them the class strung itself out over the track in the way classes do — some tight little knots of mutual encouragement, some lone sailors, some children discovering the exact weight at which pride becomes heavy. The iron didn't care about any of it. Iron never did. Jin Yang approved of iron, privately, and went on counting strides.

That was when he began to hear the back of the race.

It was one boy, at first — the plain boy with the soft black hair, the one who made up the light half of a water-gourd ruination at the fountain the first week. Huo Yuhao ran last, and the distance between him and second-to-last was worse each time the class came around. His steps weren't really steps anymore. His plates had long since stopped looking like training equipment and started looking like an argument someone else had decided to settle for him.

And the law of the drill said: last one through the marker runs again at dawn.

Lap eight, Jin Yang was sure the boy couldn't make twenty. Lap nine, he was also sure the boy had no intention of stopping short of twenty.

The two certainties rode around in his chest together, and — the way it happens when two things are both true and refuse to argue with each other — something had to pick them up.

His grandfather's road-rule came up out of him like water coming up out of a well: you don't walk past a man who's going down or about to go down on the road, whether or not you can see the wagon coming. Whatever had happened in the week since he had stood on the wrong end of that rule at home and lost the argument with the rain, the rule had kept him. It had not kept the other fellow.

Jin Yang eased off.

Three runners went past him as though permission had been issued. He drifted back half a lane, and then the rest of one, until he was even with the thin shoulders and the rasping breath, and then he settled in there at Huo Yuhao's side at last-place pace, saving nothing, saying nothing.

The boy's eyes came up a fraction. Whatever they asked, Jin Yang didn't have words for. He just held the slow pace beside him.

*Counted*, the eyes said eventually, and went back to the ground.

Wang Dong noticed at lap eleven, because Wang Dong noticed everything that had dignity within two hundred paces of him. He came back down the track with the particular indignant energy of a sunrise being asked for the time, slowed into place on Huo Yuhao's other side, and gave way to the truth with the suspicious cheerfulness of a man pretending it was his idea the whole time.

"Of course. Of *course* the block got here first— no talking, I'm saving all my breath for explanations nobody is going to believe." He looked ahead. "The flag is green, Yuhao. We move."

"That's," said Huo Yuhao, "a lamppost."

"Recently green."

Nobody asked Deng Wu to come — there was no point, he was going to come anyway, suffering did that to the ox. Xiaolei arrived about the same time, cursing at somebody behind her. Zhu Lu came back with no expression and no explanation and stayed, which among her people passed for a speech. Qiu Yuan was seven steps ahead of everyone returning, making a noise like a broker on the last day before winter, but he was returning.

The class trickled back in ones, then twos, then stops. No one gave a speech. No one called for courage. The only arithmetic moving was footstep after footstep, and the quiet decision of a hundred children — many of them so proud of their own mornings that they had forgotten what it felt like to be last — to see what last would feel like inside a wall instead of outside of one.

At lap twenty, when the boy at the middle of the wall had stopped being able to lift his feet high enough to finish a stride before someone else started one, the whole class came over the line together.

There was no last one.

Zhou Yi watched them cross, her face unmoving, her hands behind her back in a very studied way. The silence from the square was practically breathable.

"Now," she said eventually, "someone tell me who was slowest today."

Nobody even looked around, because the point was academic anyway.

"No one, Teacher," said Jin Yang, who had been closest to it.

She held his eyes for a full breath longer than was comfortable. Whatever she was measuring, it wasn't his skin.

"Armor goes home," she said at last. "Tomorrow we run without it."

Nobody cheered, because nobody had the lung. But along the long trudge back to the dormitory wing — sweating through shirts two days from being anyone's best — members of Class 1 began to discover things. Not big things, and forever it would be hard to say where they started. A boy here found out the girl behind him came from his own province. A boy there learned that two people could suffer in the same key and produce, instead of misery, just barely enough breath. By the time the lamps came on over the wing, Class 1 felt, for the first time since its hundred and some children had piled off at the school gates, like a place. Like a place that you were inside of.

Zhou Yi stood at the drill-ground gate alone when the last lamp went up, looking at the empty plates and the chalky ground, her mouth finally letting itself do the small, unparsed thing it only did when she was alone.

"File this," she said, to nobody, because nobody else would ever have to know.

## Part 2: The Lakeside Economy (week 5)

The stall shore by the lake ran on its own time.

Evening after evening the little stands lit up under the willows, strung out along the water's edge the way a market drops a line of lanterns and forgets to pick them up. Some sold tea. Some sold steamed things. One sold fried noodles to a loyal conspiracy of older students who claimed it was reviving local doctrine. The shore wasn't large and it wasn't fancy; it existed, the way shores exist, because people exist and water is better to sit next to than walls.

The bracket went down for the first time, because Qiu Yuan had heard things, and Deng Wu's nose had also heard things, so they had teamed up, which — as Deng Wu pointed out — was logistics.

The bracket's finding was that Huo Yuhao ran a fish stall.

This was not a finding anyone else had found yet; it still counted. The stall was at the end of the strand nearest the dorms, keeping plain hours with a plain board facing the water. Two copper a skewer. The boy behind the grilled fish worked with the concentration of a craftsman and the ease of somebody who had been at it longer than a month. His back was turned to the arriving students — or it was turned to the shore, which had been there first — and the fire-shine off the water made his plain face flicker.

"He sells *fish*," Deng Wu said, amazed. It seemed, actually, to strike him as more exotic than anything since the iron. "To students. For *money*."

"For costs?" Qiu Yuan guessed, sharp-eyed not by birth but by schooling. "Look at the prices. This boy is running an accommodation, not a business."

"A what?"

"—he feeds people. Wait on the wing."

They took up a spot by the corner of his counter and ate fish — which was excellent, crisp-backed, threaded through with something that tasted like summer evenings in the districts where people know how rivers work — and they watched the shore happen.

Huo Yuhao's evening happened like most of his evenings did, the boys learned. Regulars came through in easy waves. Upper-year students with the easy gait of people gloriously unimpressed by everything. A pair of teachers from the middle years, after discussing the day's drills with the special animation of people thrilled to disagree. Someone from the soul-guidance halls buying half the evening's catch in paper, already spirits to be delivered to somebody's workbench further into the dark.

Near the end of the first watch, a small girl came down the shore as regular as the tide and bought her three skewers one at a time, with the kind of patience usually reserved for things that happen anyway. Snow-pretty and quiet-eyed, she was the kind of face a stall already knew by name. Jin Yang didn't know her own, but the shore did: Jiang Nannan, from the second class, whose reflection the lake had been keeping tabs on for two years already.

What happened next took under a minute and rearranged half the shore's geography.

Three older students came down the strand in the mood outer-court swaggers wear when they mistake the world for their sleeve. They did not quite block her. But they did not quite let her pass either, and the lake watched them the way lakes watch tourists being disappointing at water level.

Then a fifth-year appeared out of the lamplit dark behind them like weather finishing its sentence — broad-shouldered and immediate, with the hair-raising confidence of a boy who has no idea how loud the world keeps telling him he is.

"You're blocking other people's evenings," the fifth-year said, which was, in content, fair.

In execution, it was three young men discovering that conversation was not the only thing a fist could handle. They went down in the sort of brisk, slapped-together fashion that nobody was going to tell their parents about, and the entire shore watched it happen with its mouths full.

The fifth-year surveyed the wreckage of his evening's charity. Half of Huo Yuhao's board was now on the beach. Several fish had achieved escape velocity.

He reached into his belt — without looking, without asking — took two gold soul coins, set them down on the cutting board, said "For the fish," in a large voice that sounded like somebody who hoped the apology could be signed with a thumbprint, and walked after the girl.

Two gold.

More money than Jin Yang's father might see outside of a good tax season, spread over about eight seconds of a scuffle.

Huo Yuhao did not even look up. He wiped down the boards, tucked the coins in his apron, flipped the next skewer, and caught the light off the water with the indifference of a boy who undergoes comets like weather and weather like rent.

Jin Yang watched the two shadows go up the strand, the small girl walking exactly as far behind the large one as loyalty outpaced pride.

He didn't say it aloud. But inside his head, where the blacksmith's boy had long been keeping track, he wrote it down anyway: strength, in this world, could do more than hit. Strength at that scale could pay for itself without asking the price of fish. It was a kind of wealth the villages didn't print.

## Part 3: The Works (weeks 6–10)

The soul-guidance division announced itself the way halls built by actual workers announce themselves: without fanfare.

On a Sunday late in the sixth week, a boy with a fish-stall history was walked through the big doors at the superintendent's elbow. Fan Yu moved steady, the way furnaces moved, conserving heat he had no need to show. They went down between the hull-tall presses with the particular quiet of places that intend to outlast the hour, and the masters who knew Fan Yu by sight bowed along their gaze like grass to a draft animal, showing the hall what mattered in the department that weekend.

Jin Yang stood at tolerance-station six breaking wire, the way he did on Sundays, and was, he supposed, as far as superintendent-grade attention went, smaller than the equipment.

Huo Yuhao's arrival made no announcement either. Those that knew knew; those that didn't learned soon after.

Jin Yang watched from across the floor as the fish-stall boy's eyes did the slow, unblinking work of somebody being shown a larger map than most of them were used to. He looked around the hall — at the stamp lines first, then through them, at the inner machines rolling like heavy seas — and then kept looking, down the rows of tolerance benches, past the drill ranks to where the humble edge work happened.

And he found Jin Yang, standing at a lever frame with calipers in hand, looking back at him.

For two beats the superintendent's hall held them like scale pans. Then the boy at the front of the division's attention did exactly what the boy in front of the forge had once done for him: he nodded.

It said nothing about coins. It said nothing about the iron run either, or the lamp posts at the fountain. It said, precisely: *I see you.*

Then he bowed his head back to his superintendent, and began his education in earnest, in the language of workshops.

Instructor He materialized at Jin Yang's elbow in the fashion of work-masters everywhere — appearing just before you think of him, with his tea.

"That's the school's future," observed He, in the tone of somebody discussing the next delivery schedule. "Remember where you're standing to see it. You saw the start. You'll feel the turns before the class does. I'll expect you to do the work of your watch."

"Yes, teacher."

"Good," said He, in a tone not markedly warmer, — which was, for He, already a toast. "Tolerances are in your Saturdays now. Straight orders. The School pays by the board, same as the old hands. Six coppers a fitted edge, honest work, and no slipping the blanks I haven't passed."

Six coppers.

Jin Yang made a space at the corner of his mind for putting things, and put it there.

---

The season proceeded the way forge-seasons go: in layers. Saturdays passed. Straight orders got straighter, and Sundays got their own small weather, and at the works hall he became one of the boys the masters knew by the last syllable of their names. Somewhere around week seven, the hall's door-minder began letting him stay an hour past bell because somebody had to watch the long plates cool.

Meanwhile the rest of Class 1 went about becoming a unit the way live steel learns to become — slowly, in daily reflows that looked after the fact like they'd been hammered obviously all along.

The team's triangle found its natural seams without having to be told twice, once the drills got honest. Deng Wu stood where fronts stood: first, patient, wearing the full-mountain patience of somebody expecting nothing more complicated than the next shoulder to set. Qiu Yuan went to the rear-eye spot he had never stood without complaint for his term at home — whingeing, wide-awake, and seeing the whole square at once, calling wasp-sharp spots and turns with the nose of a gossip who had finally been unleashed on respectable material. Jin Yang made the middle, because that was what the middle was for: the place you set yourself when both sides need passing, when the charge splits and your formation has wind in it.

They found, in other words, the shape that nearly every schoolmaster in history has found trooping into the drills one winter morning: the shape that had been there all along, unmoving, since three boys first learned where to stand against a wall and each other. You only need a chart to tell you, if you haven't spent three months finding your place without one.

## Part 4: Nines (week 11)

The chalk card for square two read the same names it had read for nine weeks, plus a lattice of small scores along the bottom limb of the square that only one person ever read without squinting.

**WANG DONG / JIN YANG**: eight bouts.

**Eight 3–0s.**

The first bout of theirs anyone remembered by hand had lasted — genuinely — under thirty seconds. Wang Dong had flown across three meters of lime like a daybreak someone had figured out how to aim, making the short square look like an enormous single step, and Jin Yang had gotten one knuckle per wing before giving ground. The first time his stance folded, the square had made a noise that said nobody had seen this coming.

The fourth bout had taken Wang Dong four minutes and three passes.

The sixth bout had taken him five minutes, by which time he had stopped taking the obvious line into the post's guard — because it had started arriving after an eighth of a second delay only he apparently noticed.

And by the eighth bout, which lasted eight minutes and cost Wang Dong something nobody would talk about in the wing without being shushed, people had started arriving early and leaving late to watch the post.

The ninth bout was scheduled for a Monday.

People knew. The way it happens in halls with long histories of keeping track of children's weather, the word went around: *watch the post come up through the nines.* Xiaolei, betting four skewers against a mended back, was offering various propositions. The brackets' own famine-or-feast posture had showed up to spectate, genuinely competitive to avoid clashing with his principles.

In the event, it came down to one moment, like always — only now, after nine bouts, everyone in the stands understood how a person could take the moment to meet it.

Wang Dong came in on his banked turn at the second pass of the third touch, his step honed by eight delivered lessons into a thing of precise beauty. On the turn's third beat — the money beat, where the wing opened and the cross came in to finish people — the Block moved.

Not back. Nobody had ever got anywhere going back against sunlight. Not forward, either — because there was nothing in front of him but the place he would have been in two seconds and a boy who knew exactly how long a second lasted.

He moved sideways, a very small distance, the kind of step a person learns on long roads carrying ploughshares and knowing each inch of mud privately. And then he kept going on around — like he was following something through, or just come knocking for the first time in months — and a clenched hand knocked once hard on Wang Dong's shoulder blade.

Zhou Yi, looking down from her mark, did not write.

For a breath she looked like somebody watching the weather inside her own cup, only the cup was up and the weather was chalking around her.

Then she wrote the score down.

Three to one.

The square detonated. Around the lime line people were actually shouting, which happened only about once a season when the girls lost anything to the boys these days. Qiu Yuan was up a post expressly to see better. Deng Wu fell off the pile of plates sitting beside him, keel-hauled by hilarity. Xiaolei, on the back of four skewers and a mended back, watched with an infuriating smugness.

Down on the square, Wang Dong stayed on one knee in the follow-through position, his whole body tilted at the sky, one hand still on the touched place as though his shoulder blade might have had its own opinion. And then — the only thing the sunrise could do that was entirely native — he started laughing. Actual laughing. It came out of him the way morning broke out of hills.

"Who was that?" he asked the post, though he knew.

Everybody knew.

"Did you see that, Teacher?" the sunrise announced, standing up. His delight had transformed everything, as it did when he was awake, no matter whose side he was on. "My *warranty*!!"

"Take your turn at mine sometime, softy," said Jin Yang — because if the lion was going to be household-famous for anything, it might as well be this.

"Mine," said the sunrise, and considered it, and allowed, past the crowd's held breath, that Wang Dong would keep the score long enough to win it back nobly. He walked off the square still grinning — the way sunrise grins, which is to say like a boy whose favorite game just got harder. Nobody was beating Wang Dong on his own equipment yet. But the post was coming along nicely.

## Part 5: Now I Know Each of You (week 12)

The notice was on the class board by morning: two square-meters of her chalk, written small and hard enough to scrape through the bones of anyone who crossed its field.

> **NEW-STUDENT ASSESSMENT. FIRST DRAW AT DAWN TOMORROW.**
>
> **TEAMS OF THREE — SIGNED, REGISTERED, ACCOUNTED FOR.**
>
> **ZONE 33. FIRST ELIMINATION COUNTS.**
>
> **NOW I KNOW EACH OF YOU.**
>
> **Z.**

At the wall, the bracket stood where they always stood before the hard weeks, watching the lamps come up around the assessment squares. Row on row of chalk-white lime lifting out of the cold ground and dark, steady, reasonable, as if all the geometry they'd been chalked in over months had finally gotten up and agreed to become weather.

Qiu Yuan stood before the board, unmoving — the way people stand when a ledger they kept in pencil comes back in ink.

"Now I know," murmured Qiu Yuan, and his voice didn't have its usual whine of accountancy. "She tipped her hand."

Everybody had been walking toward that sentence for three months; it was funny how few people had said anything about it.

At the wall's usual arrangement, the bracket did its evening arithmetic in the open, the way it always did before the hard weeks. Jin Yang started, without being told.

Purse: twenty-four gold, one silver. The works had paid its board, exactly. The dawn rites ran eighteen minutes where twelve had once been a limit. Deng Wu stood where fronts stood now, the shoulder-setting now paying out twelve-plus years of pile drivers at the moment of impact, and slept through falling. Qiu Yuan had three hundred eleven files in the ledger of the watching world, most wrong — two hundred of them, give or take, were going to matter this month. Then Jin Yang took his own stock, the way he had every evening since the road:

Two rings. A claw he could spend maybe once in every twenty counts. A hand that had learned what iron was saying under it. And four new words he had no column for yet — assault, defense, control, support — whatever the examiner meant by them, come morning.

The wall shifted. The evening lights did their slow ritual of turning off the bright side, steady as weather.

"Third," Qiu Yuan declared from the wall, banker-quiet. "Third, behind the sunrise's three and the cold ones. On my books it measures closest."

"Third," said Deng Wu, entirely without elaborating, which in his case was a pledge.

"Third," Jin Yang said, and under it, on the page of the air where they'd stood so many weeks together, laid the last line of the ledger the season had been keeping without them: their three names on the same page, still.

The lamps made their slow circuit to the dim side then, steady as weather, and above them the winter stars came out impartially. Somewhere across the wing, a hundred children were having the same quiet thought about morning.

The bracket stayed at the wall until the cold made a case for bed, and then they went up together, saying nothing more, three names on the same page in the dark.

Assessment day came in the morning the way real weather comes: with chalk, with breath, with a board of zones, and with the whole term of waiting standing under their boots like a floor.

## Part 1: Roster Dawn

The freshman rosters went up before the dining tables were warm, and boys read them the way soldiers read casualty lists — from the bottom, because the bottom was where mercy lived if it lived anywhere.

Class 1.

Jin Yang found the block he wanted halfway down the notice wall, in clerk-tight columns: surname, given name, recommendation province. STAR LUO entries ran long. His own name stood two-thirds through, unremarkable, mid-alphabet of an empire — and four lines above it, a name that four girls were already pointing at, and one line of girls' whispers above that:

DAI HUABIN.

"Prince's house," said Qiu Yuan beside him, unnecessarily and with feeling, because the White Birch Qius kept genealogies the way other clans kept swords. "The White Tiger Duke's second. Celestial rank, they say; they say it *down south.*" A pause; his eyes slid sideways, tallying. "You're in. I'm in with you."

"Four lines apart," said Jin Yang, who had counted.

"You finished reading and counted the distance between our names?"

"It was four," said Jin Yang, and went to breakfast, because there were no more questions that arithmetic was going to answer about the matter.

He ate the way smiths eat — bread, broth, and attention — while the dining hall performed its first-morning weather: a hundred boys being new to each other at the top of their lungs. Twice he marked faces the queue had marked for him before. Once: hair like a clear dawn over water, pink under blue, attached to a boy eating as though the porridge had personally insulted his house. And twice: three tables away, a plain black-haired boy who ate quickly and neatly and thanked the server while doing it, who had a bruise high on one cheekbone, fresh enough to still be deciding its color.

*Dormitory bruise,* filed the part of Jin Yang that was Master Chu's discipline wearing his eyes. *Somebody's roommate objected to the terms.*

He did not know, then, that the bruise would soon stand up in class and answer for itself by rule of canon. He finished the broth down to the grain, because porridge at Shrek was not going to be the day's problem.

The day's problem came written on the classroom blackboard in a hand like drawn razors, and was already waiting for them at the head of the room when Class 1 shuffled in near the first bell.

## Part 2: "I Raise Monsters"

She looked like what the juniors had spent the morning promising she would look like: an old woman with a scholar's iron face, white hair drawn back so hard it seemed to argue with the spine, eyes that did not so much inspect a hundred freshmen as weigh them against replacement value.

"Zhou Yi," she said. "Your class teacher. I will be brief, because teaching is brief in this room: **I do not produce soul masters. I produce monsters.** Any boy or girl in this class who cannot be made monstrous within the season will cease, with equal brevity, to be in this class."

The room received this in the kinds of silence classrooms keep in stock: the hopeful, the offended, and the incurious. Jin Yang contributed the fourth kind. He was remembering Headmaster Chu Shan, who had said it with a bow instead of a threat: *Shrek won't grade you small.* The same fact, made of ice here and oil there.

"Prerequisite audit," said Zhou Yi. "Stand, those of you who fought before the enrollment gates cooled."

Nobody stood. Then — one boy stood, mid-rows, plain black hair, a bruise finishing its decision to be purple: the boy who thanked servers.

"Huo Yuhao," he said, to the room and not to her. "I fought my roommate. In the dormitory, in our room."

A beat — and then the dawn-haired boy stood too, all six-feet-of-eleven of him, incandescently unrepentant. "Wang Dong. Same room. Same reason." His eyes said the reason remained un-settled.

A classful of freshmen inhaling at once made a noise like wind in rigging. Two. Out of a hundred. Zhou Yi's face did something that was not a smile but kept a smile's bones.

"One hundred of you were measured yesterday," she said. "Two of you fought. The other ninety-eight were observed, assessed, and found *watchable*. Cowardice is a curriculum too, children — it teaches you to stand still when told to stand. I prefer the second lesson." She turned her back on them and wrote three lines on the board, big enough to read from the corridor:

**SHREK PLAZA. ONE HUNDRED LAPS. ONE HOUR.**

"You will run the plaza, every soul, within the hour," said Zhou Yi. "I will tally. Names and lap counts go in my book; the dishonest go in another. Completed run: you remain. Failed run: you cease." She let them picture the second book. "Begin."

## Part 3: The Hundred Laps

Shrek Plaza was a square of pale stone three hundred meters to the lap, flat as a measuring rule and about as merciful, and a hundred laps of it came out, in the ledger of Jin Yang's head, at thirty kilometers inside sixty minutes for boys whose average soul rank read twenty-one.

Not steep arithmetic, for anyone who had spent three years swinging a six-jin hammer over a bellows-fed fire while his strange body quietly widened the pipes of him. His plan was written before the first stride: no heroics. The list file of a smith is the ledger file of a runner — *finish. Not first. Finished.* He set his pace at the upper third's shoulder and held it like a cadence struck on metal.

The morning went to work.

Athletic souls with wind or rigor attributes made weather of themselves early and paid with their knees by lap sixty. The Qiu heir burned his pride hot at the front of the hunt and discovered, somewhere in the seventies, that twenty-one ranks buy fewer finished laps than twenty-three, and that a boy raised under a hammer does not announce his arrival; he simply arrives, every lap, on the tick. Dai Huabin's white-gold humiliation of the pack supplied most of the morning's noise: the Duke's son ran as if distance had been invented to be avenged.

At the front of everything, some three laps behind the timetable and then suddenly not behind anything at all, went the boy called Wang Dong — *late to the run,* Jin Yang's count reported, *and making first anyway,* light-footed as grammar and smiling as if a hundred laps were a corridor to somewhere better.

And at the pack's far tail, with a stride that looked modest and was counting everything, the plain boy Huo Yuhao ran his own private race against the lap clock, and was losing it in public.

Fifty-one minutes. Sixty-two. Jin Yang crossed the last tenth with the upper third and walked until the heart came down, and only then let himself stand at the finish flag with the rest and watch the last business of the clock: the tail thinning, collapsing in ones, in twos — and the plain boy, alone out on the far side of the stone, still running with the movement of a man sewing a seam he refused to leave unfinished.

Six minutes to the hour. Twelve laps out. Not even talent could spend what did not remain — and from the finishers' shade of the flag, suddenly, carrying the morning's speed back out into the morning: Wang Dong, dawn-haired, swearing halfway between a prayer and an argument, who reached the seamstress, took his arm over a shoulder of light, and pulled him home over the last twelve laps as the plaza counted seconds with them.

Shrek Plaza, somehow, found breath to cheer, freshmen or no freshmen.

"Huo Yuhao and Wang Dong," said the registrar-student beside Jin Yang, scribbling, when the pair broke the line with eighty seconds in the grave. "Pass. One hundred." And the plain boy lay on the finished stone with his face to the sky, breathing as if he had just purchased mornings wholesale.

Zhou Yi's book opened once more, and it did not open kindly.

"Zhuge Yun," she read, standing very upright in the plaza wind. "Cheng Cheng. Tang Dao. Lin Zeyu. Shangguan Chentian. Qiu Jianluo. Tang Ling. Yun Xiaopiao. Tailong."

Nine boys and their excuses rose from the stone. It did them no conceivable good.

"You counted your laps to me," said Zhou Yi, in the voice of a woman tearing up nine leases. "The plaza counts them to everyone. You are done at Shrek Academy — the dishonest do not stay, they are expunged; there are no 'but-Teacher's in arithmetic. The rest of you: the dormitory is at your backs. **Afternoon, second bell, this plaza. Weighted drill.** Bring what legs you have left."

## Part 4: Thirty Jin to Sundown

After noon, the plaza received a hundred freshmen and a cartload of weight.

Thirty jin. Fifteen kilos of dull black plate that hung off shoulders and hips and pressed boys' knees towards a past and future of sullenness, and the afternoon's law, written by that same razor hand, read simply: TWO HOURS. RUN UNTIL THE CLASS ENDS. **THE LAST TO FINISH IS EXPELLED.**

There is a point in every weighted drill where the pack stops being a queue and becomes a subtraction, and it comes sooner in armor. Jin Yang ran the arithmetic halves of himself loose and held the smith's cadence — *tock, tock* — while the fleet's firsts went by beyond and then came slowly unglued. Wang Dong went through the fleet's van like a rumor out of chase, lapping boys at will; the second, fifth, and ninth rankers saw nothing of him but the intertwining halo of dawn-colored light and plate-steel doggedness. Lap mountains were his to make today. Jin Yang marked them from his own slower shoulders — seven circuits more than the hour had earned from anyone else — and built his own honest mountain beside them, eleven and a half, until, at one hour forty and change, forge-drilled legs finally answered "no more" in a tone that ends all family conversation.

He sat down on the merciful stone with eighty-nine others — where Qiu Yuan already lay like a field casualty who had kept his ranking. And, across the sun-flat expanse of the plaza, a curious disaster assembled itself:

Wang Dong, the indomitable Wang Dong, lapped the whole world twice and caught, in his last minutes, the stone-block with fifteen meters to run. It did not console him that he was far and away first of the hundred; it consoled him about ten times less to find his soul power bankrupt and his gold crown plate framing a collapse. He stopped — not from surrender; the money was simply gone.

And still — alone at the back, in the ruins of the march, with empty face and labeled colors purpling — the plain boy, Huo Yuhao, was still running. Slow enough for grief, unstopping as a litany. The entire plaza sat like seed in autumn — and his stride planted itself over it in patient rows. The file of the sitting changed. Boys and girls began it in ones and twos, ashamed by weather, and stood again and ran — behind him or beside him, because no conscience under thirty jin wants to be outlasted by the boy the field is lapping.

Jin Yang hauled his scorched legs back under his plate, and joined the moving.

"Class counts," panted Qiu Yuan, who had found breath enough for outrage but not enough for speed. "Why — why stand -"

"Because the drill's arithmetic changed," Jin Yang said. "One boy's still solving it."

At the bell, the plaza contained: one hundred minus nine students, two rows of dented pride, several inclinations toward vulgar language, one class teacher whose hard-eyed something was not a smile but kept its bones, and two boys standing at the front with their plates shaded — one the afternoon's fastest, one the afternoon's last-fallen.

"The office of class president," Zhou Yi declared, "was assigned this afternoon to Wang Dong — by strength, by right of arrival, and challenge by any who think they can take it and keep it longer than a day." Her eyes catalogued the hundred for the ambitious; receiving none in the air of collapse, they went down rows and came home. "But there is another kind of arrival that tallies a man for me, and I will name it now: this afternoon, when the rest of this class had stopped being students and become audience, one boy still held a stride. Because he would not stop, thirty of you found legs again, and found them running. Because he would not stop — no one will be expelled today. **Huo Yuhao: the job earns you, as of now. Class president.** Your whole class's work-forfeit is set aside. Today, it is the only prize at Shrek you cannot challenge."

The plaza stood quiet. Then, from somewhere in Qiu Yuan's income-tax of resentments, came a sound: applause — the lean, duty-paying sort — which spread through the boy ranks until it was actually kind. Across the line, Wang Dong's face said he had not lost a presidency but acquired a phenomenon, and he disliked the second more.

Later, on the dormitory stair, the heir of the Qiu clan of White Birch looked sideways at the roommate he had spent two days pricing and said, suddenly: "You could have taken the office. I saw the arithmetic this morning — you finished first of every province boy in the run."

"The office was priced to defeat and keep," Jin Yang said. "The keep-half hires your week." And then, on the threshold of 105, as if closing accounts: "Today belonged to the boy who thanked servers. Note the pattern, Qiu Yuan — the lap-count is the least of what she tallies."

## Part 5: Night Ledger

Shrek's freshmen dormitory did its accounts at night in every room at once: sore legs tallied by the groan, new enmities by the silence under the door. Room 105 balanced its own smaller books under a single oil-shaded lamp, two desks facing opposite walls in the approved manner of boys who had not yet decided whether the other was weather or landscape.

Qiu Yuan had the clan habit out before his boots were off: a stiff-backed notebook, page ruled in three columns — *name, house, threat* — which was already longer than the visible class. "Dai Huabin, first column, first line, underlined twice," he recited, in the tone of a man reading tomorrow's obituary column. "Wang Dong, house unlisted — unlisted, where is he *from?* — ninety-lap finish while carrying a man. Huo Yuhao: no house, no known attribute, nothing except —" he touched the pen to the page and would not quite write the word — "— except *legs.*"

"Write 'legs'," said Jin Yang, from his own desk, of whose own deliberately brief ledger more anon. "It's the true column today."

"A clan debt book is not a diary, Jin Yang."

"Everything a man carries is a debt book, Qiu Yuan. Some are just ruled in arithmetic."

The heir of the White Birch Qius considered this, found one corner of it acceptable, and wrote *LEGS* with the flourish he reserved for insults that would outlive him. Then: "You'll sign the protocol, by the way. I drafted room terms after the stair." He slid a page across the lamplight — numbered, in triplicate of neatness. Item one: *no visitors after third bell.* Item two: *forge wake-time announced the previous night, never sprung.* Item three: *questions of a man's cultivation are forfeit of tea.* He had even left a signature line.

Jin Yang read it twice — the second time slowly, because it deserved to be taken exactly as seriously as it had been written. "Agreed," he said, and signed, which formally constituted the entirety of the White Birch-Shrek treaty of the year, and was filed instantly in Qiu Yuan's notebook with the reverence of pedigree. "Add item four."

"Item four."

"Nothing that matters lives on paper, or in mouths, or in this room. What matters is getting up tomorrow at the same pace we ran today." He tilted his own small worksheet across the lamp — and Qiu Yuan saw three bare ruled lines: *roster, laps, office.* Nothing else. No attributes, no ranks past the publicly announced, no hometown detail beyond the registrar lists. Written plainly enough that the whole academy could read it and learn nothing it does not already sell at the gate.

"That," Qiu Yuan said, with the pity of the lettered for the literal, "is the thinnest diary I have ever seen."

"It is the only page of mine I'll ever sign."

They banked the lamp before the night bell, and 105 quieted to two breathing rhythms learning to tolerate each other the way river stones do, while thirty kilometers of finished laps settled into young muscle down the whole freshman wing — the first night inside Shrek's walls when both doors answered to their own keys. A home, measured strictly, is any room whose arithmetic you agree to keep.

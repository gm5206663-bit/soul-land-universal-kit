## Part 1: What a Team Is (day 1)

"The assessment," said Zhou Yi, "is not a tournament for children who want applause. It is the School taking attendance of the future. Humor the terminology-from today, because the examiners will: **an assault-system soul master does not stand where his safety-system brother must stand. A control-system soul master does not do the assault's dying for him. You have been a class.**" The chalk made four columns on the board — assault, defense, control, sub-support — and raked them with a line like frost across a window. "From this week you are instruments. Stands are carved by the team, not the boy. Square three will pair mixed, square four will pair real, and God help the team that picks its three favorite friends and calls the mixture a formation."

The class laughed the thin laugh of people who had all picked their three favorite friends.

"Filed," Qiu Yuan breathed, two seats over, with the shiver of a man watching his whole world arch into regulation. Jin Yang, beside him, was doing different arithmetic under the lecture's noise: the bracket's three names, the four column names, the honest holes between them.

*Ox: front-stand, close, patient — defense.* *Weigh-man: seeing, angles — control, or near it.* *And the post between them…* The smith's boy looked down at his own hands. Eleven and hammer-worn. Two rings that belonged to none of the four columns in any way he could feel proud of. He thought what boys in his village thought when the harvest chart came out strange: *the chart is not the field. The field is what stands in it.*

Still. The columns were the first piece of school language that smelled, even faintly, of craft.

## Part 2: The Class Walks the Works (day 3 — canon ch 10, caught late: G-row logged)

Tuesday's third hour, the notice on the course slate read: *Class 1 — Soul-Guidance Works orientation. Attendance compulsory.*

The whole corridor went down the east slope in a chattering clot — most of them stepping into the giant furnace-hall for the first time in their lives: sixty-odd freshmen milling under the big beams, goggling up at the presses like calves in a cathedral, while masters in forge-aprons went on about their business with the unbothered speed of weather.

Instructor He and two colleagues did the walking — the founding speech, plain as bread: the age of the soul-tool, the age of the tool that thinks for you, the wars fought since the forging machines grew wings. The class drank it in a version of silence.

Jin Yang walked near the back, in the strange billow of a boy revisiting a place through sixty fresher pairs of eyes.

He watched Huo Yuhao most of all. Not spying — how you watch weather when weather matters *this* week. The fish-stall boy stopped longest at the tolerance benches, head bent over a machine-formed edge. Nobody could see what his eyes were doing there. But his quiet was the kind the bracket's own post recognized from classrooms. A boy receiving a second education through his pupils while the first one went on around him. *Division-bound,* Jin Yang thought, unjealous, filing it in the language of hammering: *different ore. Same earth.*

At the hall's far end, his own bench — tolerance-station six, his edge-scraps still pinned to the blotting-board — a master leaned over and noticed a freshman standing beside it with the proprietary patience of somebody home.

"And yours?" the master said, eyes crinkling toward Instructor He.

"Six Sundays," said Instructor He dryly, from under his tea. "The boy with the spare key."

By evening there were eleven new dark-fingered freshmen sworn to weekend visitation, and the wing's talk of the works had changed its sound, and the block of Class 1 said nothing at supper about having stood at center of anything. The ox said it for all of them without being quite sure what he meant: "Brother, the hall looks *smaller* once they've all breathed in it."

"No," said Qiu Yuan, with rare quiet accuracy. "The class got bigger."

## Part 3: The Spring (day 4, evening — the lake stall; G09 paid)

Thursday dusk: a first-autumn wind off the water, smoke-thread rising from the stall line, and the bracket at *their* boards by now — which is to say, at Huo Yuhao's fish counter at the hour the regulars thinned and the owner could finally lean on his own stack of skewers and let his shoulders come down from the day.

They were trading weather for fish, the standing economy of the shore. Deng Wu demolishing. Qiu Yuan interrogating the menu's margins. Jin Yang paying by the old rule of his purse. Then the proud young woman two stalls up — the pretty-sharp one with the river-governor skirt and the flickable temper — cursed in a most un-lake-like register, and shook out of her sleeve a thing that went *tik* and clattered to the counter in pieces.

Tang Ya of the Tang Sect scooped it up and sat glaring at the world as though the mechanism had betrayed HER.

"Third time this week, sister Tang," said Bei Bei, mildly, from the neighboring stall — big-brother calm, big-sleeve grace, and the tribal patience of somebody who has watched many springs die of over-enthusiasm. "The sleeve-box hates rebuilds in this damp. You should let it rest at the workshop."

"Let it rest," the sect-master of the Tang Sect echoed, dangerous with innocence. "The legendary hidden weapons of the glorious past, *resting*. I can see the ancestors *resting* — oh, hush, they've felt worse rooms than mine—"

And her sleeve spilled again the little dismemberers of her trade: the spring-coil, three tiny feather-pale darts. Then the whole cursed thing, mechanism and all, rattled loose onto the fish-counter between the stalls, and slid. It came to rest against Jin Yang's five fingers, which had come down over it like the lid of a toolbox, half out of reflex.

The shore hushed itself as shores do.

What the boy from White Birch Province was holding was a failure of manufacture. The spring-coil was mis-seated by less than a wheat-grain's thickness; the feather-darts had picked up grit between their folds; the firing-lever wore a burr where — *three minutes on Wei's bench, old bench files, one heat, one polish, half a thought* — where in his family's forge, this would have been finished work before the tea got cold. All of it crossed his face, unmerchantlike, entirely legible to the two people watching him hold the broken thing.

Jin Yang did what White Birch men do before saying anything about somebody else's tools: he set the pieces down, aligned, exactly where he'd found them, and said with careful, vagrant-boy respect: "The spring's seating shifted. Not the spring's fault. The seat just needs closing half a grain, and the lever wants ten seconds at a light file."

Tang Ya stared at him.

"Half a *grain*," she repeated, not mockingly… precisely. "And the lever — *ten seconds*?"

"At a fine file," said Jin Yang. "Coarse would eat it."

"Bei Bei." Tang Ya turned on her senior brother with her face absolutely blazing, speaking in the rapid whisper of people who have just received divine instruction at a fish stall. "Do you see. The marble-hall students of majestic Shrek walk past my broken box every day for *three years* — and this boy, in less time than it takes to order dinner— do you *see*— we can't— he *already* speaks hidden weapon! This isn't recruitment, this is *reclamation*—"

"Deep breaths, sect-master Tang," said Bei Bei, smiling at last with something that was not wholly mild. "I see it."

What with one thing and another — the two gold on the board from three weeks ago, the calm fish-stall boy sliding to the stall's far side, the whole shore tucking its knowledge quietly away — what with all of it, the next minutes moved like a rope's end finally finding its splice. (Sect-brothers, everyone would learn much later, run errands about one another like departments of a very small weather system.)

Tang Ya planted herself in front of Jin Yang, folded arms, and looked *up* into the face of the tallest boy of the bracket with the full momentum of the Tang Sect's several thousand years sailing visibly in her stern little body.

"Name."

"Jin Yang. White Birch Province."

"Your people. Smiths?"

"Three generations back before me. Iron, farm-gear, and winter mending. My grandfather added anvil-keeping to weather-watching," he said plainly, and then, because evasion had never been a White Birch crop: "My family grew up on the Tang Sect's legends, sirs. The old stories. The heroes before there were soul tools to name them. We were told them for winter work."

*That* stopped even Tang Ya a moment.

"The old stories," she said, more quietly. "You listened in the winters." Her chin came up like a banner lighting. "Then hear a new story, boy from White Birch. The heroes you were told about came out of a brotherhood that the soul-tools age is even now swallowing, an inch at a time — and the Tang Sect is not dead. It is *small*. There's a difference. You're looking at its head. And it wants, for the first time since your grandfather's grandfather had his hair, a smith's apprentice-mind with three generations of anvil-nerves in his hands and a weather-eye in his brain — to learn our techniques, our hidden weapons, and *everything else we are*, in exchange for a promise to be something it can be proud of, somewhere it can no longer reach."

"Yuhao," Bei Bei said, warmly, over the stall's little fire, not looking up, "you're about to have a small sect to look after."

"The little one's already troublesome," Tang Ya fired at him without heat, in the voice siblings are made of. "Sect-brother senior!" she declared to Jin Yang, with the glee of a fortune she fully meant to keep properly. "Say it back to your senior, so I can see whether you can do it without choking—"

Huo Yuhao, behind his board, set his knife down with the kind of perfect dignity reserved for these lake-side ambushes — and looked across, full-faced, at the boy who had once dropped back half a lap for him in the iron, and smiled without any staging at all: tired, small, entirely real.

"Sect-brother," Huo Yuhao said.

In the pond of clear evening quiet after the word, Jin Yang from White Birch Province — son of anvils, admitted student of monsters, the standing post of Class 1 — stood looking between the brothers and sisters this age had managed to leave: the legendary brotherhood patched and poor around a lakeside fish fire, still deciding not to die.

"Sect-brother senior," he answered, steadily, without choking. To *both* of them.

## Part 4: The Old Man Is There (day 4, night)

On the way back through the dormitory gate, the lamplight lay down the passage like a measuring tape of brass, and at the gate's foot the old doorkeeper dozed in his chair as usual, at midnight's first edge, all bones and weathered plainness, no crest anywhere on him, no weight in the world that Shrek usually bothers weighing.

Deng Wu, ahead, bumbled through the gate unthinking, lore-drunk on sect-tales. Qiu Yuan was already three corridors deep in contingency-filings about the sect economy. Jin Yang stopped.

No one at Shrek stopped for the old man. The boys of the wing had two years of afternoon practice at not-stopping. The old custodian's hour-strung face lay open to the lamp like a page that hadn't been picked up from the floor.

"Old man holds the gate," thought the boy who had been raised by people who hold gates.

He could not have entirely said why he did it, only that the village of his bones found it intolerable to go into a warm building past a man asleep in a draft, unacknowledged. He stopped and — young-loud as a door-brace being driven home — said, "Good night, old man. The gate's watched over well this year, and we don't thank it nearly loud enough."

The old gatekeeper's eyes never opened. One could almost have believed the mild face hadn't heard. Except the old man murmured — sleepily, shatteringly gentle, all age and quiet — "Gate doesn't thank back, boy. **On, go; nights are for your own candle, not for standing in mine.**"

A little draft of mild warmth seemed to pass the passage, nothing more. Jin Yang bowed an inch lower than he had intended, and went on up the steps feeling slightly scalded and inexplicably soothed.

(He would come back, of course. He would, without ever once being told why, make a whole habit out of that gate's mostly-sleeping tenant. That is the end of what this chapter can prove.)

## Part 5: Eve (day 5, before the divide)

The bracket's wall, last light: the lamp-dole done, and the school breathing around them the breath of a place existence-tested tomorrow.

"He joined it," Qiu Yuan said. It wasn't triumph; it was reverence, bankerly. "The *Tang Sect*. The one from the legends. The boy signs with the *folklore* anchored to his ancestry — I keep the boy between an ox and a ridiculous amount of weather-henceforth prognostication, and he *brings home the sect of heroes*—"

"The quiet at the stall though," Deng Wu said, slowly — the ox had said little of it all evening, generous consternation working through his mountain of loyalty. "When senior-fish-brother said the word back. Brother." He looked across at Jin Yang with the world's most guileless astonishment forming on the great porch of his face. "You feel like home now. You felt like one of us before, it is true. But something got *younger* in you today, at the shore. For the first time I seen anything get younger in you."

Jin Yang, half-silhouetted in the sundown rest, did not answer right off. He had the manual of the Xuantian Skill folded small in his inner robe-pocket, like a fresh-winter woodnote. The Mysterious Jade Hand's first six movements stood pressed into knuckle-memory from Bei Bei's two lantern-hours' showing at the fence behind the works. and at stall-adjusted mathematics, the whole exchange — the spring, the seat, the reel of smallest machines the ancestors left behind — sat very small, and very solid, against the great big machines thumping away east of the dormitories.

He picked up his slate; wiped its whole day clean; with the carpenter-pencil drew, once and only once, the same small neat figure his father chalked on the shed-beams back home at every good closing: the slant-ticked five of a ledger summed out. He pressed the chalk down through the centermost line.

"Some legends," said the weatherman's boy, "aren't for your remembering."

His eyes left the shed-beam figure and found the arc-lamps — no — the east-gate sky, which had sluiced itself properly toward dark while they spoke, as if the weather itself were filing.

"They're for closing the seam they're meant for. And tomorrow," he said, cool as the chalk the whole wall knew by heart: "the assessment counts a seam. So get up at dawn."

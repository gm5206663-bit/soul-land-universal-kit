## Part 1: Four Jobs (day 1)

"Assault. Defense. Control. Support."

Zhou Yi wrote the four words on the board and underlined them once, hard enough to shave the chalk.

"They are not titles," she said. "They are jobs. In the assessment, standing the wrong job costs your teammates bruises and costs you a match. The examiner is not grading your heart. He is grading whether the boy in front is where the boy in the middle needed him to stand."

She set the chalk down.

"Most of you have already picked your three. Most of you picked your friends. That is not automatically wrong. Loyalty is material, like iron. But a team of three fronts is three dead fronts. Carve what you have into the four jobs you have to fill." She looked over the room without hurry, the way she did everything. "Thursday, square four: teams against teams, mixed opponents. File what breaks."

The class came out of the room already arguing in threes.

At the wall, Qiu Yuan worried at the bracket's arithmetic, which was his version of worrying.

"The ox is a front. A patient front, but nobody is paying him to think sideways—"

"House Qiu," Deng Wu said mildly. "I am sitting right here."

"—and you," Qiu Yuan went on, to Jin Yang, "fights like a wall that owns a map. So which job is the wall-with-a-map? It's not assault. It isn't control. The chart has no square in it for you, brother."

He meant it kindly. It landed without padding anyway.

Jin Yang looked down at his hands. Two rings, neither of them in any of Zhou Yi's four words. He could smash and stand — which was assault's job or defense's, depending on the hour — but the team-drills the last three days had shown the truth anyway: the bracket had one front, one eye, and a big open seam wherever the other team decided to be fast.

"The chart's new," he said. "Thursday will tell us which job breaks first. That's what Thursday is for."

## Part 2: The Class Walks the Works (day 3 — canon ch 10, caught late)

The slate at third hour read: *Class 1 — Soul-Guidance Works orientation. Compulsory.*

Sixty freshmen went down the east slope into the furnace-hall chattering. For fifty-nine of them it was a first visit. They gawked under the beams, whispered across the big presses, reached fingertips toward the formation plates until the nearest master cleared his throat once, like a barn door opening.

Instructor He took point with two other masters and the standard speech: the age of the soul tool, the wars it changed, the craft of making a weapon think before its wielder does. Plain words, steady voice. The hall listened with sixty sets of clean ears.

Jin Yang walked at the back and watched his classmates learn his Sundays.

Near the tolerance benches he spotted Huo Yuhao standing still over a machine-cut edge. The fish-stall boy studied it a long time without touching it, head down, hands folded. You could not see his eyes from this side. But six weeks of classrooms had taught Jin Yang a thing about stillness like that. Some boys froze because they were lost. Some froze because they were missing nothing.

Huo Yuhao was missing nothing. Jin Yang filed it with the nod the run had taught them both, and let the boy's education stay his own.

At station six, a master found himself standing beside a freshman who had the easy leaning patience of a boy at his own gate.

"And this one's yours?" he said to He.

"Six Sundays," said He, through his tea. "The boy with the spare key."

The word went up the wing and down it, and by next morning three more freshmen had applied for Sunday privilege on the strength of nothing whatsoever — which was, everyone equally agreed, the most Shrek thing that had happened all term.

## Part 3: The Spring (day 4, evening — G09 paid)

Free-night shore. Smoke over the water. The bracket at their spot by Huo Yuhao's board, eating fish in the slow hour when the line thins and the owner can lean on his stack of skewers.

Deng Wu had stopped chewing. Beside him, Qiu Yuan had gone very still with his skewer halfway to his mouth, filing at speeds visible to the naked eye. Jin Yang was aware of them the way you are aware of weather on the edge of a roof — warmly, and without turning to look.

Tang Ya's stall stood two spaces over, next to Bei Bei's. Half the shore knew her by sight — sharp face adapted largely to make trouble, loud laugh, took offense at her own luck faster than most people's insults. Nobody bought much at that stall. It wasn't clear what it sold.

That night it sold moment.

Something in her sleeve went *tik*.

She swore — loudly, in a register you don't use at a fish counter — and a mechanism spilled onto the counter between the stalls. A spring-coil, three feather-pale darts, their housing, all in pieces. The whole mess skidded, came apart, and fetched up against Jin Yang's hand, which had come down on top of it flat, like the lid on a toolbox. Reflex.

The stretch of shore went politely quiet.

He looked at the pieces instead of at her. A spring had walked off its seat by under a grain's width. Grit in the dart folds. A burr on the lever. In Wei's shop that was three minutes. After, the tea wouldn't even have gone cold.

He put the pieces back exactly where they had landed — a White Birch habit, which said: not my tools, not my call — and spoke with care.

"The spring isn't the trouble. The seat is. Close the seat half a grain, and give the lever ten seconds with a fine file, she'll shoot true."

Tang Ya stared at him.

"Half a grain."

"More than that and the spring won't ride."

"Ten seconds," she said. Then she turned, at a volume sharing equally with the far shore: "Bei Bei. *Bei Bei.* Three years this thing eats sleeve. Fifth-years pass it every night, nobody sees past the rattle. This boy just fixed it with his eyes. Are you taking this in? We can't— Bei Bei, he already *speaks* hidden weapon."

"Breathe, sect-master Tang," said the tall soft-mannered man at the next stall. He was smiling, but it was the smile of a man who had stopped being amused about the wrong thing. "I have taken it in."

Bei Bei rolled a reed mat off the stall's rear peg and spread it on the grass behind the counter, and on it, from his sleeve, he laid out five things: a round coin-light capsule no longer than a thumbnail, a coil of bird-pale wire, a small thick needle, a six-finger bracer of dark steel. And finally a folded strip of hide, covered in needle-prick dotting. Tang Ya stopped mid-roundhouse and went quiet with a feeling like envy being managed.

"Show him the brotherhood," said the sect-master, sitting down on the grass in front of everything, crosslegged like a merchant receiving an ambassador. "All boy. All ears. Sit."

Tang Ya wheeled on Jin Yang. She was half his size, arriving like weather.

"Name."

"Jin Yang. Jinxi Village."

"Family work?"

"Three generations before me. Iron, farm tools, winter mending." And straight — because White Birch doesn't dodge and his family had raised him on this exact story: "We were raised on the old tales, miss. Tang Sect ones. Heroes from before there were soul tools with names. That was winter work, the story-telling."

That stopped her. A beat. Two.

"Winter work," she said, lower. "Then hear one told warm, smith-boy. The brotherhood those tales came out of is getting swallowed by that big hall over the hill, one inch at a time, and the Tang Sect is *not dead*. It is small. There is a difference." Both hands landed on the counter between them. "I am its head. It wants a boy with three generations of anvil in his hands and weather in his skull, to learn our techniques, our hidden weapons, and everything else we are — in return for one promise. That he takes the craft somewhere it can't reach anymore, and becomes something it would put on a banner."

Bei Bei opened his mouth — then shut it, thoughtfully, like the man had decided to watch history happen instead of narrate it.

Huo Yuhao set down his knife. He looked up at the boy who had dropped back half a lap for him in the iron — looked for a moment at Tang Ya, a moment at the pieces of the sleeve-box — and smiled, small and warm and real, in the way of somebody who has finally found a relative about the same size as their trouble.

"You're about to have a sect-brother junior, Yuhao," Bei Bei said.

"Sect-brother senior," Tang Ya snapped at Jin Yang, elegant as a magistrate with a writ. "Say it. If you can."

The poor, patched-up, still-standing brotherhood of heroes waited around its fish fire. Jin Yang looked from the sect-master to the senior, and bowed, straight-backed, to both at once.

"Sect-brother senior," he said. Both the words.

He didn't choke.

"Good," said Tang Ya, flushed like she'd just won money. "Then the terms are simple. We don't sell the craft piecemeal — it's a brotherhood before it's a kit. You get the Xuantian Skill from the beginning, the slow engine everything else runs on. You get the Mysterious Jade Hand's foundations when your knuckles stop being soft — Bei Bei will look after that; he knocked me through six moves on a fence and I've never paid it back. Hidden-weapon basics start as *reading*: what this spring is for, why this dart's tail is cut that way. You break down the old stock before you're allowed to make any new. And you come fix my sleeve-box. That last one's not a perk, that's the toll." 

"Yuhao does stall half the week, fence lessons the other half," Bei Bei said, unspooling a forearm measure of pale wire and handing it over without ceremony. "You'll shadow his schedule on the fence side; the stall side is optional. On Sundays you hammer metal like always. The Tang Sect doesn't poach Sundays from a forge that taught you right."

## Part 4: The Old Man Is There (night)

The dormitory gate lamp burned down to brass. The old doorkeeper was asleep in his chair at the gate's foot. He was always asleep there, bones and a thin coat, no crest anywhere on him. Nothing for Shrek to weigh. So Shrek walked by.

Deng Wu had already gone through, loudly retelling the stall. Qiu Yuan had gone three corridors inward, filing. Jin Yang stopped.

Nobody stopped for the old man. In three months Jin Yang hadn't either. Tonight it came out wrong: going in from the cold, past a man asleep in a draft, without a word.

*A man holds the gate,* his grandfather would have said, about the old fellows who took duties nobody noticed till they stopped being done.

"Night, old man," he said, young enough to be clear and old enough to carry across the passage. "Gate's kept good this year."

The old man didn't stir. But he murmured, clear as day behind the shut eyes, so gentle you would have sworn it was nobody you should have surprised:

"Gate doesn't thank back, boy. On you go. Nights are for your own candle — not for standing in mine."

The draft moved down the passage. Somehow warmer.

Jin Yang stood a breath longer than he'd meant to. The old man's face in the lamp was as plain as bread and as old as fields. Whoever he was, whatever the school paid him to lean on this gate — if the school paid him anything — the boy from Jinxi could feel, animal-plain without any argument, that the lamp stayed good wherever that face was.

He bowed an inch lower than the reply covered, and went in.

He'd come back the next night without deciding to. That is as far as this chapter can prove.

## Part 5: Eve

Last light at the wall. Tomorrow, the draw.

"The Tang Sect," Qiu Yuan said. Not crowing — the banker voice, quiet, of a man whose account has proven out. "The one from the legends. I spent half the term taking notes on a boy for a file, and the boy went and joined the folklore I was raised on, for techniques the world forgot it knew."

"The stall, though," Deng Wu said. He'd been quiet all walk home. "When the fish-stall senior said the word back. Brother." He looked at Jin Yang with the world's least complicated amazement. "Something in you got younger tonight. First time I ever watched anything in you get younger."

Jin Yang sat quiet in it for a while. The Xuantian Skill manual stood folded small in his inner robe. The Mysterious Jade Hand's first six movements rode in his knuckles, sore and pleasing. Small things. Foundation things. Heavier than their weight, in the way a winter pack is heavier than its weight.

He picked up his slate and wiped it, and drew on the clean face, once, the little slant-ticked character his father chalked on the shed beam when a day's accounts closed true.

"Some legends aren't for remembering," he said. "They're for closing the seam they were made for."

He looked east along the wall, past the lamps, toward the grounds where the zones stood chalked and cold and waiting.

"Tomorrow the draw counts a seam. Up before dawn, both of you."

The wall didn't argue.

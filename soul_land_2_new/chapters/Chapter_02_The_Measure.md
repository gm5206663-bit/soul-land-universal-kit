## Part 1: The Slow Line

By mid-morning the enrollment line had become a small town.

Clan banners stood planted wherever a servant could find dirt. Mothers did collar repairs on sons who had stopped asking for them two hills back. Somewhere ahead, a candidate in mourning-gray silk was explaining the concept of appointments, in a carrying voice, to the back of a registrar's head. The two parasol sisters, three places behind Jin Yang, had opened their second basket and were quietly feeding everyone within reach.

And four places ahead, the young master of the Qiu clan of White Birch City was telling a half-circle of younger boys, at leisure-class volume, how his family's letter differed from certain other letters one might see in the line.

Qiu Yuan. Twelve years old. Twenty-one ranks. Dressed for a coronation.

He recognized Jin Yang at the fourth turn of the line and drifted over, hands folded, walking like a magistrate entering a village he expected to purchase.

"You," he said. "The boy from Master Chu's academy. They accepted your papers?" His eyes did a quick tour — dusty shirt, rope belt, a pack that had seen weather. "Well. Shrek measures the soul, I hear. Not the sleeve."

"Shrek measures," Jin Yang said, and finished his steamed roll.

That was the entire reply. His father had repaired a plough for the Qiu estate two springs back, and his father had opinions about men who looked at sleeves before blades. Qiu Yuan waited for more words, got none, and gave his verdict anyway, louder, for the benefit of the neighborhood.

"They measure properly here, you know. Rank crystal. Age-slate. Strike stone. No favors for hometown paper."

"Good," Jin Yang said. "I don't have any other paper."

Somewhere within the Qiu system of etiquette, that sentence landed in a folder with no label. Qiu Yuan's mouth did a small administrative motion, settled on dignity, and he retreated back to his banner.

"He trains three hours a day to talk like that," said the elder parasol sister, behind Jin Yang.

"Longer," said her sister. "You can hear the practice."

---

The tents, when the line finally bled there four by four, had three instruments.

A palm-size **rank crystal** in a brass bracket. A flat **age-slate** of bone-white stone for the wrist. And behind both, standing free in the dust like an old soldier, a waist-high **stele faced in soul-steel**, chalked over a lifetime of old scars.

A senior student with a registrar's brush called numbers. A teacher sat at a tea table under the awning, and his head came up only when the crystal said something interesting. This being the Shrek gate, his head was mostly down.

Two candidates in front of Jin Yang, the boy in gray silk produced two yellow rings with ceremony, and received Shrek's exact level of wonder.

"Approved," said the registrar, without looking up. "Fee, two gold coins."

The silk took that badly, and kept taking it badly all the way into the compound.

Then it was Qiu Yuan. Wrist to the slate: twelve years, two winters. Palm to the crystal: the stone lit, climbed, stopped.

"**Twenty-one**," the registrar said, with professional warmth.

Down the line, the Qiu banner rose two inches, like it had been trained to.

"**Jia — one hundred five.** Jin Yang, of Jinxi, White Birch City? Papers."

## Part 2: Twenty-Three

The letter passed two pairs of hands. The registrar broke the wax and read; the seal answered her thumbnail with five soft pulses of light — mountain-sign, one pulse per ring of the man who had pressed it. Real sender, or very expensive forgery. She nodded it along.

"Age-slate."

He laid his wrist on the bone-white stone. The slate lit up from underneath, like frost catching sun.

"*Eleven winters and a spring.*"

"Crystal."

He set his palm on it and sent a thread of soul power up his arm, as Chu Shan had taught him — measured, like pouring from a kettle you plan to set down full.

The crystal climbed. Ten. Fifteen. Nineteen — the place where prodigies usually stopped and the brush was already moving — twenty-one. Twenty-two.

**Twenty-three.**

The brush did not move. The registrar's eyes went from the crystal to the boy, and then back to it, checking it for a thumbed pan. The crystal stayed where it was.

"Twenty-three," she said, in a voice that had lost its professional warmth and found arithmetic instead. "Age eleven. Cultivation twenty-three. Star Luo provincial recommendation — verified." A short silence while procedure caught up. "Martial soul, please."

Under the awning, the tea-table teacher set his cup down.

---

Jin Yang called it as he had been taught: all of it, because Chu Shan's other lesson — the one after foundation — was that a lion you hide learns to hide.

Gold light stood up in the tent.

The mane came down across his neck and shoulders, bright and nearly solid. His eyes went amber, pupils drawn thin. His knuckles broadened and the claws came out of the nail beds, gold-edged, a straight twenty centimeters. His whole frame widened a handspan inside his shirt seams. And the aura of the golden lion went out over the measuring ground like a question asked very politely of every soul present: *kneel, or be counted?*

Half the line forgot it had been measuring anything. Two candidates flinched as their own souls twitched against their skins. The younger parasol sister stopped chewing her date, mid-chew.

Behind Qiu Yuan's face, several expressions filed reports and were overruled. The sound he made instead, he would insist for the rest of the year had been a cough.

Then the rings came up.

**Yellow.**

**Purple.**

The registrar's brush recorded the first ring by reflex, as it had recorded hundreds that morning. For the second, it simply stopped in mid-stroke. Every doctrine in the world put a second ring's ceiling near seven hundred sixty-four years. A ring with the purple color of the thousand-year tier did not orbit an eleven-year-old boy.

It was doing so.

The tea-table teacher came around the awning, mug forgotten. He was a middle-aged man with quick pale eyes, the kind of face that read three questions into one scratch of a beard. A senior student murmured "Teacher Wang."

"Golden Lion, metal type, king of the ground-cats," the teacher said, not as a question. He looked at the mane like men at rare books. "The dueling stockyards could tell me your species' temper without asking." He turned the full weight of the pale eyes onto Jin Yang. "First ring yellow — four, five hundred years by the depth of it. I'll not quarrel. But the second — a second ring cannot be purple. Yours is." A pause, perfectly pleasant. "Explain that, boy."

The tent had gone so quiet you could hear the canvas creak.

"My teacher paid my life into those hunts," Jin Yang said. "The price is mine to pay. It isn't mine to sell."

Teacher Wang looked at him for a long, still moment. Then he smiled like a man whose favorite kind of morning had just walked in.

"Good," he said. "Keep it. We've heavier doors at this school than a dossier. Shrek does not collect doctrine, young man. Shrek collects the possible." He gestured at the stele. "One strike. Your best. Solidly on the face, please. You will not hurt it — better arms than yours have tried with mo—" the claws extended the last notch "—more rings."

## Part 3: Two Cun

Jin Yang walked to the stele.

In his head, the ledger opened by itself, as it always did. Reserve: **one hundred hundredths**. Window available, twelve minutes at full pour; not needed, not opened. The stone asked for a strike, not a war. One claw. Full measure. Nothing else spent.

His father: *strike true or don't strike.* Gongshu: the stance sets itself, your job is to listen. Chu Shan: a strike is arithmetic before it is anything else.

He drew the right arm back and drove the morning's first **Golden Lion Claw** into the face of the soul-steel stele.

The sound was a bell going off inside a mountain — and then stone itself shrieking, five furrows of chalk-white opening in one breath. When the gold light folded back into his knuckles, five parallel gouges stood faintly smoking on the old metal face.

The registrar was on her feet. Teacher Wang was already crouched with his thumb in a furrow, pressing, and then — scholars — smelling it, like the stone was a letter in a difficult hand.

"**Two cun**," he announced, thumb to knuckle, like a man measuring a fish. "Two clear cun of soul-steel face. One strike. A Grandmaster of the twenty-third rank." He straightened up. "Registrar. Your brush is dry."

"Approved," said the registrar faintly, dipping it at last. "Freshman dossier, class assignment at dawn. Certification and dossier fee — **two gold soul coins**."

Two gold coins left the oilskin purse. Twenty-nine gold and four silver remained to him, and because he had been a smith since he was eight, some part of him did the translation automatically: *one third of a six-gold commission set; sixty-odd days at a bellows.* He put the thought down with the money and did not wince for anyone.

"Badge **Jia-105**," said the registrar, handing him a wooden tag with the number branded into it. "Rules folded inside. Key on the cord. Freshmen dormitory, second court, east flank. Second floor, the room where the light lands." And then, because she had measured four hundred souls that morning and this was the first one who had made her day strange: "Your teacher's letter said you were ready. It understated."

---

He was nearly out of the tents when the footsteps came alongside — calm, unhurried, smelling of tea.

Teacher Wang did not fall in like a guard. He fell in like a colleague, which was rarer. He said nothing for thirty paces, only watched the crowd mill around them as if choosing where to set a thought down.

"That strike," he said at last. "You did not spend the second skill. The rings sat still. The claws came out, gold came down, argument over. Most students your age would have spent everything in the purse to impress a stone. Did your teacher also train you not to spend?"

"He trained me to count first," Jin Yang said. "Spending is the easy half."

"'Count first.'" Wang repeated it with approval, like a man tasting a new rice. "Half our inner-court graduates still spoil it. Keep the habit — it will embarrass your seniors and it will make you friends worth having." They came to the edge of the tent shadow. "All year, people will ask about the purple ring, waiting for a number to carry home. You'll come to know the two kinds of askers — the ones who want to learn, and the ones who want to repeat. Feed the first kind. Starve the second. The faces differ by about this much." He measured a hair between two fingers. "Enjoy the dormitory, young master Jin. The rules are long and none of them matter except the one about tea."

He was gone back into the shade before Jin Yang could thank him.

## Part 4: Room 105

The freshmen's dormitory stood on the second court's east flank — old stone, wax-polished floors, sunlight in the corridors. At the foot of the second-floor stairs an old man in grey was sweeping with the patience of a monk, white beard against the broom handle, no hurry in the universe.

Jin Yang set down his pack and bowed — one worker's bow to another — his father's bow to estate stewards. "Good morning, Elder."

"Morning," said the broom, agreeably, and swept on.

Room 105's lock accepted his key without complaint. Beside the door, on fresh paper in a registrar's running hand, was the room's plaque. Two names, two columns:

**JIN YANG — QIU YUAN.**

Two beds. Shrek did not ask freshmen whom they liked; the academy sorted them by list and home district and let the boys do their own settling — the same plain system that, somewhere down another corridor, on another morning, had reportedly put two boys of identical ignorance behind a door they had then spent the morning arguing about.

Jin Yang read the second name twice and let the morning give him the face. Qiu Yuan. White Birch's certificate of nobility, looking at sleeves.

Well. A roommate from home could steal no secret that mattered, because the secrets that mattered did not live in dormitories. And on the other hand — and he would not have said this for gold — after a corridor of strangers, there was a schoolboy's comfort in a face he'd already had a conversation with.

Inside: two beds, two desks, one window divided fairly, pine scrubbed to the grain. He weighed it for three breaths and took the window bed — not because it was better, but because a smith keeps his hammer where his hand knows to find it, and the bed by the window had a bedpost that could take one. Blanket rolled out. Hammer set at the bedfoot. Chu's broken-wax letter into the desk drawer.

On the board at the end of the hall, a clerk was pasting a fresh scroll in still-drying ink: **FRESHMAN CLASS LISTS — POSTED AT DAWN.** Down the corridor, behind some other door, a boy's voice was enumerating what sounded like room rules, one through four, in a tone of great and wounded precision.

Then the boots came back up the corridor — fast ones, stopping at 105 — and the door opened on Qiu Yuan of White Birch, travel-dusted boots, key in hand, his smile clearly prepared for an empty room and now being put away.

They looked at each other across one scrubbed pine floor.

"House Jin," said Qiu Yuan, in the voice of a man reading a difficult verdict aloud.

"House Qiu." Jin Yang nodded at the second bed. "That one's yours. Window's mine — the hammer stays with me, and that's not negotiable for religious reasons. I wake at forge-hour. It's early. I'll tell you the night before, every time."

"Warning the night before."

"That's the rule. A smith's room runs on announcements." Jin Yang sat on his bed and began, at last, six days of the road coming off with the left boot. "The lists go up at dawn. Whatever class we're in, the teachers here begin with running. My advice is to eat like you mean it and sleep like you're owed it."

"Running," Qiu Yuan repeated, suspicious of the mattress already. He set his case on the second bed and opened it with the ceremony of a man unpacking an embassy. "They hardly run *clans* this soon."

"House Qiu," Jin Yang said, working the second boot, "they'll run us first."

Qiu Yuan's unpacking paused, halfway through a hairbrush. Something in the boy's face — not fear; inventory — counted his roommate's certainty and weighed it. "You say that like you've seen the teacher."

"I saw the *gate registrar* lose her profession over arithmetic, House Qiu. The teachers'll be worse." The boot came off. "Sleep well. Tomorrow we'll see whose pride was worth what."

Down the corridor the rule-giving voice had reached number seven, with no end in sight. Jin Yang put the hammer exactly where a tired boy's hand would find it in a dark room, and went to sleep before the summer dark had finished settling, which was a village skill no academy taught.

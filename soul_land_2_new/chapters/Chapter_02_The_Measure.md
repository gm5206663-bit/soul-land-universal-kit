# Chapter 2 — The Measure

The enrollment ground of Shrek was less a queue than a slow river, and by mid-morning its bank had grown the usual debris: clan banners planted by servants who then stood sweating in their shadow, mothers re-pinning the collars of sons who had not asked for it, and one young master of the Qiu clan of White Birch City who was telling a semi-circle of juniors precisely how his family's recommendation differed, in quality of paper alone, from *certain other documents* one might observe in the line.

Jin Yang, whose document lived in an oilskin sleeve stitched by his mother, stood three paces downwind of the pennon and ate the second of Old Wei's steamed rolls, watching the measuring sweep of tents ahead the way he once watched grain settle to see where the stones were.

Qiu Yuan recognized him at the fourth turn of the coil.

"You," said the Qiu heir, twelve years old and twenty-one ranks loud about it, drifting over with the confiding solemnity of a magistrate. "The village boy from Master Chu's little academy. They admitted your dossier?" His eyes made a polite inventory: road-stained shirt, rope belt, pack gone soft-crowned from six days of weather. "Well. Shrek measures the soul, I am told. Not the sleeve. Commendable." Which was, in the arithmetic of boys, a garland cut from nettle.

"Shrek measures," Jin Yang agreed, and finished the roll, and dusted the crumbs from his palm, which was the entire reply, because his father had repaired a Qiu plough two springs ago and his father had opinions about men who noticed sleeves before blades.

Ahead, the queue bled four-by-four into canvas shade, and above the tables hung the three instruments of the morning: a palm-size **rank crystal** set in a brass stand; a flat age-slate of gray bone-white stone whose characters you were invited to lay your bare wrist upon; and behind them all, freestanding and battered like a siege veteran, a waist-high **stele** faced in soul-steel, its surface a museum of old gouges under a fresh coat of chalk dust.

A senior student with a registrar's brush called numbers. A teacher at the tea table looked up from his cup only when the crystal did something interesting.

Qiu Yuan stepped up on the thirteenth bell. Wrist to age-slate: *twelve years, two winters.* Palm to crystal: the stone kindled, counted, and settled. "Twenty-one," the registrar announced, with professional warmth. The Qiu pennon lifted two inches higher down the line, as it had been trained to.

"Jia — one hundred four," called the bell-man. "Jin Yang, of Jinxi, White Birch City? Dossier and letter."

---

The letter passed two pairs of hands. The registrar cracked Chu Shan's wax and read, and the wax — pressed by a Soul King's own ring-handed stamp — answered her thumbnail with five soft pulses of sequencing light, mountain-sign, one per ring, which was either proof of sender or proof of forgery, depending on whether the light behaved. It behaved. She nodded the dossier along.

"Age-slate."

Wrist laid cool on stone. The slate lit from underneath like frost catching sun: *eleven winters and a spring.*

"Crystal."

He put his palm where six thousand boys a year put theirs, and called a thread of soul power up the channel of his arm, the way Headmaster Chu had taught him — measured, like pouring from a kettle you intend to set down entire.

The crystal climbed. Ten. Fifteen. Nineteen — where it usually stopped, this or a finger better, and the registrar's brush had already dipped for the standard commendation — twenty-one, twenty-two —

Twenty-three.

The brush stayed dipped. Somewhere behind Jin Yang's shoulder the Qiu pennon lowered itself, unwitnessed, two inches.

"Twenty-three," the registrar said, in a voice that had lost its profession and been slowed by arithmetic. She checked the crystal as one checks a scale for a thumbed pan. It stayed climbed. "Age eleven, cultivation twenty-three, Star Luo provincial recommendation — verified." A pause. "Martial soul, please."

And the teacher at the tea table set down his cup.

---

He called it the way Headmaster Chu had taught him: all of it, because there comes a day when hiding the lion only teaches the lion to hide, and Chu Shan had spent five years feeding it the opposite lesson.

Gold light stood up out of the morning.

It ran up him the way sunrise runs up a blade of standing metal — from the ground, with weight behind it — and his hair rose and solved itself into the mane, golden and near-substantial, crowning down along his neck and shoulders; his eyes came up amber-gold with the pupil's black gone narrow as a scratching-awl; his hands had already thickened at the knuckle, and the claws came out of the nail-beds like drawn wire, gold-edged, measuring an honest twenty centimeters of malice. The forge-built frame of him broadened a handspan to every eye in the tent, and with the frame came the aura — the king-of-beasts' old quiet question, put to every soul in the ground of enrollment at once: *will you kneel, or will you be counted?*

Half the queue forgot, for a breath, what they had been queuing for. Two candidates' own beast souls something-twitched against their skins and were still. The Qiu heir produced a small sound that he would spend the rest of the year claiming was a cough.

And over Jin Yang's possessed form the rings rose and took their orbits.

Yellow. … Purple.

The registrar's brush recorded the first color automatically, as it had recorded five hundred yellows that morning. It did not record the second. It simply stopped, mid-stroke, because the hand behind it had joined the entire measuring ground in doing the only possible mathematics: a second ring begins, by every doctrine any academy has taught for ten thousand years, at *seven hundred-ish*. It ends, at the most heroic and theoretically stretched ceiling, one thousand years of age — *for the purple color to show, the kill must take the beast in the thousand-year tier itself, and the child before them stood eleven springs tall.*

"Martial soul: Golden Lion," said the teacher from the tea table, coming around the table with his cup forgotten. "Metal type. King of the ground-hunting cats." He had the pale, quick look of a man who read three arguments in a single beard scratch; a senior student murmured "Teacher Wang," and he made the noise teachers make when the recognition is kindly meant but the lesson is urgent. "The dueling stocks of this Academy could tell you his species' temper," he said pleasantly to the registrar, by way of credentials, and then, to Jin Yang, in the same pleasant tone in which other men ask after weather: "Yellow first, of five hundred years or thereabout, one deduces from the color's depth. Purple second. The second, boy, by doctrine, may not be purple. And yet there it stands, orbiting, unspeakably here. How?"

"My teacher priced my life into the gambles, Teacher," Jin Yang said. "The pricing is mine to pay, not to sell."

Teacher Wang regarded him with the smile of a man who has just had his favorite kind of morning. "Good," he said. "Keep your secrets; Shrek has heavier doors to open you than your dossier. You will find we are not connoisseurs of doctrine here. We are connoisseurs of the possible." He waved a hand at the stele. "One attack. Your best skill, on the stone. It will not harm it; better arms than yours have tried with mo—" a beat, as the claws extended a final notch "—more rings."

---

Jin Yang stepped to the stele.

In his head, the count ran itself, the way it always ran: reserve, one hundred hundredths. Window available, twelve minutes at the hundred — held closed. This stone wanted a strike, not a war. One claw then; full pour, honest valuation; nothing else spent.

He drew the whole right arm back into the mane's shadow and put the morning's first Golden Lion Claw into the soul-steel face of the stele.

The sound was a bell struck inside a mountain — and then the sound of stone giving up five furrows of itself in one breath, squealing chalk-white to the sky. When the light folded back into his knuckles, five parallel gouges stood smoking faintly on the battle-scarred face, and the registrar had risen, and Teacher Wang had stepped closer with a scholar's unbecoming eagerness, pressing his thumb into a furrow and smelling it, as if the stone were a manuscript in a dialect only he remembered.

"Two cun," he announced, thumb to knuckle, like a man measuring fish. "Two clear cun of soul-steel face, one strike, from a Grandmaster of the twenty-third. Registrar, you may un-dip your brush."

"Fifty-fifth of the morning's class list," the registrar said faintly, inking again at last. "Approved for freshman dossier, class-assignment pending. Certification and dossier fee: two gold soul coins."

Jin Yang counted the coins out of the oilskin purse — thirty-one gold and four silver, of which two gold now walked across the table to become a wooden badge with his number burned into it — and, because he had been a smith since he was eight, he thought *twenty days at the bellows*, and set the thought down with the coins, and did not wince for anybody to record.

---

They gave him a badge (Jia-105), a fold of residence rules in tight official character, a key on a cord, and directions: freshmen's dormitory halls, second court, east flank; boys' hall, second storey; the door at the end of the corridor where the light wanted.

He found it near midday — a long, sunlight-honeyed corridor that smelled of wax, old stone, and broom-straw, and at the corridor's foot an old man in grey was sweeping with the unhurried devotion of a monk at a shrine, white beard on the broom-handle's rest, eyes mild as fasting. Shrek employed, it seemed, philosophers to sweep. Jin Yang set down his pack end, bowed exactly one worker's bow to another — his father to the Qiu steward, his master to an elder smith — and said, "Good morning, Elder."

"Morning," said the broom, agreeably, and swept on.

Room 105 took his key with a merchant's own oiled obedience. Inside: two beds, two desks, one window whose light had been rationed fairly, pine-everything, scrubbed to the grain. The second bed stood made but nameless; whatever boy belonged to it had not yet found this corridor, or had failed to. Jin Yang chose the bed nearer the window because the window framed an elm whose crown had decades of swallows' jurisdiction in it, unrolled his blanket, set the hammer of his second trade at the bedfoot like a loyal dog, and put the letter — Chu Shan's wax, broken now by registrars — into the desk's single drawer.

On the notice board at the corridor's end, a clerk was pasting a long scroll, ink still breathing: **FRESHMAN CLASS ROSTERS — READ AT DAWN.**

Somewhere down the hall, beyond several other closed doors, a boy was arguing in a pretty, exasperated voice about room rules with a roommate, enumerating them, it seemed, one through four. Jin Yang could not hear the rules. He did not need to. The morning had measured his rank, his years, his soul, and his strike, and the arithmetic was all filed with registrars now, and a smith who has delivered finished work sits down and waits for the next order.

He lay back on the good pine bed. The money-clock in his head filed today's costs: two gold, one claw-strike — five hundredths — one walk, six days, one promise. Recovery until full: the afternoon. Tomorrow: a roster on a wall, and whatever class the roster named.

Outside Room 105's door, down the long honeyed corridor, the broom went shik, shik, shik, patient as an old foreman pacing an anvil, all the way to the stair.

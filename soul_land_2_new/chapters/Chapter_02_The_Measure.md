### THE SLOW RIVER

The queue to be measured was less a queue than a slow river, and by mid-morning its banks had grown the usual debris.

Clan banners stood planted by servants who then sweated in their own shadow. Mothers re-pinned collars on sons who had not asked for it and would pay for it in posture for the next three hours. Fathers inspected rival fathers over their sons' heads and found them wanting. A candidate in mourning-gray silk was telling the rope of hopefuls that he did not object to waiting, he objected to *procedure*, and the rope ignored him with village patience. The parasol sisters, three places behind Jin Yang, had by now unpacked breakfast for half the neighborhood, and were discussing in food-merchant detail which academy banners were cheap dye and which were honest thread.

And four places ahead, in the full ceremonial dress of a house that had never once worn road dust, the young master of the Qiu clan of White Birch City was explaining to a semi-circle of juniors how his family's recommendation differed, in quality of paper alone, from certain other documents one might observe in the line.

Jin Yang, whose document lived in an oilskin sleeve stitched by his mother, stood downwind of the pennon and ate the second of Old Wei's steamed rolls, watching the tents ahead the way he had once watched grain settle to learn where the stones were.

Qiu Yuan recognized him at the fourth turn of the coil.

"You," said the Qiu heir, twelve years old and twenty-one ranks loud about it, drifting over with the confiding solemnity of a magistrate at a village hearing. "The boy from Master Chu's little academy. They admitted your dossier?" His eyes made a polite inventory: road-stained shirt, rope belt, a pack gone soft-crowned with weather. "Well. Shrek measures the soul, I am told. Not the sleeve. Commendable."

Which was, in the arithmetic of boys, a garland cut from nettle.

"Shrek measures," Jin Yang agreed, and finished the roll, and dusted his palms, which was the whole reply, because his father had repaired a Qiu plough two springs ago and his father kept opinions about men who noticed sleeves before blades.

The heir of the Qiu clan waited for more reply, received none, and pronounced judgment anyway. "They admit by measurement here, you know. Real measurement. Rank crystal, age-slate, strike stone. There is no home-paper favor at a Shrek tent." He said it as if warning Jin Yang against a disappointment; he said it loud enough for the parasol sisters, who rolled their eyes at each other with the synchrony of long practice.

"Good," said Jin Yang, with his first visible interest of the morning. "I don't have any other kind of paper."

Qiu Yuan paused. Somewhere in what was probably an excellent education, that sentence filed itself perfectly and arrived nowhere. "We shall see," he said, retreating in good order.

"We shall," said the elder parasol sister, from behind Jin Yang, in a tone that suggested the morning was already paying for its cakes.

---

Above the measuring tents hung the instruments of the morning, and the whole rope of families bent around them like wheat around a millstone.

Three things stood on the tables: a palm-size **rank crystal** in a brass stand, cloudy until called; a flat age-slate of bone-white stone on which you laid your bare wrist; and, freestanding behind them, battered like a siege veteran and chalked fresh, a waist-high **stele** faced in soul-steel, its surface a museum of other mornings' scars.

A senior student with a registrar's brush called numbers from a board. A teacher at a tea table under the awning lifted his head only when the crystal did something interesting, which, this being Shrek, still meant his head was mostly down.

The rope bled four-by-four into canvas shade. Somewhere ahead, the mourning-gray silk produced, with noise, two yellow rings, and discovered Shrek's exact level of admiration: the registrar said "Approved, two gold fee," without looking up. The silk took it badly. The rope took it like weather.

"Ma," said the younger parasol sister suddenly, to nobody's mother in particular, "why does everyone hold their breath at the crystal, they know their own rank at home—"

"Because home numbers are kitchen weights, duck," said her sister. "And this is the market scale. Hush now and eat your date."

Qiu Yuan stepped up on the thirteenth bell.

Wrist to age-slate: *twelve years, two winters.* Palm to crystal: the stone kindled, bored politely through the teens, and settled. "Twenty-one," the registrar announced, with professional warmth. The Qiu pennon, ten paces down the rope, lifted itself two inches higher, trained as the family hawks.

Jin Yang was next.

### TWENTY-THREE

"Jia — one hundred five. Jin Yang, of Jinxi, White Birch City? Dossier and letter."

The letter passed two pairs of hands. The registrar cracked Chu Shan's wax and read; the wax, pressed by a Soul King's own ring-handed stamp, answered her thumbnail with five soft pulses of sequencing light — mountain-sign, one per ring — proof of sender or proof of forgery depending on whether the light behaved.

It behaved. She nodded the dossier along.

"Age-slate."

Wrist laid cool on stone. The slate lit from underneath like frost catching sun: *eleven winters and a spring.*

"Crystal."

He put his palm where six thousand boys a year put theirs, and called a thread of soul power up the channel of his arm, the way Chu Shan had taught him — measured, like pouring from a kettle you intend to set down entire.

The crystal climbed. Ten. Fifteen. Nineteen — where it usually stopped, or a finger better, and the registrar's brush had already dipped for the standard commendation — twenty-one. Twenty-two.

Twenty-three.

The brush stayed dipped. Behind Jin Yang's shoulder, unwatched and spontaneously, the Qiu pennon lowered itself two inches.

"Twenty-three," the registrar said, in a voice that had lost its profession and been slowed by arithmetic. She checked the crystal the way one checks a scale for a thumbed pan. It stayed climbed. "Age eleven, cultivation twenty-three, Star Luo provincial recommendation verified." A short pause, while the morning's procedure caught up to its own news. "Martial soul, please."

Behind the awning, the teacher at the tea table set down his cup.

### THE LION, MEASURED

He called it the way Chu Shan had taught him to call it: all of it, no rationing, because there comes a day when hiding the lion only teaches the lion to hide, and Chu had spent five years doing the opposite lesson to his spine.

Gold light stood up out of the morning.

It ran up him the way sunrise runs up a standing blade — from the ground, with weight behind it — and his hair rose and solved itself into mane, golden and near-substantial, crowning down along his neck and shoulders; his eyes came up amber-gold, the black of the pupils gone narrow as a marking-awl; the knuckles thickened and the claws came out of the nail beds like drawn wire, gold-edged, an honest twenty centimeters of it; and the forge-built frame of him broadened a handspan to every eye in the shade, and with the frame came the rest of it — the king-of-beasts' old quiet question, asked of every soul in the enrollment ground at once: *will you kneel, or will you be counted?*

Half the queue forgot, for a breath, what it had been queuing for. Two candidates twitched where they stood as their own souls answered before they could. The younger parasol sister stopped chewing mid-date. The Qiu heir produced a small sound that he would spend the rest of the academic year claiming was a cough.

And over Jin Yang's shoulders, the rings rose and took their orbits.

Yellow.

…Purple.

The registrar's brush recorded the first color automatically, as it had recorded four hundred yellows that morning. It did not record the second. It stopped mid-stroke, because the hand behind it had joined the entire measuring ground in the only mathematics available: a second ring begins, by every doctrine taught for ten thousand years, at seven hundred-something — and *ends*, at the very most heroic and theoretically stretched ceiling, at one thousand years. For the purple color to show at all, the kill must have taken the beast inside the thousand-year tier itself.

The boy in front of them stood eleven winters and a spring.

The teacher from the tea table came around the awning, cup forgotten. He had the pale, quick face of a man who read three arguments in a single beard-scratch; a senior student murmured "Teacher Wang," and he made the small sound teachers make when recognition is kindly meant but the hour is urgent.

"Martial soul: Golden Lion," he said, not asking. "Metal type. King of the ground-hunting cats." He looked at the mane the way other men looked at rare books, and made Jin Yang a small, pleasant nod whose pleasantness did not soften the interrogation underneath it: "Yellow first, five hundred and thereabout, deduced from the depth. Purple second. The second, boy, by all our books, may not be purple. And yet there it stands, orbiting, unspeakably here. How?"

The tents went quiet enough to hear canvas breathe.

"My teacher priced my life into those hunts, Teacher," Jin Yang said. "The pricing is mine to pay, not to sell."

Teacher Wang was still a moment. Then he smiled the smile of a man whose favorite kind of morning has just arrived unannounced. "Good," he said. "Keep your secrets. Shrek's doors are heavier than your dossier; we will open you by them or not at all." He waved at the stele. "One strike. Your best, on the face. It cannot be harmed — better arms than yours have tried with mo—" the claws came out a final notch "—*more* rings."

### TWO CUN

Jin Yang stepped up to the stele.

Inside his head, the count ran itself, the way it always ran — reserve, one hundred hundredths; window, twelve minutes at the full pour, held closed. The stone wanted a strike, not a war. One claw, then; full valuation; nothing else spent. His father: *strike true or don't strike.* Gongshu: the stance sets itself. Chu: *strike is arithmetic before it is violence.* The drake in the red dark: *patience.* The whole syllabus of a short life, present and voting.

He drew the arm back into the mane's shadow and drove the morning's first Golden Lion Claw into the soul-steel face of the stele.

The sound was a bell struck inside a mountain — and then stone giving up five furrows of itself in one breath, squealing chalk-white. When the gold folded back into his knuckles, five parallel gouges stood smoking faintly on the battle-scarred face, and the registrar had risen without deciding to, and Teacher Wang was already there with a scholar's unbecoming eagerness, pressing his thumb into a furrow and smelling it, as if the stone were a manuscript in a dialect only he remembered.

"Two cun," he announced, thumb to knuckle, like a man measuring fish. "Two clear cun of soul-steel, one strike, from a Grandmaster of the twenty-third rank." He turned. "Registrar. You may un-dip your brush."

"Fifty-fifth of the morning class list," the registrar said faintly, inking at last. "Freshman dossier approved, assignment pending. Certification and dossier fee: two gold soul coins."

Jin Yang counted the coins out of the oilskin purse — thirty-one gold and four silver, of which two now walked across the table to become a wooden badge with his number burned into it — and, because he had been a smith since he was eight, he thought *twenty days at the bellows*, and set the thought down beside the coins, and did not wince for anybody to write down.

"Badge Jia-105," said the registrar, returning his letter's husk. "Residence rules. Key. Freshmen halls, second court, east flank. Boys' hall, second storey, the door where the light wants. Dismissed, young master of Jinxi — and young master?" He looked back. The registrar, who had measured four hundred souls that morning, was smiling like a villager. "Your teacher's letter said you were ready. The letter understated."

He was barely out of the canvas shade when the footsteps fell in beside him — unhurried, academic, smelling faintly of tea.

Teacher Wang did not match pace like a guard; he fell in like a colleague, which was somehow more unusual. For the first thirty paces he said nothing at all, only watched the freshmen's press eddy around them, as if deciding where to dock a thought.

"That strike," he said at last, conversationally. "You did not open the second skill. The rings sat still as church plate; the claws came out and the gold came down, and that was the whole transaction. A power-skill strike, cleanly spent. Tell me, young master Jin — out of what you can spare to tell — did your teacher also train you to *not* spend?"

"He trained me to count first," Jin Yang said. "Spending is the easy half."

"Count first." Wang tasted it, nodded, filed it somewhere behind his eyes. "Half our inner-court graduates still haven't learned it. Keep the habit; it will embarrass your seniors." They came to the fork where the tents' shadow gave way to open court, and the teacher stopped and looked at him head-on for the first time — the pale, quick face gentle with a scholar's real delight, which is a rare creature and usually short-lived in the wild. "People will ask you about the purple ring all year," he said, "waiting for a number they can tell their friends. You'll learn who asks for knowledge and who asks for gossip — the faces differ by about this much." He held two fingers a breath apart. "Answer the first kind. Feed the second kind nothing. Shrek is a good breakfast; eat at the right tables."

"I'll remember it, Teacher."

"See that you do." Wang turned for the tents, then turned back half a step, with the air of a man adding a postscript to a letter already sealed: "If the inner court's theory lectures ever hear of a boy who crossed a ceiling twice and priced it himself, some of us teach with our whole sleeves rolled, young master of Jinxi. Dismiss that. Enjoy the dormitory; the rules fold is quite long, and none of it matters except the part about tea." And he was gone back into the canvas shade, where the registrar's brush had already lifted for the next name.

Jin Yang stood a moment in the sunlight with the badge warm in his fist.

*Faces differ by about this much.* He had known the difference his whole life, from seed-sack mornings: his mother could read a hundred characters, and every one of them was a face or a price. He walked on toward the second court.

---

### ROOM 105

The freshmen's dormitory halls stood along the second court's east flank — old stone, honeyed light, a corridor that smelled of wax and broom-straw, and at the corridor's foot an old man in gray was sweeping with the unhurried devotion of a monk at a shrine, white beard resting against the broom handle, eyes mild as fasting. Shrek employed, it seemed, philosophers to sweep.

Jin Yang set down his pack's end and bowed — exactly one worker's bow to another, his father to the Qiu steward, his master to an elder smith. "Good morning, Elder."

"Morning," said the broom, agreeably, and swept on.

Room 105 took his key with a merchant's own oiled obedience. And there, mounted beside the door on a bed of fresh paper, stood the plaque in a registrar's quick ink — two names, two columns:

**JIN YANG — and, on the second line, QIU YUAN.**

Two beds, then. Shrek did not consult freshmen on whom they could stand; the Academy roomed them by list and province and left the settling to the boys — the same flat system that had, by long legend, once roomed a plain-eyed boy and a dawn-haired one together down some other corridor on some other morning. Two beds, one lamp, one window, split by ink. He read the second name twice, and the morning supplied the face: twelve years old, twenty-one ranks, an heirloom opinion about sleeves.

Well. White Birch had watched his awakening-lion years before the Qiu heir had. A roommate from home could steal no secret that was truly his, because the secrets that mattered did not live in dormitories. And there was a schoolboy's comfort in it that he would not have confessed for gold: a corridor full of strangers, and one face he already owed an answer to.

Inside: two beds, two desks, one window whose light had been rationed fair, pine everything, scrubbed to the grain. He weighed it three breaths and took the window bed — not because it was better, but because a smith keeps his hammer where he knows it, and the bedfoot by the window could hold one. Blanket unrolled. Hammer set at the bedfoot like a loyal dog. Chu's broken-wax letter into the desk's one drawer.

On the board at the corridor's end a clerk was pasting a long scroll, ink still breathing: **FRESHMAN CLASS ROSTERS — POSTED AT DAWN.** And somewhere down the hall, behind one door among many, a boy was enumerating, in a pretty and exasperated voice, what sounded like room rules one through four, to a roommate whose replies were too laconic to carry through the wood.

The money-clock in his head filed the day's bill: two gold, one claw-strike — five hundredths — one walk of six days, one promise. Recovery till full: by tonight.

Then the corridor gave back boot-heels — quick ones, stopping outside 105 — and the door came open on the heir of the Qiu clan of White Birch, travel dust on very good boots, a key in one hand and a grin dying quietly on the other, taking in the window bed, the hammer at the bedfoot, and him.

They regarded each other across the width of one scrubbed pine floor.

"House Jin," said Qiu Yuan at last, with the expression of a man quoting scripture under duress.

"House Qiu," said Jin Yang. "The rulers room us two-by-two. The registrar keeps the names. The beds keep their own counsel." He tipped his head at the second bed. "That one's yours. The window's mine — not because it's better, because the hammer stays where I put it. I wake at forge-hour. I'll strike warning the night before."

"Warning."

"You knock loud, at a smith's hour, or you don't knock. I speak from bitter experience of uncles."

The Qiu heir stood in the doorway, sorting this into whatever system sorted him. Something in the corner of his mouth moved first — surrender, of a small northern garrison. "Your pennon," he said, "dropped two inches at the crystal."

"Yours too."

"It did *not—*" A pause, of the family-hawk variety. "…It did. How did you see it? You were facing the registrar."

"Smiths see everything that isn't bolted down, House Qiu. It's the whole trade." Jin Yang sat on the window bed and began, at last, six days of wear undone, with the boots. "Settle in, Qiu Yuan. Dawn, they post which class we're for. Rest while the rooms are free of running."

"Running," Qiu Yuan repeated, settling his case on the second bed with very White Birch suspicion, as if the mattress might invoice him. "They run us that soon?"

Jin Yang thought of the stele, of the purple orbiting, of four hundred freshmen and the eyes of Teacher Wang, smelling chalk-dust like scripture.

"House Qiu," he said, "they run us that soon."

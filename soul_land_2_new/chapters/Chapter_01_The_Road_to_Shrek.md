# Chapter 1: The Road to Shrek

## Canon Reference: SL2 canon ch 1–5 AS ORE (entry-morning era texture; Huo Yuhao's parallel departure for Star Dou runs behind our boy's road; canon persons appear ONLY as background). Facts cited; trajectory OURS: the lion walks his own road to the same gate.

---

Dawn came up white over the Star Luo plain, and the boy met it walking.

He had been walking since the fifth watch of the night, because that was the hour his body chose now, the way it chose everything — quietly, without asking him, and always a little better than he would have chosen himself. The road dust had worked into his sandals and ground there like fine sand. His pack rode high on his shoulders, mostly flatbread and a waterskin, and inside his shirt, against the bare skin of his chest, sat a letter in a wax-sealed oilskin sleeve. He could feel its corner with every step. He had not taken it out once in six days. He did not need to. He knew the weight of it the way he knew the weight of his own name.

Jin Yang. Eleven years old. Soul Grandmaster of the twenty-third rank.

The numbers still sounded like somebody else's son when he said them in his head.

---

Jinxi Village had no wall, no arch, no soul lamp over the gate — it had no gate. It had forty roofs and a brook that ran gold in the evenings, and old men who said the name came from that, and were probably right. His father grew millet and fixed other men's plough blades on a stone anvil that had never once held soul steel. His mother kept geese and could read about a hundred characters, all of them from seed sacks. As far back as any Jin went, the family had been ordinary — ordinary the way the brook was ordinary, the way bread was ordinary. Valuable only in being there every day.

Then, in the awakening hall of White Birch City, on a morning of rain-on-tile, the ceremony master had set a six-year-old's palm on the crystal and watched light climb it like something climbing out of a well.

Behind the boy, the air had filled with a shadow of gold.

A lion. Mane like poured metal. The ceremony master — a Soul Elder of the thirties who had awakened six thousand village children and expected to sleepwalk through six thousand more — had gone down on one knee without deciding to. Innate soul power, he had whispered, as if afraid of awakening something else in the room. *Rank five.*

The geese at home ate better that month than the family did. And in autumn, on the strength of a recommendation the ceremony master wrote with a trembling brush, Jin Yang walked thirty li to the city and into its junior soul master academy, where the headmaster, a lank, bow-backed old Soul King of the fifty-sixth rank named Chu Shan, looked at the boy's thin arms, then at the gold-maned shape coiled behind them, and said four words that decided the next five years of his life:

"You'll train with me."

Five years. From the sixth rank to the twenty-third.

People in Jinxi said that was fast the way they said the river was fast in flood season — as something that took houses sometimes. They were not wrong to be careful with the word. Speed in a soul master was never the miracle; the miracle was surviving the things that speed demanded.

---

He remembered the first ring the way you remember the scar it left.

A golden lion, five hundred years grown, old for its kind … older, Headmaster Chu said, than a first ring had any rightful business being, because the ladder every academy taught — the ladder measured out by the great theorists ten thousand years deep — put a first ring's ceiling near four hundred and twenty-three years. Four hundred twenty-three was doctrine. Jin Yang was eleven and doctrine had never fed his family.

"He's your own kind," Chu Shan had said, crouched in needle-grass two hundred paces from the lion's den, breath fogging. "Same blood, same metal. Your soul will want him. Whether your body can hold what your soul wants — that is the gamble. And Yang-er, understand me: if the ring tears you open, there is no second throwing of this die. I can kill him for you afterward. I cannot unkill you."

The lion had very nearly opened him twice, once through the ribs and once through the thigh, and the ring had poured into him like the brook in flood season, like the river the village feared, and his body — his strange, quiet, womb-born body — had *held*. Held and made room. Held and sifted the beast's offering, and what came back out of the sifting was not the lion's gift anymore: it was his, purified, the dross burned off, the bloodline in his veins suddenly a shade purer, a shade nearer to whatever golden ancestor had first made men kneel in awakening halls.

The skill that settled into the ring was the lion's own killing blow, remade for his hands. Golden Lion Claw.

The second hunt, two years later, had been worse, the way deep water is worse than rapid water. An earth-armoured iron dragon — a drake, not a true dragon, iron-plated and earth-tempered and nine hundred years into a thousand-year skin — had waited for him in a ravine of wet November clay, and the hunt had lasted from noon until the dark came down red, and Jin Yang still carried the memory of that animal's patience in his spine. A thousand-year ring for a second. Doctrine put the second ceiling near seven hundred and sixty-four. Chu Shan had gone grey-faced and said nothing for a full day afterward, and then, in private, had bowed — a Soul King, bowing to a boy of ten — and said: "The academies will lie to you about what you are, to make you small enough to grade. Shrek won't. Go to Shrek."

And out of that ring, besides the skill, besides the earth-and-iron heaviness that had poured into his frame and been *adapted* — re-worn, fitted over his lion the way a smith fits a man's armor to the man and not to the armor's last owner — something stranger had settled into the soul itself. A second hue under the metal. Earth. Old, patient, ground-seated earth, grafted root-deep by the perfection of the absorption. His lion stood on two legs of the world now.

Metal for the kill. Earth for the standing.

---

The road opened up where the farmlands thinned, and three days out of White Birch City it had put him, by chance and kindness, behind the canvas wagons of Old Wei's grain caravan — four carts, mules spavined, six guards on contract whose leader was a sweating soul master of the fifteenth rank who chewed betel and told the same war story eight different ways. Jin Yang had said nothing about what he was. He walked in the dust of the rearmost cart and ate flatbread, and the mules, who should have shied at him — dumb beasts feel a predator the way grass feels weather before the storm — somehow did not shy. They only watched him with their wet dark eyes and stayed strangely, peacefully calm, the way animals are calm in the sun of a king's pasture.

The boar came on the sixth night.

It came out of the brush-line at moonrise like a landslide given tusks — an iron-bristle boar, three hundred years of bad temper packed in hide like studded mail, mad on the smell of grain, rank-one soul beast and all the more dangerous for having no idea what it was charging into. Betel-juice leader wet his blade-hand, shouted "formation," and discovered his five men had already formed one — *behind the wagons*. Old Wei stood gaping in the boar's path with a lantern in his hand like a man trying to reason with the weather.

Jin Yang put down his flatbread.

"Soul ring," he said, mostly to himself, the way a man checks a latch. And called his martial soul.

Gold light stood up out of the dark.

The second ring rose with it — one yellow, one purple — and the purple one bloomed.

**Golden Lion Body.**

The amplification took him the way the brook takes the first rain: strength doubled, defense doubled, the numbers his body had counted out among themselves months ago, in the ravine, when the drake's ring had first taught him the price of the word *hundred-percent*. Gold luster ran over his skin and settled, not as scales — his soul wore no foreign scales — but as a forged-plate sheen that followed the lines of his arms and shoulders and the high golden mane at his back, and through the soles of his feet the earth came up to meet him and hold. The drake's old ground-seat. Worn now as a lion's stance.

The boar hit him dead center at a full gallop.

He did not move. Not a step, not a slide, not an inch of dust pushed back. Three hundred years of iron-bristle fury broke against an eleven-year-old boy standing in the road as against the gatepost of the earth itself, and the sound of the impact was a temple bell rung the wrong way. The boar staggered, squealing, tusks snapped-white. Behind the wagon line, the contract guards made a single sound, all of them, like geese at home when the shadow crossed the yard.

Jin Yang's arm came up. Soul power answered the way it always answered him — counted, measured, poured.

**Golden Lion Claw.**

Light gathered into his claws and lengthened them into five blades of living gold, metal-forged and metal-hungry, and he put one rake — one — across the studded hide, shoulder to ham. Armor that had turned the guards' hacks for a dozen years opened like wet millet-husk. The boar came apart out of its charge in two heavy, steam-breathing halves, and was finished before the lantern Old Wei was still holding had stopped swinging.

A yellow-white ring lifted off the carcass, drifted, faded. Jin Yang stood in the settling dust and checked his count: the claw had drunk, by his body's honest ledger, near a twentieth of his reserve. The body amplification, running, was sipping steadily; he closed it down. Twelve minutes, the lion in him marked quietly, from full pour to running on dregs. Twelve minutes at the hundred. File it. Costs mattered. Headmaster Chu had beat that into him harder than the drake ever had: *a soul master who doesn't know his costs is a soul master's funeral notice.*

Old Wei lowered the lantern. "Immortal young master," he said, which was what village people said when the world showed them a shape they had no shelf for.

"Jin Yang," said Jin Yang. "Of Jinxi. Is any of the grain cooked?"

---

They tried to pay him at the parting — the guards most fervently, the betel-chewing fifteenth-rank loudest — and he took one meal and two steamed rolls for the road and, after Old Wei's third attempt with the purse, one copper coin, because his mother had told him once that a man who will take nothing makes helpers feel useless, and his mother was right about nearly everything except geese.

"Where does a young master of the twenty-third rank walk to, at eleven years of spring?" Old Wei asked him at the fork, half curious and half praying, the way you ask a magistrate's road.

"Shrek," Jin Yang said.

Old Wei stopped trying to press the purse then. He just looked at the boy the way men look at a storm they have already decided to pray to. "May the lion carry you, child of the brook-gold."

---

He came in sight of Shrek City on the seventh morning, climbing the last rise out of the plain with the sun still soft on his back.

It was not a city so much as an argument the world had had with the sky and won. The walls ran beyond both edges of his view, and past them the roofs of Shrek Academy climbed in tiers toward, somewhere deep inside, the old heart of the place, where — if half the stories half the junior academies told were true — a god had once planted a golden tree with his own hands. Beneath the gate towers, the enrollment press had already gathered: the line of hopefuls and their families coiled down the switchbacks like a living rope, banners of city academies, clan-pennons in every heraldic color, recommendation letters held up like passports to heaven.

Jin Yang took the letter out of his shirt at last. The wax was warm from six days against his skin. Chu Shan's mountain seal, unbroken.

*Go to Shrek.*

He walked down into the coiling line.

And so it happened that the same morning — the same sunlight on the same gray stones, forty paces ahead of where a village boy ended the line — a thin, plain-faced boy with soft black hair bought roasted wheat-cakes from a gate vendor with careful coppers and received, with them, directions to the queue's tail from a girl whose eyes were kind and whose sect was very small, and so it likewise happened, twenty paces behind her, that a boy with hair the color of a clear dawn over water, pink-tinged blue, stood glaring at the length of the enrollment line as if length were a personal insult and he intended to personally reduce it.

Jin Yang noticed none of them. The line moved. A clerk's bell struck. Somewhere ahead a child flared three rings — yellow, yellow, purple — and the crowd made its goose-sound.

"Next," the bell-man called, as the boy with golden blood stepped up to be measured.

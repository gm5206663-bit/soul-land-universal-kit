## Part 1: The Rotation Finds Its Weight

A term, it turns out, is a kind of weather: it doesn't knock, it settles in around you while you're busy under it.

Combat basics ran on schedule now. Square One cold and fast — Dai Huabin's square had stopped being a lesson and started being a processing center for falls. Square Two chalked weekly **WANG DONG / JIN YANG** in Zhou Yi's razor hand, for reasons she had not deigned to attach commentary to. "File it," was all she'd said, the first morning, when the bracket had stood before the card like three boys looking at weather. "File everything."

So they filed.

Wang Dong took Square Two that week too, three touches to none — *faster*, second time, like morning learning the room. The first two Jin Yang paid for honestly in chalk-line bruises of pride: the sunrise had adjusted his entry by half a breath, somewhere in the week, and the adjustment cost exactly what good adjustments usually cost. Nothing wasted, everything recorded — there was a freezer wind in Jin Yang's head filing footwork, as his grandfather once filed weather patterns that only mattered three years later.

The third touch mattered though.

Not because it changed the score — three to none, clean, chalked, done. The post took the third touch standing so awkwardly wrong-shaped that Wang Dong's follow-through landed on geometry that hadn't been there when he launched it, and the sunrise came up out of his own momentum with his eyebrows informally taking minutes. "That's not a stance," Wang Dong said, breathless and delighted about it. "That was a *warranty*."

"Try it again in three weeks, softy."

"Oh, *when* the warranty expires!" Wang Dong announced to no one, and walked off the lime happy.

Deng Wu learned to fall, the week's true curriculum. Teacher Zhou demonstrated it once — how a body meets lime without arguing — and then refused, by method, to demonstrate it ever again. "You'll have the ground whenever you like," she said, "for free." On Thursday the ox finally came down from **Xiaolei**'s shifted hip-throw — Xiaolei across the hall, who had threatened this on day two and kept her accounts — with all four limbs agreeable about it, sat up in the chalk, and announced to the sky, "**DEPOSIT**," which the girls' side of the room took up as a blessing.

Qiu Yuan, who fought by information, drew a second-week spar against the wing's long-limbed girl with the steel fan `[design: Fang Ruo, Class 1]` and lasted seven touches past his own expectation by the simple device of *having watched Tuesday*. The brackets' evening debriefs — instituted Wednesday, unplanned, at the usual wall — had acquired an unofficial title by Friday: *the filings*. File the butterfly's acceleration line. File how the Duke's son never feints high twice. File, and file, and file.

The weatherman had become strange and small: properly their own, by then. Strangers said it about desk-gossip on Tuesdays — *storms earlier this year; the Block's line said so Tuesday* — and Jin Yang had stopped blinking at it by then, which Deng Wu said was growth, and Qiu Yuan said was *corridor citizenship*, and both were probably correct.

## Part 2: The Prince's File — evening practice (Dai Huabin panel — canon: his persona (proud, exacting, strongest by work); canon-adjacent venue: extra-hours training ground; AU: subject `[design]`)

The east training ground kept its light late for people who paid for it in effort.

Dai Huabin was still drilling turns when Zhu Lu came out of the shadowed colonnade with her hair down from the day and the wing's whole report carried in her bearing like laundry. She brought him news in the evenings without being asked — had since they were small, since White Tiger halls first — not because he demanded it, but because she had opinions about what the house of the Duke's second son should *notice*.

This week the wing had only one headline anyway, and both of them already knew it.

"Square Two's the talk," she said, settling on a plinth at the court's edge, lacing her fingers with tidy finality. "The whole week. You saw the card today — they're leaving those two paired, Teacher Zhou's keeping it."

"I saw the card."

"They say," Zhu Lu reported, with professionally level cheeks, "'block takes two and walks; sunrise takes three and *lauds* it,' and that the farm post said *warranty*—" she paused, because the story was also annoying her, visibly, in its seventh retelling. "It's just chatter, then, Huabin. Chatter going *-up*, though. Corridors. Girls' side. Wash-price of a boy's entire reputation, these days."

Dai Huabin finished the rotation of his turn — heel, center, the White Tiger balance he had spent nine years on — and only then answered, without heat, like a man commenting on arithmetic done well by other hands:

"Then the corridors can't read."

"Hm?"

"Watch Square Two next week, Lu. Actually watch." He set to the next repetition, unhurried, not one syllable performing. "Three to nothing twice, and he'll be there for *three-to-nothing* every week until it isn't — because every week he isn't **spending** anything. Two years somebody taught that boy to cost every motion twice, and now he comes to a spar like a *receipt*." The turn completed, clean. "Butterfly's faster. Butterfly will stay faster six months. The other one — " and here the Duke's son did smile, faint and completely without humor, "the other one is filing **us**."

Zhu Lu considered this with the seriousness it deserved; it sat fully inside her training. "He's sharp-clawed, the third-year aunties say — rings, and they say the measure-officer had teachers coming to look at a *pair* round his name—" then, with precision: "Strength to waste, White-Tiger-blood. Even so. Sharp is what sharp *does*."

"To waste," said Dai Huabin, evenly. "He's had a year of yellow and one of *nearly millennial ring* under this roof, and in ten days of drills he's spent a claw and a half, by my count. That's not waste, Lu. Something priced his strength for him before Shrek did. Find me his square-card changes and his Sundays, both." No malice anywhere in it — a prince's homework, assigned in the tone of a man ordering the weather. "'Storms earlier this year,' the wing quotes him now. Fine. The Duke's house reads weather too."

"And the softy-butterfly?"

"Wang Dong." For the first time in the exchange, edge: a cold, collegial, genuinely unmet-yet edge, aristocrat sized. "Huo Yuhao's friend." He considered. "He can stay reigning six months. Sunrise is paid for in *mornings*. It's a currency that runs out at noon."

Zhu Lu filed both, which was her whole education, and rose to leave. At the colonnade she paused — loyalty's last errand — "The gap line, from the first day? In the plaza. He's kept it unpaid, Huabin."

"I know." White Tiger eyes catching the court lamps. "That's why he's interesting."

## Part 3: Hands, Week Two (day 13, Sunday, soul-tools hall `[canon-era system]`)

Instructor He had a way of being already at his bench when you arrived, no matter the hour — a hall truth Jin Yang had classified under *settlement*, like dew.

Week two, they moved off blanks and onto **edges**: real blade-steel, plain park-grade, learning the single line of work a soul-tool edge would be — a cutter's bevel, drawn inch by inch under a lens-small formation plate that warmed the steel just shy of arguing. Watch a machine-press form a blank in one down-stroke, first, as wolves watch courier hawks. Then take the same edge a hand-length at a time, two hours, believing in increments.

Jin Yang hammered. The work came easier than anything else in this city — easier than a three-touch loss, easier than memory post claw-days — and by the second hour the line he was chasing had stopped being He's assigned line and become *his own argument about the same steel*.

He stopped the boy gently once, by sitting on the tool rail nearby, holding his tea.

"You're pulling the bevel long again. On purpose, now, I hope?"

"Shorter dies," said Jin Yang, between strokes, on the exhale. "You showed us Thursday — press blank, snap-test at notch, it *sings* there. A drawn bevel bends before it breaks. Weight for weight, the hand one stays a tool longer. Not… a better edge. A more patient one."

"Hm. And if the School only pays for the fast one?"

The hammer stopped, resettled.

"Teacher," said Jin Yang, eyes still on the steel, in the tone of somebody asking for a true number, because he had been raised by people for whom numbers were bread — "if the blanks are made by machines and the School buys the fast one… why are hands still on the workbench at all?"

The instructor took a slow drink of the cooling tea, and set the cup down, and gave the boy the honest unspiced version, as the hall gave everything:

"Because a machine stamps a thousand edges alike before lunch, boy — and learns nothing from any one of them breaking. A hand remembers. Mines a life of breaks into forearms; *listens*, strike by strike. The age of the tool, son, is an age of people who want to hold a thousand of a thing." He retrieved his tea. "I keep the old bench because sometimes a thing needs holding *once*, and right, and by some poor fool who means it. Your salary can read it however it must." A pause; the small dry smile. "The paid word for you this week is *tolerances*."

The boy hammered until the bell, thinking — chiefly of a man named Chu Shan who had once snorted at a "fancier than the window" blade, and of what exactly was stamped and what exactly listened, and he made the bevel long again anyway, and Instructor He watched him do it and did not stop him a second time.

*Stamped,* he thought, slipping his chalk notes into his satchel at gate-light. *Or listened to.* He kept both words, in the drawer with the silver-fish sign, which was starting to fill.

## Part 4: Three (day 14, Monday — the year's first official shape)

Monday before first bell, the class board carried a new chalk in a class-board script:

> **NEW-STUDENT TERM ASSESSMENT — 10 WEEKS.**
> **TEAMS OF THREE. SELF-FORMED, SIGNED, FILED BY FRIDAY.**
> **Z.**

The plaza crowd pressed in and began transforming in place into negotiations — Class 1 had officially become a marketplace, currency: each other. Overhead, the cloud of the term's business; underneath, boys and girls trading harmonies and doubts at three voices each.

The market, predictably, found them before the board did.

The bracket had drifted to its usual wall — out of the negotiation crush, the three of them in surveyor's spacing — and it was exactly there that the offers arrived, in the order of everything else at Shrek: fastest first.

"Jin Yang." A girl from the southern rows — House Zhe, Zhe Yun `[design, Class 1]` — ponytail fierce as a flag, two friends bracketed behind her like economic policy. "Team of three. We lack a front. One of any number of boys would answer; I'm asking you because you didn't fall in ten days and I keep accounts."

The ox beside him was opening his mouth with the comprehensive wonder of a man watching lightning count.

Jin Yang answered like a man asked the price of a thing he wasn't selling: "Grateful for the asking, House Zhe. Our three's already together."

Ponytail considered this without offense — Shrek bred them brisk — marked the flank with a glance, and withdrew with her cabinet in good order. "When it isn't," she said.

"You won't last a week waiting," Qiu Yuan murmured behind his hand, delighted and appalled in practically equal shares; and Deng Wu said, still awed, "It counted *ten days*, brother. Nothing escapes these people."

Two more inquiries before the morning bell — one from the twins with the staves `[design]`, politely declined; one from a boy of the crafts rows `[design]` whose entire pamphlet was *steady*, declined with regret — and then the bracket stood alone again at the wall with nothing visibly achieved and everything, in fact, concluded.

The bracket didn't negotiate.

It stood looking at the board in its usual configuration — fence-line spacing, like three boys habitually covering three directions — and Deng Wu said, with pain and nobility both, "I'll be understanding, brothers, if you're shopping up—"

"House Deng," said Qiu Yuan, already withdrawing the treaty notebook, "*if*," in the clan-office voice that had no exits. "You may sign first, then, as the one whose name needs the clerks' correction least."

The three of them signed the form against the wall of the corridor, leaning, taking turns with Jin Yang's carpenter pencil, their three names standing four square in a line and nearly even: **JIN YANG · DENG WU · QIU YUAN**.

It took the whole bracket's combined composure not to react when, on their way in, Square Two's other signed name caught their eye — across the yard at the second form-desk — **WANG DONG · XIAO XIAO · HUO YUHAO**, inked in handwriting so confident it was already famous on sight.

Wang Dong caught Jin Yang looking, and wasn't even decent enough to smirk: just touched his fingertips incline-wise to an invisible hat brim, courtly and fake-rustic as a cat in a parlor, like spring bowing at winter.

"Neighborhood teams!" announced Deng Wu, inspired, aggrieved, immensely pleased with them all. "Named north and south of the same *dawn*."

"Oh, *look* at everybody, all matched up," Qiu Yuan said, with genuine warmth and utter irony both in the same voice somehow.

Zhou Yi watched the signatures come in from her sill above the plaza. Unreadable, right hand sheltering chalk-dust, left hand shading her eyes against morning. *Ten weeks,* she was thinking, like someone laying out a whole winter. *Ten weeks to learn what they cost.*

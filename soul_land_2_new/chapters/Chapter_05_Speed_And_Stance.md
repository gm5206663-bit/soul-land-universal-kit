## Part 1: First Bell, First Blood-Rules

The notice had said: bring soul, leave pride. Class 1 brought both anyway — you could hear the pride scraping through the doors ahead of the students.

The drill ground this morning was not the plaza but the **combat hall** — stone floor ruled into sparring squares with white lime, padded posts at the corners, and Teacher Zhou Yi standing at midfield in her iron Monday shape.

"You have run for me," she said, no good-morning anywhere in evidence. "You have suffered for me. Today you knock each other down for me — because running teaches legs, and fighting teaches **information**. About yourselves, first. About your neighbor, faster. Rules." The chalk counted on her fingers. "One square at a time. I call the pairs. Touch-count to three: shoulder and above. Rings stay home — this is body and footwork, not cannon work. First blood of pride counts double against you." She did the thin thing that passed for her smile. "Fall down *well*, children. It's the only thing you'll definitely need all year."

The square-assigning began in chalk-scratch order. The room did what rooms do the first time combat comes on a schedule — the loud boys got louder, the quiet ones went water-smooth, and everyone looked at everyone else with a slightly revised valuation.

"The Duke's son," Qiu Yuan murmured at Jin Yang's elbow. "First square. Watch him."

Dai Huabin's square was over in about four breaths, one white-gold blur of it, the other boy deposited respectfully and wholly on the floor. *Fast,* said the smith's habit in Jin Yang's head, *and cold, and already measuring the room.* The class made a collective small sound.

"The presidents," said Deng Wu nervously.

"Wang Dong," read Zhou Yi's chalk, harsh and level, "second square. Against — " her eyes ran the class, found the second name on some private register of balance, and lifted: "— does anyone object to my choice of **Huo Yuhao**?"

From among the crowded rows, the dawn-haired boy leaned out into the lime-light with joy undisguised, cracked his knuckles once, and gave Class 1 its first real show of the term.

## Part 2: The Butterfly, Public (his soul's display: canon-true by this era; the bout itself + all banter `[design]`)

Wang Dong fought like morning comes to a hill — fast, at angles, and prettier than should be legitimate.

His martial soul came up to a roar of half the class: wings of blue-gold light opening out of his back like the sky had decided to give him a second set of shoulders. The **Radiant Butterfly Goddess**, in the open air, in front of everyone. The hall's packed rows made the noise classes make when the gossip they've been trading for a week shows up wearing wings.

"Wings," said Deng Wu, in the tone of a man having vindications. "The roommate got carried by *wings*. No justice, brothers. None anywhere."

Huo Yuhao lost — approximately nobody expected otherwise — but he lost with the particular stubbornness of a boy who had spent the morning with his palms on dirt and kept refusing, by a stubborn half-step, to be exactly where he'd been aimed — twice. Wang Dong won the third touch overhead on momentum and said, into the fallen boy's ear and loud enough for the first two rows, "Yield, sweetheart roommate," and got dust and two fingers to the ribs for the compliment, on a technical and wholly uncounted fourth point.

The class's laughter had weather in it.

"Who else," said the chalk, tasting the room. "Who volunteers?"

Wang Dong did not look at the chalk. The sunrise boy was looking across the square, at a point halfway down a row of tired freshmen, with the provocative ease of somebody selecting a dessert with the whole shop watching.

"You," said Wang Dong, cheerfully, to Jin Yang. "**Weatherman.** Two days you've watched everything like it owes you numbers. Square with me."

The room's breathing changed again — gossip shifting gears.

Zhou Yi's eyebrows reached the question for her. Jin Yang met the sunrise stare the way you meet weather — neutrally.

"Touch to three," said the teacher. "Rings home. Square."

## Part 3: The Sunrise and the Post

He was fast.

The smith in Jin Yang clocked it in the first exchange — fast like a forge-fire's first second off the bellows, no waste, no wind-up, the wings themselves folded away as ordered and *still* fast from the floor. Wang Dong hit him in the shoulder, twice feather-light, before Jin Yang's stance had entirely agreed with reality about which angle the morning was arriving from.

Two touches. One left. The hall was making a pot-ready noise.

Some people, when they are behind, hurry.

Jin Yang stopped hurrying. The earth under his soles — the drake's ancient ground-seat, worn these two years like an old boot — settled one notch further through the lime, and for the third touch Wang Dong came in in a flash-slide of gold and *stopped* like he'd hit a door frame.

On the tile: a boy, still planted. Not moved. Not even much rearranged — shoulder already forgotten about the previous two defeats, eyes possessed of calm working information.

"He's *planted*," said someone in the rows, with awe.

"The ox said he doesn't move," somebody answered.

Wang Dong rocked back on his heels, tilted his head — re-measuring, from a whisker away — and there went the little gold-winged grin that the rest of the school year was going to have opinions about. Like morning having fun.

"Three," said Zhou Yi dryly, by the chalk. "Wang Dong, the square."

The class let its breath out. A spattering of applause started somewhere — the room liked winners, and it liked anything else that kept the morning strange.

And this was the moment the boy from White Birch stepped down off the lime, dusted the tile-chalk from his knuckles, and said, in the level voice of a man repeating basic facts of weather —

"Fast, **softy pretty boy**. Nobody's moved me like that. Ever."

The hall went absolutely still, and then — louder than it had been all morning, so loud the chalks rattled — **exploded**.

Wang Dong had gone the color of his own dawn.

"—*softy*?" Someone in the girls' third row was already weeping with delight. "—he said *pretty boy* — he said it to *Wang Dong*—" Half the boys were standing; Deng Wu had both fists pressed into his own cheeks like the hull of a ship around two trying pumps. "—oh he'll die — oh this is — **softy pretty boy**—" "—bleach the floor, they're *foaming*—"

The sunrise, being glared at from the center of the square in profound, total cold by its own self, tasted the room's verdict and made the only move actually native to him: he smiled back, sharp as slivers, and matched fact for fact.

"*Softy*," he said, tasting it. "Block." And let the word leave the tips of his teeth like the last item on a receipt. "Fallen twice in a square, still lying about measurement. **Block.** An anvil that talks. You hear me, farm post — *good because immobile*?"

"Good because still standing," said Jin Yang, walking off the lime.

"Tomorrow," said Wang Dong, to his spine, with enormous cheer. "We do it until you *move*, block-boy. Three touches compares all arguments — including whether a pretty thing can flip a stone."

"Whenever you like, softy."

The word had a life of its own by third period. By lunch the whole wing said *softy* with the reverence of a password; by the fourth, less delicate rumor had exported the complete exchange, inaccurate by then in both directions.

## Part 4: 108 (Wang Dong panel — canon: the room he rooms, the voice he keeps; AU: subject (the block) `[design]`)

Night in Room 108 held, as usual, one plain boy still awake over some small work of thread-and-pen at the desk, and one sunrise pacing the room's three available meters with the particular fury of the genuinely amused.

"Block. It stands there like a *door*, Huo Yuhao, in the lime, and asks me — softy — like reading the weather." Wang Dong's voice had climbed a full register two hours in and showed no signs of landing. "Don't *hum*, I know that hum, that's the hum that means you think something's funny—"

"Two defeats to one," said Huo Yuhao, pen still moving, mild as porridge. "You did win."

"I had two points INSIDE his reach! HE doesn't lose points, he only takes them, like — he's not like anybody." The dawn-haired boy finally stopped pacing and dropped onto his own bed edge, direly amused, deeply unwilling to be reassured. "The whole wing says *softy pretty boy* now. One day. One sentence among the children of this school. It's — it's *upsetting* to the *weather*, Yuhao, that's what he is. A blunt country storm-front."

"Qiu Yuan's boy," Huo Yuhao offered, with kindness, sewing. "The *three*, they call the bracket."

"Both of mine are better *than* his fast one," Wang Dong noted smugly, wings folded somewhere between the curtains of pride — and then ruined the preen with an honest small frown, the kind of boy-face he saved for after-second-bell in his own room and not on any field. "…He didn't buck. You saw that. No swallow, no hurry. Two touches down and he didn't spend *anything* getting angry about it. Who *is* that, at eleven, from a village?"

"The same boy who passed me water and told your president work to earn its salary," said the plain boy placidly. "New legs drink first."

"He was decent to my *roommate*, block—" Wang Dong raised one finger, with the gravity of a magistrate laying down law. "And THAT," he announced, in the voice of Titled princes conducting diplomacy, "is why I'm going to make him move."

"You'll be on the square a long time, then."

"Preparation is what mornings are *for*. Train more with me — the forge will be good and hot by winter." He blew out the last lamp, and stone-glass Shrek night took the room, but his voice had somehow gone on enjoying the whole thing in the dark: *"Block."*

## Part 5: The Post's Own Evening

The gossip arrived at Room 105 by its normal freight, of course — Qiu Yuan came home with the wing's report on the tip of his nature, but he was preceded through the door by Deng Wu, who was so full of quotable material he had forgotten to knock.

"—told the whole east wash-line — 'the farm post didn't *blink*, I think he yawned' — and by the girls' wing it's *softy* said three different ways — brother, they're going to start it as a *greeting*—"

"The plural of sunrise," said Jin Yang, scrubbing chalk-dust off his forearms in the water bucket, methodically. "Somebody told the tile it was a door, and the tile believed them?"

"HOUSE JIN." Qiu Yuan, at the desk, the full-White-Birch voice of office. "You have been *named*. *To the whole student population.* Counter-name and *all*. The wing takes these things seriously. You're the *Block* of our corridor — it makes everything we do *news*, we're *notables*—" he caught this about a half-breath too late to unsay, and had to recalibrate. "…I mean — *children* say these things cruelly—"

"You're delighted, House Qiu."

"—*delighted* doesn't come into it, it's a political *position*, anyone useful to the — put it this way, I know what it buys in corridors: until somebody beats 'block,' we're safe from being teased. **The room's pronouncements.**" He flipped open the treaty notebook and put the day's balance in writing, all the gravity of the clan: **Block welcomed — softy pretty boy returned — courage accounts satisfied; the sunrise content remains, though, its morning's complaint.** He looked up, and there was something in his look which was not entirely unlike friendship. "So you chose a *prince* for study purposes, farm post. Why him and why words?"

Jin Yang drove the towel down over the back of his neck and thought about it, honestly, like a boy with experience sorting iron.

"Because he flies like it's free," he said finally. "Everything else I know costs." He set the hammer at the bedfoot, the day's first quiet motion that had no square underneath it. "Somebody in this room should learn what a boy who isn't weighed can do. I decided it should be me — and somebody should tell a sunrise it can be looked at plain. I decided that should be me too."

"'It costs at *his* side,'" said Deng Wu slowly, from the floor, like a boy unexpectedly touching something that agreed with his whole education. "*That's* his price."

"What is?"

"Friendship with a prince. Brothers, that's *dear*. Not due to him, mind. Even sunrise — dear, as coin goes. The Duke's second won't care what's owed, nor should he." He nodded to himself, happily lost in some provincial economy now, and then remembered his errand. "Teacher Zhou left cards. Spar files, signed by her own chalk — monday slots all month. *Second square*, you know whose—"

There were two cards indeed: **CLASS 1 · COMBAT ROTATION · SQUARE TWO** in that razor script. The paired names were written in underneath. As of this week, for the following weeks —

— *Wang Dong*, first name, in her board-razors' hand.
— *Jin Yang*, under it.

"Monthly," said Qiu Yuan, warily, formally. "He will not let go."

"No," said Jin Yang, pocketing.

"Good," said Deng Wu, wide, content. "Future running weeks, brother." And then, intoning as the freezer wind: "Watch out around the corner about you, sunrise. Ah — what — that's *superstition*, brother, in the hills you see *ominous things* if a name be too long followed — go ahead and follow."

"Now," said Qiu Yuan, "you're suddenly superstitious?"

"I'm from a thousand years of hills! I am *announcing*!" the ox said, muffled in the mattress, and the night held four boroughs of Class 1's liege-laughter, and then Shrek's lamp budget went out.

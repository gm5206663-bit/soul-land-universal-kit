### THE WAKING *(Yuhao's POV — with the secret already shared)*

Yuhao woke in a waystation supply room with a fish-smell in the air and a brother at his elbow.

Not a stranger. That was the thing his body knew before his mind did — that the blue-haired boy dozing against the wall beside his cot had been there all night, and would be there tomorrow, and had been *told.* Everything. The fox had kept her promise to herself before the dew dried: her human knew about the worm under the lake, the negotiation, the four terms, the thimbleful of godhood sleeping behind his sternum. And when Yuhao's eyes opened, the first thing he saw was the courier's face, and the courier said — not *how do you feel*, not *what happened*, none of the questions that would have made it strange:

"Two days of fish debt. Tang Ya's counting. She's charging interest."

And Yuhao laughed. Which was the correct medicine, and which no one else on the continent could have administered — because no one else knew, and no one else would have led with lunch.

"You know," Yuhao said, quieter. It wasn't a question.

"I know." The courier's voice was the flat route-report voice. "The fox told me. She told me everything — the terms, the seals, the — the passenger. She told me because she doesn't lie to me, and because she decided, on the road, that you were mine to stand near. That's her word. *Mine to stand near.*" A beat. "Nobody else knows. Not the two whites. Not anyone. The story they have is the baboon, and the story holds, and it's *kind*, and we keep it."

And the boy who had been alone with every secret of his life — the scales, the woodshed, the grief, the million-year weight now sleeping in his sea — lay in a waystation cot and discovered what it was to have a secret *carried.* Not taken. Not shared as currency. Carried — the way his mother had carried his father's dagger, keeping it for a day it would be needed, asking nothing.

"He talks in my sleep," Yuhao said. "The worm. He's — he's *funny,* Hao. He's a million years old and he's *funny.*"

"Good," said Jiang Che. "You could use funny. And when he wakes up and wants to talk, I'll be there. Not to hear it — you don't have to share the inside. Just so you know the door's not locked from the other side."

From the coat on the nail, a small sleepy weight said: "*Adequate.*" And went back to sleep. And that — not the oath that was coming, not the sect, not the belt or the arts or the year — was the moment the brotherhood actually began: a boy with a god in his chest, and a boy with a fox in his coat, and a secret between them like a fire they both tended.

### THE TWO WHITES *(the waystation — the baboon story lands)*

Tang Ya had heard it from Bei Bei, who had assembled it from the evidence, and she heard it again now from Yuhao himself — *the ring flared when the baboon died; the shock took me; it's a ten-year ring* — and she bought it the way kind people buy kind stories, with her whole heart, and held it, and never once pulled the thread.

What she did instead was feed him. What Bei Bei did instead was assess him — quietly, professionally, the file growing pages — and what they both did, by the time the party reached the forest's southern rim two days later, was *decide.* The decision happened the way all Tang Ya's decisions happened: at speed, out loud, and centered on food.

They had stopped at the tree-line to eat — the last safe ground before the deep woods, a place with a bench and a spring and a view of the green wall going up forever — and Yuhao had grilled the fish (fourteen; the courier ate two; the arithmetic held), and Tang Ya had eaten five and was working on the accounting of everyone else's portions when she put down her fish and said, with no preamble at all:

"Xiao Yuhao. Do you have anyone in the world?"

"...No one. Just me."

"*Perfect,*" said Tang Ya, and Bei Bei's knuckle rapped her crown with the weary precision of long practice, and she said "*I mean—*" with her hands over her mouth, and Yuhao laughed, because he had spent two days with these people and the word *family* had already started leaking through the cracks in his guard.

The Tang Sect was laid out plainly, because Tang Ya at her most serious had decided that this boy had been lied to enough for one childhood. Ten thousand years ago, the first sect of the continent, built by a man named Tang San who broke the ceiling of the soul master's world — the legends Yuhao's mother had whispered in a woodshed. The sect that had armed an age. And then the age turned: the Sun-Moon collision; the soul tools that broke Tang steel; the orders thinned; the great house seized. The current roll of the greatest sect in history: *one Sect Master, one first disciple.* The arts intact. The treasury a fishing rod and a grudge.

"There's one thing nobody could seize," Tang Ya said, and held up a finger. "Every year, Shrek Academy — you've heard of Shrek—"

"The continent's first academy—"

"—honors the old debt. **One exam-exempt place. One seat, no test.** Bei Bei and I study there. This year's seat is open. Join the sect, and it's yours."

"The condition is the real one," Bei Bei said. "The arts are the ancestors. Join us, and you are Tang Sect for life. If you ever leave, you give back everything you learned. Every page. Consider it *before* you answer."

And the boy who had been alone for eleven years considered it for four heartbeats, and looked — once, briefly — at the blue-haired boy sitting at the edge of the fire cleaning fish bones with his nails because the fox had told him the grill needed doing and he had done it without comment. And the courier looked back, and nodded, once. Not *do it.* Just: *I'm here. It's yours to choose.*

He knelt. Three bows.

"Disciple Huo Yuhao greets the teacher."

"Second disciple," Tang Ya corrected, hauling him up by the collar with immense tenderness, "and there — now the Tang Sect is three. Wait." She counted the fire's edge. "Three. Four if the hammer-boy—"

"The hammer-boy," said Bei Bei, dryly, "has been standing at that fire for two days being either the most dangerous person we've ever met or the most useful, and I have been waiting for someone else to say it. Tang Ya. His soul is *grass.* Like the ancestor's. Like yours."

The silence that followed had weight and history and, at its center, a girl who had spent her whole life being the keeper of something extinct.

"...Show me," said Tang Ya.

### THE SHOWING *(the tree-line — the ignition)*

Jiang Che had been ready since the third watch, in the way of boys whose fathers taught them that being asked is a ceremony. He stepped into the clearing's light, set his feet the way the Hall set its feet — nothing theatrical — and let go.

The rings rose first: two yellows, bright as struck coins. And then the grass came up out of him the way morning comes out of a river — dozens of vines, blue-silver, dew beaded down every strand like strung glass, a faint gold at the tips where the sun caught them.

Tang Ya did not gasp. Later, everyone agreed; she was proud of it.

She walked into the vines the way you walk into a relative's house, and put her hand flat against the nearest strand, and her own rings answered — two yellow, one purple, rising unbidden — and her grass came out to meet his grass, blue-glowing vines winding curious and gentle among the blue-silver ones, and the two souls stood in the light holding hands.

"Blue Silver," she said, and her voice had gone somewhere else. "It's Blue Silver. You *mutated* it. Water in it. There's — what is that, *life?* The whole thing is full of—" She looked up, and what was behind her eyes was a furnace lighting: ten thousand years of the master line's stubborn honor, and the girl who had carried it alone since her teacher died, and the road, in its infinite comedy, delivering to her fire — *days after her sect became three* — a courier boy with the heirloom soul in a form nobody had ever seen.

"Show me the binding," she commanded, wrecked and imperial at once. "*Show me.*"

So he showed her. The Captain's Forms, one by one: the Trip-Line, the Two-Point Anchor, the Pressure-Seal — *hold without harm*, the Hall's rule, and Tang Ya made a sound like a woman finding extra dumplings — and she matched him form for form with her own vines, correcting one thing, stealing three, teaching two back, and the two grasses wove a fence in the clearing that no monkey in the forest could have explained.

Then she stopped. Sat down on the bench, rather suddenly, with his vine still in her hand.

"You understand," she said, not quite to him, "that I have spent my whole life being told I was the keeper of something extinct. Everyone *so respectful*, the way people are respectful at funerals." Her thumb moved along the dew. "And it's *here.* Alive. In a boy my sect fed fish to on a road."

"I know," said Jiang Che, and meant both things — the grass and the weight.

"I'm not asking you on a road. Not again." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist, briskly, a commander's weather clearing. "When you answer — if you answer — it'll be somewhere that means something. But there's a thing you should have first, and it isn't mine to make pretty." A breath. "You asked what happened to the sect. Bei Bei — tell him the tools. I'll tell him the rest."

### THE GRUDGE *(the collision, told at the tree-line)*

Bei Bei told it the way a man tells a wound that has shaped his family: precisely, without self-pity, with the numbers.

"Four thousand years ago another continent struck ours. The war that followed — our three empires carried **Tang steel**, and the Sun-Moon Empire came with **soul tools**: weapons driven by soul power, longer-ranged, heavier-blowing, that a soul master with no combat talent can fire like a crossbow. Our steel broke their lines; their tools broke our steel; the empires bled; and the sect that had armed the world watched the world stop needing it." The orders thinned; the masters drifted; the great house was seized in the second war. "What's left is what the tools can't take, because it isn't *made* of anything. It's made of us."

"The tools aren't evil," he added, into the quiet. "They're *clever.* The academy that adapted stands first in the world. A soul master who pretends the age isn't happening is a soul master the age will happen *to.*" His eyes moved, without hurry, to the courier. "You'll see both truths standing in one street, soon enough. The grave, and the forge."

And Jiang Che — who had spent the telling with his vines still out, holding dew, and who had once watched a dagger drink a baboon's light — looked at his own grass and thought about the least art and the most work, and said nothing yet, and the nothing was not empty. It was *loading.*

---

### THE GIFTS *(the oath's gifts — for Yuhao)*

The rest of the day was ceremony, because Tang Ya decided it was, and Bei Bei backed her with the purse.

From her sleeves — from *nowhere*, from a place that had held nothing a moment before — Tang Ya produced a hand-copied book: the **Mysterious Heaven Treasure Record**, the distilled arts of the first sect of the continent. The root: the Xuantian Gong, the cultivation method that would more than patch a woodshed body's slowness. Five arts: the Jade Hand for striking; the Purple Demon Eyes — and here her finger tapped twice, at Yuhao: *"This one is yours. There has never been a soul more suited to a method of the eyes."*; the Crane-Captures-Dragon; the Ghost-Shadow Track. "Hidden weapons come *after*, when your hands are worth poisoning."

From his belt, Bei Bei drew a length of black silk set with twenty-four round pieces of white jade. "**Twenty-Four Bridges Under Moonlight.** A copy of a soul tool that belonged to the ancestor Tang San himself. Twenty-four stones; each holds a space half a meter wide. My gift. Consider what it means that the Tang Sect gives its family its best things first."

And Yuhao — who had owned seven silver coins and a dagger at this time last week — held the belt like a relic and said thank you in the smallest voice any of them had yet heard from him.

---

### THE SECOND BROTHER'S NIGHT *(room-mates at the waystation)*

They walked north through the afternoon, and the walk was *different* now — a sect of three (and a fox), a sect-brother pair riding at its center — and by evening the party had made the forest's rim-camp, and the two boys ended the day as they would end every day for the next year: sitting against the same tree, because the tree was big enough and nobody had said otherwise.

"Can I ask you something," Yuhao said, into the dark. "About the — the thing you know. About me."

"You can ask anything."

"Do you…" A breath. "Do you think it's *me* still? With the — with him in there. Am I still me, or am I the thing's — house?"

And Jiang Che, eleven years old, courier's son, fox's human, third disciple of nothing yet, considered the question with the seriousness it deserved — which was total — and answered it the only way he knew how, which was the way his mother answered *does it hurt*:

"The fox has been in me since I was eight," he said. "And I'm still the one who complains about the fish. The worm's in you since Tuesday. And you're still the one who walked out of a mansion for your mother's name. Whatever else is in there — that's the house's *furniture.* You're the house." A pause. "And for what it's worth — the fox says he's funny. Million-year-old things that are funny are rare. Keep him."

In the dark, against the same tree, the boy with a god in his chest laughed — wet, quiet, real — and the boy with a fox in his coat let the laugh run without touching it, because some things you just stand near.

"*Little tide,*" the fox murmured, from the coat, more than half asleep. "*That answer was adequate.*"

From the fox, that was a coronation. And tomorrow was the forest, and after it the academy, and after it the year — but tonight the sect of three (and a fox) slept at the tree-line of the biggest forest on the continent, and the two keepers of the biggest secret on the continent slept against the same tree, and the tree did not mind.

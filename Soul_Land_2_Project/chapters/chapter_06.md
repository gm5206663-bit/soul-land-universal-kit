### ╱╱ THE SEA, BRIEFLY ╲╲

On the second night, in the deeps of a small meditation, the white place returned, and the Skydream Iceworm with it — draped across the golden sea of the boy's inner world like a satisfied landslide.

"Not bad," it said, which from a million years was a parade. "The Gong suits you. The eye-method suits you. And the four gifts are sitting up nicely." It counted them off on nothing, grandly, as a landlord counts rooms: **"Spirit Detection. Spirit Sharing. Spirit Interference — a crowd-trick, thirty meters, a blink of daze, three seconds, no more yet. And Soul Shock — the only tooth of the four. Single target, one needle. Point it with your eye-method when you must; a needle in the right half-second is a knife anywhere else."** A yawn that moved weather. "I'm going to sleep, brother. A long one. When you come to the wall where the second ring must be, I'll wake — and *then*, little host, the cold I left in you finds its true shape, and you'll understand what a million years buys." It settled, vast and dimming. "If death itself comes for you, call. Only if you live do I live. It's a good lease. Sleep well."

And Huo Yuhao woke with the dark outside and his body one grain warmer, one grain *more*, and ate his dry rations in the dark like a rich man.

### ╱╱ AFTER THE HAMMER ╲╲ *(the post-trial — the brothers, the comedown)*

The proctor left. The pens stopped. And the first thing Jiang Che did, having publicly sewn a fourth-year's hammer to the ground, was find the nearest wall, slide down it, and put his head between his knees.

"...You okay?"

"No." Muffled. Completely honest. "I used the Sun-Baptism at exchange seven. On *breathing.* That's supposed to be for *bleeding.* Exchange six was — the floor was *moving,* Yu. Thirty percent left and a plan I made up between five and six."

"You looked like you were reading a newspaper."

"I was *dying* beautifully." He lifted his head — gray-faced, grinning, entirely eleven for four seconds. "Did you see his *face?*"

"Which part. The hammer stopping, or him lifting you with one hand to make it look like a draw?"

"That never happened."

"He picked you up like a *delivery,* Hao. Then said *match's yours* like it was his idea. That's what sixth-years do when a first-year beats them in front of three examiners: they make it a favor."

"...It was a good favor." The courier slid further down the wall. "I'm going to be sick."

"You are not going to be sick."

"I might. It's been a big morning. There was a *hammer.*"

And Huo Yuhao sat down next to his brother on the assessment-square wall — shoulder to shoulder with the boy who had beaten a hammer and was currently losing to gravity — and this, too, was the brotherhood: not the moments that looked like legend, but the wall after, and the gray face, and the *I might be sick,* and the shoulder against the shoulder until the world stopped spinning.

### ╱╱ THE ROOMMATE ╲╲

The next midday, coming back from a three-copper breakfast and a lecture from a senior brother about never skipping meals and taking school work like a man, Huo Yuhao found his own door standing open, and a stranger in his doorway.

The stranger was — there was no plainer way to put it, and the whole corridor would be putting it for years — *beautiful*. Clean-cut features, skin like good paper, big eyes a pale, impossible blue, and short hair the same blue, and an air of standing slightly above the ground he stood on. The best-looking boy Huo Yuhao had ever seen, and radiantly, structurally *annoyed* at being looked at.

"You are?" Huo Yuhao managed.

The blue eyes ran him up and down like a customs officer. "Move."

"...Oh." He moved. The boy swept past, stopped, and — as if conferring a dukedom — issued the terms: five rules. No guests. No indecency. No snoring. No disturbing *him*. And the cleaning was Yuhao's now — "but don't touch my bed. Understood?"

"Why," said Huo Yuhao, who had spent ten years under a Duchess's rules and one night swearing at a back gate, "would I obey *you?*"

"Because otherwise I'll throw you out. Try me."

"I'll try you," said the woodshed boy, with a calm that surprised them both. "Outside."

"**Wang Dong,**" said the boy, delighted, offering the name like a card before a winning hand.

"**Huo Yuhao.**"

"Then good-bye, *Huo Yuhao*," Wang Dong said, smiling like a blade coming out of a cane. "Your name is about to leave this academy. Walk, idiot."

### ╱╱ THE DORM FIGHT ╲╲

The old doorman's chin lifted off his chest, and his cloudy eyes acquired, for the first time in possibly decades, an audience.

Wang Dong did not use his martial soul. He said so, aloud, twice, in the tone of a man mentioning the weather — and then he *moved*, and the moving was the point: a body like a drawn bow, legs longer than the law should allow, a first kick that arrived before its own sound. Any ordinary new boy would have been a rumor by the second one.

But the white ring was already turning at Huo Yuhao's feet, and the golden light stood in his eyes, and the *world told him things* — the loading of the hip, the half-breath before the strike, the corridor of the next kick drawn in the air like a map — and he read the map. He slipped the first by a step; slapped the ankle of the second; ducked the third and rose *inside* it, shoulder into thigh—

—into a thigh that merely rose with the blow, up, up, into a standing split no human spine had authored, while its owner looked down the length of his own leg at Yuhao with genuine academic interest, as though Yuhao were an equation behaving oddly.

Then the storm. Two legs like braided steel, a whirlwind that cracked flagstones where it missed, and Yuhao — slower, weaker, *fragile* by every measure but the one that mattered — danced in its middle on half-learned ghost-steps, reading each blow a half-breath early, a small boat that would not, *would not* go under. His palms sang where he blocked; the Jade Hand answered without being asked. He was hit, and hit, and grazed — edges only, each one a burn — and the gap between their bodies closed on him minute by minute like water rising, and he understood with perfect calm: *not enough. He is far above me. I have one needle.*

So he looked up.

He looked up with everything — and Wang Dong, mid-storm, instinct-glanced at his eyes, and caught the **purple flash** needle-deep in the gold: Soul Shock, fused with the Purple Demon Eyes, one half-second of white.

Yuhao hit him like a thrown dog, arms locked, legs cinched at the waist, and the two of them went down together — pinned, chest to chest, faces an inch apart, both breathing like bellows.

"Yield," Huo Yuhao snarled.

"In your *dreams*—" but the pause was already there: *if he'd meant it. If it had been a knife, I was dead twice.* "...Fine. I lost. Get *off.*"

Yuhao stood, backed, never turned. "Your five rules — I'll keep them. Not because of the threat. Out of respect for a roommate. But hear mine: insult me again and you'll have to kill me, because I'll have bitten a piece off you on the way down."

Something in Wang Dong's face — the hate draining out of it, and something younger looking out, startled — filed that away. He went in without another word.

### ╱╱ THE VERDICT ╲╲

At the dormitory door, an ancient voice stopped the blue-haired boy like a hand.

"You lost," the doorman said, without opening his eyes. "The fight. And the heart."

"He *beat* me with a trick! I'm twice—"

"He beat you with a *heart*." One finger moved, and tapped the old man's own chest. "Yours is a good sword-arm, child, and it has never once been offered. His has been on the table since the day he was born. Fearlessness is not a skill. That's why it wins." The finger settled. "Go on."

Wang Dong stood a moment in the sun. Then he bowed — a deep, correct, furious, *real* bow — and went in to think.

For two days the room held its own weather: two beds, two silences, one boy on fur rugs a finger deep, sleeping like the wealthy, and one boy on bare boards who — the wealthy boy noted with increasing insult — *never lay down at all*, but sat like a stoic in a wheel of white breath, hour after hour, growing.

He could *feel* it, that was the unforgivable part. Every soul master's sense said the same thing: a soul power like a candle in a shed, ten levels of anything-but-special — and every fact of his own body, his own two unbruised hands, said *and he beat me.* The arithmetic refused to close. Wang Dong had grown up being the answer to other people's arithmetics, and being a question mark's *subject* kept him awake those two nights longer than the hard bed deserved.

"Madman," Wang Dong informed the ceiling.

The ceiling, like fate, did not disagree, and the days ran out, and enrollment closed, and the night before the first bell, both beds were occupied by boys pretending the other wasn't there — which is how the greatest partnership of an age began: badly, in a ten-meter room, one inch and one ocean apart.

### ╱╱ THE TRIAL ╲╲

Across those same three days, on the far side of the academy, the standard door stood open: the Assessment Square, where the continent's unprivileged culled elite bought their chance in front of examiners with no favorites.

Jiang Che had spent the eve the way he spent everything — preparation as ceremony. His father's forms, run slow. His mother's knots, checked. The Treasure Record's first pages, read twice by candlelight, and an hour's first lesson in the **Jade Hand** — the tempering of the hands, the art of the sect's dead smiths — which mostly felt, at hour one of year one, like holding a warm stone and being patient. The fox had supervised all of it from the bedpost with the expression of an empress reviewing troops, and said only: "*Don't be interesting. Be enough.*" Then, softer, in the register she used perhaps twice a year: "The big ones swing at what they think you are. Let them. What you are is a river — and rivers are only ever met, never hit." He carried that into the square like a second knife.

The trial was simple and cruel, as Shrek liked its doors. Demonstrate the soul. Demonstrate the kit. And then — the part that thinned the line — **last ten exchanges against a fourth-year proctor.** Full contact, surrender or stop.

His proctor was a fourth-year of fourteen with three rings and a soldier's boredom, a war-hammer soul the size of a door, and the professional courtesy of a person breaking crates. Two yellow, one purple, at fourteen: another continent-pride, exactly the caliber Shrek grows like a crop.

The rings lit. The grass came out. And Jiang Che fought the honest ceiling of his life for ten exchanges, and the record of it went into the examiners' ledger like this:

Exchanges one through three: the courier *read* the hammer — the dew-beads' lens, the moisture-map, the half-breath tells — and was never where the door-size weapon landed; the square's flagstones were not so lucky.

Four through six: he bound. Trip-lines the proctor burst by weight; anchors that held exactly long enough; a pressure-seal around the hammer's haft that the fourth-year had to *work* to break, and did, and stopped being bored.

Seven: the Sun-Baptism — the only burst-mend he had — spent not on damage taken but on breath, the light feeding the tide so the tide could keep reading. The examiners leaned forward. One of them, a gray-browed woman with the stillness of long service, said nothing at all, and wrote three lines without looking down — clerks at Shrek learn early which way the wind sits.

Eight: the proctor stopped being polite — and it stopped mattering. That was the discovery the square watched happen: the fourth-year brought three rings and a soldier's whole craft to exchange eight, and the courier had already spent seven exchanges *buying the ground*. The trip-lines burst by weight at exchange four had never been removed; they had been *re-tensioned*, and now the square itself was the web — ankle, knee, hammer-haft, one moisture-read map the older boy could not see, because it was written in dew.

Nine: the hammer came down like a door closing — and struck a floor it had already been sewn to. Two-Point Anchor, Pressure-Seal, every vine he owned; and the weapon that had rung like a bell all morning rang against the flagstones and *stayed there*.

Ten: the fourth-year — three rings, fourteen, a continent's pride in miniature — set both feet, took the haft two-handed, and hauled with his back and his soul and his considerable dignity; and the hammer moved one hand-width, and stopped, held by grass.

The senior examiner's hand rose. "**Enough.**"

The square stood very quiet. The proctor leaned on his freed hammer, breathing like a man who had discovered stairs in his own house, looking at a two-ring eleven-year-old who was not, in any visible way, tired.

The examiners' pens moved. Shrek does not record victories in its trial ledgers. It records, in the old script, a single word for what it screens: *monster?* — and beside Jiang Che's name the word was written, and underneath it, in a senior examiner's hand, the annotation that would follow him through the academy for years, because clerks are gossips:

*"Took a fourth-year's hammer in ten exchanges and gave nothing back. Control-craft beyond rank. ADMITTED — flagged for the year-tutor watch."*

The ledger closed. Later, at the work-board, the fourth-year found him by the forges, on no instruction from anyone, and stood chewing a bread-heel in professional silence for a while. "The hammer-lock," he said at last, as one craftsman to another. "You didn't learn that in a ring." — "My father binds things for a living," said the courier. The proctor considered this, nodded slowly, as if a long-standing question of natural philosophy had been settled, and left. It was, in its way, a second admission.

The tuition was ten gold; the courier's savings, contract by contract since he was nine, came to seven and a half; the work-board by the dining hall — canon of the academy's own economy — supplied the rest by noon: morning forges, evening greenhouses, water for the soul-tool hall's quenchant vats. A boy who had never once in his life been afraid of work signed three lines, took a white badge and a key, and went to find his door.

In his pocket, an empress who had watched grass hold a fourth-year's hammer to the flagstones permitted herself one sentence: "*Adequate.*" It was, from her, a coronation.

### ╱╱ THE FIRST BELL ╲╲

Morning. The white teaching hall opened its doors, and the new year walked in — white uniforms, and over each heart the little green monster, grinning on ten thousand chests like a promise the academy intended to keep.

But the door that mattered most that week was a different one, and it was assigned the evening before, by the oldest man in the world.

The white dormitory's gatekeeper had looked at the courier's white badge, and at the ledger, and then at nothing at all for a long moment — the way old men look at nothing when they are reading something.

"Room 108," he said at last. "Corner room. Three beds — the only triple in the white hall. Third one's been empty eleven years." He handed over the key without quite looking at it. "Doors choose," he added, to the middle distance, in the tone of a man quoting something that had no author. "Boys just knock."

Room 108, that evening, was a war fought in silence: two territories, one bed in furs a finger deep radiating offended aristocracy, one bed bare as a plank radiating cultivation, no words exchanged in a day and a half. The door opened on this weather, and a third boy walked into it carrying a pack, a hammer, and a fox.

"...There are *three* beds," said the aristocrat, affronted, as though personally lied to.

"There's one of me," said Jiang Che. "Jiang Che. Courier, healer's son, new third bed." He set the hammer on the desk with complete courtesy. "You've met my sect brother. I've heard about the five rules; I'll keep them. My rule is one: what sleeps in my pocket sleeps in my pocket." Inside his coat, a small white weight shifted in a manner he would later describe as *insubordinate*.

The fur-bed's blue eyes narrowed at the hammer, then at the courier's hair — blue regarding blue with the suspicion of a monarch sighting a rival flag — and then, unable to find anything actually wrong with either, issued the verdict cornered aristocracies fall back on: "*Hmph.* The cleaning rotation is on the wall."

The bare-plank boy said nothing. But two days of monastic silence had built up in Huo Yuhao a pressure only the arrival of a normal human being could release, and the look he gave the third bed was unmistakably *thank heavens* — and Jiang Che, who could read a room the way his mother read a wound, saw the whole war, its history and its future, in one glance, and hung his coat on the third nail.

And in the morning the first bell rang, and a small class of monsters began to learn what fearlessness costs — and out of one corner room, three boys walked into it together: two blue-haired, one of them furious at the world and one calm as a river; the third gray-clothed and quietly astonished to be walking anywhere with anyone.

The doors had chosen. The boys had knocked.

The two blue-haired boys had both come through their doors.

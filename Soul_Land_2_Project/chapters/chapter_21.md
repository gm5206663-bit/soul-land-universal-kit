### THE KITE *(the spar's ending — canon's dual control shown whole)*

The spar's first minute taught everyone what the year would learn to fear.

Wang Dong at thirty meters, wings lit, one yellow ring and one *purple* — and then he moved, and "moved" was the wrong word: the Light Goddess Butterfly's **instinct** — no soul skill, just the martial soul's own gift — threw him forward on jets of gold from the wing-V, and he crossed ten meters in the time it takes to be surprised.

And the field went gold.

The sharing poured into Xiao Xiao — fifty meters of forest standing up in her mind in three dimensions, annotated, *thought-for* — and the black cauldron rose above her, a meter across, three-legged, two-eared, ancient beyond its shadow — and it did not chase the prince. It *intercepted* him. His mandatory path, read half a second before he took it; the cauldron arriving where he would be; the prince banking; the cauldron *already there.*

"Huo Yuhao—" the prince's voice went up an octave, "—have you any *humanity?*"

"None," said the monitor of Class One, serenely, gold-eyed, reading the prince's soul-power loading through his meridians like a man reading weather. "Left."

The cauldron moved left. The prince, mid-bank, found the sky full of bell.

And the courier — the third of the room, the third of the team — stood at the field's edge and did the thing the class would spend the year describing badly: **nothing, at volume.** His vines were out — had been out since "begin" — but they were not attacking, not binding, not even visible for the most part: they were the *floor*, threaded through the grass and the roots at ankle height across the whole dueling ground, dew-beaded, reading every footfall, every shift of weight, every landing the prince was going to need. He had not told anyone he was doing it. It was not a trick. It was the Captain's first form: *before the fight, own the ground.*

So when the prince — frustrated, brilliant, proud — finally did the inevitable and committed his first soul skill, the golden wing-V blazing as soul power flooded his forewings for the piercing dash that would go *through* the bell instead of around it—

—the ground itself reached up and took his ankles.

Not hard. Not cruel. *Exactly* enough: one stride's worth of deceleration, one heartbeat of geometry — and in that one heartbeat the cauldron arrived, gently, like a temple bell being introduced to a fly, and the prince sat down in midair with his eyes crossed and his wings folding around him like a dropped curtain, and the fox, from her stump, said "*Adequate*" to nobody, which from her was a parade.

"...Again," said Wang Dong, from inside the wings, with terrible dignity.

### THE RULES OF THE AFTERNOON *(the assessment grounds — ensemble)*

The grounds had been converted at noon: a dozen roped fields in the square before the stands, referee-teachers in gray, the draw-boards on wheels, and the first year's three hundred teams milling in a noise like a market having a nightmare.

The rules were read out once, at volume, by a referee with a voice like a法规 textbook: each team fights a minimum of ten matches across the round; wins are the currency; at the cut, one hundred fifty teams advance and one hundred fifty go home. Soul tools permitted *within* regulation — no, that was next year, don't ask — contact stops at the referee's call; surrenders honored; and the medics' tent, a white pavilion at the square's edge, was staffed by healing-attribute teachers whose whole afternoon was about to become a syllabus.

Which was where Group One's third member had already quietly been: the courier had walked the medics' tent at noon, introduced himself as a healer's son with a mend, and been assigned — by a tired teacher who saw rank badge and forge-calluses and correctly identified *useful* — a corner station and a queue number.

"*You,*" the fox said, from the coat, watching him set his station with the same care he set a forge, "are going to mend the children *we* beat."

"Somebody has to. And it means I see every team's damage profile before we fight them." A shrug. "Courier arithmetic."

"*Devious.* I approve."

### ROUND ONE *(Group One vs Group Nine — the first real fight)*

The draw came up after the first hour: **Group One versus Group Nine** — the bear team, exactly as promised, exactly as bet upon — and Wang Dong collected his laundry with the serenity of a man who had never doubted the mathematical certainty of bears.

The bear was real: a fourth-class assault soul master, rank twenty-two, whose martial soul turned his forearms into something between a grapple and a landslide, and whose team — an agility spear-girl, a support with a shield-array — was built entirely around delivering him. Sound. Drilled. The exact machine the assessment was designed to reward.

They had not met a field with two hands and a floor.

It went, the whole thing, in ninety seconds, and the stands' version afterward was a lie, because the stands saw a blur and a bell and a boy sitting down very suddenly — but the *true* version was the team's own, and it ran like this:

**Second zero:** the sharing opened, and three minds became one field. Wang Dong felt the bear's charge-line before the bear committed to it. Xiao Xiao felt the spear-girl's flank-route. Jiang Che — in the field, in the grass, in the water on every breath — felt all of it, and the floor was already where the fight wanted to go.

**Second five:** the bear came on, magnificent, a rank-twenty-two landslide aimed at the smallest target — the girl — and the *ground disagreed.* One vine, ankle height, placed a quarter-second of stumble exactly at the charge's apex — no damage, no drama, just *geometry* — and the bear's own momentum spent itself against his own footing, and the cauldron was already falling.

The bell-note rang across the square. The bear sat down in a daze, and the referee's arm was half-raised—

**—and the spear-girl, to her eternal credit, was faster than the stumble.** She had flanked hard, low, along the rope-line, and her spear came at Xiao Xiao's blind side with real speed — a good strike, a clean line, the kind that ends fights — and the Light Goddess Butterfly met it in the open air.

Wang Dong did not kill beautifully, quite. He killed *correctly:* one pass, one golden wing-strike carrying his full soul skill, and the spear left her hand at a distance of three meters and she left her feet at a distance of two, and landed — precisely, deliberately, *gently* — in a vine-cushion the courier had placed at the landing spot before she had chosen to fall into it.

**Second forty:** the support surrendered with his shield-array still glowing, because his entire team was on the ground being *not-died at* and he had done this math before.

"*Group One wins,*" said the referee, and it had taken less time than the reading of the names.

### THE SECOND-ROUND-BEAT *(the afternoon's middle - the routine establishes)*

The second fight was a spear-trio - all agility, all angles - and it lasted three minutes because the spear-trio was clever: they kept their distance, probed the edges, and tried, with real intelligence, to split the team - one spear for the girl, two for the wings, nothing at all for the boy with no visible soul skill, which was the correct read and the fatal one.

You do not send *nothing* at the floor.

The sharing mapped them. The cauldron herded the pair. And the vines - patient, threaded, invisible until they were load-bearing - took the lone spearman at his finest moment: mid-lunge, committed, perfect form, alone in the middle of a field that belonged, entire, to the third member of Group One. The Pressure-Seal held him gently but totally - hold without harm, the Hall's law, applied to a boy who would wake in the medics' corner being told he had lost to *geography*.

The third fight: a tool-soul team with a fire-array, and this one the prince finished almost alone - the Light Goddess Butterfly through smoke, correct and merciless - because the fox had read the array's heat-cycle from the coat during the countdown and the courier had passed the timing forward in one word: "*between.*"

The fourth: over in ninety seconds. The bear's cousins, come for revenge, meeting the same floor plus the year's rumor - and the rumor turned out to be the team's third weapon: the cousins charged *wary*, and wary was worse, because wary feet listen to the ground, and the ground was the enemy.

"*Group One, four wins,*" said the boards, and the stands said *the weather* in the tone people had begun using, and the day went on being, against all odds, a good day.

### THE DEBRIEF *(the medics' corner — after)*

The bear, mended — the courier's clean current washing the concussion's fog out of the boy's channels while the fox supervised from the coat with the air of a health inspector — was the first of Group Nine to achieve speech.

"You *floored* me." He said it with the specific indignation of a boy whose soul had been beaten by a *root.* "I didn't even see a soul skill. Was that a soul skill?"

"It was a floor," said Jiang Che, honestly.

"A *floor—*"

"You charged very well," the courier added, because his mother had raised him to say the true thing and the kind thing when they happened to overlap, "directly along the line the grass was thickest on. It was the correct line for you. It was just mine first."

And the bear stared at him, and then at his own team, and then — being, like most bears, fundamentally sound — started to laugh, and the laugh tore something in the square's rumor-mill that would never quite reseal: *Group One doesn't even fight you. They rearrange the ground you were going to use, and then the ground wins.*

The prince, arriving at the medics' corner with the laundry settlement glowing on his face, overheard the rumor being born and delivered the team's official position, at volume, for the stands:

"We are *very* dangerous and *extremely* handsome and the bear was a strong bear and we are *sorry about the bell.*"

"You're not sorry about the bell," said Yuhao.

"I'm not sorry about the bell," Wang Dong agreed, radiantly. "But it sounded *magnificent.*"

### THE TEN *(the day's arithmetic — ensemble)*

By the dusk bell, Group One had fought four times, and the four fights had taken eleven minutes total, and the boards had them at four wins and a rumor-column all their own — and the courier had mended nineteen children in the medics' corner, fourteen of them from other teams' matches, five of them from his own team's, all of them filed in a ledger whose real subject was the year's damage-profiles.

The night's debrief, at the drowned grill: the fox on the table, the team around it, the year's first day cut into the boards behind them.

"*Assessment of the day,*" the fox said, because she had appointed herself. "*The gray one's field is now fifty meters and the white ring has not finished surprising me. The blue one kills correctly, which is worse than beautifully. The small one hid the flute all day, which was correct, and will win them something important later. And the hammer—*" the gold eyes found the courier "—*the hammer mended nineteen children and read a year's worth of wounds while his team won four matches in eleven minutes, and the year has begun to say his name in the tone people use for weather.*"

"Weather," Wang Dong repeated. "They're calling you *weather.*"

"The courier, the weather, the hammer, the boring one," Yuhao counted, on his fingers, delighted. "You have more names than the sect has members."

"One more," the courier agreed, and stole the last fish — the arithmetic holding to the end — "and all of them are mine."

### THE NIGHT-LEDGER *(the drowned grill - the team, the fox, the arithmetic of a good day)*

The debrief ran long because nobody wanted it to end, which was its own finding.

"Four matches," Wang Dong said, counting on fingers. "Eleven minutes. Nineteen mends. One bear - floored. Two spears - one geographied, one correctnessed. One fire-array - *betweened.* And the cousins - waried." He looked at his hand. "We need more words. The year is going to need more words for what we do to people."

"We could just say we win," said Yuhao.

"Where is the *drama* in - fine. We win. Boring. Terrifying. Ours." The prince laced his fingers behind his head, prince-satisfied at maximum radius. "The bracket's coming. You know that. Somebody good is in it."

"Somebody good," the fox confirmed, from the table's edge, tail over paws, gold eyes on the boards' distant chalk. "*Two teams in this year are genuinely dangerous, and one of them is yours, and the other one has not shown itself yet. Archives hate being patient. I am being patient.*"

And the courier - the weather, the hammer, the boring one, the floor - closed the damage-ledger for the night, nineteen entries in a courier's shorthand, and looked at his team in the grill's last light, and said the thing the whole day had been walking toward:

"Same time tomorrow."

"Same time tomorrow," said the room - all of it, in stereo, in the cadence that was theirs - and the year's first day of cutting ended not in chalk or glory but in fish and arithmetic and the specific, unremarkable, load-bearing comfort of people who had decided, months ago, to be a team, and were now, finally, being allowed to prove it.

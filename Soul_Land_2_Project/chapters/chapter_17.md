## Timeline: Assessment eve, morning — the rules, the draft

### THE RULES *(Class One — ensemble; Zhou Yi's POV)*

The papers came first: *write your future direction* — assault, defense, control, support — because after the assessment the classes split by nature, and three months of togetherness ended in classification.

Yuhao wrote **control-type battle soul master**, and signed his name under it. Wang Dong wrote his own paper without needing to look at anyone else's, because the answer had been the same since a dormitory fight on the second day of the year. And Jiang Che, four desks behind and one row left, wrote **control-type** as well — because grass that binds is control, whatever else it is — and thought, as he signed, that the whetstone was about to have an aneurysm.

He was right.

"**Combat.** All of you. The tool masters, the food-system, the support — on the field, same as the battle soul masters." Zhou Yi's broken-gong voice carried the way war-drums carry. "The soul tool blurred the categories the day the Sun-Moon continent arrived. Battle instinct comes from battle. So: battle, for everyone."

The format: **team warfare, three to a team.** No more than one assault-type per team. **Rooms grouped first** — the academy had learned long ago that the team that sleeps together fights together — and the remainder free-form; the unformed, drawn by lot. The scale: near **three hundred teams in the year — and only the top hundred-and-fifty survive.** Every team guaranteed **ten fights**, ranked by wins; the top sixty-four into a knockout bracket; **"the champion team's prize will be extremely rare."**

Sixty-seven heads did the arithmetic of survival.

And Zhou Yi deployed her last weapon: "**Song Qinghan.**" The small boy stood, stunned. "Each homeroom teacher passes one student through untouched. Mine is you. My reading: you belong in the **soul-tool department**. Two choices — transfer, or pack." And Song Qinghan, who had come here *for* that department, nearly levitated saying yes.

Sixty-six remained. Twenty-two even teams. The rooms grouped first.

Zhou Yi looked at her list, and her eye did the thing it did — the half-second rest, the whetstone reading metal — and her mouth opened, and what came out was the sentence the whole room had been waiting for without knowing it:

"**Group One: Huo Yuhao. Wang Dong. Jiang Che.**"

The room of three stood up.

---

### THE ROOM OF THREE *(the draft, shown)*

It took less than a second. That was the thing the class would say afterward — that the strongest team in the year assembled itself in the time it takes to breathe, because it had *already assembled*, months ago, in a dormitory with three beds and no effort at all.

Yuhao stood, and Wang Dong stood, and Jiang Che stood, and the three of them looked at each other across four desks and a row, and no one said anything, because there was nothing to say that the standing hadn't said. They had been a team since the second night of the year — since a dormitory fight and a ruffled fox and a secret shared between three beds. The whetstone hadn't grouped them. She had *recognized* them.

"*Hmph,*" said Wang Dong, sitting back down with enormous dignity. "Finally."

"About time," said Yuhao, grinning.

"Modest and restrained," said Jiang Che, deadpan, and the class laughed, and the whetstone's eye-corner twitched with something that in another face would have been satisfaction.

The draft rolled on. Group Two, Group Three — and it was, everyone slowly realized, *good*: every grouping balanced, every weakness buffered, the temper paired with its antidote. And when she reached the eleventh name on her list, Zhou Yi paused again.

"Xiao Xiao."

A small girl stood up from the front row — *small*, a head shorter than everyone, pretty in the way clean water is pretty rather than the way fireworks are. She had spent three months being exactly middling at everything, visible at nothing, and stood now with the bewildered stillness of someone whose cover has just been blown by an authority.

"You're Group Eleven's anchor," Zhou Yi said, flatly. "You'll know why when you get there." And the small girl sat down, ears pink, and the class did not understand, and would.

*(Natural ripple: the strongest team in the year is a room — not a draft pick, not a tactical assembly, but three boys who chose each other before the academy knew they existed. The class's whisper-line moved: the sect, the room, the triangle. Whatever they called it, they meant the same thing: those three.)*

### THE BREAKFAST-BEFORE-THE-DRAFT *(the council, on the morning)*

"They'll split us," Wang Dong said, over cold eggs. "The draft. One assault per team. They'll *split us.*"

"They can't split the room," said Yuhao.

"Rooms split all the time. That's what rooms are — three people who got lucky at a door."

"That is the *bleakest* thing you've ever said about a *door—*"

"It's not bleak, it's *logistics—*" and Jiang Che, quietly, into his eggs: "If they split us, they split us. You'll be rank eighteen across a field and I'll be rank twenty-nine across the same field, and we'll still be room 108 by dinner. A draft is a *draw,* not a *divorce.*"

Silence.

"…Did you just make us all feel better with a *scheduling metaphor?*"

"I'm a courier. Scheduling is my love language."

"*Scheduling is your love language,*" Wang Dong repeated, to the ceiling, to history. "We are being comforted by a boy whose love language is LOGISTICS."

"It's a good language," said the courier, mildly, and stole the prince's last egg, and the room was ready.

### THE SHEATH *(the eve — the room's strategy + the class's read)*

Room 108, that evening, held strategy — and for once the strategy was not on the floor in chalk, because the chalk map's whole architecture had already been built, night after night, in the bones of how they moved.

"I'll take the field," Wang Dong said, flat, matter-of-fact, a prince stating his function. "Assault. The wings, the strike lanes. Anything the detection marks, I kill."

"Detection," Yuhao said. "The sharing — I cover the field. And the new range—" he glanced at Jiang Che, who had been the first person outside the secret to know what forty meters meant, and who had never once said the word *Skydream* aloud where the walls could hear it. "Forty meters, pointed. We'll test it tomorrow."

"And you bind," Wang Dong said, to the third bed — not asking, assigning, the way you assign the strongest piece to the center of the board. "The vines, the anchors, the pressure-seals. Anything that needs holding, you hold. Anything that needs *not dying*, you make it not die."

"The mend runs through me," Jiang Che agreed. "Field triage between rounds. And the clean current if anyone burns." A pause, and then the courier's addendum, the one that made it a room and not a roster: "And the fox reads the field before we step on it. She's been reading this academy for three months. She knows more about the other classes than their own teachers do."

From the coat on the nail: "*Flattery will get you everywhere, little tide.*"

"Is it flattery if it's true?"

"*Yes,*" said the fox, "*but I allow it.*"

And the room settled into the last night before the gate — three boys and a fox and a chalk floor and the certainty, unspoken because it was structural, that whatever tomorrow brought through the gate, it would meet the room, and the room was ready, and the room had been ready since a stream on the road north, when a courier with a hammer had looked at a gray boy with a bundle and been told by a fox to walk with him.

They had walked. This is where the walking went.

### THE FOUR-THIRDS PROBLEM *(the class's read — ensemble)*

Class One did the arithmetic that every class does on assessment eve, and the arithmetic produced a problem that had no name yet but would have one by the third round: **the four-thirds problem.**

Three boys. One room. One team. And the class, watching them for three months, had slowly assembled the private knowledge that the trio's combined capability was not the sum of three students — it was the product. The detection that saw everything. The wings that hit everything. The grass that held everything. And behind all three, moving between them like a fourth player the rules didn't know about: a fox that none of them had ever actually seen but all of them had heard about, in the way you hear about weather.

The other classes, the whisper-line said, had been doing their own arithmetic. The names moved through the year's rumor-mill like currencies: *the room, the trio, the triangle, the three.* Nobody had the whole picture. Everybody had a piece. And the pieces all agreed on one thing: whatever Group One was, it was more than three.

"I heard the courier's soul is grass," a Class Three boy said, at the gate, in the fish line.

"I heard it's *life,*" said his friend. "Like, the attribute. Pure life."

"I heard his fox talks."

"I heard his fox *fought a teacher.*"

"I heard," said a fifth voice, dryly, from three places back in the line — a voice that belonged to a mountain that had been orbiting a fish stall for two months — "that you should all worry less about rumors and more about the fact that my — that the Sect Master's disciples are going to sweep this year's assessment like a tide going out." And Xu Sanshi bought his fish, and left, and the line discussed the mountain's use of the word *tide* for the rest of the evening.


### THE THREE PAPERS *(Zhou Yi's scene — canon rebuilt for the room)*

The papers hit Zhou Yi's desk at the end of class, and her eye-corner twitched.

Three of them. Same word.

"*Control-type.*" **Slam** — Yuhao's paper on the lectern.

"*CONTROL-TYPE.*" **Slam** — Wang Dong's, and Yuhao turned in mild astonishment because he genuinely hadn't known.

"***STILL control-type.***" The third slam nearly broke the lectern — and this time both boys turned, slowly, to the third desk, where a courier with a hammer was studying the grain of the wood with enormous interest.

"You three," said Zhou Yi, with the dangerous patience of a woman being messed with by an entire dormitory, "did this on purpose."

"Teacher," Jiang Che said, mildly, not looking up from the wood. "Grass binds. Binding is control. I didn't write control to match anyone — I wrote it because the alternative is *support*, and the last person who called my grass support got wrapped to a tree."

The whetstone stared at him. The whetstone recalculated. The whetstone revised her afternoon's opinion of the word *support.*

"...Fine. Control it is. But the rule stands: one assault-type per team. Wang Dong — you're assault. You wrote control because—"

"Because if I can control the field through overwhelming attack, is that not control? And I fly, and I strike at range, that's field control, technically—"

"You wrote control because you don't want to be separated from Huo Yuhao, and you didn't know that assault-types and control-types train *together.* You mutilated your own paper for nothing. Change it."

The paper hit Wang Dong in the chest. He changed CONTROL to ASSAULT wearing the radiant expression of a man who has just learned his marriage is legal after all, and the third boy in the room caught Jiang Che's eye across the office and both of them, simultaneously, silently, in perfect room-brotherhood, did not laugh. Out loud.

"Out," said Zhou Yi, who had seen the not-laughing. "All of you. Tomorrow we cut half the year. Come back sharp."

And at the door, as the three of them left, she said one more thing — quietly, to the air, but meant for the room: "That team. The one you three are. I have been teaching for thirty years." A pause the weight of a whetstone. "Don't waste it."

### THE GIRL *(Xiao Xiao — Group Eleven's anchor)*

She found them at the stall.

Not the boys — the *fox.* Or rather, she found the fish, and stayed for the fox, because Xiao Xiao had spent three months being unnoticed and had therefore developed the observer's gift for noticing what others missed, and what she had noticed — from three rows back, in theory classes, through the iron days and the iron nights — was that the strongest team in the year walked with something small and white that was never quite a coat-button and never quite a cat.

"You have a soul beast," Xiao Xiao said, without preamble, without greeting, in the tone of a girl who had decided that if she was going to be drafted as an anchor she would start anchoring *now.* "It rides the coat. It reads rooms. And it is not a beast soul in the way the academy means beast souls."

Jiang Che looked at the small girl — really looked, the moisture-sense reading her the way it read everything: the surface calm, the deep watchfulness, and beneath both, not one soul-signature but *two*, stacked like a hand of cards held close. He said nothing about the second signature. He said:

"She's a fox."

"She's a *person,*" said Xiao Xiao, with absolute certainty. "Foxes don't sit like that. That's a throne-sit. My grandmother sat like that, and she was—" and here the small girl stopped, abruptly, the sentence closing like a drawer, and the boys of room 108, professional keepers of doors, moved gently past the grandmother.

"Group Eleven," Jiang Che said instead, offering the change of subject like a hand up. "You're anchoring."

"I'm *what?*" But she said it standing straighter, and something behind her eyes — the second signature, the one she kept in a sheath — *shifted*, like a sleeper turning toward morning.

"Zhou Yi doesn't group by rank," Wang Dong said, around a fish. "She groups by what you *are.* You're the anchor because whatever you're hiding—" the room's collective professional courtesy, applied without thought "—is the kind of thing a group of three builds *around.*"

Xiao Xiao looked at the three of them — the strongest team in the year, the room, the triangle. The gray one, all will. The blue one, all lightning held on a leash of manners. And the third: the one the rumor-line could not settle on, because the rumor-line kept stalling on the details — the face that photographed as *older than its years*, like something carved and then age-checked and carved again; the shoulders that a tailor would have wept over from pure gratitude; the hands with their jade-pale lines, one of which was, at that moment, absently steadying a fish-skeleton upright on the table the way a smith trues a billet, without looking, without effort; and around all of it the smell — rain on a river, green things, the storm undertone — which three months of quiet observation had taught her meant *the room's strongest piece has entered*, even before she looked up. She had assumed, for most of those three months, that he was a sixth-year the first-years somehow borrowed. Learning he was eleven had been one of the stranger Thursdays of her life. And the sentence she had been building for three months — the sentence about being middling, about being visible at nothing, about the front row as a place to hide — fell apart in her hands, and what she said instead was:

"I'm going to win my group through to the bracket. And then—" the flash-grin, the sheath showing for exactly one tooth's worth "—we'll see what happens when anchors meet."

"*Oh,*" said the fox, aloud, into the silence, in front of everyone — because she had decided, that day, at that stall, that this particular secret could bear the weight of one more keeper: "*I like this one.*"

And Xiao Xiao, who had spent three months being unnoticed by everyone, sat down at the table with the strongest team in the year and was *noticed* — and the year's assessment, already complicated, had just grown one more hinge.

### THE LAST NIGHT *(the room — the eve)*

Room 108 held its last night as a single unit before the gate, and the night was quiet the way a drawn bow is quiet.

Three beds. Three boys. One fox on the third nail. The strategy had been spoken; the chalk had been drawn; the arithmetic had been done by the whole class and the whole year. What remained was the thing the arithmetic couldn't hold: that whatever came through the gate tomorrow, the room would meet it, and the room had been meeting things since a stream on the road north, and the room was — the fox's word, filed under a heading only she could read — *adequate.*

"First place," Wang Dong said, to the ceiling.

"Top ten for the whole year," Yuhao said, to the ceiling.

"Thirty," Jiang Che said, to the ceiling. "And a ring, before the year turns."

"You're going to hunt during the assessment?" Yuhao turned his head.

"After. The sect hunts — all of us. Tang Ya's precedent, Bei Bei's guard, your detection, Wang Dong's air cover, the fox reading the woods. It's the sect's first hunt. We've been planning it for a month."

"...The *sect* hunts," Yuhao repeated, and the way he said it — the weight of *we* in it, the weight of being a team that was also a family that was also, now, a hunting party — was the sound of a boy who had been alone for eleven years discovering, for the hundredth time this quarter, that he was not alone anymore and was never going to be again.

"Sleep," said the monitor of Class One. "Tomorrow we cut half the year out of their futures."

"Copy that," said the strongest first-year at Shrek Academy, put the hammer where his pillow should have been, and slept first, because couriers sleep when the route allows.

And the fox, on the third nail, administered the clause, and permitted the ruffling, and filed the day under a heading that had one entry and was not labeled in any human language, and the last sound room 108 heard before the assessment year's first morning was not a voice at all — just a small empress, settling her tails over her nose, purring into the dark, for no one, in the register of archives being correct about everything:

"*Tomorrow, then.*"

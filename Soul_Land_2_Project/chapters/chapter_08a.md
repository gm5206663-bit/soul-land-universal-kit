# Chapter 8-B: The First Rank

## The blacksmith-craft chapter that was missing — the three months' forge arc shown on-page the way canon shows craft: the rank system, the test, the Jade Hand's mechanics, the first creation
## Timeline: Within the three months — week two through month two

---

### THE HUNDRED REFINEMENTS

The forge yard's master was named Old Qian, and he had no time for prodigies.

"Rank system," he said, the first morning, not looking up from his tempering. "You know it?"

"Some. My father is a smith."

"Your father binds things." Old Qian had read the work-board slate. "Binding is a different trade from forging. Forging is this: **ranks one through three — hundred refinements.** Take a billet of standard iron, fold and hammer it one hundred times without a single flaw, you test for first rank. Four through six — thousand refinements, and the metal starts to *answer* you. Seven through nine — you're not hammering anymore; you're *asking*. Saint Craftsmen. Divine Craftsmen." He spat into the coals. "You start at nothing. Fetch billets."

Jiang Che fetched billets.

The first week was exactly that: fetching, sorting, watching. The forge yard ran on apprentice labor — boys his age and older, carrying, quenching, sweeping ash — and the work-board's forge shift slotted him among them at one copper an hour. He did not complain. His father had started the same way, and his father's father before him.

But he was not watching nothing. The AT was watching *everything* — every strike Old Qian's journeymen landed, every angle of hammer to billet, every color-shift in the metal as it moved from cherry to orange to the near-white that meant *now or never*. By the third day he could predict the strike count between heats. By the fifth he could read the metal's grain from the way it received the hammer — where it was willing, where it resisted, where a fold would take and where it would crack.

The fox, riding the coat on a nail, supervised with the air of an empress inspecting a kitchen. "*Your hands are wrong,*" she observed on day four. "*You hold the tongs like a courier holds a parcel. A smith holds them like a swordsman holds a sword — the grip is the whole art.*"

She was right. He adjusted. The AT recorded the adjustment and rebuilt his grip overnight.

### THE JADE HAND

The Treasure Record's pages on the Jade Hand had seemed, for the first week of reading, like philosophy dressed as instruction. *Temper the flesh; conduct the force; let the whole of it move through you.* It took the forge itself to make the words mean something.

**The mechanics, as the forge taught them:**

The Jade Hand was not armor. It was *conduction* — a way of channeling soul power through the skin and bone of the hands so that force passed *through* rather than *into*. Where an ordinary smith's palm took the hammer's shock and accumulated damage — bruising, blistering, the slow calcification of a career — the Jade Hand's practitioner received the shock and passed it through the flesh into the bones and out through the feet into the ground. The hands did not harden against the work. They became *transparent to it*.

**Stage one — the conditioning (weeks one through four):**

Every night, after the shift, Jiang Che sat cross-legged on his bed with a bowl of heated water and practiced the Record's first exercise: circulating soul power through his palms in the specific pattern the text called *the riverbed* — a slow, deliberate flow that traced the meridians of the hand from the wrist across the palm and out through the fingertips, warming the tissue from within. The effect was subtle and cumulative: the skin thickened without callusing; the small bones of the hand densified; the nerves learned to report heat as *information* rather than *pain*.

By week two he could hold a billet fresh from the fire for three seconds before the burn registered. By week three, five. By week four, he picked up a bar at working temperature — hot enough to raise blisters on ordinary skin — and held it while the AT read the heat's path through his palm, and the burn never came. The heat entered, and the conduction passed it through, and the fox said, from the pillow: "*The Record's author knew what he was writing about. That is not a technique. That is an education.*"

**Stage two — the strike conduction (weeks four through eight):**

The second exercise required a hammer and a billet — and the forge provided both. The Record's instruction was precise: *when the hammer falls, do not resist the shock. Receive it. Pass it through the wrist, the elbow, the shoulder, the spine, and into the ground through the heels. The smith who resists the hammer fights the metal. The smith who conducts the hammer* becomes *the hammer.*

The first attempt nearly broke his wrist. The second was worse. The problem was not strength but *timing* — the conduction had to activate at the exact instant of impact, a window of perhaps a tenth of a second, and the AT was learning to find it the way it learned everything: by repetition, by failure, by the slow accumulation of micro-adjustments that eventually crossed the threshold from effort to reflex.

By week six he was conducting eighty percent of the hammer's shock through his body and into the ground. By week eight, ninety-five. And the effect on his forging was immediate and dramatic: where an ordinary smith's arms tired and their strikes degraded over a long session, his strikes were as precise at the hundredth as at the first — because the shock was not accumulating in his body. He was, as the Record promised, *transparent to the work*.

Old Qian noticed. Old Qian did not say anything for two more weeks, because Old Qian had been burned by prodigies before. But he noticed.

### THE TEST

At the end of month two, the work-board posted a notice: **rank testing, month's end, Association branch, Shrek City office. First-rank candidates: bring your own billet and your own hammer. Fee: two silver.**

Jiang Che had been ready for a week. He told no one except the fox and the sect — and the sect, being five people, told him to stop being ridiculous and take the sect's best wishes, which arrived in the form of Tang Ya packing him a fish for the road and Bei Bei's quiet "the forge knows its own."

The test itself was simple and merciless.

The Association's branch office in Shrek City occupied a stone building near the east gate — the same street as the fish stall, which had made the walk feel like a parade. Inside: a testing hall with six forges, three examiners, and a waiting bench of candidates ranging from a nervous fourteen-year-old girl to a thick-armed twenty-year-old on his third attempt.

The procedure: each candidate received a standard billet of iron — the same weight, the same grade, the same grain. The task: **one hundred refinements.** Fold and hammer, fold and hammer, until the billet had been worked one hundred times, and the metal's impurities drawn out, and the grain aligned, and the result judged on three criteria — *purity* (how much impurity had been expelled), *density* (how tightly the grain had been compacted), and *consistency* (how uniform the hundred strikes had been).

Jiang Che drew billet number eleven. The examiner — a third-rank smith with gray in his beard and the permanent squint of a man who had looked at hot metal for forty years — watched him set up with the detached assessment of someone who had seen four hundred boys do exactly this.

He did not watch detached for long.

The first strike was a test — not of the metal but of the boy's own calibration. The hammer fell; the AT read the billet's grain from the feedback alone; the second strike adjusted by a hair's width. By strike ten the rhythm was established: heat, fold, strike, turn, strike, fold again. By strike thirty the billet was no longer a billet but a *column of intention* — the impurities rising to the surface as a dark scale that flaked away under each hammer-fall, the grain compacting into something that rang differently every ten strikes, a rising note, the metal's own voice climbing as it purified.

The Jade Hand conducted the shocks into the ground. The AT found the optimal fold-angle for each successive lamination. The fox — who had insisted on attending, riding the coat, because "empresses attend coronations" — watched the metal's color-shifts with the professional interest of a creature who had seen ore born in the earth.

At strike seventy-three the examiner left his bench and came to stand at the forge's shoulder. At strike ninety he called another examiner over. At strike one hundred the hammer fell, and the billet — now a compact ingot the size of a man's fist, gray-silver, dense as sin — rang a note that hung in the hall like a small bell.

The two examiners and the third-rank smith looked at the ingot. Then at the boy. Then at the ingot.

"Purity," said the gray-beard, at last, in the voice of a man revising his afternoon's opinion of the human race, "ninety-six percent. Density — high. Consistency —" he paused, because this was the part that did not happen, the part that separated masters from apprentices, "— I could not find a single strike's deviation across the hundred. What in the eight hells are you, boy?"

" Courier's son," said Jiang Che. "My father binds things."

"Your father," said the examiner, writing the rank badge out by hand, "*forges* things, and he taught you better than he knows. **First rank.** Score: superior. Come back for second when your hands tell you they're ready — not before." And then, half to himself, as the boy left: "Ninety-six at first rank. Mu Chen is going to want to see this."

### THE FIRST CREATION

The first-rank badge was a small iron disc stamped with the Association's mark — a hammer crossed over a flame — and it weighed almost nothing, and Jiang Che wore it under his shirt for a week before he mentioned it to anyone, because a courier does not announce his own deliveries.

But the badge was not the creation. The creation happened the night after the test, in the academy's forge yard, after hours, with Old Qian's grudging permission and the fox supervising from the anvil's horn.

**The item: a hidden-weapon frame.**

Not a weapon — not yet. A *frame*: the skeleton of a sleeve-dart mechanism, the kind the Treasure Record described in its chapter on 暗器百解 (the Hundred Solutions of Hidden Weapons) — the chapter Tang Ya had said was "for later, when your hands are worth it." His hands were worth it now, rank-badge or no rank-badge, and the first project was the simplest and most fundamental: the **track** — the channel down which a dart travels when released from a wrist-mounted launcher.

The work was precision-forging at a scale the billet-test had not approached: channels a finger's width wide, walls as thin as a coin, the whole piece no larger than a man's palm. It took four failed attempts — four pieces of iron that cracked under the hammer's precision-demand, because the tolerances were finer than a first-rank smith's ordinary work and the AT was learning a new scale the way it learned everything.

The fifth attempt held. The track was rough — a first-rank piece, no more, the walls uneven in ways a master would see at a glance — but it was a *track*: a channel that would guide a dart from wrist to target, the skeleton of the Tang Sect's most basic tool, forged by the sect's third disciple in an empty forge yard at midnight.

He showed it to no one but the fox.

"*Show it to your Sect Master,*" the fox said, nose to the metal, reading it as she read everything. "*It is bad work. It is also the first bad work of a smith who will one day do work that makes this piece look like a child's mud-pie — and she will want to have kept it.*"

He showed it to Tang Ya the next morning. The Sect Master turned the rough iron track over in her hands three times, and her eyes were bright, and she said, with enormous dignity, "The sect's treasury is now two fishing rods and an iron track," and put it in her sleeve, and was seen touching her sleeve at odd moments for the rest of the day.

### THE ARTS

The Treasure Record held five arts before the hidden weapons chapter, and in the first three months Jiang Che began — as Tang Ya had instructed — with the least. But "least" did not mean "only," and the forge's labor made the others practical in ways they had not been before.

**The Jade Hand — the foundation.** The conditioning and conduction described above, reaching stage two by month's end. The visible signs: the palm-lines gone faintly jade-pale; the hands immune to working-temperature iron; the hammer-shock conducted to ground.

**The Mysterious Heaven Skill — the engine.** His soul power had converted in the first week — the AT performing in three days what took Yuhao a week of diligent practice — and the result was a soul power that was *springier*, denser, running the wider meridians like a river in its banks rather than a stream in a ditch. Every other art drew on this engine, and the engine was compounding daily.

**The Crane-Captures-Dragon — the grip.** The seizing-and-locking art was the natural partner of the vine-work: a set of joint-locks, pressure-point seizures, and redirections that turned an opponent's force into their own capture. He practiced on the forge's iron bars — not the locks themselves, but the *hand positions*, the angles, the timing — until the AT had the geometry filed, and then on Wang Dong, once, in the training yard, with the prince's outraged "that's a *grappling* art, you can't just *apply* it to — *okay, yes, I see, that's effective*, let go of my wrist."

**The Ghost-Shadow Track — the footwork.** Begun but not mastered: the art of the weightless step, the movement that left no trace. His courier's training gave him a foundation no aristocrat had — ten thousand li of road discipline — but the Track required the opposite of endurance: *absence*. Weight, sound, intention — all negated. The AT was building it slowly. The fox said it would take a year, and the fox was honest about the arts because she had no stake in flattering them.

**The Purple Demon Eyes — NOT BEGUN.** The eye-cultivation art was Yuhao's, assigned by Tang Ya to Yuhao's eyes and Yuhao's alone — because the art demanded a spirit-attribute soul or decades of dawn-gazing, and Jiang Che's eyes were hawk-eyes, not Spirit Eyes. The Treasure Record's chapter sat unread for him — a door that was not his door, and the knowing of which was its own kind of discipline.

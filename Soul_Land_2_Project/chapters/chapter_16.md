### THE MIDNIGHT RUNNER *(Zhou Yi)*

The whetstone had a secret, and she found it by accident.

She had been out late on academy business, crossing Shrek Square past midnight, when she heard it: chain-links shifting, softly, in the dark. A figure on the track, shirtless, drenched, moving at a pace that was barely a run and would not stop — each footfall heavy enough to leave a shining wet print on the stone.

Hu Yuhao. In an iron shirt. At midnight. Adding two hours of punishment to days that had already ground his classmates to powder.

She asked him what he thought he was doing. The boy — swaying, soaked, polite — gave her the plainest answer she had ever been given in thirty years of teaching: *"Teacher, my talent is poor. I just work harder than the others. Training to exhaustion and then meditating — the improvement seems to come faster."*

She suspected a performance, staged for her benefit. The suspicion lasted exactly until the weather answered it: rain came, and he ran, the shirt rusting soft and brown at the seams; wind came, and he ran, leaning into it like a man leaning into an argument he intended to win; the academy's short winter came, and the frost burned his lungs, and he ran. Every midnight, the square's dark held one small stubborn figure, two hours and more — and then, in the second month, the figure got heavier: *two* iron shirts, sixty jin, the boy stacking his own disadvantages like a mason building a wall against his own weakness.

The class found out, as classes do — nothing in this academy stays secret except the things the academy itself seals. They came, the proud ones, in the first week: four of the front-runners, sure that if the trash-soul could do it, they could. One lasted a single night. Two lasted two. The last of them made it to the third midnight and stood at the track's edge in the dark, watching the small figure do its slow impossible laps, and went back to bed, and never spoke of it again — because there are things a proud boy cannot say out loud, and *he is made of something I do not have* is the first of them.

Word of the three-night failures moved through Class One the way word moves: silently, totally. And it changed something. The midnight runner never asked anyone to follow, never mentioned it, never made it a sermon — and that was precisely why they followed. By the third month, the class did not run at midnight with him. They simply *stopped quitting.* The iron days ground, and the theory ground, and sixty-seven children discovered one morning that they had been holding a standard for so long it had stopped feeling like a standard and started feeling like a spine. The class found out, as classes do. Some tried to join him. Not one lasted three days. This was not training anymore; the word for it was the one Zhou Yi had never needed to use for an eleven-year-old: *discipline.*

And that — not the fish, not the pills, not even the monitor's post — was the thing that had held sixty-seven of ninety-one together through her quarter. A class is a whetstone's dream only if somewhere in the steel there is one grain that will not be filed. She had found her grain, running in the dark.

*(Natural ripple, the deepest one: the class's spine was not her work alone, and she knew it. Somewhere in her files, the word "monitor" stopped meaning a title and started meaning a foundation.)*

### THE PILL LEDGER *(Huo Yuhao)*

The numbers, honestly kept, because the boy who ran at midnight kept everything honestly:

**The second Mysterious Water Pill, month one:** taken after the first had fully settled — a whole rank, twelve-peak to thirteen-peak, and the body scoured a second time. Cultivation speed: seven times the original.

**The Soul-Raising Pill, mid-month:** into a body now twice-cleansed, channels widened and elastic — and the pill repaid the preparation exactly as Wang Dong had promised: **three ranks broken in a night, and a bottleneck besides, settling at seventeen.** Seven times became ten. Ten *times*. The trash-soul boy who had needed five years to reach rank ten now cultivated at ten times that pace — a number that would have shamed middle nobility, arrived at in ninety days.

**The three months' grinding:** seventeen to seventeen-peak, the edge of eighteen, the roots deliberately allowed to settle after the medicine's sprint. His body kept its own ledger alongside: three inches of height — he now looked Wang Dong in the eye — and a build that was not muscle-mass but *whipcord*, every gram of it earned between midnight and dawn.

The woodshed orphan had, in one quarter, stopped being the academy's joke and become its average — and its average was the finest cohort on the continent.

*(Room 108 heard the arithmetic read aloud that night - Wang Dong kept the class standings the way princes keep ledgers of things they intend to win - and the courier filed one line in his own: the twin-soul rumor. A soul that came in a pair. He had thought, once, about how rare such things must be; the fox had told him, long ago, that twin souls were older than the academies and rarer than the deep-forest kings, and that they came in two kinds: the fortunate, and the *troubled.* He hoped, mildly, professionally, that the small quiet girl was the first kind. The mend worked on both, but it worked gentler on the first.)*

### THE THIRD BED *(room 108 - the parallel ledger)*

Room 108 kept two ledgers now, and the second one belonged to the third bed.

Jiang Che's quarter, honestly kept: **twenty-nine**, the wall at thirty, the hunt planned. But the room knew — because rooms know — that the number was the least of it. The Xuantian Gong sat in his meridians like a second heart; the forge had rebuilt his hands twice over; the Jade Hand had crossed some invisible line in its third month where the conduction stopped being a technique and started being a *property*; and the mend-work — thirty, forty, fifty applications of the clean current a week — had done to his spiritual depth what the midnight running had done to Yuhao's body.

And the two ledgers had begun to talk to each other. On the last night of the quarter, past midnight, Jiang Che came back from the forge and found the room empty and the iron shirts gone, and knew exactly where his roommate was — and went, because the triangle went where its legs went, and stood in the dark at the edge of Shrek Square with a hammer over his shoulder, watching the small stubborn figure do its two hours.

He did not join the run. He had his own.

He sat down on the square's edge, laid the hammer across his knees, and meditated — but not idly, because nothing the AT did was idle. The Xuantian Gong turned under the stars; the fox a warm weight in his coat; and the dew-lens mapped the running boy's breathing, the rhythm of it, the architecture of a will converting itself into rank one wet footfall at a time. And in the meditation's second layer, the courier ran his own route: the wall at thirty, the ring beyond it, the hunt's shape - the forest south, the water-kin beasts his father had hunted for twenty years, the Sun Flower's precedent, and the question the fox had planted months ago and left like a seed: *what does the grass itself want to become?* Because a ring chosen by the AT's user was not a tool acquired. It was a door opened, and he had three months of proof, in the bed across the room, of what a door cost and what it gave.

Two boys, two trainings, one square: one building a body the world would have to reckon with; one planning a soul the world had not made a shelf for yet.

When Yuhao finished — two hours, two shirts, a boy-shaped watermark on the stone — the courier was still there, and had hot bread, because couriers plan routes and this one had planned his roommate's.

"You ran," Yuhao managed, between breaths.

"You ate," said Jiang Che, handing him the bread. "Rank eighteen by the assessment?"

"...Rank eighteen *by the assessment.*" The gray boy grinned, wolfish now, nothing woodshed about it. "Thirty for you?"

"Thirty for me." The courier stood, shouldered the hammer, and the two of them walked back across the wet square in the last dark before the assessment year's first morning. "First place, second place, and a monitor. The sect sweeps the board tomorrow."

"The sect," said Yuhao, around the bread, "is going to make Zhou Yi's year."

### THE WATER-BREAK BROTHERS *(the midnight run — the courier joins, in his own way)*

The midnight runner had company on the fourth night of the quarter.

Not a joiner — the courier had his own hours — but at the water barrel between laps forty and forty-one, a courier appeared with bread and a hammer and sat on the barrel's rim doing the Jade Hand's conduction through the cold iron hoops, because a courier trains where a courier is.

"You're doing forge-homework at my water barrel," Yuhao managed.

"I'm doing forge-homework at *a* water barrel. You happen to be running around it."

"That's not how barrels work—"

"It's exactly how barrels work." He passed the bread. "Lap forty's the wall. The wall's always forty. Eat, then break the wall."

"…Did the fox tell you forty's the wall?"

"The fox told me *you'd* say forty. She's been running your route in her sleep. She says your left ankle clicks on the curves." A beat. "She worries about your ankle, Yu. Out loud. It's very undignified and she'd deny it under torture."

And the boy running the worst laps of the night discovered that his brotherhood had a *night shift* — that even his solitary penance had been adopted by people who could not run it for him but could, god help them, be *there*, with bread and barrel-hoops and a fox who worried about ankles.

### THE ANNOUNCEMENT *(Class One - ensemble)*

"Physical training is suspended."

Sixty-seven spines straightened as one — Pavlovian, total, slightly hilarious — and Zhou Yi looked out at them with the not-warm-not-cold face that her classes eventually learned to read as *satisfaction, heavily encrypted.*

"Three months since enrollment. Tomorrow, your first assessment. Pass it, and you are formally first-years — Shrek students in fact as well as letter, with a place through to the year-end examinations." A pause. "Today, the points."

And she gave them the shape of it — the rules, the format, the stakes — and the class wrote down every word, because this class had learned the price of things. But under the notes, the thing itself moved through the room like weather: *tomorrow.* Everything the iron had built, everything the midnight runner had shamed them into, everything the fish stall had fed and the third bed had mended — all of it banked, and tomorrow the bank opened.

And the stakes she gave them plainly, because she never lied to a class: the assessment was real, the failure was real, and the academy's gate opened both ways. But she gave them one thing more, in the whetstone's way - by not giving it: no threat. For three months every lesson had ended in one. Tonight, none. Because the class that did not need the threat was the class that had arrived, and both the teacher and the taught knew it, and neither would say.

Zhou Yi's eye passed over them and found what she had secretly hoped to find: sixty-seven sets of shoulders that did not flinch. Her batch, she knew — and would have died before saying — was the finest will she had ever been handed, and the reason for it was a rank-seventeen orphan who ran at midnight, a rank-twenty-three prince who had promised them fish money, a rank-twenty-nine courier who fixed what broke, and sixty-four others who had chosen, night after night, to be worthy of the example.

"Class One," said the whetstone, in the voice that meant it, "do not disappoint me."

And when the format came - the classes drawn against each other, three to a side - the middle rows began the arithmetic every class does: who stands, who folds, who is dangerous. Names moved through the whisper-line. And one name moved through it twice, because nobody could agree what it was: a small, quiet girl from Class Two who had spent three months being unnoticed - Xiao Xiao - and whose soul, the rumor said, came in a *pair*.

Sixty-seven voices, one answer — and in the middle rows, three boys sat in a row without arranging to, because that was simply where the triangle's center of gravity lived now, and the strongest of them was not the one anyone outside the room would have guessed.

### THE EVE *(the sect - the seventh seat)*

The last supper before an assessment, at a grill already drowned: five disciples and a mountain, and the Sect Master holding court with the day's takings as her scepter.

"Rules of engagement," Tang Ya announced. "Wang Dong takes first place overall - that seals the fifth seat the proper way, by the promise, not the back door. Yuhao - top ten, monitor's honor. Jiang Che -" she paused, and the grin went sly, "- the sect's official position is that our third disciple should be modest and restrained."

"His unofficial position," said Bei Bei, "being that the academy's trial files already say *flagged*, the class already calls him the hammer, and the whole first year has spent three months watching him mend everything they broke."

"Modest and restrained," Jiang Che agreed, deadpan, and the table laughed, and the mountain laughed loudest, because Xu Sanshi had learned this month that being the sect's unsworn seventh seat came with unlimited fish and unlimited honesty, and both suited him.

And later - the fire drowned, the walk home, the three beds - the last exchange of the quarter, in the dark, three voices that had learned each other's silences:

"First place," Wang Dong said, to the ceiling.

"Top ten," Yuhao said, to the ceiling.

"Thirty," Jiang Che said, to the ceiling, and then, quietly, with the fox settling against his collarbone: "and a ring, before the year turns."

The fox said nothing at all, which was her way of saying *yes - and the hunt is already planned, and you will not go alone, little tide. The sect hunts now.*

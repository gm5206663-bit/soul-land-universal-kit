### ╱╱ THE BROKEN GONG ╲╲

Class One held one hundred of the continent's culled pride, and when the door opened, it held one old woman.

White hair coiled on the crown; a white robe; a back straight as a spear-shaft; and eyes — bright, black, lit from behind somehow — that swept the room left to right and left a stripe of pressure across every spine they crossed, the way a whetstone leaves a stripe across a blade. Huo Yuhao, whose own soul was eyes, felt that sweep like a hand on the back of his neck.

"I am Zhou Yi. Your homeroom." A voice like a broken gong — cracked, nasal, and carrying like war-drums. "I don't know how many of you will walk this year with me. Here is what I do know: in my class, no *garbage* passes. I train monsters. Not fools."

A hundred faces of the elite went red. A hundred prides of a hundred nations chewed the word *garbage* and found it did not swallow.

"Since registration day: those who have *fought* — stand."

Silence. Nobody moves — this is the oldest trap in the world and these are the newest students in it — and into that silence, with the sensation of a man stepping off a cliff he has correctly measured, Huo Yuhao stood. Not honesty. Arithmetic. *This teacher does not play by rules; therefore the rule-shaped answer is wrong.*

A heartbeat later, annoyed to be second at anything, Wang Dong stood too.

And a third, from the middle rows, calm as a river deciding to be noticeable: Jiang Che. He had fought — a trial bout, a proctored match, but a *fight* was a fight.

One hundred students. Three upright. Zhou Yi's eyebrow climbed a millimeter — her version of open delight.

"Only three? What a class of *waste*." The broken gong rang off the walls. "Did no one ever teach you that the one who dares not make trouble is a mediocrity? The rest of you — OUT. Around Shrek Square. One hundred laps. One hour. Unfinished means expelled — and understand this *is* your first assessment."

The explosion of protest had a leader — one boy, up and outraged — and Zhou Yi answered him the way winter answers a late harvest: "Because I said so. Disagree and leave; I hold the power to expel any of you. One minute." And when the protest did not instantly die, her soul rings rose — **two yellow, two purple, and two BLACK** — and a Soul Emperor's pressure walked the room like a plowed field, and a hundred prodigies discovered the exact size of children.

They went. Jiang Che among them — the third who had stood — and Zhou Yi's eye rested on him one second longer than on anyone else. Not warm. Not cold. *Measuring.* The trial annotation sat in her mind: *flagged for the year-tutor's watch.* She said nothing. Whetstones note the metal.

They went. At the door, the field of them, molten with injustice.

Zhou Yi turned to the three. And the whetstone showed its other face, the one only her survivors ever saw — the face that had put one in four of her students onto the island in the lake.

"Good," she said, quietly. "Three with blood in them. You two — roommates, are you? Fought each other? *Good.* Roommates who fight sharpen each other. Fight the other classes next time — the rules permit it. Hear them: seniors who provoke juniors are expelled. Juniors may challenge seniors, with a teacher witnessing. Within your year, fight as you please — no deaths, no interference. Combat," said the monster teacher, "is an excellent way to improve. Now."

"...Now?" said Wang Dong.

"Now you run too. You fought your roommate on day one — so ambitious! — so your assessment is *harder*: one hundred laps, the same deadline as the others."

"And you," her eyes found the third boy, the one whose trial file she had read at four in the morning with a cup of tea going cold beside it, "you fought a *proctor*. In a *trial*. That is not fighting. That is interviewing. Run with the class and learn the difference." The broken gong sounded almost sweet. "And you've already lost a quarter hour standing here admiring me."

### ╱╱ THE FIELD ╲╲

Shrek Square measured three hundred meters a lap, and the first morning of the new year ran around it like a millwheel of pride.

Jiang Che ran in the pack — not the front, not the back — and beside him, forty laps in, a gray-uniformed boy running on fumes and stubbornness matched his stride for three steps before realizing he had.

"You —" Hu Yuhao panted, recognizing the third bed. "You're not even — breathing —"

"I walk a lot," said the courier, at conversation volume, which was its own kind of cruelty.

Wang Dong, passing them both for the ninth time with the offended majesty of a hawk being overtaken by traffic, looked down at the exchange from a height of moral and physical superiority, and *nearly* smiled.

The courier ran at the pace of a man who had walked sixty li before breakfast since he was eight. Jiang Che had learned his trade's central secret young: *distance is not conquered, it is administered.* His feet fell the way his father's fell on patrol; his breath ran the Hall's four-count; and where the sun lay on his shoulders and the back of his neck, something in him drank it — light feeding life, life feeding stride — so that the laps went by beneath him like ledger-lines, counted without counting.

*Forty-one. Forty-two.*

In his breast pocket, pressed flat, a fox conducted an inspection of the apocalypse. "*This,*" she remarked, to her human alone, "*is the famous forge of monsters. It is a vegetable market with better posture. If she wants to see running, she should visit a forest fire.*"

"Shh," said the courier, at sip-volume, and a neighboring runner decided, wisely, he had heard nothing.

He saw everything else, because seeing the field was the family trade. He saw the leaders' dozen form and reform, one blue-haired boy among them like a blade in a drawer of knives. He saw the gloaters point at two late starters and be ignored by them. He saw the back-markers crack one by one, and among them — *there* — the small gray figure from the road, the fish-saint, the mended boy, running at the very tail on a woodshed body that was giving everything it had and then one more thing—

—and he saw, on the far side of the square, in the unwatched first minutes, a big boy stroll two laps like a gentleman viewing property before joining the grind. The courier's feet counted it and filed it, and his mouth said nothing, and his silence was not cowardice but craft: a hall that checks a room's knots before judging them has no need of his testimony. *She'll know,* he thought, with a craftsman's certainty about another craftsman. *She strikes me as the type who counts.*

*Sixty,* said his feet. The sun climbed. The light in him banked itself, patient, the way his mother's hearth banked coals for the loaves not yet kneaded.

### ╱╱ THE TERMS ╲╲

They ran, the two of them, a quarter-hour behind a hundred grudge-bearing colleagues and gaining on none of them, and somewhere in lap twenty the silence broke.

"That day." Wang Dong, at shoulder, not looking at him. "What was it? What beat me? I've been through it two hundred times."

"Spirit attribute," Yuhao said, between breaths.

Wang Dong nearly tripped over the information. "*Really?* Then the ring I couldn't read — hm. Then I didn't lose to a *trick.* I lost to a soul that sees." He brightened, in his own peculiar way, like a man whose defeat had been promoted to a mystery. "You realize you're still hopeless. That candle of soul power you're carrying — I can feel it from here. If I'd used my martial soul, you'd have had no chance at all."

"Then it's fortunate," said Huo Yuhao, with great evenness, "that you were too proud to use it."

"*Fortunate*—" The blue eyes narrowed; the legs, offended, accelerated. Then, oddly, eased. "...You covered for me. Just now. In there. 'Roommates, small conflict.' You didn't have to." A pause of three laps' length. "I'll allow it. From today I'll cover you. Anyone troubles you, they answer to me. I'm the strongest in this year's intake. Nearly."

"I don't need covering."

"You *do.*"

"I need a roommate," said the boy from the woodshed, who had never had one, and had thought about it, "who treats me as an equal. Everything else is decoration."

Wang Dong ran a full lap on that, jaw working. "In my whole life," he said at last, honestly, amazed at himself, "I've never met anyone my age I could see that way."

"Then you've been running in a small yard," said Huo Yuhao, and — because the woodshed had taught him exactly one form of luxury — put on speed, and left a genius staring at his dust.

"话不投机半句多!" Wang Dong announced to the sky, offended to his roots, and then, being who he was, *blazed* past the entire complaining hundred — a quarter-hour's deficit erased in half an hour of legs like whips — and finished first of all one hundred, barely breathing, so angry that he was almost happy, which is the exact temperature at which friendships set.

### ╱╱ THE MILE-TALK ╲╲ *(the gauntlet's middle — the brothers, in step)*

Somewhere around lap forty, when the iron had stopped being weight and become weather, the courier drifted back through the pack — deliberately, past his own comfortable pace, to where the gray boy ran at the tail with his jaw set and his world reduced to the next step.

They ran in silence for a lap. That was the whole point of the arriving.

"Scale of one to ten," Jiang Che said, at conversation volume, which was its own cruelty.

"Seven. You?"

"Six. But I cheated — I run roads for a living and someone stuffed me full of light."

"*You* stuffed *you* full of light. That's the — that's the sunflower thing. You photosynthesize. You're a *plant,* Hao, you're literally a plant with a hammer."

"I'm a plant who can't feel his legs. We're even."

Another lap. The iron shirts clinked their idiot music.

"He's still going," Yuhao said, not looking forward — but forward was all the prince at the head of the field, untouchable and unbearable. "He hasn't slowed once."

"He's rank twenty-three and his family bought him a cradle made of thousand-year ingredients. Slowing would be a *scandal.*" A beat. "Don't measure yourself against his front. Measure against your own yesterday."

"That's a fortune cookie."

"It's courier arithmetic. Same shape, more walking."

And Huo Yuhao — rank seventeen, tail of the field, iron on his shoulders and a woodshed on his back — laughed, in the middle of the hardest morning of his life, and found the laugh was worth two laps of energy, which was a trade neither of them had expected and both of them would spend a year making.

### ╱╱ THE WINGS ╲╲

With a quarter hour left, the field had become arithmetic: ninety-some finished, seven desperate, and at the very tail one boy thirteen laps short of an impossible sum — and Huo Yuhao, at the end of everything, did the thing that made even this field's cold go quiet.

He hurt *himself.* Soul Shock, turned inward — a needle into his own mind, white pain as a whip for flagging flesh — and the small gray figure *sped up*, and passed two bodies, and the field's roar of disbelief began in a dozen throats at once.

And then the square went beautiful.

Out of the crowd, catching the tail-runner, a boy's back *opened* — and wings stood up against the morning: butterfly wings, vast, impossible, blue on blue on blue, deep and shallow and luminous as weather, a V of light across each like dawn striking a wave. Not cloth, not feather — *light wearing a shape* — and a thousand of the continent's proudest children cried out at once, without a syllable of shame, because the sight reached past rank and sense and took the eye directly, the way sunrise does.

Wang Dong did not pause to be admired. His arms went under Yuhao's shoulders from behind — "*You* — run *lighter*, you ox—" — the wings struck down once, and the two of them left the ground, and the millwheel of the field watched two boys *fly* the last laps, the impossible sum paying itself out in wingbeats while a broken gong somewhere said nothing at all.

At Zhou Yi's back, so low only the field's nearest runners caught it: "The Goddess of Light Butterfly. The most beautiful butterfly soul on the continent." A pause; and the whetstone added, to no one, with something that in another face would have been warmth: "*Good.* Not all disappointments, then."

They came down at the line together, staggered, caught, collapsed — one atop the other exactly as etiquette demanded — and the field heard the tail-end of it as they untangled, breathless, salt-soaked:

"Thanks. Last time I pressed you down. Now you've pressed me back."

"You *smell*," said Wang Dong, with his whole heart.

And two thumbs rose, and met, and the last of the grudge between them went wherever old enmities go when both parties have proven too stubborn to hate — which is to say: into the beginning of the thing it would become.

### ╱╱ THE ROLL ╲╲

When the last runner crossed and the hour closed, ninety-one students stood in the square's morning sun, sick, triumphant, changed.

Zhou Yi stood where she had stood all hour, dry as paper, and read nine names from no list at all.

"Cheng Cheng. Qiu Jianrao. Tang Dao. Shangguan Chentian. Lin Zeyu. Zhuge Yun. Tai Long. Tang Ling. Yun Xiaopiao."

Nine faces came out of the ranks, stunned — and the big boy, Tai Long, earliest finisher of the front-runners, angry enough to be brave: "Teacher, *why?*"

"Shrek does not keep students who cheat at running or at anything else," said Zhou Yi, in the voice of a verdict that had happened before and would happen again. "Strength without an upright heart is a disaster wearing a uniform — for any nation that buys it. Strength *with* one is what this academy sells. Tai Long. You walked your first two laps while my eyes were elsewhere." The square went very cold. "You ran ninety-seven, not one hundred. Pack your things."

"You weren't even *here*—"

"I am always where your conduct is," said Zhou Yi, and it was not a figure of speech; it was a *creed*, and a hundred children learned it in one sentence, and nine children packed.

In the ranks, a courier who had counted the same two laps at minute three kept his face like a ledger and closed the page. *The type who counts,* he thought. *A whetstone with eyes.*

Beside him, unnoticed by everyone, a small weight in a pocket turned a slow circle — the fox's equivalent of removing her hat — and said nothing, which from an empress was a standing ovation.

### ╱╱ THE LECTURE ╲╲

Fifteen minutes to change clothes — ninety-one students did it in ten, terror being the finest of perfumes — and Class One sat again beneath the nine empty seats' silence.

"Hu Yuhao. Stand. Your soul power."

"...Eleven, teacher."

The class's disbelief was a weather system; Wang Dong covered his eyes outright — *eleven?* — and then did arithmetic backwards and arrived at *how was he even ADMITTED?*, which was precisely the question Zhou Yi answered, once, finally, on the first morning so that it would never be asked again: **a special invitation of the academy, exempt from the entrance examination — and from nothing else.** "Anyone who questions him on it again answers to me." It was, though no one in the room had the experience to notice, protection — the whetstone's other face, sharpening the small gray swordsman against the class's contempt before it could dull him.

Then she taught. Two characters on the board: **Attack. Defense.** Wang Dong, invited, gave the textbook answer — assault, agility and control attack; support, food, defense defend — and was told, before the whole class, that he had answered like a fool: correct four thousand years ago, wrong on the day the Sun-Moon continent arrived. **The soul tool has blurred the line** — a food-system tool master with a strong enough weapon strikes like an assault soul master; an agility fighter with a fortress shield *becomes* a wall. The age chases soul power and lets the rings rust, and "past rank fifty, they are all garbage," because a man whose body is weak dies first no matter what he carries — the enemy picks the soft one, always — and a man whose body is a weapon *plus* the age's tools is the only thing the word *strong* actually means. "Your bodies," she told ninety-one of the continent's proudest children, "are the syllabus. Three months. Fail me and follow the nine."

And the class — brilliant, furious, alive — took notes.

---

### ╱╱ THE OFFICE ╲╲

"Hu Yuhao. My office." — which every other teacher in the world says as a threat and Zhou Yi said as an appointment.

Behind a closed door she asked him, straight, blunt, strong: his martial soul. He gave it — *Spirit Eyes; spirit attribute; a body soul* — and watched sixty-some years of a Soul Emperor's composure crack, once, into something young. "...Show me the skill."

The white ring rose; the gold filmed his eyes; and he did for the teacher what he had done for the sect on a forest road — the *world, shared*: distances, densities, the drift of dust, her own office rendered into her mind as order itself.

For a full minute the monster teacher of Class One stood inside an eleven-year-old's soul, and the quiet that followed was not the classroom's quiet.

"*That* skill," Zhou Yi said finally, "is worth a war." And then, more quietly, mostly to herself, the answer to a question a fortnight old: "No wonder the Tang Sect chose you. The girl's smarter than her cooking." She stood. "Come with me. Now."

### ╱╱ THE CARRY ╲╲

What happened next, Hu Yuhao would tell his sect brothers that evening in a dormitory room with the awe still leaking out of him: the monster teacher *took him by the shoulder* — and the academy went away.

West, then north; the white halls, the green squares, the lake's rim all pouring past like water off a lock-gate, a Soul Emperor's speed wearing a courier's economy, the world blurred and barrier-calm around a boy too busy being carried to be frightened — until the movement stopped, and before them stood a building the size of a small mountain's opinion of itself: long, gray, half metal, humming. The ground buzzed faintly underfoot. A sign: **SOUL TOOL TESTING GROUND.**

Inside: all metal, a dark bronze he had no name for; a corridor of enormous doors; a hexagonal silver token pressed to a slot; a door — two *feet* thick — grinding sideways on rails. Beyond it, caverns divided by metal walls, and at the heart of the greatest of them, a two-thousand-square-meter field where men and women shaped the age into being, and the air tasted of ozone and purpose.

"**Fan Yu!**" Zhou Yi's broken gong, delighted.

Out of the ozone came a mountain in simple cloth — a head and shoulders over one-point-eight meters of granite calm, bare arms like ship's cable, a face of settled stone — who looked at the intrusion the way cliffs look at weather. "Zhou Yi. What now."

"You remember what you said to me last time?" — and it was said with the enthusiasm of a woman about to win an argument that had been running for years —

— and this is where the evening's telling always stopped, in room 108, because the rest belonged to the next day, and the age, and a gray building whose doors knew a teacher's token; and three boys sat in the lamplight of the only triple room in the white hall, and the smallest of them said, quietly, to no one: "*soul tools,*" as a man says the name of a country he has just learned he will one day live in.

### ╱╱ THE CODA ╲╲

Evening, after the telling: the work-board's forge shift, an hour's walk from soul-tool thunder and exactly its opposite — coke, iron, patience. The Jade Hand's first true test had come there again as it came every night now: *conduction*, not armor — the heat crossing tempered palms and going through, the art of letting the whole of a thing move through you. His hands were a map of small earned burns. The old Journeyman at the last furnace had said it again, exactly as before, because smiths are precise men:

"Come back tomorrow. Bring your own hammer."

He had brought his own hammer. It sat on the desk of room 108 beside a page titled **玄玉手**, in a corner room with three beds and two roommates already breathing — one aristocratic and fur-swaddled, one somewhere deep in a white-ringed meditation — and in the pocket of a coat on the third nail, an empress who had listened all evening to a tale of gray buildings and thundering doors permitted herself one footnote:

"*Little tide. The age you mean to upgrade is bigger than the one you were told about.*"

"Good," said the Tang Sect's third disciple, already half asleep, honest to the last. "More to work with."

Across the academy the whetstone's window was dark at last, and the testing ground's hum went on all night, and between the two sounds a city of monsters slept on its futures.

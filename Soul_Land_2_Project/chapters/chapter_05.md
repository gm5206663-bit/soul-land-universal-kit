### ╱╱ THE CITY OF MONSTERS ╲╲

They came at Shrek City out of the morning like sailors coming at a harbor: the road widened, and widened again, and then the road gave up and became *streets*, and the age poured past them in both directions — caravans of soul-tool freight humming west, auction houses with their doors open like mouths, tea-shops three stories tall, and monsters everywhere, wearing school badges of four colors and no awe at all.

"Every one of them," Bei Bei said, pleasantly, "is the pride of somewhere. The continent culls a thousand years of children down to a thousand a year, and those are the thousand. Try to feel the size of it," he advised his junior brother, "and then stop, because the size of it is not your business. *You* are your business."

Hu Yuhao walked the strangest street of his life with his spine straight and his hand never once touching the dagger, because for the first time since the back gate of a mansion fifty li from a capital, he was walking toward a door that had his name on a list beside it.

And behind them, at the same pace as always, a blue-haired courier looked at the same city and did what he always did with new ground: read it. Caravans. Auctions. Forges — a whole street of forges, their chimneys making the east sky gray — and, in the pocket over his heart, a weight that had stopped giving him commentary days ago and now only watched.

---

### ╱╱ THE TEN STATUES ╲╲

The academy began where the noise ended.

Past the gates, the main road ran green and enormous between the trees, and at its heart stood what the first-comers were already circling: statues. Ten of them, facing ten thousand years.

Front row, three: a smiling roundish elder in spectacles — Flender, the founder. At his left a lean middle-aged scholar with unreadable eyes — **Yu Xiaogang, the Grandmaster, who built the very system by which every soul master on the continent was taught**, and of whom Bei Bei said, simply, quietly, with a whole ancestry in his voice: "My mother's line. The academy's true soul." At the founder's right, a handsome woman mid-shape — Liu Erlong. The Golden Iron Triangle.

Behind them, seven more — the first Seven. A double-pupiled tiger of a man first; then a gently smiling one; and third—

Third, a young man in simple clothes with long blue hair, blue-silver vines climbing the statue's arms, a faint smile carved at the mouth and the whole of the gaze aimed across the row at a fifth figure with a scorpion braid, forever.

"*Thousand-Hand Douluo,*" Tang Ya said. Her voice had gone somewhere else again — the furnace-voice. "Tang San. The ancestor. Our ancestor — the sect's, I mean," and she looked, for one heartbeat, at the courier beside her, and did not correct herself, and neither did he.

"Ten statues in ten thousand years," Bei Bei said. "No eleventh has been added since. Understand this place early, little brothers: Shrek does not build statues for the excellent. It builds them for the *turnings of the world.*"

Behind the statues, through the trees, water glinted — a lake so wide it held weather, with an island at its heart.

"The Sea God Lake," Tang Ya said. "Built to honor the ancestor — he took a god's throne, the stories say, and one of them was of the sea. The *inner academy* stands on that island. Everything we do, everything this place does, aims across that water."

Jiang Che stood before the third statue the longest of any of them. A boy with grass in his sleeves, looking up at the man who had made grass a legend — feeling, without any way to say it, the weight of a ten-thousand-year expectation settle onto his shoulders and finding that it fit, the way his father's strap fit, the way his mother's knots fit: made for someone bigger, and then, one day, not.

In his pocket, very low, so only one boy in the world could have heard it: "*Hm. He kept his eyes on his people, not his height.*" And then nothing more, which from the fox was a eulogy.

### ╱╱ THE INHERITANCE ╲╲

They took the lakeside path slowly, because Tang Ya had decided — with the particular courage of a woman about to spend the last of something — that the whole story should be walked, not sketched.

The sect at its height: thousands of members, the continent's armory, rich beyond reason — and giving it *away*, most of it, because the first master had written the giving into the sect's bones: the poor districts, the schools. "We were never a treasury," she said. "We were a *furnace*. Everything that came in, went out as light." And then the collision, and the tools, and the orders thinned, and two hundred years unmade two thousand —

"— and by my childhood it was three," Tang Ya said, to the water. "Mama. Papa. Me." A breath, level, rehearsed by years of not-crying. "They died in the hunting conflict, the year I was nine. The sect's last masters. I got the Treasure Record, the fishing rod, the arts, and the grudge." She turned and looked at her sect: at the tall boy who had joined her madness for love and stayed for the truth of it, and at the woodshed boy breathing white streams at the lake's edge, and — for a moment that she let be seen — at the courier. "You asked on the road what happened. That's what happened. And here's what happens *next.*"

And she told them the decision — hers and Bei Bei's, taken years ago in a smaller room than this morning: that the Tang Sect would not die proud and useless; that hidden weapons were not *wrong*, they were unfinished; that the sect's chance — the only chance anyone had been able to see in two hundred years — was the thing everyone said with a laugh: **fusion**. Steel and soul-tool. Craft and age.

"Every disciple of the Tang Sect, from this generation on, studies the soul tool as well as the soul," she said, in the words she and Bei Bei had chosen long before this morning. "That's the law we wrote. There's just—" and for the first time in four days the furnace-voice cracked, just at the edge, just once, "—there's just been no one who could *hold both ends.* We're fighters. The arts of making are gone out of the sect; the last smith died before I could walk. We wrote a law nobody can keep."

The lake moved. Nobody filled the silence, because the silence was a door, and doors get filled from one side only.

### ╱╱ THE REGISTRATION ╲╲

But first the world, which does not wait for chapters.

The white new-students' hall was chaos with a queue: children of eleven and twelve from every nation under the sun, each one the pride of somewhere, each one clutching a badge or a terror. With Bei Bei and Tang Ya walking him past it all, Huo Yuhao's registration was minutes — name, origin, soul, ring; the *exam-exempt* chit from the Tang Sect's ancient privilege presented by the Sect Master herself, to a clerk who sat up very straight indeed.

The tuition was ten gold soul coins for the year. Bei Bei paid it without ceremony, and told him firmly, before thanks could form, that it was the sect's law and not the sect's generosity — "you'll repay it in the *currency we actually use*" — and the ledger closed on: Huo Yuhao, first-year, Class One.

At the notice-board, Bei Bei's eyebrows did the journey they'd been dreading. **Zhou Yi.** The new class's homeroom teacher. The academy's most infamous teacher — demoted to the first-years for harshness that had thinned a third-year class to a tenth; and, said Bei Bei, quietly, the other truth: her survivors reached the inner court at a rate one in four, among the best the academy had ever recorded. "Shrek's monster students are famous," he said, steering his junior brother bodily away from the board, "but the *monster teachers* are the ones who make them. Survive her, little brother. That's the whole first year: survive her."

For Jiang Che, the clerk had colder arithmetic. Standard admission — no chit, no exemption: the *entrance trial*, three days hence, and the same ten gold coins a year, no refunds, wages and prize-money a student's own affair thereafter.

"Three days," he said.

"Three days," Tang Ya agreed; and then, with the enormous brightness of a woman who has already decided not to say the thing: "Well. *Somebody* in this sect has an evening to spend, then."

### ╱╱ THE WAITING BENCH ╲╲ *(the morning before the oath — the brothers, alone)*

Before the statues, before the lakeside — a bench outside the registration hall, two boys on it, and the particular silence of a boy about to be asked something enormous.

"You're doing the thing," Yuhao said. "The silence thing. Where your jaw does the — *clench.*"

"My jaw doesn't do a thing."

"Your jaw does an entire thing. It's doing it now." He looked at the lake, not at his brother. "You don't have to. She said *when,* not now."

"It's not the when." The courier's hands turned the Treasure Record's pages over and over, corner to corner, the way a man shuffles a card he's already decided to play. "It's that they're *right.* A four-person sect with a law nobody can keep, and the one craft that keeps it walks in wearing my face. I'm eleven, Yu. What kind of idiot looks at that and thinks *yes, my problem now?*"

"The same kind that wraps a baboon for a stranger. The same kind that eats two fish out of fourteen." He turned. "Hao. They're not asking you to be the smith. They're asking you to be *theirs.* The smith part is just the shape it comes in."

A long quiet. The lake said nothing, expertly.

"...Did you just give a *speech?*"

"I've been saving it. You weren't supposed to need it until the fox's birthday."

"She doesn't have a birthday. She's nine hundred years old."

"Then I've got time to write a better one," said Yuhao — and his brother laughed, actually laughed, the bark startling a fisher-bird off the bench — and the clench, wherever it had lived, was gone.

### ╱╱ THE THRESHOLD ╲╲

She took them back along the lakeside path as the sun went long, and stopped — of all the places in the world she might have chosen — between the water and the statues, where the ancestor's stony eyes could see both his grass and his sea.

"Jiang Che of Linjiang." Formal. The full name, the way sects say names when names have started to weigh. "Four days ago I told you I'd ask you nowhere again until somewhere that meant something. This is somewhere that means something." A breath. "The Tang Sect asks. Third asking. Last asking." And then her voice stopped being formal, because Tang Ya had never in her life been formal for a full minute: "*Please.* We wrote a law nobody can keep. You're a keeper. Your soul is the ancestor's own. Your hands are a healer's. Your head — four days of watching you think has taught senior brother and me *this* much: you don't see a broken thing and walk around it. You see a broken thing and ask what it wants to be when it's *upgraded.* So." She held out her hand, palm up: the oldest gesture her arts knew. "The life-condition is cruel and I'll say it plainly anyway: join, and you are Tang Sect until death. The arts, the name, the grudge, the *furnace.* Leave ever, and you give back every page. There's no shame in no. But there's — there has never in my life been a *yes* I wanted more."

The lake said nothing. The statues said nothing, expertly.

Jiang Che looked at the hand. And because his answer had been assembling itself since a stream and a bell and a triage in the grass — since the grudge told plainly and the arts'-door held shut and the fusion law written for hands no one had — he gave it the only way he knew how to give anything, which was the whole of it, out loud.

"My father binds things for a living," he said. "My mother mends them. I was raised in a house where nothing got thrown away because nothing was *finished* — and I've spent four days learning that your hidden weapons aren't dead, Sect Master. They're the weakest thing on this road." He looked up, and the furnace in his eyes answered hers. "I don't choose things because they're strong. I choose them because they're *weak.* Weak is where the work is. You wrote a law nobody can keep —"*and his hand closed on hers, courier's grip, smith's grip*— "so keep it. I'll take the Jade Hand tonight. The hidden-weapon canon when my hands are worth it. The forge-craft and the soul-tool craft until the two are one thing and the thing is *ours.* I'll write my parents tonight — a courier's son knows how a letter travels; they'll say yes, they raised me to say yes to exactly this." A breath; the last of it, quiet. "I am Tang Sect. From this handshake until death. Ask me for the pages back never."

Tang Ya held the grip with both her hands, and her eyes were streaming and her grin was enormous and she said, wetly, with total command presence: "Sect of *four.*"

Behind them, Huo Yuhao whooped like the eleven-year-old he had finally been allowed to be. Bei Bei, who did not whoop, took off his belt-purse with the air of a man completing a plan years long — inside it, wrapped in oilcloth against exactly this day, lay a second hand-copied **Treasure Record**, begun the winter after a girl took a boy into a dying sect for love.

The Trade, then, by the last light, on the lakeside grass: Xuantian Gong for the root. The five arts named. And when the Sect Master, smiling like sunrise, asked her newest disciple which art he would take up *first*—

"Which one is least?" said Jiang Che of Linjiang, with the whole forest's patience.

"The Jade Hand," said everyone at once, including the fox, from a pocket, ruining the solemnity forever.

---

### ╱╱ TWO BEDS, TWO CANDLES ╲╲

That night, Huo Yuhao — badge on, key in hand — met the most ancient doorman in the world at the white dormitory door, was pronounced "a rare polite child," found room 108 with its month of dust and its two beds, bought a basin and a used rag with his own small coin, and cleaned the little room to a shine a palace would not have recognized, and sat on bare boards, and laughed once, alone, for no reason he could have named, with his whole life on the other side of it.

Two beds. A roommate coming, said the ledger. Somewhere in the strange sea of the school, a stranger who would share ten square meters of the world with him.

And across the city, in a courier's inn where the lamps were soul-tools and cost a copper the hour, a new disciple of a four-person sect sat at a bad desk and wrote the hardest letter of his life home — *Mother, Father: I have joined a sect; it is very small, and very old, and it needs us; also I need my forge-hammer sent* — while beside his elbow, on the desk's edge, in the terrible posture of the supremely comfortable, sat a small white fox with nine tails, watching the candle with golden eyes, keeping — for once in her long, long, unguessable life — the watch with him.

The trial was in three days. The lake lay between the city and the island like a held breath. Somewhere below the world, at the bottom of a young spiritual sea, something in an eleven-year-old sleeper dreamed of ten thousand years, and smiled in its sleep.

Ten statues. A thousand years of children. A sect of four.

The turning had begun.

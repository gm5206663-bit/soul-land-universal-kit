### ╱╱ THE TEST ZONE ╲╲

The mountain in cloth looked at the small gray boy the way a cliff looks at rain.

"Spirit martial soul?" Fan Yu said. His voice was granite with a hollow in it. "Can't use it. His body's too weak, his soul power's too thin. He couldn't lift the lightest fixed-load cannon we build. And his talent —" a pause, not cruel, just honest as stone — "ordinary."

"Ordinary," said Zhou Yi, in the tone of a woman about to move a mountain, "*this*," and turned to her special invite. "Show him."

Hu Yuhao's white ring rose. The gold filmed his eyes. And the world's most practical eleven-year-old did the only thing he knew how to do — he *shared*.

Fan Yu's frown lasted a quarter second. His perception — and this was the part that nearly broke Yuhao's nerve — *pushed back*. Something vast and old and shaped like a hunger stirred behind the man's calm and opened one eye at the boy's probing mind. Inside Yuhao's sea, something million-year-old and sleepy *woke* — a cool wash of ice, and the hunger's eye closed, and the sharing completed, and Fan Yu's whole face changed.

"*That* is a ten-year-ring skill."

"It mutated," said Zhou Yi smoothly.

"It would have to," Fan Yu breathed, staring at the white ring like it had lied to him personally. "A spirit martial soul absorbing a *wind* ring without dying is a miracle; getting *this* out of it is —" He caught himself. "**Cai Tou!**"

A mountain came running. Two meters of bald, black, cheerful muscle on legs, shirtless and gleaming, grinning at the small visitor with the uncomplicated warmth of a large dog. He Caitou — "*Cai Tou*, everyone does, you too, little brother, it's fine!*" — hoisted from his black belt-band a weapon that made Hu Yuhao's understanding of the word rearrange itself: a metal tube a meter and a half long, runes crawling its surface, a loading port in the stock. A **fixed-load soul cannon** — Fan Yu's own design, his decade of work: a weapon that fired its payload with minimal soul-power injection, freeing the user from the rank requirements of ordinary soul tools. The fee: the shells cost a small district's annual budget, and the accuracy at range was, in the current state of the art, *aspirational*.

"Little brother," said Cai Tou, shouldering the tube, his simple face going *focused* — and the weapon tracked a launched flying disc across two hundred meters of test-field, and fired, and the *sound* alone staggered Yuhao, and the blast went wide by four meters and detonated against the far wall like a small, furious sunset.

"Still can't hit," Cai Tou said, aggrieved.

"He can't hit," Fan Yu said, "*because* the targeting is the bottleneck." He looked at Yuhao with the eyes of a man seeing a road and not being able to walk it yet. "Thirty meters. That's your detection diameter? The cannon's effective range is four hundred. If you could see at a hundred — *one direction, just one*, a hundred meters of forewarning — you come to me, and I will teach you to build soul tools with my own hands."

He meant it. The whole gray building heard him mean it.

"Thank you, teacher," said Hu Yuhao, who had no idea what a soul tool was but had a woodshed-taught nose for a door opening, and bowed.

### ╱╱ THE FISH STALL ╲╲

Outside the canteen, a girl of fifteen pounced.

"Xiao Yuhao. You have no money."

"...Yes, Teacher."

"*Good.*" Tang Ya's scheming face was a masterpiece that should have been in a museum. She had, she explained — with the particular rapidity of someone who has been planning this since a stream — arranged everything: the canteen's purchasing auntie would bring twenty extra fish a day; a grill was ordered; charcoal, spices, and a spot at the academy gate secured. "Cost: one copper a fish. You sell at five. Your fire-control — those *eyes* of yours — means every fish is perfect. You, Xiao Yuhao, are about to become a *businessman.*"

"...And Teacher's role in this?"

"Investor," said Tang Ya, with enormous dignity. "And first customer. *Priority* customer. Also I get to eat while you cook. Also I brought my own stool."

The Tang Sect's founding and complete membership, having thusly armed her second disciple with the most economically sound business model in the Academy's history, advanced to the fourth year that afternoon — she and Bei Bei both passing the promotion trials, the new purple ring earning its keep — and by evening the gate of Shrek Academy smelled like perilla and salt and futures, and a line of senior students who had no idea they were being fed by the rebirth of a dead sect's economy stood twenty deep for grilled fish.

The fish-saint cooked. His eyes measured the fire's grain. His hands turned each fish at its exact moment. And the canteen aunts, the porters, the first-year sweat-soaked and the fourth-year smug, discovered that the worth of a boy is not always in his rank.

## The Afternoon

The afternoon was Zhou Yi's body-training — the whetstone's first grind.

Ninety-one children of privilege learned what *training* meant to a woman who had put one in four of her students on the island in the lake. It meant the body. Only the body. Soul power forbidden — "your rings are crutches; today we break legs" — and then the exercises: stance holds, sprint intervals, body-weight circuits that a woodshed boy survived by woodshed math and a butterfly boy survived by sheer physical privilege and a courier boy survived by *being a courier boy who had been doing this since he was eight.*

Jiang Che did not stand out. That was the discipline of it — the AT-optimized body, the courier's endurance, the life-core's sunlit recovery all humming underneath, and his *choice* was to hold the same pace as the middle of the pack, breathe the same four-count, finish the same rep count as the boy beside him. Not because he couldn't do more. Because a courier who outpaces the caravan is a courier who gets left behind.

Zhou Yi noticed anyway. She noticed everything. She said nothing — whetstones note the metal — but her eyes came back to the third boy twice during the circuits, and the second time there was something in them that was not disapproval. Perhaps the thing a knife-sharpener feels when a blade comes through the shop that does not need sharpening yet.

Class dismissed. Wang Dong collapsed theatrically onto his fur. Yuhao went to the fish stall. And Jiang Che — his forge shift waited.

### ╱╱ THE THREE-BED RHYTHM ╲╲ *(the days settle — the room finds its shape)*

The week that followed found room 108's rhythm, and the rhythm had three beats and a fox.

Mornings: three alarms, or rather one alarm and two victims of it. Yuhao woke at dawn because five years of woodshed discipline didn't know how not to; Wang Dong woke at noon because princes did; and the courier woke when the fox sat on his chest, which was 6:04, every day, because empires do not recognize weekends.

"*Up,*" she said, every day. "*The continent does not smith itself.*"

"It's Saturday."

"*It is Tuesday. It is always Tuesday. Up.*"

Evenings: the triangle. The forge-shift's return (smelling, always, of a fireplace with opinions) was room 108's unofficial dinner bell — Wang Dong's term, adopted by acclamation, vetoed by its subject, adopted again — and the three boys ate together at the desk or the floor or once, memorably, standing at the window watching the lake mist because Wang Dong refused to sit until his hair "settled" and Yuhao refused to miss the spectacle and Jiang Che refused to eat without them.

And between morning and evening, the craft grew on-page: the Jade Hand entering its second month, the burn-threshold now past working-iron and into *quenching* — the night he pulled a blade-blank from the oil-bath bare-handed and Old Qian, watching from two stations away, put down his own tongs and stared at his own hands and then at the boy's and then said nothing for the rest of the shift — and the first crude sleeve-dart track took four failures and a fifth that held, and the courier did not show it to anyone except the fox, who pronounced it "bad work by a smith who will do work that makes this look like a mud-pie," which from her was a *commendation.*

### ╱╱ THE ROOM ╲╲

Room 108, mid-afternoon, between the morning's laps and whatever the afternoon held. A three-bed corner room: the fur territory empty and immaculate, the plank territory occupied by a boy in meditation, and the third nail holding a coat in which something small and opinionated was pretending to be asleep.

The door opened — Wang Dong, back from an errand he hadn't explained, sitting on his fur mattress and staring at Yuhao with the particular intensity of a boy who has been *thinking*.

"Your martial soul. I've shown you mine. Fair's fair."

And so, in the ordinary way of roommates becoming friends, two boys who would one day matter to each other more than either had words for traded their truths on a ten-meter room. *Spirit Eyes. The eyes are the soul. The white ring is the baboon's.* (Wang Dong's face performed the arithmetic of *how* and could not close it.) *The detection reads what's coming before it comes. That's how the dorm fight went.*

And Wang Dong, in the trust that is only possible between people who have bled and not killed each other, gave back: *the Goddess of Light Butterfly. The most beautiful butterfly soul on the continent, nearly extinct. My second ring is thousand-year.* (And when Yuhao asked *how*, Wang Dong smiled a smile that had a whole locked house behind it, and said, "My secret.")

Then: "Show me the detection."

Yuhao shared. And Wang Dong — who had flown under his own wings since age six, who had never in his life met anything he couldn't outpace — stood inside another person's *world* for the first time, and the left and right and behind of everything stood up in his mind at once, and the blue eyes went wide and stayed wide, and something in the handsome, furious, arrogant boy rearranged itself very quietly around the fact that this boy whose soul power was a candle in a shed carried a *cathedral* behind his eyes.

"Again," Wang Dong said, hoarse. "Try pointing it. One direction. *Push*."

And Yuhao — because the mountain in the test zone had said *one hundred meters* — tried. He narrowed the sphere to a spear. He *pushed*. The golden light deepened, the forward arc extended, thirty-one meters, thirty-two — and his soul power left him like water from a kicked bucket, and the world tilted, and Wang Dong caught him before the floor did.

"*Hey—*"

"'M fine." He wasn't. He was shaking. But the number in his head was thirty-two, and yesterday it had been thirty.

From the third bed, the sound of someone else breathing. Jiang Che had come in from the evening forge shift — coke in his hair, a fresh burn on his left thumb, the Jade Hand's warmth still fading from his palms — and had heard the tail end of the martial-soul exchange. He had not been there for the beginning because he had been at the forge. That was the trade: class in the day, fire at night, the room in between.

He caught Yuhao's eye. "Thirty-two?" he said — because the number had escaped during the experiment, and a courier remembers numbers.

"...Thirty-two," Yuhao admitted.

"Yesterday?"

"Thirty-one."

The courier nodded. One meter a day. He didn't say *good* or *fast* or anything that would have been a flattery. He just nodded, the way a man nods at honest work, and sat down on his bed, and the room held three boys and one number and the silence of people who understood each other slightly better than they had that morning.

But in the dark of his coat on the third nail, a fox who had also heard everything said, very low, to her human alone: "*The small one carries an ocean in a teacup. And the proud one has a locked room behind his smile. Your room, little tide, is already the most interesting on this hall.*"

### ╱╱ THE FORGE ╲╲

The gray buildings stood west of the white halls, and between them ran the road Jiang Che now walked every **evening** after class — forge shift at dusk, the work-board's honest coin, the Jade Hand's nightly conduction — and every evening the same intersection: the **soul tool testing ground** to his right, humming; the **smithy yard** to his left, roaring; and between them a boy carrying a hammer, walking the seam of the age in the hour between school and sleep.

He had begun, this week, to notice things.

The smithy's Journeymen used *tools*. Not soul tools — honest tools, hammers and tongs and quirks — but the metal they worked was not always honest: ingots came in from the testing ground's offcuts, alloyed with minerals his father's forge had never named, and when the old Journeyman at the last furnace quenched a blade in oil, the oil *sang* at a pitch the Jade Hand's conduction could feel — a soul-resonance in the quench-still, a note that said *this metal has been near soul power and remembers it.*

The two buildings were not neighbors. They were *partners* — the smithy shaping what the testing ground dreamed, the testing ground breaking what the smithy made so the smithy could learn. And nobody, as far as the courier could see, had ever put a boy in the middle with a hammer and asked what fell out.

He asked the Journeyman, once, at the coke break, what the alloyed ingots were. "Soul-conductive metals," the old man said, with the shrug of a man who shapes what he does not own. "Sun-Moon minerals. They carry soul power the way copper carries lightning. The test yard builds their weapons out of them; we get the offcuts; we make tools out of them. Nothing special. It is just metal that remembers." Another shrug. "Go fetch the next batch."

And that was all. No revelation. No question nobody had asked. Just a boy carrying ingots between two buildings, learning what they were made of, because a courier's son knows that knowing what things are made of is half the trade. The Journeyman did not look at him with amazement. The Journeyman looked at him the way Journeymen look at boys who carry things and do not complain: with the grudging tolerance of a man whose morning is slightly less annoying than usual.

"Go back to work," he said, gruffly. But he showed Jiang Che, that morning, how to hold the alloyed ingot so the grain ran true — a thing he had never shown any student before, because no student had ever made him want to.

"Jiang Che." The old Journeyman, at the end of shift, not looking up from his tempering. "Your hands. Turn them over."

He turned them. The small burns from the first three nights had already faded to nothing — the Sun Flower's gift, closing what the fire opened — and the palms' skin had begun to take on a faint, deep jade undertone in the lines, where the conduction art was writing itself into the body the way rain writes itself into stone.

"How long you been doing the Jade Hand?"

"Six nights."

The old man grunted. Six nights was nothing. Six nights was a boy holding a warm stone and being patient. And yet. And *yet.* He had seen sect disciples come through this yard before, in his forty years of it — sent by teachers who thought manual labor was character-building — and not one of them had ever had the skin change color in the first week, because not one of them had ever practiced the tempering art *every single night* with the patience of someone who understood that the least art was the one that needed the most work.

"You're going to be here a while, aren't you," the Journeyman said. Not a question.

"Years," said Jiang Che, with the forest's patience, and picked up the next ingot.

### ╱╱ THE FIRST NIGHT ╲╲

That evening, room 108's three lamps (two soul-tool, one stolen candle) burned past what was sensible.

Yuhao sat cross-legged on his boards, meditating, recovering, his mind's forward arc measuring itself in single meters against a mountain's four hundred. On his way back from the canteen he had bought — with fish-stall money, first earnings, face carefully blank — a single egg and a bowl of rice, and eaten it sitting on the floor because chairs were furniture and furniture was for people who had arrived, and he had not yet arrived, he was still *arriving*.

Wang Dong lay on his furs, hands behind his head, staring at the ceiling with the expression of a boy doing arithmetic he refuses to show his work on. *A hundred meters. If the small one can push that arc to a hundred meters, the mountain in the test zone teaches him to build weapons. What does a butterfly need with a builder?* He didn't know. He only knew the locked room behind his own smile had just acquired a window, and the view was strange.

Later — after the forge, after the room, after the day — at the third desk, under a candle stub that was itself a luxury bought with forge wages, Jiang Che read the Treasure Record — the Jade Hand pages, as he did every night, one paragraph at a time, the way his mother taught him to read anything: slowly, and twice. The Jade Hand was not dramatic. It was a method: how to temper the hands, how to conduct heat, how to be patient when the stone is warm and nothing is happening and nothing is going to happen for months. Six nights of it. His palms itched. That was all. That was honestly all.

The fox, on his shoulder, was asleep, or pretending, which from her was the same courtesy.

He closed the book. He set the hammer on the desk. He went to bed.

And in the other two beds, two boys slept or meditated or stared at ceilings, and the third one went to sleep too.

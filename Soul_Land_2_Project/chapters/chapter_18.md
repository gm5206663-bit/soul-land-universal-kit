### THE LAST NIGHT *(the stall — ensemble)*

The gate's crowd was thinner than usual — the year was in its rooms, sharpening — but the line at the grill was the line at the grill, and first in it, radiating the dignity of a man performing a sacred rite, stood a mountain in a fifth-year uniform.

"Xu-dage," Yuhao said, catching the flicked silver coin without looking. "*Fengyu wuzu* — rain or shine."

"Every night without this bite," Xu Sanshi said, solemnly, "supper doesn't taste like supper." And then, lower, with the abrupt earnestness that lived under all his bluster: "The assessment. You'll pass. You hear me? You *pass.* Where else am I going to eat?"

It was at exactly this moment — the fish half-on the grill, the mountain mid-sentence, Wang Dong at Yuhao's shoulder counting the take — that the temperature of the evening changed.

A voice said, "**Xiao Xu.**"

Cold. Clear. The kind of voice that files people. And the mountain — the fifth-year whom sixth-years steered around, whom teachers handled with gloves — *shivered.* Actually shivered, the full length of him, and turned around slowly with his head dipped, and said, "…Xiao Tao-jie."

The line went white. Not quiet — *white*, as if the word had pulled the blood out of thirty faces at once.

### THE RED VEIL *(the stall — Yuhao's POV)*

She came out of the academy gate the way weather comes off a lake.

Red dress, long, moving like it was poured. A figure that made the evening feel underdressed — the exaggeration of it, the cinch of the waist, the long legs, all of it arranged by a tailor who had clearly been paid in empires. Red hair. And across the lower half of her face, a **red veil**, so that the only readable part of her was her eyes — and her eyes were the problem: pale pink, and *lovely*, in the way a hot stove is lovely, and cold enough that the loveliness filed the room instead of warming it.

The gate's crowd did the thing Yuhao had only read about in Tang Ya's gossip: parted — not scattering yet, just *bowing* open, a whole street of students suddenly finding their own shoes fascinating.

Beside the grill, Wang Dong went very still. Yuhao went very still for a different reason: a prickle at the edge of the detection, the sense of a soul like a **banked furnace** — and stranger than that, a *familiarity*, both his and Wang Dong's, though neither of them could have said from where. (The reader knows. The lake's madness wears a veil now; and the two boys the lake nearly cooked are standing eight feet from her, unburnt, unaware, and alive because of a worm and a fox.)

"Xiao Xu," the red woman said again, arriving. "Eating fish?"

"Yu… Yuhao's fish," Xu Sanshi managed, eyes on his own boots. "It's — it's really good, jie."

The veil turned. The pink eyes found the grill — and the boy at it — and something flickered in them that was not the furnace and not the cold but genuine, uncomplicated **curiosity**.

"Then I'll have one."

"Sorry," said Huo Yuhao, who had said this exact sentence to dozens of people and meant it the same every time, "I only sell thirty a day, today's thirty are queued, please come early tomorrow—"

The line **detonated.** Thirty customers went from white to *gone* between one heartbeat and the next — wallets abandoned, places abandoned, a boy vaulting the grill's table in his hurry, two girls achieving speeds their iron-training instructors would have wept at, a fourth-year pretending to receive an urgent message via his storage ring — and when the dust settled, the queue was Xu Sanshi, the fish, and nobody. The whole flight had taken perhaps two seconds, and it said more about the hierarchy of the outer court than any org chart the academy had ever drawn: one sentence from a rank-eleven fish-seller, addressed politely to the wrong woman, had nearly caused thirty casualties of *diplomacy*.

Yuhao stood at his grill with a fish half-turned, surveying the empty street, the abandoned coin where the second-in-line had stood, and the mountain trembling beside him. He looked at Wang Dong. Wang Dong looked at him.

"*What,*" said Huo Yuhao, to the entire situation, "just happened?"

"*There,*" said the red woman, with what was unmistakably, under the veil, a smile. "No queue. I can eat."

Xu Sanshi's eyes were performing semaphore at a frequency that should have been visible from the moon. Wang Dong, whose pride was load-bearing, stepped forward — "*Who* are you, and why is everyone—" and stopped, because the feeling had reached him too: the animal certainty, older than manners, that the person in front of him was the largest thing on the street.

The red woman raised one hand — the gesture unhurried, imperial — and on her right index finger a **ruby the size of a pigeon's egg** caught the lamplight; a light flash; and a **gold soul coin** hung in the air between them.

"Today's fish," she said. "All of it."

Wang Dong caught it. Wang Dong, prince, instantly and shamelessly converted: "…Sold."

"That's too much," Yuhao said, honest to the end. "Five coppers each."

"Then hold the rest for me," she said, already bored with money, "and I eat free when I come back." And she turned her whole attention — the furnace and the cold and the curiosity all at once — to the grill, and watched him cook.

### THE READING *(the stall — the courier's POV, at the edge)*

Jiang Che had come from the forge for the last night's fish, and stood at the stall's edge with the hammer over his shoulder and the coal-smell dying off his hands, and he did what he always did: read the street.

The red woman read as **six rings** — the same wall of pressure he had felt once, behind stones, over a lake — and as *managed*: not raging, not mad, banked hard, held by will the way a dam holds a river that has opinions. And under the banking, in the way the AT and the moisture-sense read all fires now, since a certain morning: **a wound.** The biggest wound on the campus. The one the fox had named — *a wound, not a weapon. I have seen her kind of wound before.*

Against his chest, the fox had gone from sleep to full, absolute, nine-tailed stillness. Not fear. *Archival.*

"*Little tide,*" she said, for his ear alone, in the flattest register she owned. "*The red one from the lake. Two hundred days, by my count of her channels' scarring, since the ice pressed her fire flat — and she does not know what pressed it. She is standing at our fish stall. Do not move. Do not greet. Do not be interesting. She is a queen walking a garden she believes is safe, and the garden has us in it.*"

"I know," Jiang Che breathed, at sip volume, and held his hammer like a man holding a tool, nothing more.

"Fire control so precise," the red woman mused aloud, watching Yuhao's hands turn the fish at the exact instant the flame asked. Under the veil, the amusement warmed by a degree. "You — a fire-attribute martial soul?"

"No, senior sister."

"Ah." She accepted the first two fish — took the veil aside only as far as the chin, ate with an odd, charming, feral neatness, and stopped. Looked at the fish. Looked at the grill. Looked — briefly, sidelong, past everyone — at the place where a courier stood very boringly at the edge of the lamplight, and the courier understood that she was not looking at him at all, but at the *smell of the night*: rain-on-a-river, green things, storm — because a fire soul at her tier reads air the way he reads water, and somewhere under the street's ordinary smells the memory of an impossible cold was trying to surface.

Her pink eyes narrowed — one heartbeat of a frown.

And then the furnace blinked, and the moment closed like water over a dropped stone, and she said, aloud, to no one in particular, in a voice that had decided to be lazy again: "It's good fish." She flicked one finger at Yuhao — *I'll be back* — and one at Xu Sanshi — *walk me* — and went, the mountain falling in beside her like a landslide choosing loyalty, and the street breathed again, and the evening reassembled itself out of the wreckage of its queue.

The fox did not move for a full minute after the red was out of sight.

"*Two hundred days,*" she said at last, quietly — and to see the fox quiet was itself a measure of the evening, for she was loud about empires and fountains and breakfast, and quiet only about the things that had teeth. "*And tonight the lake's cold was three feet from its own answer, and the answer smelled like rain, and she blinked. Do you understand what I am telling you, little tide? Somewhere under that banking, under the will and the veil and the two hundred days, something in her knows the cold exists. It reached for the memory. The memory has your smell on it now. She will come back to this stall — queens always return to gardens where something interesting brushed them — and every time she does, the file and its answer will be standing eight feet apart, selling fish.*"

A very long pause.

"*We are going to be so careful. And you are going to keep being boring. It is, of the many things you are, the one the situation needs most.*"

"Copy that," said the courier — and then, because he was eleven and the night had earned it: "Am I actually boring?"

"*You,*" said the fox, with immense dignity, "*are the single most interesting thing on this continent, wearing the world's most convincing coat of ordinary. Now buy the mountain's fish and come to bed.*"

"Copy that," said the courier, and bought the mountain's vacated fish, because couriers do not waste.

### THE MORNING-AFTER-THE-RED *(the breakfast — the room decompresses)*

"She was looking at you," Wang Dong said, with investigative intensity. "At the stall. The red one. She looked at you the way teachers look at *problems.*"

"She was looking at the fish."

"She was looking at the *air near you.* I saw it. The *sniffing.*"

"Some people appreciate atmosphere—"

"*Atmosphere.* You are *atmosphere* now. The inner court's worst monster sniffed you twice. Explain."

"I smell like rain," said the courier, with total serenity. "It's a whole thing. Ask the fox."

"The fox *scares* me—"

"*I heard that,*" said the fox, from the coat, without opening her eyes. "*And no.*"

And Huo Yuhao — who knew exactly what the red woman had been sniffing at, and who had held the biggest secret on the continent for four months with his brother and a fox — laughed into his bowl, quietly, at the single funniest thing the morning had to offer: the inner court's tenth-strongest and the boy in the cold-egg seat, three feet apart at a fish stall, and only two people and a fox in the world knowing it.

### THE EVE'S END *(room 108)*

They closed the stall early — no queue left to serve — and room 108 held the last hours before the gate, and the three boys and one fox spoke, at the end, the assessment-eve liturgy: first place; top ten; thirty and a ring; *the sect hunts.*

But under the liturgy, unspoken, the night's weight settled differently in each of them.

Wang Dong, who had caught a gold coin from the largest thing he had ever stood near, lay on his furs and stared at the ceiling and privately re-scaled his ambitions, the way boys do when the world shows them its actual ceiling.

Yuhao washed the grill's iron and thought about fire control, and precision, and the prickle of a familiar furnace, and slept without knowing why the veil had looked familiar, and dreamed of cold.

And Jiang Che sat with the Treasure Record open and unread on his knees, doing what the fox had taught him to do with dangerous knowledge: nothing, loudly. The academy's file walked in red. The file's answer smelled like rain. And between the two of them, at the assessment, would stand a team of three boys and a fox who had promised a worm to be polite.

"*Bed,*" said the fox, from the nail. "*Tomorrow you cut half a year's futures in half, and I have a clause to administer.*"

The clause held. The candle was long out. And the last sound in room 108 was a purr — a small empress settling her tails, filing the red veil beside the white ring, the hammer, and the standard, under the only heading that mattered:

*the year turns.*

### THE PRE-GATE-QUIET *(the last minute before the assessment grounds open)*

The boards were chalked. The year was assembled. Nine hundred children stood in their three-hundreds and did the arithmetic of survival, and the quiet before a war has a texture that is not silence exactly — it is nine hundred held breaths, and the fox, riding the coat toward the grounds, felt every one of them the way she felt weather.

"*Little tide,*" she said, into the one ear she owned. "*Nine hundred children. Three hundred teams. One hundred and fifty futures. In four hours, half of these held breaths will be somebody's worst memory, and the other half will be somebody's beginning, and there is no way to tell them apart from here.*"

"Is that a warning?"

"*That is an archive's note. I have watched ten thousand years of first days. They all smell like this one. The only thing that is ever different is who the children become after.*" A pause, her chin tipping toward the boards, toward the chalked names, toward the room of three standing in their line like a load-bearing wall waiting for a roof. "*Ask me about this one in a year.*"

"Copy that."

"*Adequate,*" said the fox, and the gates opened, and the year's cutting began.

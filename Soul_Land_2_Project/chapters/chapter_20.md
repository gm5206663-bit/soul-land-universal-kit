### THE MORNING *(Class One — ensemble)*

The classroom hummed like a struck hive — three-headed clusters everywhere, whispering tactics. Zhou Yi silenced it with three knocks of her fist on the doorframe, delivered the whole of her coaching in one sentence — *"Whoever blocks you through the assessment, beat the hell out of them"* — and left, and the class sat in the ringing after, collectively delighted.

"Is it just me," Wang Dong said, into the quiet, "or is Old Zhou getting *cooler* by the week?"

"Not cool," said Yuhao. "*Imperious.*"

"Qi-chang," said Xiao Xiao knowledgeably, from the next desk, and then ruined the expertise by blushing.

The elite-teams warning came from her too, and it landed hard enough that even the prince shut up for four seconds: every class had assembled its crack units, all aimed at the championship — *"We won't get through on talent. We get through on* coordination." — and the room's three-headed clusters all bent lower over their tactics, and somewhere in the middle of it the monitor of Class One formalized the command structure of Group One with the only currency he held:

"Wang Dong. For the duration of the assessment: you obey the monitor. In exchange—" the gray boy's face did not move a muscle "—there is fish in it for you daily."

"You — that's *extortion.*"

"You can choose not to eat."

The prince, cornered by his own stomach, surrendered. Terms were extracted in return (dorm cleaning: *forever Wang Dong's*; the monitor, unimpressed: "you have cleaned it once in three months") — and Xiao Xiao, now permanently resident on the monitor's side of every argument, laughed, and got teased for the speed of her allegiances, and went red, and the morning before the war was, in short, *a morning* — the kind armies spend and remember.

---

### THE EXCHANGE *(Group One — the abilities laid out)*

They took it outside — past the lake path, out the gate, into the tree-shade off the main roads, a flat clearing in the green — because three months of shared classes had taught them everything about each other *except the truth*: cultivation was private; nobody actually knew anybody's soul.

So it was laid out, soul by soul, in the clearing's quiet:

**Yuhao:** "Spirit attribute. Detection and sharing — you'll *feel* the field: fifty meters now, everything in it, moving, annotated. Some foreknowledge of strikes. No attack worth the name." A shrug that had stopped being apologetic somewhere in the iron quarter.

**Wang Dong:** "Assault. Close or ranged, and I fly." Delivered with the modesty of a sunrise.

And the courier — because the room of three had never once, in three months, actually *stated* it — let the rings rise: two yellors, the grass fanning blue-silver and dew-bright, vines threading the clearing's edge like a tide exploring a shore. "Control," Jiang Che said. "Binding, anchoring, pressure-seals. Field triage between rounds — the mend runs through me. And the grass reads water: air, ground, breath. I'll know where they are even when the detection is pointed elsewhere." A beat, and the honest addendum: "I don't kill well. I *hold* extremely well."

"I can work with held," said Wang Dong, with bloodthirsty serenity.

"And I can't do anything about *that* smell," said the prince, one heartbeat later, nose in the air with the precision of a hunting beagle. "You came from the forge. You always come from the forge. You smell like a chimney that won a fight."

"I smell like *work.*"

"You smell like a fireplace with opinions." He turned to Yuhao for arbitration. The monitor of Class One, soul-reader, field-commander, judge of weights and fire, considered his brother with total gravity and said: "Both. You smell like both."

"Traitor."

"I'm the monitor. I'm neutral."

"You're *bought and paid for,* your fish budget comes out of his winnings—"

"—which is why I'm neutral *out loud* and honest in my heart," said Huo Yuhao, and the clearing's morning collapsed into the specific chaos of three boys who had spent a quarter learning each other's buttons and pressing them for sport, and the fox watched it from her stump with her chin on her paws and did not say one word — because this, the noise, the mess of it, was the thing she had chosen him for the edges of, and her commentary was not required where the boy was already *real.*"

But the moment of the morning belonged to the small girl.

**Xiao Xiao:** "My main soul is the **Three Life Soul-Suppressing Cauldron** — attack and defense in one, with control: it *rams*, and the ram stuns — anyone I strike is dazed and hurting. My second soul—" and here the front-row middling-girl voice dropped into something older "—is a *flute*. **The Nine Phoenix Greeting Flute.** Support-control. One hundred-year ring, and what it does is *slow people down.*"

Wang Dong frowned: "Support for a control soul should buff *us*—"

"Sometimes," said Xiao Xiao, with the tiny smugness of a person about to close a trap she laid days ago, "weakening the enemy beats strengthening ourselves. You'll see." And then, briskly, the smugness packed away: "I won't use it unless we must. We hide some strength. *Slowly*." — and Yuhao and Wang Dong traded the look of two boys recalculating a teammate upward for the third time in a week, and Jiang Che, who had known the shape of the second signature since the fish stall, said nothing and was, professionally, delighted.

---

### THE SPAR *(two against one — the rehearsal)*

Xiao Xiao designed it, because Xiao Xiao had Opinions about rehearsal: no point showing each other tricks — *resist* each other. Wang Dong alone on one side; the monitor and the cauldron-girl on the other.

"You two? Against *me*?" The prince wagged one finger. "No chance. None. I want this minuted—"

The minuting stopped when the field went gold.

The sharing dropped over both of them — Yuhao's world, given away — and Xiao Xiao experienced it for the first time and made a small sound like a kettle achieving enlightenment: fifty meters of forest, three-dimensional, *annotated*, the enemy a bright arrogant point of soul-light with a trajectory written on it. And Wang Dong — one against two — came in *hard*, wings already up, learning in real time what it is to attack people who can see the attack being *born.*

It went the way genius goes: the prince was faster, stronger, and airborne, and the monitor's team could not touch him for the first thirty seconds — and then the cauldron came out.

It rose behind the small girl like a bronze bell the size of a bath-house — ancient, engraved, absurd — and it did not fly so much as *arrive*, and where it arrived, physics filed a complaint. The prince banked; the sharing showed the bank; the cauldron was already where the bank ended. He pulled up hard enough to hear his own soul complain—

—and the ground under him had become *rope.*

The grass. Two vines, placed ninety seconds ago at the start of the spar and never mentioned, because the third member of Group One had been standing at the clearing's edge being boring. The prince hit the bind at speed; the bind held — rank twenty-nine against rank twenty-three's momentum, no contest — and the cauldron's rim tapped him, once, gently, like a temple bell being introduced to a fly, and Wang Dong sat down in midair with his eyes crossed and his wings folding around him like a dropped curtain.

Silence. The gold faded from two pairs of eyes. Somewhere, a bird resumed.

"*Minuted,*" said Xiao Xiao.

"...Again," said the prince, from inside his own wings, with terrible dignity. "*Again.*"

And they ran it again.

The second run lasted a full minute — the prince solved the vine-tell in three passes, or thought he had, and discovered that solving a thing the fox has named is not the same as solving the thing: the third vine from the left no longer telegraphed, which meant it no longer needed to, which meant something *else* had started working while he watched the fixed tell. He landed one clean strike on Xiao Xiao's guard that time, and the cauldron's answering tap rang his teeth, and from inside the sharing Yuhao called the field like a helmsman — *"left, low, he's loading the right—"* — and it began, run by run, to be what it would be: not three talents stacked, but a single instrument with six hands.

The third run, they swapped problems: the prince attacked from concealment, from glare-angle, from above through canopy — every advantage a flying light-soul could manufacture — and the field *held*: the detection caught the intent before the wingbeat, the grass re-loaded the ground the moment his eyes left it, the cauldron made the air a question with no good answers. He won, that run, on points — one touch delivered, none taken — and stood in the clearing breathing like a bellows and grinning like a knife, and demanding, again, *again.*

The fourth run they did not finish, because Xiao Xiao's control cracked — the sharing, she admitted afterward, was *too much*: fifty meters of annotated world pouring into a girl who had spent three months pretending to be middling, and the sheer luxury of *seeing everything* made her try to answer everything at once. The cauldron scattered. Yuhao pulled the feed to a trickle mid-fight without being asked. The team learned its fourth lesson unbidden: the sharing, too, was a weapon with a caliber, and its gunner calibrated it on the fly — the whole shape of Group One assembling itself in an afternoon sun like a machine being timed — until the fox, who had supervised the entire proceedings from a stump with the expression of an engineer watching a bridge she designed carry its first load, announced the finding that mattered:

"*Little tide. When the gray one's eyes and your vines run together, you are not two controllers. You are one field with two hands. I have seen courts with fewer moving parts fall to pieces over a border. This team does not have a weakness it has not already met.*"

"High praise," said Jiang Che.

"*I said the team,*" said the fox. "*You still telegraph the third vine from the left. Fix it by Thursday.*"

---

### THE CALLING *(the afternoon — the gate)*

They walked back through the gate at noon with the year's future unrolling ahead of them: the assessment grounds filling, three hundred teams finding their boards, the arithmetic of survival going up in chalk.

Group One's name went up on the board's left edge — first alphabetically, first chronologically, first in everything but rank, where it sat third and did not mind — and beneath it, the draw's first cruelty: **Round one. Group One versus Group Nine:** three fourth-class students with a standard build — one assault, one agility, one support — solid, drilled, unremarkable.

The kind of team that had not yet met a field with two hands.

The fox rode the coat to the grounds, because empresses attend wars, and delivered the final briefing to one ear only: "*The gray one sees. The blue one kills — he is going to try to kill* beautifully, *contain that. The small one hides the flute until it wins something. You hold everything else, you mend what bends, and if a certain red dress appears anywhere near these grounds—*"

"A boy with grass and no ambitions."

"*Adequate.*" A breath against his collarbone, almost — if empires did such things — a squeeze. "*Go be boring, little tide. It is the most interesting thing on this continent.*"

The bell rang. Three hundred teams turned to the boards.

Round one was called.

---


### ╱╱ THE PRE-GATE-MOMENT ╲╡ *(the boards — the last minute before round one)*

They stood before the boards with the year's future in chalk, and the last minute before a war is a specific kind of silence, and the three of them filled it the only way they knew:

"Bear," said Wang Dong, reading the opposing team's roster. "There's a *bear.*"

"There's a bear," Yuhao confirmed.

"*There's a bear,*" Wang Dong repeated, with the slow dawning ecstasy of a man who has just won the most important wager in the history of wagers. "There's a bear. Cold eggs. *A week.* Pay the man."

"The bet was *conditional on the first match.* This is the *roster,* not the match—"

"*THERE'S A BEAR.*"

"—and my exact words were 'first match *has* a bear soul,' and the match hasn't — you know what, fine. Fine. One week of laundry." The courier looked at the boards, and at the bear, and at his team. "It was worth it. For one week of laundry, I would *gladly* have told you about the bear in advance."

"You DID know about the bear—"

"I knew about *bear frequency.* Knowing about the bear is *canon.*" A breath. The bell was rising. The teams were turning. The gate was opening.

"Now," said Jiang Che, and the room's three boys stopped being boys for exactly as long as the fight would take: "the gray one sees. The blue one kills. I hold everything else."

"*And the fox,*" said the fox, settling into the coat, one final time, for one ear only:

"*reads the field.*"

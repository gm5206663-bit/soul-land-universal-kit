### ╱╱ THE MONITOR ╲╲

When the Soul Saint's leaves finished and ninety-one children stood on legs that remembered being broken, Zhou Yi spoke.

"No one is eliminated today." Three seconds of relief. Then: "You should thank Huo Yuhao. I set a task: run until the bell. Only he ran from first step to last without stopping. Wang Dong — out."

The blue-haired monitor stepped forward, already knowing.

"You ran the most laps. But as monitor, you were told to make the rest better — and you ran for yourself. I strip the position. **Huo Yuhao — you are Class One's monitor.** The position floats. Wang Dong: if anyone challenges him, you answer."

Then, the whetstone's other edge: "Jiang Che. You ran at the front because I ordered you there. The front is the ceiling. The back is the floor. Learn the difference between showing and *lifting.* Dismissed."

The class charged Yuhao. Three times they threw him in the air.

Jiang Che stood at the edge, iron shirt on his shoulder, and caught his sect brother's eye on the third throw. Neither of them said anything. Neither needed to. The woodshed boy was grinning like a sunrise, and the courier was grinning back, and between them was the thing that had been built on a road between a forest and a city: not pride, not envy, just the quiet certainty that they were exactly where they were supposed to be.

*"He earned that,"* the fox said, from his coat. *"So did you. Different currencies."*

### ╱╱ THE ROOM ╲╲

Room 108, twenty minutes later, held weather.

Wang Dong washed first, behind his screen. Yuhao went to the hall's public bath. Jiang Che came back from the forge — coke in his hair, a fresh burn on his thumb, the Jade Hand's warmth fading from his palms — and sat on his bed to read.

He had read exactly one paragraph when the door slammed open and Yuhao — shirtless, wet, red-faced — appeared in the doorway at the exact moment Wang Dong — shirtless, wet, and midway through pulling on clothes — was standing in a shaft of evening light that the universe had aimed with malicious precision.

There was a cry. There was a *snap-hum* of soul power, and the room filled with the blue-purple afterimage of wings, and Jiang Che's Treasure Record page bent in the backdraft, and Yuhao was gone — door shut, running, face the color of a boiled lobster.

Wang Dong stood in the wreckage of his own privacy, wearing his shirt and an expression that was equal parts fury and something else that had no name yet.

Jiang Che turned the page.

"...You have wings," he said, mildly, to the room's second atmosphere. "I have a fox. We've all got things."

There was a silence so long that the fox shifted in the coat. Then Wang Dong sat down on his furs with the controlled dignity of a prince sitting on a throne that had just been kicked, and said, without looking at him:

"Your fox. It's real?"

"Sleeping. Or pretending, which from her is the same courtesy."

"...How many tails?"

"Nine."

Wang Dong was quiet for the length of a held breath. Then: "If you tell anyone about —"

"I won't."

"— what just happened."

"Neither will she."

Another silence. Then, from the fur bed, almost too low to hear: "Thanks."

Jiang Che turned another page. In his coat, the fox, who had been watching the whole exchange with the golden intensity of a creature who understood more about both of her roommates than either of them understood about themselves, said one word to her human: "*Interesting.*"

He did not ask what she meant. He was learning that with the fox, not asking was sometimes the whole relationship.

### ╱╱ THE STALL ╲╲

The east gate smelled like every cuisine the continent had invented. Tang Ya's operational plan was flawless: grill, charcoal, spices, twenty cleaned fish, a table, a stool (for the Sect Master). Yuhao cooked. The smell was a weapon.

Jiang Che arrived from the forge, hammer on his shoulder, and found his sect brother surrounded by a mob of paying customers. Tang Ya was on her stool, eating the inventory.

"Fish!" she commanded, spotting him. "Third disciple! Sit! Eat! This is a *sect economic summit.* I'm the chair. You're the treasurer."

"I'm the what?"

"Treasurer. You carry things for a living. Sit down and eat your sect's product."

He sat. He ate. The fish was, objectively, excellent — and he said so, in the way a courier's son says things: with his mouth full, nodding. Yuhao's face did something complicated that belonged to a boy who had never been told his work was good by someone who wasn't trying to recruit him.

"You need a bigger grill," Jiang Che said, between bites. "And a second set of hands at the fire, or your Sect Master eats the margin."

"I *heard* that," said Tang Ya, around a fish.

"You were meant to."

Wang Dong appeared at the edge of the crowd — drawn by the smell, or by the fact that his roommates were there, or by the fact that he was Wang Dong and staying away was harder than coming — and stood with the particular rigidity of a boy who does not know if he is welcome. Jiang Che looked at him, looked at the empty space on the bench, and moved the hammer.

It was not an invitation. It was a cleared seat, which from a courier was the same thing.

Wang Dong sat. Tang Ya put a fish in front of him without asking. The room's weather from the bathroom incident was still present, but it was the weather of a room shared by three boys who had all, in the last forty-eight hours, seen each other at their worst and their best, and the weather was *improving.*

And then a girl who moved like moonlight walked past, and the street went quiet.

### ╱╱ THE BREAKFAST COUNCIL ╲╲ *(the morning of the gate — the room, at the canteen)*

They ate breakfast like a war council, which meant Wang Dong ate like a king, Yuhao ate like a woodshed, and the courier ate like a man cataloguing the dining hall's exits — a habit his brother had noticed and never once asked about, because you don't ask a healer's son why he counts doors.

"Eggs are cold," Wang Dong announced.

"Eggs are *free,*" said Yuhao. "You're eating free eggs."

"They're cold AND free. Both things are true. The academy can't do one thing with eggs—"

"I like them," said Jiang Che, and both heads turned, because it was a known position, aggressively held, defended in three prior councils: the cold eggs were *his* and he would hear nothing against them. "They taste like a route. Cold eggs and a road. It's a good taste."

"That's the saddest sentence you've ever said."

"It's Tuesday," said the courier, philosophically, and the table cracked — Yuhao into his sleeve, Wang Dong very unwillingly into his eggs — and somewhere in the middle of the laughing the room stopped being three boys at a table and became, for good, the room.

### ╱╱ THE GATE ╲╲

**Jiang Nannan** — fourth year, purple uniform, gold hair in waves, a face that made every other beautiful person on the street into a rehearsal. She smelled the fish. She came to the stall.

"How much, little brother?"

"Five coppers, senior sister."

"Three. The cost supports three; five is greedy."

"It's not negotiable, and today's sold out."

For one heartbeat, the two most stubborn people on the street regarded each other with mutual professional respect. Then a mountain wearing a black uniform stepped between them and threw a gold coin at the grill with a throw that embedded it in the metal.

**Xu Sanshi.** Sixth year. Three rings. A boy whose love for Jiang Nannan had been rejected so many times it had become a geological feature.

The mountain reached past the space Jiang Nannan occupied and grabbed for the two remaining fish — the two saved for Tang Ya — and Yuhao, on pure detection-forged instinct, snatched them first.

And Jiang Che was already moving.

Not dramatically. Not with a battle cry. With the specific, unhurried efficiency of a boy who had spent five years learning what to do when someone reaches for something that belongs to his family: the grass came out — two yellow rings lighting at his feet, the blue-silver vines fanning — and the mountain's outstretched wrist was wrapped before it closed on air.

Xu Sanshi looked down at his arm. Then at the boy attached to the vine. Then at the two yellow rings.

"...Who —"

The vine tightened. Not cruelly. Evenly. A Pressure-Seal, applied at the wrist of a boy who outweighed the courier by forty kilos and outweighed him in rank by two rings, and the vine held, because the vines had sewn a fourth-year's hammer to the ground two days ago and a sixth-year's wrist was not a hammer.

"*Let go of the fish,*" Jiang Che said, at walking volume, in the tone of a boy who did not raise his voice because raising his voice was something that happened to people who were not in control.

Xu Sanshi's three rings lit. The Xuanwu Turtle shield materialized in his free hand — black, domed, the size of a door — and the street cleared around them like water around a dropped stone.

The shield-arm swung. The vines released the wrist and caught the shield's *rim* — Two-Point Anchor, the trial-proven grip — and for exactly one second the sixth-year with the fortress soul and the first-year with the grass soul stood locked, strength against craft, and the street watched a three-ringer fail to move a two-ringer's vines.

Then Bei Bei arrived — fast, cold, his lightning-arm already lit — and the situation, which had been about to become a real fight, became instead a *conversation,* because the first disciple of the Tang Sect stepping between his junior brother and a sixth-year with a shield was a sentence the whole street understood.

"Bei Bei." Xu Sanshi's voice was a landslide. "This isn't your business."

"He's my junior brother." Bei Bei's voice was a lake. "And the boy holding your wrist is my third junior brother. Everything here is my business. Fighting Spirit Zone. Now."

The mountain looked at Jiang Che. Jiang Che looked at the mountain. The vine released. Neither of them blinked.

"Fine," said Xu Sanshi. He dropped a gold coin on the ruined grill — "for the fish" — and turned to Jiang Nannan with a tenderness that was painful to watch. "Nannan —"

"Xu Sanshi. There is nothing between us. Stop." Ice, absolute. She walked away.

Jiang Che watched the mountain's face do something strong faces do when the thing they want most walks away in front of a hundred people. He felt — not sympathy, not pity, just the recognition of a force meeting a wall it couldn't break. He knew what walls looked like. He was learning what forces looked like, too.

Tang Ya appeared at his elbow, holding the returned fish, her face doing something between fury and pride. Bei Bei was behind her, arms folded, wearing the expression of a man who had watched his junior brother stop a sixth-year and was recalculating several things.

"You moved fast," Bei Bei said to Jiang Che. Not a compliment — an assessment. The first disciple's eyes were doing the thing they did: reading, filing, adjusting the file.

"He grabbed my sect brother," Jiang Che said, as if this explained everything, because it did.

"Your vines held a three-ring sixth-year's shield-arm."

"They held a fourth-year's hammer two days ago. The wrist was easier."

Bei Bei looked at him for a long moment. Then, quietly, to Tang Ya: "The sect has four disciples and I am no longer certain I am the strongest one in the room."

"You never were," said Tang Ya, with her mouth full, and the Sect Master's grin was the grin of a girl who had known since a lakeside oath that the boy with grass in his sleeves was going to be a problem for everyone who wasn't family.

"*That,*" she said, biting a fish, "was excellent sect work. Come on — we're going to the Fighting Spirit Zone. *Family* event."

### ╱╱ THE FIGHTING SPIRIT ZONE ╲╲

The Fighting Spirit Zone was a colosseum for young monsters. Ten gold bought a match; one silver bought a seat; the seats were full.

Jiang Che sat between Tang Ya and Yuhao. Wang Dong sat on his other side, having come without being asked, the bathroom weather between them still present but *shared* now — two boys who had each seen the other at a disadvantage and neither of whom had mentioned it.

The fox was in his coat. "Watch the mountain's left foot," she said, low. "*He leads with the shield but he watches with his heart, and his heart is watching a girl who just left. He will fight angry, and angry mountains leave their foundations.*"

Below, in the arena's white circle, Bei Bei and Xu Sanshi faced each other. The Tyrant Dragon's arm was already transformed. The Xuanwu Turtle's shield was already up. The referee's hand fell.

Jiang Che watched the way his father had taught him to watch a forge: not the hammer, but the metal. The lightning tested the shield three times — fast, precise, each strike at a different angle, mapping the defense the way his moisture-sense mapped a room. The mountain held. The mountain was *built* to hold. But the fox was right: Xu Sanshi's left foot was half a beat late on the third exchange, and the late half-beat was on the side facing the gate where a gold-haired girl had walked away.

"*Left side,**" Jiang Che said, almost to himself, and Wang Dong — who had been watching with the intense focus of a boy who cataloged everything — heard him.

"What?"

"His left foot. Senior brother's lightning will find it by the fourth exchange."

Wang Dong looked at the arena. Looked at Jiang Che. Looked back at the arena. "How do you know?"

"I watch metal for a living."

On the floor below, Bei Bei's Thunder Dragon Claw turned mid-air — and struck the shield's left rim, and the mountain's balance shifted one degree, and the mountain grunted, and Wang Dong looked at Jiang Che with an expression that was rapidly evolving from *roommate* to *something else.*

"...Forge-trained," Wang Dong said, filing it.

"*Courier-trained,*" Jiang Che corrected. "The metal doesn't care what you call it."

The fight went on. Tang Ya ate the evidence. And in the third row of the Fighting Spirit Zone, three boys sat shoulder to shoulder — a gray one, a blue one, and one with grass in his coat — and the shoulder-contact was the kind that only happens between people who have decided, for reasons they might not be able to name yet, that they are on the same side.

Below them, the fight settled into its rhythm: the mountain holding, the lightning probing, the seams widening. It would take time. The fox had said so. But in the stands above the arena, something was happening that the fighters below could not see and the crowd around them did not notice: three boys who had entered the academy as strangers — a woodshed orphan, a closeted aristocrat, and a courier with grass in his sleeves — were becoming, one grilled fish and one named seam at a time, the thing that the ancestor had built the Tang Sect to hold.

A team.

Not because anyone had ordered it. Because the fish was good, and the fights were interesting, and nobody at that table was trying to be anything other than what they were. And what they were, together, was more than any of them were alone.

The academy had a word for that. The academy had been built for that.

*Monsters.*

### THE BRACKET *(the square — chalk, the cut, the year drawn as a tree)*

By the time the sun cleared the stands, the square had a new smell to it: wet chalk, fried fish, and one hundred fifty teams' worth of fear.

The two big boards had been wheeled to the center of the square at dawn, and the clerk who filled them had the handwriting of a monk and the pace of a man being chased, and by mid-morning the year had been drawn as a tree. Three hundred names on the first board, one hundred fifty of them crossed out in the clerk's red chalk, the rest of them standing. On the second board — the one the whole first year was reading sideways, on tiptoe, in a noise like a market deciding what to be afraid of — the rest of the year, in rows:

The top sixty-four. Knockout. One line each, from the edge of the board to the center of it, and the line that bent was the line that went home. And under the sixty-four, in the same red chalk, the cut of the cut: **eighty-six more names**, crossed out with the same steady hand, and the square doing the thing the year would need a word for later — the specific silence of people watching their own futures get *counted.*

"Champion's prize," the referee read, at volume, the way a man reads something he has been told not to think about, "extremely rare."

The square leaned in.

Extremely rare. The two words hung over the top of the tree like a weather front. A ring, somebody whispered. A *bone*, said the voice next to him, quieter. And the whisper that made the rounds by noon was that nobody who had ever seen a prize called "extremely rare" had come back from asking twice.

Zhou Yi stood at the square's edge, arms folded, and watched the first year read its own year, and the fox — from the coat, from the stump, from the whole architecture of the day — said the only tactically correct thing there was:

"*The board is the truth now. The chalk dries at four. Learn the shape of it before it dries.*"

And the room — all four of it, because the room was four now and the first round had stopped the year pretending otherwise — read the shape of it.

Group One's line was chalked on the second board in the clerk's red: the first round's four on it like a receipt — four wins, eleven minutes — and the knockout's first match against a team the class had spent the whole afternoon trying to name and failing, and had settled, for want of better, on the number: **Group Forty-Seven.**

### THE RED AT THE VENUE *(the venue's edge — the inner court, the bank, the law of careful)*

The inner court came at the hour the fish stall's coals went blue.

They came the way the inner court always came: as a change in the weather. The delegation's red — the color the fourth-years had learned not to discuss, the color that meant top ten, that meant its own rankings and its own ferocity — walked the venue's edge in a line that the queue simply *opened* for, the way a river opens for a stone it has decided not to fight. The fish stall, which had made the day the way it made every day, stood at the square's edge with its line of senior students twenty deep and its brazier throwing sparks, and the whole machine of the venue's afternoon had one more thing to manage that it had not been priced for.

And in the line, the red veil.

The pink eyes did not look for anyone. That was the thing the year would file under terror: she did not look for it. The venue's noise arrived at her the way noise arrives at a banked fire — present, whole, *managed* — and the brazier at the stall's coals, which had been throwing sparks for the last hour, went quiet as the red line passed it. Not blown out. Not smothered. The flames simply leaned away from the heat the way a room leans away from a person who has decided to leave, and every coal on the stall's line remembered itself at once, and the line of senior students stood in a silence that had a texture to it, and the whisper the outer school had been running since the veil, grown a day of venue, came around one last time:

*Fire control so precise. A fire-attribute soul?*

It was the wrong answer. It was the only answer the outer school had, and the inner court had its own, and neither of them was on the board.

In the coat, the fox was writing.

"*The bank holds. Two hundred days and a day of venue, and the turtle's pressure is still the load-bearing wall. She does not know what pressed it; she does not know it does not know. The cascade runs through the venue the way it ran through the lake — same fire, same banking, one day of chalk between them. File it. Bury it.*"

The courier, at the medics' corner he had kept through the night — because the board's day had started at dawn and the board's day would end at dusk, and useful things do not rest — had already read the structure of it. The banking. The wound under the banking. The load-bearing wall, and the day's worth of sparks a brazier could throw and would not. He had read it the way he read a forge: not the fire, the *structure* of the fire — and the fox, watching him watch, said the law of the day in the smallest voice the coat had:

"*The ledger can hold it. You can hold it. That is the whole law, courier. The rest is the venue's.*"

He did not answer. He was already mending a sixth-year's wrist from yesterday's draw, and the red line was past the brazier, past the board, past the square's whole noise, going into the inner court's own ground where the outer school's chalk did not reach, and the year's arithmetic updated itself in a ledger nobody would ever be shown:

*The red is at the venue. The bank holds. We are going to be careful. We are already careful. Careful is the floor, and the floor holds.*

### GROUP ONE'S LINE *(the second board — the four of them, the shape, the unshown team)*

The class's afternoon belonged to Group Forty-Seven.

That was the rumor's name for it, because the board had given them nothing to work with: a clean record from the first round, ten matches won without a single one of them being described by anyone who had watched, no injury list, no second that had cost them a fight, and a number — Forty-Seven — that the clerk had chalked between two teams the class had not yet learned to fear. A class that could name the bear, the spears, the fire array and the cousins, because the first round had given it four textures to hold, found that it could not do this one — and a class that cannot name a thing gets *creative.*

"The ledger is empty," said Yuhao, at the board, reading the team the way he read a field — the distances first, then the structure, then the absence of both. "Ten wins. No description. No injuries. When a team that clean has no ledger, it's either because it hasn't been watched, or because it doesn't want to be."

"Or because it's the other one," said Wang Dong, and the board's whole row went quiet the way the field went quiet when the prince read it.

The fox, from the coat, from the middle of the three of them and the courier who stood at the edge with the ledger open and the chalk dust on his cuff, said the beat the day had been walking toward:

"*Two teams in this year are genuinely dangerous. One is yours — the board knows it by now, the chalk knows it, the eraser knows it and has given up. The other has not shown itself yet. I am telling you where I think the ledger is: not on that board. In the watching. The team that wins ten matches without a description is the team that is still deciding what it is. Watch the watching. That is the whole read.*"

Xiao Xiao, at the board's edge, small and shy and the only one of the four who had not spoken yet, said the quietest thing in the panel, and it was the load-bearing one:

"The cauldron is still," she said. "It's been still since the board went up. When it's still, it's not resting. It's *listening.*"

And the prince, who had been reading the row with the correctness he brought to everything, closed his eyes for one second — the way he closed them when he was checking his own arithmetic — and said, to the board, at volume, for the stands:

"I would lose to them once. Cleanly. That is what they are owed."

The class wrote it down. The year would need the word for it later; the prince's official positions had a habit of becoming the year's vocabulary.

### THE HUNT'S SHAPE *(the forge — the ledger's page, the wall at thirty, not yet)*

The forge smelled of the stall's coal — the stall's coal, because the stall had started selling coal to the forge three weeks ago at a price that made the old Journeyman's eye twitch and the economics make sense, and the year's little closed loop ran as it always ran: the stall fed the forge, the forge fed the sect's tools, the sect's tools fed the stall's line, and the line fed the stall.

Jiang Che stood in it with the ledger open, and the page was the day.

At the top of the page, in the clerk's red chalk as he had copied it: *extremely rare.* The two words, and under them the structure the words bought — the window (the bracket's week, the cut, the ground that would stand open after the top of the tree bent), the prize (whatever "extremely rare" was, it was at the top of the tree, and the top of the tree was a shape, and shapes could be planned) — and under both, in a courier's shorthand, the page the whole day had been walking toward:

*The wall is at thirty. The ring must come at the wall. The shape: the forest south. The water-kin. The Sun Flower's precedent — seven hundred forty, at the rank's ceiling, eaten into a property. The question, planted months ago, still a seed: what does the grass itself want to become?*

And under the shape, in ink the rest of the ledger would have to wait for: **NOT YET.**

Planned. Measured. Window marked. Not started. The hunt did not leave the ground this day; the hunt got a *page*, and a page, in a courier's shorthand, was a door with a lock on it, and a name on the lock, and the name was thirty.

The gray boy came in at the hour the coal went blue — the monitor, the field, standing in the forge's doorway with the look he got when he was about to read someone's ledger through the person.

"You've been planning," he said. It was not a question. It was a field-read: the arithmetic's look, the not-calm texture, the page that was a door.

"Planning," said the courier, without looking up from the coal. "The wall is at thirty. I've been planning since I was twenty-six."

"And the ring?"

The coal went under his hand, and the hand did the thing the class had learned not to describe, and said, in the mild voice the class had learned meant trouble for somebody:

"At the wall. The hunt's shape is drawn. The window is marked. The rest is the forest's." He turned the page, which was the whole of the answer, and the gray boy read it the way he read everything — the distances first — and said, after one second, "I'm not going."

"No," said the courier. "You're not. You're the field. The field stays."

"And what am I?" said the voice from the forge's other side, where the prince had been standing for a minute with his arms folded, listening the way he listened to a board, and Xiao Xiao beside him, small, the cauldron's stillness in her eyes, the blush starting at the exact moment the prince looked at the page instead of her.

"The rest of the room," said the courier, at the coal, at the page, at the year. "The room goes with me. That's the whole design."

### THE NIGHT-LEDGER *(the grill — the bracket's night, the arithmetic of the shape)*

The grill went to its last coal at the dusk bell, and the boards — both of them, standing behind the table like the year's two witnesses — held the square's edge while the year's first day of the bracket's shape ended in fish and arithmetic.

"One hundred fifty to sixty-four," said Wang Dong, counting on fingers, the way he counted everything that was owed. "And eighty-six going home before the dusk bell. Group One: four on the board, one line chalked, not yet fought. Group Forty-Seven: ten wins, no ledger. The red at the venue, and the bank holds. The prize: two words and a weather front." He looked at his hand. "The eraser had its best day of the year. The clerk is buying a second one."

"It's not the eraser," said Yuhao. "It's that the ninety seconds don't come off. You can get the names off. You can get the wins off. The *time* stays."

"The time stays," the fox confirmed, from the table's edge, tail over paws, gold eyes on the boards' distant chalk. "That is how a rumor gets a spine. A number stays, and the words build around it."

"Then give one to this," said the prince, at the board's row, the correctness of a man who had found an item in the ledger that had no entry for it. "The team that won ten without a description. The year will need it on the board, because the board will have a line for them, and the line will need a name before the fight does."

"The watching," said the monitor, serenely, with the whole class's record in his voice. "Group Forty-Seven: the watching. The team that is still deciding what it is. Filed. The class can dispute it at the chalk."

Xiao Xiao, at the end of the table, had the cauldron's stillness and a question the cauldron had been holding since the board went up, and she said it the way she said everything — small, shy, at the exact weight it needed:

"The cauldron heard them in the first round. Group Forty-Seven. It heard them, and it went still. I want to know — when it's still like that, is it the watching, or is it the *other* watching? The kind that has been waiting for a team it can finally use its whole self on?"

The table looked at the fox. The fox looked at the boards. The boards, which were chalk, and which had no answers, and which were the whole truth of the day, held their two witnesses' worth of names, and the fox said:

"*Both. That is what a still cauldron is: a question with a lid on it. The lid comes off in your fight, not before. That is your doctrine, and your doctrine is correct, and the cauldron knows it, and it is still because it is patient — and patience, in a cauldron, is a weapon the year will need a word for.*"

The grill's last coal went under, and the year went quiet for the word, and the courier — the courier, the weather, the hammer, the boring one, the floor — turned the last fish with the exact turn he gave everything, the Captain's first form at the scale of a fish, and closed the ledger on the page that was a door, and looked at his team in the grill's last light, and the boards behind them, and the inner court's red at the edge of the venue, and the shape of the rest of the year, and said the thing the whole day had been walking toward:

"Tomorrow: the first line of the bracket. The name: the watching. The page: not yet." He put the ledger down, flat, the way he put down everything that was done and everything that was owed. "After: the rest of it. Same time."

"Same time," said the room — the four of them, in the cadence that was theirs, and the grill's last coal, and the boards' two witnesses, all of it in it — and the year's first day of the bracket's shape ended not in chalk or glory but in fish and arithmetic and a door with a lock on it, and the particular, unremarkable, load-bearing comfort of people who had decided, months ago, to be a team, and were now, finally, being read by the year the way the year was going to read them all.

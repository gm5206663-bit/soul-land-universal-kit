# Chapter 1: The Two Hiders

## Canon Reference: Novel Prologue (the God Realm) + ch 1-3 (the departure; the road; the stream; the baboon; the Skydream merge) — each beat given its full space; the scene rebuilt through the OC and the fox
## Timeline: Age 11 — the road to the forest, and the day the world blinked

---

### THE GOD REALM

Soft light lay on the clouds like a mother's hand.

A man stood at the edge of it, looking out over distances that had no bottom. His hair fell to his feet, water-blue, a spill of quiet sea; his robe was blue, and if you looked at the robe too long, the ripples in it took your soul gently by the collar and invited it in for a swim. He was handsome the way mountains are handsome, and his eyes — sometimes empty, sometimes holding everything, sometimes flashing a thread of violet that made spring and autumn change places — were, at this moment, unhappy.

The Sea God sighed. The Law Enforcer of the God Realm sighed. Tang San sighed — one sigh, three names.

"San-ge." A pink skirt stepped out of nowhere and took his arm in a motion so practiced it had become a law of nature. A long scorpion braid swung against his shoulder, and Xiao Wu rested her head there and wound the end of the braid into his hair, proprietary as weather. "Still sulking."

"Not sulking. Thinking." He folded her in. "One day here is a year down there, Little Wu. The Good and Evil Kings made their bet and went down to play, and I came back up to keep the house — and twenty years for us was ten thousand for the world, and the Tang Sect—" The sentence ended, the way old griefs end: unfinished, and finished.

"Withered. I know. You built it with your hands."

"I built it with my hands." His eyes went far again. And then they changed. "But something has changed, Little Wu. *New stars* have risen over the Douluo mainland." He held up two fingers, as if the hand had decided before the god did. "One burns like a bonfire seen through mist — ten thousand threads, and every one of them tied to my sect. I have tried to read its fate, and it is fog all the way down. But there is a second. Quieter. Low on the world's edge, a river-light — and here is the strange thing: it is not new at all. It was *born* with this age, Little Wu. It has been shining eleven years, and I never thought to look for it, because it never once burned the same color twice." His mouth curved, rueful. "And when I read at its fog, the fog does not lift. It changes shape, and keeps changing — as if its whole answer to being read is to grow another way of answering."

"Two," said Xiao Wu, doing arithmetic she distrusted. "There was one, in your reading, last week."

"There was one," Tang San agreed, "when there was one."

"Oho." Her eyes went bright and merciless. "New *stars*. If they're good enough to inherit a certain someone's god position someday, a certain someone could retire, and a certain someone's wife could finally travel—"

"You," Tang San said, tapping her nose, "only think about playing."

While they spoke, a small head poked out of the cloud bank nearby — a girl of eleven or twelve, big-eyed, with a face seven parts her father's, softened to seven parts her mother's art. She had been listening with the patience of a hunter.

*Always hearing Papa and Mama talk about the Douluo mainland,* thought Xiao Qi. *It sounds like fun.*

And her small figure slid back into the cloud, and was gone — and the world below acquired, unnoticed, one descending goddess and two rising stars.

### THE MANSION

The north back door of the greatest estate on the continent opened without a sound, which no door of that house had ever done for its owner's son.

A boy slipped out. Eleven, small, clean gray clothes, a bundle not much bigger than a loaf. He eased the door shut, walked three steps, and stopped as if he'd hit glass. He turned and looked back at three thousand mu of golden roof-tiles blazing in the noon.

His mother had been the Duke's own maid, taken from her family as a child, raised at his side. One night, twelve years ago, a child began in her. When the Duke rode to his wars, the Duchess ruled — and the Duchess was the Emperor's best-loved daughter, and jealousy is patient. *A contagious sickness,* the Duchess had said, when the boy was two. The woodshed behind the servants' quarters. No coin. Ten years of slow, loud dying.

His mother's last lesson had been delivered in a whisper, like all her real ones: *people can only rely on themselves.*

"Mama." The boy's voice was very level, which in an eleven-year-old is louder than shouting. "Watch me from where you are. However much it takes — one day I'll come back and put all of this under my feet." A breath. "From today I take your name. Not Dai. **Huo. Huo Yuhao.**"

He had reached rank ten the day before, after five years of work that would have broken a grown man's pride — innate soul power of exactly one level, the priests had said at his awakening, and a spirit-type martial soul so rare the continent had almost no soul beasts to feed it. It was, he had decided long ago, his leaving day.

In the bundle: one change of clothes, dry rations, a hand-drawn map, seven silver soul coins and five copper — his mother's whole estate — and, at his lower back, a sheathed short blade a foot long. The only gift his father ever gave his mother. The blade was seven inches of autumn water; the sheath was jade-green leather; the hilt was plain, old, and fit a hand like a settled promise. She had held it until the last hour of her life, and then given it to him.

*White Tiger Dagger,* she had called it. He had never asked why.

He turned north, toward the Star Dou Great Forest, and ran. His eyes stayed dry until the mansion was out of sight, and then only the wind argued with them.

### THE ROAD

He walked for six days.

He took wrong turns and corrected them, learned to read the sun, thanked strangers, ate the free things his mother had taught him to find. A rank-ten body is a good body; grief, it turned out, could be burned as fuel; and three hundred li a day carried him north, away from an empire and toward a forest with a human-beings'-forbidden-zone reputation and, somewhere in it, the one thing his soul could eat: a spirit-type soul beast.

Forty li south of that forest, a courier was finishing a delivery.

The waystation matron had stamped him paid, broken his mother's triple knots before his eyes the way the Hall's rules required, and told him to tell Hall Master Wen the blue was darker this year. The road home ran south. The courier stood at the waystation gate, a healer's sealed case going back empty on his back, a hammer over one shoulder, and a fox in his coat doing her mid-morning impression of a sleeping empress — and he read the road north, because reading the road was the whole trade, and the road said: *three travelers, half a li, coming this way.*

Two of them were noise before they were people. A girl's laugh, ringing off the trees; a boy's warmer one under it. They came round the bend — the girl first, fifteen or sixteen, light-blue traveling clothes, black ponytail, walking like the road owed her money; the boy behind, tall, gem-blue hair, hands laced behind his head, walking like a man who had never once needed to hurry. Behind them, thirty paces back and losing ground, a small gray figure with a bundle, walking with a settler's pace, chin set, eyes forward — a boy who had been walking alone for six days and did not know how to be in front of people.

The fox opened one eye. "*Strangers,*" she said, in the tone of a customs officer, and then the second eye opened, which was different, which was rare. "*Little tide. The small one at the back. Look at him with your better eyes.*"

Jiang Che looked. And what the moisture-sense and the six domains gave him, standing quietly at a waystation gate, was this: the laughing pair carried no packs and no fatigue, and their soul power sat on them like good clothes — trained, comfortable, *senior.* The small gray boy carried six days of road in his legs, grief in a knot between his shoulder blades, and around his heart a soul power so thin it was nearly a rumor — rank ten or less — and under all of it, steady as a pilot light, something that made the fox's ears stand at a different angle.

"Old," she said, quietly, only for him. "Older than his feet. Walk with them."

"Why?"

"Because you are about to," said the fox, who was sometimes honest about the shape of her knowledge and never about its source, and closed her eyes again, and that was the whole of the argument, because it always was.

So when the trio reached the gate, they found a blue-haired boy their own age leaning there with a courier's case and a smith's hammer, who nodded the nod of the road — the one that means *water, news, or help, whichever you need* — and asked, at walking volume, if the north road was quiet, because he was for the forest's edge and had heard stories.

The girl answered the way thunder answers: "Stories! The north road's *boring*, we've walked it for — Bei Bei, how long have we walked it—"

"It only feels infinite," said the gem-haired boy, and smiled the lazy smile, and looked at the courier with the professional interest of a man cataloguing him — the hammer, the case, the stance, the calm — and filed him under *later.* "We're for the forest too. Periphery business." A glance back at the small gray figure catching up. "All of us, apparently."

And Huo Yuhao, arriving level with them, looked up at the blue-haired stranger who was somehow walking *with* them now, and saw nothing he could name — only that the stranger's eyes were on him the way nobody's eyes had been on him since his mother died: reading him, and staying kind.

They walked north together. It was as simple and as irreversible as that — though it very nearly was not, because the first thing Jiang Che said to the laughing girl, unprompted, out of nowhere, in the flat voice of a boy stating a fact he has held too long, was:

"Your fish is burning."

"*My* fish—"

"The third one from the left. It's past done. I could smell it from the gate."

And Tang Ya — Sect Master of a thousand-year sect, terror of the fighting zones, keeper of the Dragon-Beard Needle — looked at her fish, which was indeed past done, and said with dignity: "I like them crispy."

"Charcoal isn't a crisp."

"I *said*—" but she was laughing, and he was almost smiling, and that was the moment the road took them — not the hammer or the case or the fox's old silence: a boy who could not watch food burn, and a girl who could not stop laughing about it.

They walked north together. It was as simple and as irreversible as that — though Tang Ya would say, later, that it had been less a decision than a *weather event*: the stranger simply fell in with them the way rain falls with a valley, and within a mile the road had reorganized itself around him without anyone consulting it.

What she could not have named — what nobody who fell into step beside him ever quite named — was the *texture* of it. The boy walked like water finding its level. The air around him smelled, very faintly, of rain on a river — clean, mineral, with something green under it and something storm-like under that — so that walking beside him was, in a way nobody remarked on and everybody felt, like walking beside a season. Bei Bei noticed the smell and filed it. Tang Ya noticed that the birds had gone quiet in the way they go quiet for *safe* things, and said nothing, because she was busy. And Huo Yuhao, walking in front of a stranger for the first time in six days without his shoulders climbing toward his ears, noticed only that the road felt narrower with three people on it, and warmer, and that the wind that stirred the blue hair beside him smelled like the beginning of the year.

### THE STREAM

The stream found them an hour later, and the grill was the girl's idea.

Her name, it emerged over the next mile, was Tang Ya; his was Bei Bei; and they were — this took some assembling, and some of it was assembled wrong on purpose — students of the academy that lay past the forest, out here on *periphery business,* which meant, as far as either of them would say, hunting. What Tang Ya hunted, presently, was lunch: she heard the stream the way a hawk hears a field mouse, and by the time the rest of them had reached the bank she had already declared the shallows a *personal territory.*

The gray boy — Yuhao, he offered, half-swallowed, and then again, steadier — was the one who did something about it. He waded in with a tree-branch he had cut and trimmed with the jade-green dagger from his back, and stood over the water, and his eyes went *strange*.

The courier watched this with the fox's whole attention. What the moisture-sense read was not visible to anyone else: the boy's soul power, thin as it was, pooled up behind his eyes and *turned the world slow.* Every stone, every fish, every flick of light off scale — read, catalogued, aimed at. The boy's body was an ordinary body. The boy's *eyes* were a instrument the continent saw once in ten thousand years, and the courier knew it the way he knew good steel — not by name, by *grain.*

Fourteen fish. Spear-caught, one after another, in twenty minutes, by a boy too polite to look proud and too hungry to hide it well.

The fish were cleaned with the white dagger — *that* blade drew a long look from Bei Bei, who said nothing, and from Tang Ya, who said "ooh" — and grilled over a fire of stream-side wood with salt from someone's pack and perilla that Yuhao picked without being asked, because he knew the free things the road offered, all of them, by heart.

And while the fish cooked, the thing happened that the road had been walking toward.

Tang Ya, mouth half-full, demanded the stranger's story — *every* story, that was her rule — and Yuhao, cornered by kindness, gave the smallest safe version: going to the forest, looking for a spirit-type soul beast, his martial soul was his eyes. And then, because the girl's warmth pulled words out of people like the sun pulls sap, the version grew: the mansion he had left at noon six days ago; the mother who had died in the spring; the five years of cultivating one rank at a time against a house that wished him invisible; the vow, which he did not say was a vow, and which everyone at that fire heard anyway.

"I'm sorry," said Tang Ya, and for once there was nothing under the words but the words.

"It's a good vow," said Jiang Che, quietly, because his mother had taught him that the thing a grieving person needs most is for someone to treat their grief like a *craft* — worth doing, worth doing well.

And Bei Bei looked at the small boy with the woodshed hands and the eyes that slowed the world, and then at the blue-haired stranger with the hammer who had said the exact right thing at the exact right weight, and made a small adjustment to several private files.

It was during the second round of fish — the girl was on her fifth; the fox, from the courier's coat collar, had begun a soft commentary on human feeding behavior that only one person alive could hear — that the fox stopped mid-sentence.

Stopped *entirely.* Ears up. Both eyes open. Every tail still.

"*Little tide,*" she said, and her voice had gone to a register he had heard perhaps three times in four years, "*there is something under this road. Something old, and patient, and it has been listening to that boy for a hundred li. It is *below us* and it is *waking* and what it wants is *him.* Do not be alarmed. And do not — **do not** — interfere with what comes.*"

"Fox—"

"*Promise me.*"

"...I promise."

"Good," said the fox, and vanished down into the coat, and against his chest her small weight was a held breath.

Twenty heartbeats later, on the far side of the fire, mid-laugh, Huo Yuhao's head turned — sharply, blindly — toward the tree-line. And the tree-line moved.

### THE AMBUSH

It came out of the trees at the speed of a thrown spear: a hundred jin of brown muscle and yellow fangs, a ten-year Wind Baboon in full hunt-lunge, aimed — because predators are simple — at the smallest, weakest, nearest prey.

Yuhao's dagger was coming up and his body was already knowing it would be late. Tang Ya's hand was in her sleeve. Bei Bei was rising—

—and none of it mattered, because the rings were already rising.

Two yellows, bright as struck coins, igniting at a courier's feet as he crossed the fire's circle at a dead run, and the grass came out of him the way morning comes out of a river — dozens of vines, blue-silver, dew-gilded, and they did not lunge; they *arrived.* A Trip-Line across the ankles at the exact stride's extension. A Pressure-Seal around the throat — *hold without harm*, the Hall's law, iron-gentle — and a third vine simply taking the beast's own lunging momentum and giving it to the tree it had launched from, winding it there, wrapped, a hundred jin of apex predator going from missile to parcel in the space of one breath, roaring its outrage into a wall of living rope.

Silence. Fish smoke. The fire popped. Tang Ya's knife was half-out of her sleeve with nowhere to put it.

"...Well," said Bei Bei, into the quiet, with the careful voice of a man revising his afternoon. "That happened."

"You *have* a martial soul," Tang Ya said, accusingly, as though he had been withholding dessert — and then, doubling down on the grievance with the energy of a person who has spent an hour being corrected about fish: "— and you eat like a *bird.* I've been watching. You had two. TWO. There were fourteen fish. Explain yourself, hammer-boy."

"I was counting them."

"*Counting—*"

"There are five of us and fourteen fish. If I eat four, someone eats two. I eat two." A shrug. "My mother's arithmetic. It's a curse."

"You are *eleven.*"

"My mother's arithmetic doesn't care." — and then, with the double-take of a girl who has just noticed the dessert is also *beautiful*, which she had been noticing, in a low-grade distracted way, since the waystation, and had been too busy to file, "— and you *are* aware that your hair is doing that on purpose? What *are* you? You smell like a thunderstorm that got good intentions."

"Grass," said the courier, apologetically. "It binds."

And Yuhao was staring — not at the beast, at the vines, at the two rings, at the quiet boy his own age standing between a predator and a stranger as if it were a delivery — and something crossed his face that was not envy and not hope exactly but the first draft of both.

Then the ground answered.

### THE WAVE

It split two meters from the stream — gold-white light breathing up through the cracks, cold rolling out like a door opened on winter — and along with the cold came a *pressure* that was not cold at all. It was a bell struck at the bottom of the world, a hundred li wide, and every living thing in it went down.

Bei Bei dropped mid-motion, hand still outstretched toward Tang Ya. Tang Ya folded over her knees with a word half-sworn. Yuhao slid from his log and lay in the grass, eyes open, unfocused, breathing.

Jiang Che fell too — the fox's warning or no warning, the wave did not negotiate with rank twenty-nine — and knew, in the last instant before his thoughts went soft and far, the specific humiliation of a courier watching his parcels tip while his body refused the order to catch them.

The fox stood up.

She stood up out of his coat, and stepped onto his fallen shoulder, and down onto the grass — and she *grew* — not much, and not violently; a body the size of a hunting hound, moonlight-white, nine tails fanning like a standard unfurled, the gold tips catching light that had no source in the clearing. She walked — unhurried, sovereign — past three frozen humans and one frozen courier, and sat down before the emerging head of something seven meters long and white as jade and ringed in ten bands of gold, and she said, aloud, in the human tongue, in a voice that made the stream run backward for a moment:

"You are loud, worm. I could hear you from the far side of the province. Come up slowly, and tell me why I should let you finish."

### THE NEGOTIATION

The Skydream Iceworm looked at the fox for a long time. It is possible that nothing in a million years had looked at *him* first.

"*You are not frozen,*" it said, into the air, into minds, its voice a landslide being lazy.

"I am *polite,*" said the fox. "There is a difference. You are a million years of something that cannot fight; you have surfaced under my human's road; your wave has put four children on the grass, two of them *mine*; and you have been listening to the gray one for a hundred li." Her tails did not move at all. "So you will tell me what you want. Then we will discuss whether you get it. The discussion will be short — my patience is a young thing next to yours, but infinitely better armed."

The great silkworm was quiet. And then, with the dignity of the genuinely ancient, it told her: of the cold-marrow cave and the ten golden rings; of the forest's ten-thousand-year kings, who had found him sleeping and full of the world's purest essence, and *shared* him — a war over the prize, and then a negotiation, and then a thousand years of sitting in concentric rings of strength around a living well, sipping eternity through a straw. Of the tenth ring, grown out of pure refusal; the hiding; the ten thousand years of searching, in mind and body and every seep of spirit-power on the continent, for the one vessel that could hold him: **a spirit-attribute human.** Of the first candidate, a Titled Douluo, who had burst like a rice cake under nothing but the first touch of his mind.

"*I am dying,*" the Skydream said, without drama, which was how the courier's son would have known it was true even if the fox had not. "*Not soon. Soon enough. I have run for three thousand years, and I am tired, little fox, tired the way stones are tired. The gray boy is the first spirit-soul I have found in ten thousand years. His vessel is small and his rank is a candle — but his *sea* — brother has looked at gods' seas, and the boy's sea is a cathedral with the doors open. I will seal myself. All of me, down to a thimbleful, seal upon seal upon seal, until what remains can live inside a rank-eleven body without cracking it. I will be his ring, and his tenant, and the least of what I was. In exchange I will not die in a hole — and perhaps, one day, he will be large enough that nothing hunts what is mine.*"

"And the boy's consent?"

"*He would give it. He is built of yes; did you watch him feed us? But he is frozen, and the seals must be laid while the body rests, and I will not wait for the thaw — the kings that chased me are not sleeping, and tonight they will feel where the wave came from.*"

The fox looked at the small gray figure in the grass — at the boy who had walked six days off a vow, who had fed strangers first and himself last, who had stared at binding-grass like it was a sunrise. Then at her own frozen human, who had promised not to interfere, and who had kept the promise by falling down.

"Terms," she said. "**One:** the merge happens now, and it happens *gently* — and the boy wakes healed, and rested, and *remembering that something chose him.* Because something did. And he will need that memory on the days he doubts, and those days are coming — I have read his road the way you read his sea. **Two:** the two whites—" her chin indicated Bei Bei and Tang Ya, frozen mid-stride, "—saw nothing. You will veil the sight as I veil mine. Their story will be a baboon and a lucky ring, and the story will hold — because it is kinder to the boy than the truth would be this year, and because I am not done hiding, and a story with holes is a story with *me in it.* **Three:** the courier is mine. When he wakes, I will tell him everything — I do not lie to my human, and he keeps what he carries the way ground keeps seeds: quietly, and forever, and standing where the boy can see him. **Four:** you do not touch the boy's *self.* You are a guest in a house, worm, and guests do not rearrange the furniture. His thoughts are his; his choices are his; when you can speak to him, you will *advise*, and you will let him refuse. If ever you move him without his leave—" and here the fox's voice went, for one syllable, to a register that had not been used on this continent in ten thousand years "—*I will find what remains of you, and I will be less polite than I am being today.*"

The Skydream Iceworm considered the largest threat and the smallest fox it had ever met, and found, to its own surprise, that it believed her.

"*Done,*" it said. "*You have my word. I am too old to break words — they are the only things I have ever managed to keep.*"

"Then finish," said the fox — and sat, tail over paws, to witness: the only waking witness in a hundred li.

The great beast laid its forehead against the frozen boy's brow, and poured itself away. Ten golden rings descended the small body like moonrise. A white radiance flowed inward — seal upon seal upon seal, a million years folded down and down and down to a thimbleful — and under the boy's unconscious ribs something sealed itself in beside his heart like a passenger taking a seat for a very long journey. Where his wounds had been — and there were none, for the vines had been fast enough even for that — the body drank. The rank-eleven candle flickered, held, and *brightened.*

And at the last, as the gold sank away and a single ring the color of moonlit frost — *lustrous white; white as no ten-year ring had ever been* — circled the sleeping boy three times and folded into him and was gone, a voice, growing sleepy, growing distant, said one final thing into the record of the world. Not to the fox. To no one.

"*Brother is going to sleep now. It was a good road. There were fish.*"

### THE AFTERMATH

The thaw came back into the world the way feeling comes back into a struck limb.

Tang Ya surfaced from her own knees mid-oath. Bei Bei finished the reach he had begun a minute and a lifetime ago. And they found the clearing exactly as a clearing should be after a baboon ambush: the beast trussed to a tree by blue-silver vines; the boy unconscious in the grass with a white ring fading beneath him and not a mark on him; and a blue-haired courier already kneeling at the boy's wrist with two fingers on the pulse, as if he had been there the whole time.

Which — in the only sense that would ever matter — he had.

"A baboon," Bei Bei said slowly, examining the trussed beast, the unwounded boy, the untouched grass, the story assembling itself out of everything his eyes could see. "It lunged, the boy's soul ring flared — shock of his first kill — and he went down. A ten-year ring. For a spirit soul." He said it the way careful men step over things they cannot name, and the story settled over the clearing like the mist the fox had promised — kind, and holding, and with a very old author.

"His pulse is strong," said the courier, which was true. It was also slow, and vast, and *tidal* — a rhythm with pull behind it now, a sea with a new tenant — and the boy's skin carried the clean cold smell of the inside of winter, and all of it was filed, all of it was kept, and none of it was going anywhere near the two kind strangers assembling their gentle story three steps away.

Tang Ya gathered the boy up with the tenderness she kept under all her noise. "We take him north. There's shelter past the tree-line before the deep woods — and *you,*" she found the courier with a finger, "you wrapped a soul beast like a parcel, you're either the most dangerous person I've ever shared a fire with or the most useful, and either way you're walking with us until I decide which."

They walked north out of the clearing: a girl carrying a sleeping boy, a gem-haired young man walking rearguard, and a courier with a fox in his coat and a secret the size of a continent — and the secret's second keeper, purring very quietly against his collarbone.

"*Little tide,*" the fox said, when the trees had closed behind them. "*What I told the worm — that the small one is yours too — I meant it. He carries a god's thimble now, and gods' thimbles attract storms. You will want to be standing where he can see you. It turns out that is your whole job.*"

"I know," said Jiang Che. "It's been my whole job since the stream."

"*Good.*" A yawn, imperial, against his heartbeat. "*Now. About the ruffling—*"

"Don't push it."

"*I permit it in advance,*" said the fox, and slept.

And the road went on north — toward an academy, toward a year, toward a sect that did not yet know it existed and a jar of monsters that did not yet know it had just grown two hinges.

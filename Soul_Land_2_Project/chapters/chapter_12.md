# Chapter 12: The Fur

## Timeline: The night after the fight — room 108, three boys, one pill, one fox

### ╱╱ THE DOOR ╲╲

Bei Bei could not enter the first-year dormitory — school rule — so the handoff happened at the door, in lamplight, with Yuhao unconscious between Wang Dong's shoulder and Jiang Che's.

The first disciple fed his junior brother a pill with the matter-of-fact tenderness of a man who had decided this boy was family and was now simply *managing the asset*: a deep blue pill the size of a cherry, veined with white, mist curling off its surface, the faint clean smell of it hanging in the stairwell like a promise. Wang Dong — educated, well-born, trained since birth to know what things *cost* — went very still. Jiang Che, standing beside him, watched the pill go into his sect brother's mouth and did not know its name, but the AT knew *quality* when it touched the boy's perceptions, and so did the fox, whose head rose from the coat collar and whose nostrils worked once, twice, filing the pill's signature somewhere behind her crown.

"Take care of him," Bei Bei said to Wang Dong — to *Wang Dong*, the stranger, because he was the roommate and the rule was the rule. And then, catching Jiang Che's eye over the unconscious boy: a nod. Between sect brothers, the nod was a whole sentence.

"*Little brother*," said the fox, as they climbed the stairs, very low, to her human alone, "*that pill your senior gave away so easily is worth more than your father makes in a year. Remember who gave it. And remember that he gave it as though it were a copper. That is what a sect's wealth actually is.*"

Jiang Che filed it. He was, by nature and by training, a boy who filed things.

### ╱╱ THE FUR ╲╲

They put Yuhao on his boards. Wang Dong stood over him for a moment with a fist half-raised and an expression that meant *you saw me naked and now you're unconscious and I can't even be angry at you properly* — and then went to his own bed to meditate, because Wang Dong's answer to every unresolvable emotion was training.

Jiang Che sat on his own bed in the dark, and the room settled, and this is what the night was for: the third desk, the candle stub, the Treasure Record's Jade Hand pages, read slowly and twice.

And the fox came out.

Not dramatically. She never did anything dramatically. She poured herself out of the coat collar onto the desk, walked the desk's edge once — inspecting, as she inspected everything, the domain's perimeter — and then stepped onto his knee, turned around three times like water finding its level, and sat. Facing him. Moonlight-white in the candle stub's light, the gold tips of nine tails catching little flames of their own, her eyes old gold and half-lidded, imperial even at rest. Especially at rest.

He read. She sat. This was the oldest contract between them — older than the speaking, older than the road: the nights when the boy read and the fox presided.

After a while, without deciding to, he lifted his free hand and put it on her head, between the ears, and ruffled.

Not stroked. *Ruffled* — against the grain, two or three times, the way you ruffle a dog that has earned it, or a younger brother's hair, or a thing you own no correct ceremony for. Her fur was what it always was: cool on the surface, warm underneath, finer than anything a merchant had ever tried to sell his mother, and it stirred under his fingers with a faint static life that was not quite fur and not quite light.

The fox's eyes opened fully.

The room's ambient dignity dropped ten degrees. Somewhere in her ancestry, a hundred generations of crowned ancestors rotated in their barrows.

"*You are rumpling me,*" she said, in the tone of an empress informing a courtier that he was standing on the throne.

"Mm," said the courier, and did it again.

"*I permit this once.*" A pause, in which nine tails arranged themselves into a posture of extreme tolerance. "*Record that I permitted it.*"

"Recorded."

"*Once.*"

"Once per night," he said, turning a page with the hand that was not committing treason.

The fox considered the amendment. The fox, who had spent four years training this human in the precise mechanics of what she would and would not endure, and who had watched him grow from a seven-year-old with burned hands into whatever he was now — this boy who had stopped a mountain that afternoon and given a fortune away without blinking and kept another boy's secret in his chest like a stone he had no intention of showing anyone — the fox let out a breath through her nose, the smallest concession ever granted by her line, and leaned one half-degree into the ruffling hand.

"*Once per night,*" she allowed, to the candle. "*While the candle burns. Do not tell anyone.*"

"Who would I tell?"

"*The list of beings who must never know,*" said the fox, "*includes the small one, the proud one, the Sect Master, your parents, the ancestors, and every god currently watching.*"

"That's everyone."

"*Yes,*" said the fox, with satisfaction, and butted her head, once, briefly, against his palm — the payment rendered, the account settled, the empire's single annual expenditure of affection booked and closed — and settled back onto his knee with her chin on her paws to supervise the rest of the reading.

He ruffled once more, very quietly, when he thought she was asleep.

She was not asleep. She permitted that one too, and filed it, unbeknownst to him, under a heading in her own private archive that had exactly one entry in it and was not labeled in any human language.

### ╱╱ THE MIST ╲╲

Past midnight, Wang Dong woke from his meditation because the *air* woke him.

Soul power — thick, clean, water-adjacent — was breathing off the third bed across the room in slow waves. He sat up. In the dark, Huo Yuhao's sleeping body was veiled in a faint blue mist that flowed *into* his skin and back out of it, and the mist carried a scent like a river at dawn, and as the minutes passed the blue deepened and the smell turned from clean to *rich* to something else entirely.

Jiang Che was awake too — had been awake, had felt the soul-power tide shift through his moisture-sense a full minute before Wang Dong's cruder instruments registered it. He watched the mist work with a healer's son's eyes and said nothing, because the night had already taught him what was his to say.

But the fox sat up on his knee. The gold eyes were open all the way now, and they were not lazy at all. She looked at the sleeping boy wrapped in blue — the boy who had nearly fainted in the stands spending three soul skills and a secret art to turn a fight — and she looked at the mist doing what mist does to a body that has finally been *given* something instead of asked for something, and what she said, to her human alone, in the voice of a creature who had eaten treasures that could buy cities, was a single word of professional assessment:

"*Wasted on the young.*"

Which was, from her, a blessing.

Wang Dong held out as long as an eleven-year-old aristocrat could — about twenty minutes — and then crossed the room, sat on his heels by the third bed, and watched the mist with the hunger of a boy who had just realized that the sect he had asked to join that evening kept *medicine like this* in a senior brother's pocket like loose change. He looked across the sleeping boy at Jiang Che.

"...This is normal for your sect?"

"We're four people and a fishing rod," said Jiang Che. "Nothing about us is normal."

Wang Dong looked at the mist. Looked at Jiang Che. Looked, last and longest, at the sleeping boy whose breathing was slowly changing from *exhausted* to something with tomorrow in it.

"Good," he said, quietly, with a prince's whole authority. "I want in."

And then — because he was Wang Dong, and feelings were for rooms with locked doors — he went back to his furs, gathered his bedding into his storage soul tool with the decisive movements of a boy executing a decision already made, and left the room entirely to find somewhere else to sleep, because the room currently smelled like a riverbed and his standards had limits.

The door clicked shut behind him, and the fox said: "*He will be the fifth.*"

"The Sect Master hasn't decided."

"The Sect Master," said the fox, resettling her chin on her paws, closing her eyes, "*has been deciding since the fish. Come to bed, little tide. The small one is about to be reborn, and rebirth is loud, and you have a forge shift in five hours.*"

### THE SECOND CLAUSE *(the contract amended)*

The courier ruffled her, twice, against the grain. The gold eyes opened.

"*We had a contract.*"

"The candle's still lit. And the clause says once per night *while the candle burns* — it says nothing about *during.* One was during. One was after the meditation ended, which was arguably a different activity period—"

"*You are lawyering an empress.*"

"I'm a courier. I read the small print. *You* wrote the small print."

A silence with weight. Then: "*Twice,* on nights when something enormous happened. But I define enormous."

"Deal."

"*Tonight did not qualify.*"

"Tonight a boy got reborn across the room."

"...*Approved retroactively,*" said the fox — and butted her head, once, against the ruffling hand.

### ╱╱ THE GRIME ╲╲

The fox was right about the rebirth. She was right about the loudness, too, though the loudness was mostly *smell*.

In the small hours, the mist's clean scent turned rank — thick, sour, organic. The pill was doing what the finest medicines of the age did: pushing out of the body everything the body had been storing that was not the body. In the morning dark, Yuhao's uniform had gone black with what his meridians had shed; his skin wore a film of grime like a man pulled from a swamp; and the boy himself slept on, smiling faintly, dreaming of warm water, while his body finished becoming something it had never been.

Jiang Che left for the forge shift before the boy woke. At the door, dressed, hammer on his shoulder, he paused — because the moisture-sense had told him, all night, in its wordless way, what was happening in that small body: the channels widening, the flow quickening, the whole sluggish river of a rank-eleven soul power being dredged in a single night into something that would *move.*

*The pill did that,* the courier thought. *The pill I gave away.* And the thought had no regret in it at all — only the specific satisfaction of a boy who had spent his life watching his mother pour her craft into other people's bodies and knew exactly what it looked like when the craft *worked.*

In the corridor, half-asleep against the wall, sat Wang Dong — who had returned sometime before dawn rather than sleep elsewhere after all, and who was positioned, with the total deniability of the naturally proud, exactly where he would hear his roommate wake up.

Which he did, at first light, through a door left ajar: a sneeze, a splash of foul language, the frantic scrape of a window thrown open, and the sound of a boy discovering that his own bed had become a swamp overnight. The smell that left room 108 in a cloud was indescribable and also, to a healer's son's education passing by on the stairs, deeply encouraging: it was the smell of a body discarding its whole past. Wang Dong, in the corridor, listened to the frantic washing-up and the scrubbing of a bed-board, and smiled once, alone, where no one could see him - and then rearranged his face into indifference.

And at dawn - as the east went shell-pale and the first purple breath of it stood over the academy's roofs - the boy who had been reborn stood at the window facing it, and did not know he was being watched from the stairwell by two people: a courier on his way to the forge, and a fox who had been awake all night. Neither of them knew the name of what he was doing. Both of them saw what it did. The gray boy's eyes drank the purple like a man drinking after a fever, and the world around him - the air, the dust, the morning itself - leaned subtly toward him, and when it was done his eyes were brighter than they had been the day before by a margin that had nothing to do with reflection and everything to do with what was behind them.

The fox, from the coat pocket, on the way down the stairs, delivered her morning address:

"*The small one will wake up reborn and will immediately believe the grime is a prank. Let him.*"

"Let him," Jiang Che agreed.

"*Also,*" she added, pausing on the third step to yawn enormously, showing teeth that belonged in older centuries than this one, "*tonight. The ruffling. Once per night was the term.*"

"It was."

"*The candle had gone out before the second one,*" said the fox. "*Technically, it was a different night. I have reviewed the contract. The clause holds.*"

And Jiang Che laughed — quietly, on the morning stairs, on his way to carry ingots between two buildings — because he had read the Treasure Record's small print for two weeks now and had finally, at last, met a contract lawyer he could not out-argue.

Behind him, in the room he had just left, a boy was washing off the first thirteen years of his life. Beside him, in his pocket, was an emperor who had permitted two ruffles and filed a third. Ahead of him, between two buildings, was a hammer and a fire and the patient years. The courier went to work.

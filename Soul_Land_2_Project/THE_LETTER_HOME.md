# The Letter Home
### *An artifact of Chapter 5 — Jiang Che's letter to Linjiang, written at a courier's inn on the night of the oath. Recovered to the project files for the record. The reply came eleven days later; both letters now live in the Hall's archive box, under the family's seal.*

---

*To Hall Master Wen, the Healing Hall of Linjiang City, south by the river road — from her son, fast post, blue ribbon. Mother first, then Father, then the both of you at once, because I know how you read.*

Mother.

I have joined a sect.

Put the kettle down. I can hear you setting it down from here.

It is called the Tang Sect. Yes — *that* Tang Sect; the first one; the one from the stories you told me when the river flooded and the lamps were out. It is very old, and it is very small. There are four of us.

I will tell it plainly, because you taught me that plainness is the only honesty that survives a postal system. The Sect Master is a girl of fifteen named Tang Ya, and her soul is Blue Silver Grass, like Father's, like mine. Not like mine — I will come to that. She is the last of her line; her mother and father are dead, and she has been carrying the sect alone since she was younger than I was when I found the fox. The first disciple is Bei Bei — you would like him; his soul is a dragon of blue lightning and he is the gentlest person I have ever watched be dangerous. They took a second disciple on the road: a boy my age named Huo Yuhao, who had no one, and who is now their family, and who is the reason I am writing the next part of this letter at all.

Mother — *the sect needs us.*

Not me. **Us.** You, and Father, and the Hall.

Here is the wound, told plainly: four thousand years ago the world got bigger, and the weapons of the age changed. The old steel — their steel, the Tang steel that armed the continent — was outbuilt. Not out-crafted. *Out-built.* Their designs are still finer than anything the new age makes. But the new age makes its weapons by the thousand, cheap, and any soul master can fire one, and so the orders stopped, and the great house was seized, and the thousands became hundreds became four. The Sect Master's law — hers and Bei Bei's, written years ago — is that the sect will not die proud and useless: they mean to fuse the old craft with the new age and build the next steel. Every disciple must study the new craft as well as the old. They wrote the law and then they waited, Mother, because there was no one left who could *hold both ends*. The last smith of the Tang Sect died before Tang Ya could walk.

And then the road sent them a courier.

You will want to know what I swore. I will tell you exactly, because you taught me that a person who will not repeat his oath did not mean it. I swore to the sect for my life. The arts are the family's blood, and if ever I leave, I leave them behind, every page. I said — in front of the lake and the statues of the ancestors — that I don't choose things because they are strong. I choose them because they are weak. *Weak is where the work is.*

Father will understand that part. Father — I took the Jade Hand. They offered me all five arts and I asked which one was least. The least one. The smiths' art. The dying art. The one that has been waiting ten years for hands like mine — and Mother, my hands are *yours*, and I will learn to put them in a forge the way you put them in a wound: evenly. The first night it felt like holding a warm stone and being patient, and I have never in my life been so sure of anything.

Here is what I ask. Not money — the Sect Master would give me her last coin and I will not let her; I have work, honest work, the academy's work-board, forges and greenhouses; I have my courier's savings; I am *paid*, Mother, stop making the face. What I ask:

**Send the hammer.** The short one, off the second shelf of the home forge, the one with the linseed handle you waxed. Not because I lack a hammer — I bought an honest one today — but because the first hammer of a Tang Sect smith in ten years should be a hammer from a house where nothing was ever thrown away because nothing was finished. And Father — when you can spare a month, any month, any year: come and see. The sect has no forge. I am going to build one, and I learned the foundations from you, and a man should not raise his first forge wall without his father finding fault with the courses. Bring the whole box. I will need the opinion more than the tools.

And Mother — Hall Master Wen — I am going to say the thing I actually sat down to say, which took me the whole first page to reach.

The sect has no healer either.

It has a girl who binds her own blisters with throwing-needle thread, and a boy who believes being mended and being looked at are the same thing, and a teacher in everything else who would not know a fever from a foreboding. They are four people, Mother, and they walk into a forest that tried to eat two of them in the first week, and when I put my hands on them and did what you taught me — the triage-read, the soothing current, the stitch-and-steady — the Sect Master looked at my hands the way Tang Ya's whole line looks at grass. Like something extinct, walking.

I am not asking you to leave the Hall. The Hall is your life and Linjiang is your river and I would sooner move the river. But the Hall trained me, and its craft does not stop at the city gate — you have always said the Hall's true patient is *the road*. So I am asking for the thing you gave me anyway, and I am asking for it formally, in ink: your leave. To practice the Hall's craft on a sect of four that the road gave me, until the day the road gives them someone better, and on every beast-bitten swordsman and forge-burned apprentice the years send me besides. The knots you tie in the world are yours. This one I tied myself.

I am well. I am fed — fed *badly*, but fed; the canteen has no chairs and you would have opinions about the onions, which are old. My room has two beds and no roommate yet, which the fox finds hilarious for reasons she declines to share. The fox is well. The fox is *insufferable*, and I would not trade her, and when I was sworn in she said one word — *'adequate'* — and I have decided to have it engraved somewhere.

I sat tonight in a city of monsters, under a lake named for a god our sect's founder carried, and I understood the thing you and Father never say out loud: that you built your lives the same way this dead-and-living sect built its house — one mended thing at a time, and nothing thrown away, and nothing finished.

I am your unfinished thing. Keep being patient with me.

Your son,
**Jiang Che**
*of Linjiang, of the Hall, of the Tang Sect — in that order, for now; ask me again in ten years*

*P.S. — The Sect Master can cook nothing and eats everything. If you send a parcel, send two of whatever it is. — J.C.*

*P.P.S. — Tell Father the blue is darker this year. I still don't know what it means. He will. — J.C.*

---

### *The reply (Hall archive, eleven days later, her hand, the family seal):*

*Son.*

*The kettle is fine. The handle is fine. Everything is fine.*

*Your father read your letter twice, put on his coat, and walked to the forge, and he has been at the second shelf for an hour now, and I am not permitted to see what he is doing, but I hear whetstones.*

*You have your leave. You had it before you asked; you had it the day you were born with river-water sense and your father's stubborn grass. The Hall's craft goes where its hands go. Mind the onions.*

*Your sect Master is heard of here — the old stories reach rivers. Tell her the Healing Hall of Linjiang says: a house of four that gives away its coin and keeps its word is not a small house. It is a young one.*

*The fox may have the second parcel.*

*— Your mother, who is not crying, it is the onions in this letter somehow, mind them,*

***Wen Lan***

*P.S. — The blue is darker because the river is deeper where you are. It always has been. Ask me again in ten years. — W.L.*

--- # Chapter One: What the Master Said

Glorybound City sat on the edge of the eastern coast with the sea laid out beyond it, and on Awakening Day the city came early to Red Mountain Academy's street. Dawn arrived and the parents were already packed tight, children in hand, in front of the white walls and red roofs.

Tang Wulin was small for six, except that he wasn't: he stood a half-head taller than most of his classmates already, with short black hair and the biggest, clearest black eyes on that whole street. Long eyelashes a girl would have traded for. He wore plain commoner's clothes and somehow drew eyes anyway.

"Dad," he said, tugging at his father's hand as they walked. "What do you think mine is going to be. My martial soul."

"Dad doesn't know either," Tang Ziran said. He was a man of average build and average height and an average face, the sort of man a crowd built itself around hiding. Inside the average face, something gloomy shifted. Awakening Day decided lives. "It depends on luck."

"I'll have soul power for sure. Right, Dad."

"Tell a story," Wulin said, bouncing a little. "The way you do at supper. A Soul Master one. While we walk."

He loved his father's stories about Soul Masters. His father knew it, and on a day like today, with the whole city's hope pressing on a small back, a story was the only pack a father could carry for his son.

"There was once a Soul Master of the Advanced Division," Tang Ziran began. The rhythm of the stories came back to him easily, as it always did. "This one could call the fish up out of the harbor. He stood on the docks and thought hard, and up they came."

"That is not a real one."

"It is on the border of the sea, son, everything here is borderline real."

"Tell the one about the man with the hammer."

"When you are older."

"I am six today."

"Soul Master logic," his father conceded. "Six is older."

"Regardless of whether you have soul power or not," his father said, "dad will always love you."

It was the truth, and it was also the thing a man says when the truth has an ugly branch in it. Wulin heard the love. He was six. The branch went over his head and was gone.

Around them the families left through the academy's gate in an unceasing torrent. Most of them sighed. Every so often, one family came out blazing, and everybody understood and was jealous. Somewhere in the queue behind the Tangs, a mother was telling a crying boy to be fierce; somewhere else, a father was telling the same boy it didn't matter. The boy believed the crying.

"Tang Wulin." A teacher stepped out with a list in hand, and — Awakening Day was a workload — he turned and began walking without even looking back. "Follow me."

---

Inside the wall, the grounds were unexpectedly quiet. White roofs, red walls, everything simple, everything clean. The new term would start in days.

They stopped before a round building.

"Parents wait out here," the teacher said, without looking up from the list. "The children go in alone."

Every elementary academy had this round building. The Awakening Chamber. Before the day, the academy would have invited a Spirit Master from the Spirit Pagoda to conduct the ceremonies — powerful, mysterious existences, a group apart, even in an age of soul masters. Tang Ziran watched his son's small back disappear inside, and what passed through his eyes was old, and his own: decades ago he had carried the same hopes through the same door.

Decades ago. A round day just like this one, and a boy just like that one, except the boy had been him, average even then. He remembered the light running down the walls. He remembered waiting for the warmth to mean something. He remembered the moment he had understood, by the careful way the Spirit Master had folded the ledger closed, that the warmth had meant nothing at all. No soul power. The rest of life went on, as life does, and it had turned out well enough — a wife fierce about the kitchen she should not be allowed into, a son with the biggest eyes in Glorybound. Well enough. Better than enough.

"Parent waits here," the gatekeeper reminded him kindly, and pointed at the bench. He sat. His foot would not stay still.

Wulin followed the teacher up. The building had seven floors, and an Awakening Chamber on each. His was the third.

The chamber was round too. The floor, the walls, the ceiling — every surface covered in wonderful carved inscriptions, elegant, decorative. A middle-aged man in a simple orange robe stood waiting, and on the robe was embroidered the form of a soul beast. Wulin had never seen a Spirit Master before. His eyes were everywhere at once. He was six. Nobody's mind is serene at six, alone in a strange and exciting room.

"Hello," Wulin said, shyly.

The Spirit Master smiled gently. "Come here, child. Stand in the middle."

Wulin blinked, then walked over as cutely as a small boy could manage and stood in the middle. He could not help asking, "Lord Spirit Master — what will mine be, when it comes out."

"Ah, I don't know either," the Spirit Master said, still smiling. "After you've awakened, you'll see it yourself. Though — eyes as pretty as yours, a martial soul related to the eyes would suit you perfectly."

Wulin's face lit up. Master was in the mood for jokes, then. Nobody had told him yet about grass.

---

On the other side of the academy street, in the same packed morning, another father kept another hand on another shoulder.

His work was letters. He wrote them for people who could not, and read aloud to them the ones that came back, and the guild's bench paid him a wage that had never once been a surprise. His name was Lan He. His wife, Su Wan, was across the street haggling a parcel out of a vendor, because even on Awakening Day somebody's errands did not stop.

His son, Lan Shen, stood watching the gate's torrent and reading faces, because he had been born, apparently, doing it.

The man ahead of them in the queue turned round, as men in queues always eventually do — a brown-smiling father with worry he was keeping in his pockets, and a boy already gone through the doors, third-floor side. "Fisherman's weather inside those walls," said Tang Ziran, nodding at the queue's own pace. "You never know what comes up."

"May it come up smiling for your son," said Lan He.

"For yours equally," said Tang Ziran. "What's his soul going to be, do you think? Everyone's hoping for wolves."

"Mine hopes for furniture," said Lan He, "in private. Something that mends chairs." Both men laughed, the way men laugh when the joke is the safest thing on the street.

"Lan He," he said, offering the name and the place both. "We hide behind the millinery door, two houses back."

"Tang Ziran," said the other, taking it. "The plum tree over the yard wall." And he had half a sec more to say — then the doors did what ceremony doors do, and swallowed the next boy, and both fathers went back, companionably, to watching their own arithmetic.

"One in a thousand," Lan He said, to his son. "That is the law of the day. You know it."

"I know it."

"And if a boy is in the other nine hundred and ninety-nine, he learns letters, since letters do not care about ranks." He said it the way men say things they have been saying to themselves all morning. "Either way, son. Either way."

A family came out blazing and the queue turned to look. A family came out walking strange, and the queue looked away.

"Lan Shen," the teacher with the list called, apathetic in the same way, turned in the same way.

"Go on," said his father. "I will be exactly here."

---

Tang Wulin stood in the center of the inscriptions.

The Spirit Master made a gentle movement with his hand, and white light shone from it, vibrated, broke upward and blossomed — silent fireworks filling the chamber with splendor. From the ceiling first, the inscription lines filled with light. It moved down the walls, line after line, until all the light converged below Tang Wulin's feet.

Something comfortably numbing crept up his legs, entered at his heart, went everywhere. His body felt electrocuted, gently. Everything blurred before his eyes, and an unclear image flickered in his mind and would not hold still. He wanted a clear look at it. He could only feel its flowing shadows. In the end he saw it.

He could not remember what it was.

Then the pain came.

It came up from the hundreds of bones in his four limbs at once, heat bubbling forth, swelling until he felt burst-full. Bone, muscle, flesh — there wasn't a single part of him the pain spared. He couldn't breathe. It felt like being ripped to shreds from the inside, all of it at once, and he screamed.

"Ah——" — the cry tore out of him, high and raw, and went through the door and down the stairwell.

The Spirit Master jumped in fright. An ordinary child felt little during the ceremony. An extremely low number — extremely low — felt intense pain, because a weak body couldn't bear the energy. His body blurred across the short distance and appeared at the boy's side, ready to stop it all and protect him.

And stopped, astonished.

Golden lines were flickering on Tang Wulin's forehead. As he watched, the golden lines began to spread — down, out, to the four limbs, weaving themselves into a network of light lying under the boy's skin like a second set of veins.

What kind of martial soul was this. He had awakened thousands of children — tens of thousands. He had never seen this. Not once.

A faint energy undulated from the boy's body. The energy came from somewhere below his stomach — the usual seat of it, when a child had power in them at all.

Soul power. The Spirit Master allowed himself a small, tired pleasure: today, of all days, luck was pretty good. Most days he conducted ceremonies for days on end without encountering one child with soul power.

The golden lines were already gone. Without a trace. As if they had never existed.

A warm thread ran through the hollow of Wulin's palm. He opened his eyes.

In his palm lay a small grass, light blue, verdant and lush, undulating softly.

"Bluesilver Grass," the Spirit Master said, and his voice exuded disappointment before he could catch it. There was a lawn of it right there in the academy. It covered the Douluo Continent; it covered Star Luo and Sky Dou. It was the most ordinary martial soul there was — the worst of the trash. And somehow it had soul power.

"Bluesilver Grass." Wulin looked at the little thing, and his chest hurt worse than the pain had. "This is my martial soul."

"En." The Spirit Master nodded at the little thing. "Yours, child. That is what woke in you."

The Spirit Master smiled, and his eyes couldn't help revealing a trace of pity.

"Child. Do not look like that." He brought out the crystal and tested as he talked, the ceremony's second half, routine by now. "You have soul power in you. Measured — third rank, innate. Not a grand number, but a real one, and it is yours. Spend great effort over the years to come, and a Soul Master is still a thing you can be."

Wulin stood very still, holding his grass. Trash, the nice man's eyes had said, in that instant before the smile. Rank three, the nice man's mouth had said. Both were the truth. He was six, and he filed the truth in the place where six-year-olds keep such things, which is next to the heart.

"Tell me, child — do you know the story of the Tang Sect."

Wulin shook his head slowly, eyes down.

"Twenty thousand years ago, the founding patriarch of that sect — the greatest sect there ever was — carried this same Bluesilver Grass as his martial soul. The same trash grass. He took what the world called trash into battle against the wicked Martial Soul Hall, and beat the Heavens and the Hall alike." The Spirit Master gave the tale its full weight; it was the one part of the ceremony he never shortened. "The Tang Sect and the Spirit Pagoda both — the legends the whole continent loves best were built by men with souls no different from this one. Effort, child."

The huge eyes came up off the grass.

"So you see," the Spirit Master said, gently. "The legends loved by the whole continent — the Tang Sect, the Spirit Pagoda — those masters were the most admired men alive in their day. Great effort, child."

He sent the boy down. The boy went down holding his palm half-closed, around the undulating little grass that the whole continent grew in its lawns, that a patriarch had once carried into legend.

Downstairs, his father was waiting where the round building let children out, and crouched, and put both arms around him for a moment too long.

"Dad," Wulin said into his shoulder. "Rank three. Bluesilver Grass."

His father held him tighter, and said nothing at all, and then said, "Good. Good, son."

---

The corridor Lan Shen was sent down was quiet after the street.

It ended in a small room with a bench. On the bench: three other six-year-olds. A girl with two tight braids swung her legs and whispered, I want a hawk, I want a hawk. A broad boy with cropped hair sat still as laundry. A thin girl held her own hands in her lap and looked at them as if they belonged to someone else.

Beyond the wall: the door, opening and closing. Opened and closed.

"How long have you been waiting," Lan Shen asked the braided girl.

"Two turns of the sand," she said. "I am next. I want a hawk. My uncle has a hawk and he is a Soul Master of the Advanced Division. What do you want."

"I have not decided."

"That is not how it works," she said, with the scorn of six. "You do not decide. It decides."

They took her. She came out nine minutes later walking strange. A rabbit, the murmur ran — and rank zero with it, carried on down the corridor to parents who had already lost the light in their faces.

"Rabbit is a sitting-in-the-sun soul," the broad boy said flatly. "My pa says grass and rabbits raise gardeners and cooks. I am getting an axe."

He got a spade. Rank two, the gowned master announced, and the boy's father, somewhere in the hall, whooped loud enough to come through two walls. A spade, rank two. A gardener, perhaps. A paid gardener.

The thin girl never went in at all. Her mother collected her off the bench between one wall-sound and the next — the fee bought back that morning, a family's mind changed in a queue. "Mama says girls of our house need not," she whispered, lost. "Mama says better to learn the loom."

Then the room was empty, and the corridor was very long and very short at once."

His ceremony took no time at all. White light, silent fireworks, the lines of light gathering under his feet. Warmth from the heart outward, a fizz in the teeth. No pain. Something in him stirred, answered, offered up what it had.

In his palm: a blade of grass. Bluish, thin, soft, ordinary, trembling slightly in still air.

"Bluesilver Grass," said this floor's Spirit Master, in the voice of a man reading weather. He had a kind, bored face. "Hand on the crystal. Hm. Soul power present. Rank one."

"Rank one," Lan Shen said.

"One in a thousand, young man. You are in the lucky column." The man was already writing. "Barely — but in it." He looked up, and there was a small kindness in it. "It cultivates. It cultivates slowly. Do not let anyone tell you it is nothing. Do not let anyone tell you it is something. It is grass."

"Master. Will it always be only grass."

"It will always be grass. *Only* is a word you add yourself. Grass grows." The pen scratched. "Go to school, child. Off you go."

His father was exactly where he had said he would be, and when the answer was given — grass, rank one — he nodded slowly, the way he treated any letter: read twice, folded away. He put his hand on top of his son's head, warm and heavy.

"Grass grows in cracks," he said.

News moved up and down the street all through that morning. Somebody's uncle had heard that a boy's ceremony had hurt him: a cry, right through the doors, on the third floor. Somebody else had heard the master came down fast, beaming. Somebody else had heard rank three — innate. At that, the whole queue made the noise a queue makes. Grass, they said. The Tang boy from two doors down. Grass, and still luck touched him.

"Pretty family," a woman said. "Quiet. Well. Not quiet today."

Tang Ziran passed them soon after with his son riding high in his arms, the huge eyes bright and full of everything. He saw Lan He and nodded, the street-neighbor nod — pump-nods, weather at the wall. Lan He nodded back. They were neighbors, so he said it: "Congratulations, Ziran." "Thank you, neighbor." Then the crowd took them apart from each other.

At the gatehouse board a clerk had posted the term notice: every child with any result at all — grasses, spades, hawks, rabbits — enrolled by week's end in the academy's primary. Su Wan read it twice and memorized the date in the way mothers memorize things that cost money.

"First bell nine days from today," she said. "Shoes will not last nine days."

"Shoes never do," Lan He said.

They walked home through the market, where his mother bargained two coins off greens and put one of them back in the vendor's hand anyway, because she liked that vendor. They passed the Tang house — a tidy house with a plum tree leaning over its yard wall. Empty yet. The festival of that house was still going on at the academy street, presumably. Or the ice-shaving stall, if its small master's rank three had earned a double scoop.

"Same grass," Su Wan said, sliding up beside her family with her parcel finally won.

"Same grass," Lan Shen said. "Different number."

"Numbers are not everything," she said, in the voice mothers use for things they hope.

Her husband and her son looked at her, and didn't argue, and didn't agree. They were related, the two of them; it showed.

---

They were at supper when the knock came.

Old Gao from the dock lane, cap in hands. The letter he had been carrying around had at last turned out to be from his brother in the west. He wanted it read. He wanted the answer written before the week turned, if the fee was the usual fee.

Lan He read the letter aloud at the table, plainly and gently. Letters in that house were always read to the room, with no doctoring of a worn-out brother's news. Two coins for the reading and one for the writing was the usual fee. Old Gao put down four, and would not take one back.

"It was the draw today, was it not," he said. "My daughter hears from a cousin who heard in the street." He looked at Lan Shen with the soft respect that his town paid to soul power, any soul power, even a blade of it in a six-year-old's hand. "The young master."

"He is grass, Gao," Su Wan said, "and he is in bed after his rice, luck or no luck."

"Grass is a living thing," Old Gao said seriously — more poetry than the dock lane usually produced — and put his cap on, and was paid and gone.

"They pay extra for you today because of what a crystal said," his mother observed, shutting the door.

"Luck is a flavor in this town," his father said. "It lasts a week."

Night. The lamp lit at the table, because there was figuring to do.

Su Wan dried a bowl and didn't ask. The dry bowl slowed. "What is it."

"What the street said today. Not to us — around us, all day." Lan He set the pen down before it could write anything. "A child with soul power walks a road, and the road has a gate on it. The masters talk about it like weather. At rank ten, a spirit soul. Bought at the Pagoda. And you do not pick one, Wan, you *draw* for it. They say it costs what a house costs."

"He is rank one."

"I know he is rank one."

"Maybe rank one never gets to rank ten. Grass, slowly, you heard them."

"Maybe," Lan He said. "But if it does, we do not hide the size of the mountain from the boy who has to climb it. That is the rule of this house. We count winters, not wishes."

From the dark of the doorway, Lan Shen said: "How many winters."

"Bed," his mother said, not cross.

"Father."

"The slow ones take years," his father said, turning. "Three, four, the world willing. Which means a house has three or four winters to become a mountain instead of a house." He came over and put his hand on the boy's head again, the day's second weight of it. "You heard that today twice, so you keep it. Winters, not wishes. Bed."

He lay in bed with his palm open on his chest and called the grass quietly, to see if it would come. It came. Thin, soft, ordinary, trembling in the still air.

The remembering came back on its own, unasked. In a life before this one he had been a man who wrote, and he had read more books than this town had houses. In one of them, a boy with grass in his hand walked out into a story much bigger than a border town. And the grass, in the end, had turned out to be the least important thing about him.

He didn't know how much of that he was allowed to trust. He knew it didn't feel like a story trust. It felt like being told a debt existed, in a currency not yet named.

Two doors down, in another house, another boy was awake too — he would be, Lan Shen thought. Rank three didn't sleep either, the first night of it. That boy would be holding his hand out in his own dark, watching his own grass tremble, feeling a warm thread where the thread had run.

He thought, too, about the boy with the rabbit who had walked out of the holding room walking strange. The gaze of a whole town slides off misfortune and fastens on luck. If the crystal had stayed dark for him this morning, his father would still have lit this lamp tonight, and his mother's eggs would still have meant what they meant. That was also a kind of luck. No machine measured it.

Same grass. Different winters.

He closed his hand. The grass went away.

An hour later his mother looked in at him and found his hand open again in sleep, palm up, as if the grass had gone to sleep too. She closed his fingers gently around nothing and set his hand under the quilt.

"Grass, then," she said softly, to the dark of the doorway where his father was pretending not to have come to look as well. "Grass and rank one."

"Grass and rank one," his father agreed. "Grass and rank one — and winters, to count."

It was the end of Awakening Day, and it was the first page of everything.

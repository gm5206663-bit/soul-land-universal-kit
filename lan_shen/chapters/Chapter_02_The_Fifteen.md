The quiet held until the lane bent home, among kitchen sounds and bucket water. Then Tang Ziran looked down at the boy beside him — head low, sandy hair still slept-wrong — and he stopped walking.

"Son," he said, and waited for the small face to turn up. "I have done the sums, in my head, halfway down the lane. One in a thousand people wake with soul power. You have soul power on a weed that everyone calls a trash soul." He put his hand on the bony shoulder. "So you are not one in a thousand. You are one in a million. That is rarer than rare, and the sums don't lie, I work with sums all day long."

Wulin blinked up at him. "Is Bluesilver Grass really a trash martial soul, Dad."

"Who said that."

"The other boys said that."

"The other boys," Tang Ziran said, "have not heard about the founder of the Tang Sect, who had exactly your grass and shook heaven with it." He walked on; the boy trotted two steps to catch his hand. "Trash is what people say when they have never really seen the thing. Hold your head up, Lin Lin. You are the only one of you there is."

It helped. It did not reach the strange heat in his chest. It helped enough to carry him through the door of home.

---

Their home was a living room about a dozen square metres, a small kitchen, a washroom, and two little rooms. His mother was at the stove when they came in, and the whole house smelled of lunch. Lang Yue was not what people called beautiful; she was what people called warm. Today she had made what counted, in their house, as a festival table: sauteed greens, stewed ribs, salad, the three-vegetable soup. "Son. You must be starving." She crouched and hugged him hard. "Sit, sit, before it goes cold."

Tang Wulin sat. He picked up the spoon. His arms weighed more than the soup bowl; the academy's daze had not gone away, only waited for somewhere quiet to close over his head.

"Lin Lin. Lin Lin, eat a little more, did you even look at the ribs."

"Leave him, love," Tang Ziran said softly. "He is wrung out like a washcloth."

"Wrung or not, a boy needs food to grow the soul he has been so busy growing."

"I know," his father said. "Tomorrow. There is food tomorrow too."

He ate three bites and fell asleep at the table.

Tang Ziran carried him into the little room and laid him down, and stood looking at the flushed small face. In the doorway behind him, Lang Yue said what they had both been turning over all morning.

"Tang-father. I keep thinking."

"There is nothing to think," he said, low. "It is done. He has it."

"That is the thing I keep thinking." Her voice was low too, and it did not reach the boy they were talking about. "If the crystal had stayed dark, he would just be our boy. Now he goes into that class, with grass in his hand, and every child in there will have something better." She worried the cloth of her skirt. "Perhaps we should move him to the ordinary class later, if it turns sour."

"Wulin has a grass soul," she went on, in the same low voice. "Rank three. I have been turning it over all day. Grass is the worst of them, husband."

"Grass is what the founder of the Tang Sect had," he said, patiently.

"That is a story," she said. "I am talking about my table. Our boy, with grass, in a class of children with hammers and knives."

"Weapons in spirit only," he said. "No actual knives."

"Knives of the spirit are knives." But she let it go, shoulders easing. "All right. All right. We watch him."

"We will watch him," Tang Ziran said, as if that had been agreed all along. His hands stayed with the doorframe a while.

"He said nothing at all on the walk," Lang Yue murmured, by the door. "That is what sits with me. Six years old, and he walks home silent."

"In shock, love," Tang Ziran said. "The daze will wear off. Better the first setbacks land with us in the next room."

"He has always been obedient," she said, and nodded, and that was not an answer, so much as a mother completing the inventory of things to be glad about.

They closed the door quietly. Neither of them knew that their son was not sleeping from worry. He was sleeping from exhaustion, and its source was beyond the house's naming.

---

At the well next morning, two mothers found their buckets side by side.

"Both in, then," Lang Yue said. "The Fifteen, as they call it this year."

"Both in." Shen's mother lowered the rope. "Yours rank three, I heard. Ours one. One is also a rank, the boy says, and I have decided my face will agree with him, in public."

"Lan, is it, yours. Mine too — the boy's father is a Lan on his mother's side, far enough back that it only counts when the town counts it."

"Then we are relations," said the other mother easily, "for the well's purposes. Bring your rank-three over for bread sometime, and we will count it properly."

At the academy gates, twenty minutes later, the two of them stood in a small chorus of parents saying the same thing six ways.

"Straight home after bell, yes."

"Straight home. No stopping by the butter shop."

"Whose shop is the butter shop," a father demanded, and nobody owned up to having a son who knew of a butter shop.

In the little room, under the thin blanket, the fever came.

Tang Wulin's skin flushed crimson. His heartbeat quickened to three times a sleeping boy's; his meridians showed faint through the heat. On his forehead the golden lines returned — the chamber's lines. They ran from his brow to his toes and turned and came back, three slow circuits of his length, and sank away into his depths as quiet as water into sand. His bones creaked, once. Then the heat fell, and his sleep deepened into a dream.

He dreamed of a prairie covered, end to end, in Bluesilver Grass, swaying in no wind, reaching toward something he could not raise his eyes to see.

He woke at low sun, light as if the heat had burned something out and left more room. Being a boy raised sensible, he bothered no one about it. He stripped and scrubbed in the washroom, and the smell went down the drain with the grey water as if it had never been hooked into him at all.

"Ah, where is Lin Lin." His mother's voice, alarmed — and then she was there, clutching a towel at him. "Get clothes on before you freeze. Honestly."

She pinched his little pink face, mother-fashion, and pushed him toward his room. Behind the door she muttered to the air: SKIP morning. What has he been eating." Then she went to make dinner.

At dinner, the pinch of his face lost its right to complain. Tang Wulin ate like a small storm. The ribs disappeared. The greens disappeared. His parents sat with their bowls half-raised and watched everything on the table funnel into a six-year-old. At last Lang Yue laid her chopsticks across his bowl, laughing and half-alarmed at once, and forbade him to eat any more. "Enough, enough." "A boy is not a storm drain. Sleep it off."

"But Mom, I am still—"

"Asleep," she said. "You are still asleep, is what you are going to be."

The house was very quiet.

Tang Ziran found his voice first. "Son, are you the legendary devourer, the one from the stories, eating because the world has wronged you."

"Dad," Wulin said, muffled, still chewing the last rice, "why would the world have wronged me."

"No reason anyone can see." Tang Ziran traded a look with his wife, and turned the talk, gently, like a man opening a difficult clasp. "Here is a question, Lin Lin. Tomorrow the class begins. Do you go to the Soul Master class, or do I ask them to put you in the ordinary one. It is your life stepping off from here, so I am asking."

"The Soul Master class." Straight out, no thinking. "I want to be a Soul Master. When I am a Soul Master I will earn a lot of money and buy you and Mom good food. Every day, food like today."

Lang Yue's eyes were red at the edges. Tang Ziran looked at the table a moment, then reached over and set his hand on his son's head.

"Good. Then it is decided, and we are behind you. Only listen. This grass-road walks hard — even in the old stories the grass was ordinary. If it gets too hard, say so, and I will move you. No shame will live at this table. We never wanted a dragon or a phoenix here. We only want you happy. Happy is the whole wish."

"I am already happy, Dad."

"Happy and loud about it," Lang Yue put in, wiping the table. "It is a new record. Yesterday you said it once. Tonight, twice."

"He is practicing," Tang Ziran said gravely, "for being happy in class."

"Then sleep." Tang Ziran cleared the table, and the boy was drunk on food and thirsty besides.

"Good night, Lin Lin," his mother said into the dark of the doorway.

"Good night, Mom. I will make you proud, you know. With the grass."

"You already do," she said. "Sleep, little man."

He was asleep before she finished hanging his school shirt.

---

Two doors down, the lamplight in the Shen house was doing arithmetic again.

Lan Shen sat facing the paper his father had pinned flat with two stones, how he pinned anything that mattered. The paper was the list from the spirit station's window: the class opens at first bell, and under it what a new boy must bring. A starter notebook. Two pencils, capped. A slate for sums. The materials fee for the practice charms, which nobody got for free.

"The class itself," his father said, tapping the first line with one mechanic's knuckle, "the class itself costs nothing. The Federation pays the teachers. The Federation built the hall. A child walks in and learns, three years here, six in the intermediate school, free. That is the law, and a good one." He paused. "The list," he said, and turned the paper a little, "is eleven coins and change, and the fee is four more. Fifteen total, if the slate can be bought used."

Fifteen, thought Lan Shen. Not a hundred thousand, not a thousand — four market weeks in the jar with the lid that sang. His hand did not need to do the sum; some part of him, trained by a life he no longer lived, ran the column anyway. Last night it had come out at a debt shaped like a house. Tonight, at a jar lid singing. A better column.

"Used," his father said to the jar, agreeing with himself. "Used is fine."

His mother set two small bowls down and caught his eye: eat up, sleep after, no worrying — there is enough. He ate up. He did not worry. He knew what worry costs a body; nobody in that room had learned it yet.

"School," his mother said into the quiet, and the word came out carried, as mothers carry words that have been kept waiting. "My boy goes to school."

"First bell," said his father, and would not say more, because a man does not spend luck out loud.

"Stand straight at them, now," his mother said later, brushing his hair. "Say please and thank you to the teacher. Give me your word on both."

"Given," said Lan Shen, both ways.

"And if they ask your martial soul —"

"Grass," said Lan Shen, evenly. "Same as two doors down. I checked the view already."

She stopped one stroke, went on. Enough.

---

---

Morning came, and with it the walk — and, waiting under the academy arch, the teacher at the doors with a board and a voice that carried:

"Listen once and remember, new boys and girls. Your schooling is given. The Sun Moon Federation pays for the halls, the teachers, the yard, the books kept here. Three years in this building, six in the intermediate school after — all free. The law, not our kindness. Walk in every morning, walk out every afternoon, and nine years from now the doors to what comes next will stand open for you: achievement, for some of you; the more expensive kind of door, for some; or a science, which spends no one and is a respectable kind of door. That is the whole shape of it. You owe us nothing but your sitting and your trying."

Around him the children nodded, the way children nod at weather. Nine years. Free. Only from the commoner's district did it have the exact weight of money.

Tang Ziran had the morning half off and walked his boy to the gates, the boy new in a hand-washed shirt, the breakfast packet gone in him.

"Eat your roll slowly," Lang Yue had said, at the door that morning, folding the school shirt's collar with something like ceremony. "Sit straight. Say good morning to the teacher properly. And Lin Lin — the grass is yours, so it is a good grass."

"It is a good grass," he had agreed, in his new shirt, radiant.

At the gates the father crouched — the second crouch-hug of this story; this serial means to count them.

"Straight home after the bell," he said. "Not with other parties, not to anyone's shop. Home. Tell the teacher your mother and I both said it."

"Why home, Dad."

"Because a first day keeps best in its own wrapping," Tang Ziran said — which was what his own father had said to him, and it had always sounded right.

"Told and told, Dad."

"Told and told, squared." He squeezed the small nose, stood, and went to work, the pail swinging.

Behind the line, the ordinary students filed in with no welcomer at all. "Are you in the Soul Master class too, do you have soul power, what is it, what rank, my father says rank five is already remarkable —"

"Sssh, everyone shouts it, one at a time."

"Look, look at the paper on the door. It says fifteen. Is that all of us."

Inside the corridor's weapons-and-books smell the class gathered: fifteen this year, the town's whole Awakening Day haul. 

Tang Wulin let the flow move past him, at the side of the room. Next to him, round as a dumpling, a chubby boy looked him over.

"Hey," the chubby boy said. "What is your martial soul."

"Bluesilver Grass." Wulin's chin came up as he said it, set there by his father's hand. "The same one the founder of the Tang Sect had." And he turned his hand over, and a soft blue light flickered, and a single grass stem danced in his palm.

He did not notice what was on the grass. Nobody did. A meticulous eye, held arm-length close, might have found near the roots a faint wash of light gold where yesterday stood green. Indistinct. Almost not there. The room passed over it without stopping, and Wulin did not look twice.

The chubby boy made a sound with his lips. "Ah. Grass. The weed one." He lost interest. "Trash."

Wulin was red-hot at the ears. "Then yours. What is yours."

"Cold light snapped — a small knife stood in the chubby boy's hand. "Knife," he said, nursing it like a cat with a mouse. "Rank five innate. When I am a Title Douluo I shall be called the Knife God Douluo. I could chop your grass to ribbons casual as cutting carrots."

Wulin looked from the bright little blade to his grass. His father's words from the night before swam up. He kept his fists at his sides. Around them the fourteen called out their souls, one over the next — an iron hammer, a sparrow, a wolf pup, a cauldron, a garden snake. To the measure of children, every one sounded better than grass. The groups formed; none bent toward Wulin.

"Wolf Pack," somebody declared, at one desk island. "Beasts sit with beasts."

"Tools over here," said the hammer boy, "we are the sensible half."

"What are you," said the knife boy to the cauldron girl, suspicious at once.

"Tool. Cookery line. Obviously."

"Fine, you may sit. — Not you. Grass. We are full."

He said it twice, the knife boy — once to Wulin, once to the small boy with the ink-dark hair who had drifted in after the count. Two grass souls. That was the whole of the sorting, and the class laughed the correct amount, which was little.

"Rank one," said Lan Shen, by way of an estate agent's apology for the house. "They say the continent is paved with it. The furniture has arrived."

"Rank three," said Wulin, half-smiling out of pure surprise, because nobody had apologized for the weed before in quite that voice. And that was the island, easy as that: two boys, two blades of grass, one deserted desk, and the class very much engaged elsewhere.

"Lan Shen," said Lan Shen, proffering his slate with all the ceremony of a man twice his height. "Millinery-door lane. We repair things."

"Tang Wulin," said Wulin. "The plum-tree house."

At the break, the knife boy drifted past their island once, idly, and saw nothing paying.

"Knife God Douluo," he said quietly, "spares the blades of grass. Think of yourself as exempted by boredom."

"Think of yourself as next to me in algebra," said Gou Dong from the wall, unexpectedly louder, and the whole corner of the room snorted, and the knife boy kept drifting.

*I will be a Soul Master anyway,* he thought, and held his fists steady.

---

Lan Shen had come in early; the habit had been paid for in some other life. Wall side, used slate. When the class filled to the noise of fifteen children he did not join it. He watched. Watching was free. That made it the best of his assets.

He watched the chubby boy produce the knife as a house cat produces a bird. He watched the sandy-haired neighbour call up a single stem of grass, chin in the air. He watched the room slide off that boy like rain off tiles. Two doors down. *Same grass. Different winters* — last night's sentence in his own head, prepared, as it turned out, for someone else.

Then his eye snagged. For one breath he looked twice.

On the neighbour's dancing grass, faint near the root, there was a wash of light gold where no gold had any business being. Almost nothing there. Everything-in-the-room-missing-it nothing.

Lan Shen's stomach filed it away before his head could decide to. *Noted,* he thought, in the colourless tone he used with ledgers: *grass-root anomaly, neighbour, day one, unobvious.* A second thought followed. *If no one sees it, and the boy does not see it himself — is what I am seeing mine to hold.* He kept his face blank and said nothing, to anyone, all morning.

 He wrote his name on the board in plain strokes: Lin Ximeng.

""

"Good morning, all of you. I am Lin Ximeng, your homeroom teacher for the three years you are here. We will begin the only sensible way — around the room. Name, martial soul, innate rank."

It went fast; fifteen only. Wolf pup, iron hammer, garden snake, cauldron, sparrow — each got its oh-s; the ranks landed like small coins: four, five, five, three, four.

"Gou Dong," said a boy with ears that stuck out. "Garden Snake. Rank four, and my uncle says garden snakes are underrated, actually."

"Sparrow," said a girl, as if daring anyone to laugh. "Rank three."

"Rank three is very respectable," said Lin Ximeng mildly.

"Lan Shen," he said when his turn came, standing as his father had shown him. "Bluesilver Grass. Rank one."

"Rank one... also a rank," somebody whispered, half-helpful.

A short quiet; inattention. He had spent a life learning to like it. The class moved on.

"Tang Wulin," said the next name along the room, the small voice lifted as his father had set it. "Bluesilver Grass. Rank three."

The laughter was immediate and unkind and very much the laughter of six-year-olds who have already established a pecking order not one hour into school. Two Bluesilver Grasses in one room, and the second one tries to be proud of it — that was the laugh-line: the second one could be laughed at without touching the first. Lin Ximeng startled visibly — grass, twice in one class — then recovered a teacher's face and let the laughter burn itself short.

Tang Wulin sat red, a small fireball behind his ribs. *Were these the challenges you spoke of, Dad.* By the wall, Lan Shen stayed still and kept his eyes on the practice board. He did not trust what he might do with them.

"Good," said Lin Ximeng, when the noise came down. "Then we begin where it all begins. What kinds of martial souls are there — who can answer."

"Beast souls," said the knife boy, who liked answering.

"Tool souls," said Gou Dong, not to be outdone.

"Both, young men, and that is the easy half," said Lin Ximeng. "Beast souls arrive on the body itself. Tool souls stand apart. And a third kind of answer that nobody ever gives me on the first day — who can rank his soul power?"

"Rank five," said the knife boy, instantly, with the air of a man stating a public asset.

"Rank one," said Lan Shen, under his breath, to his slate, alone, as if trying the word's weight out of the flow of it.

The lesson was the first post in a doorframe; a life walks through the rest. Tool souls and beast souls, the teacher said: beast souls arrive on the body itself, tool souls stand apart from it. Cultivate your soul power and the soul itself grows better with you. Rank ten is the door; below rank ten you are a student of soul power, and past rank ten the title Soul Master becomes a true thing about a person.

"Rank ten is the door," Lin Ximeng went on, and tapped the board on the number where it had been chalked. "Below rank ten, a student of soul power. Past rank ten, the title exists. How far is that, from what you have now."

"Far," said the class, in something like one voice.

"Far," Lin Ximeng agreed, "and also one rank at a time, as all far is. Do not let anyone sell you otherwise."

"Teacher," said the girl with the sparrow, hand high. "Does the grass ever win."

"Once, entirely," said Lin Ximeng, without blinking. "The founder of the Tang Sect. The story is older than this town. Ask me again in three years, when you all know what ranks mean — I will tell you what it cost him."

"And there are two great roads of Soul Masters," Lin Ximeng said, and the room hushed itself for it. "Those who cultivate to fight — Battle Soul Masters. And those who cultivate to serve — Utility Soul Masters. Food, records, healing, guiding, growing things: the federation's unseen hands. Which road fits who, we shall learn slowly, over years."

Tang Wulin sat forward. Every word was ground falling open under him; he had been starved for exactly this since the awakening light. A door shut all his life opened two fingers' width on a larger, more magnificent world.

At lunch-break, a boy with ears that stuck out stepped backwards into Lan Shen's path and said, without preamble:

"Rank one, was it. You are not worried."

"My mother says even a rank is lucky," said Lan Shen, deadpan. "One is even lucky four times."

The boy squinted, decided this was a joke, and laughed too late and too loud. "Gou Dong. Garden Snake, rank four, tools-and-beasts sits together if they are quietly." He had already decided they were friends; it cost Lan Shen nothing to be decided at, so he nodded, and walked with him, listening to nine things about garden snakes he had not asked about.

Lan Shen sat forward too, for a different reason.

*Utility Soul Masters.* He set the words against the column that never left his book-head long. A battle road wants a blade, and a blade wants a price. The federation pays its record-keepers, its food-men, its quiet school-men, without asking which of them could break a door. Money on that road as rain has money on it, for a man with a bucket. Not a fortune. Not fast. Not a debt shaped like a house. Above all, not a war.

In the afternoon, Lin Ximeng taught them to sit right — straight-boned, hands at the middle, breath long — and to feel inward along the thread each had woken with. "Meditation is the only way soul power grows," he said. "There is no other road. Sit with it until it warms."

"How long does it take to warm," someone asked, anxiously.

"It complains," Lin Ximeng said, "when you watch the pot." And because the class stared: "You will sit with it, each afternoon, for the three years of this school. Watching is not sitting. Closing one's eyes and drifting off to dreams of rank ten tomorrow is not sitting. Sit. Feel. That is all; and it takes all you have."

"Bell in a moment," said Lin Ximeng, pacing between the small seated rows. "Home straight home after. Slate packed. — Wulin, boy, breathe in longer; the grass is not late, you are early."

"Yes, Teacher."

Fourteen children shifted and fidgeted and scratched. Lan Shen sat still.

Stillness was also something he had paid for, in an earlier life. His rank-one thread was a whisp next to rank five's pulse; nine ranks from the door while the knife boy neared it. Known last night. Still known. But he could sit. He sat past the half-bell, until the teacher, chalk-halted mid-word, looked twice at him. He startled back into his body, blushed like a caught fox, and found his slate with great care.

"Bell," said Lin Ximeng at last, clapping them awake. "Same time tomorrow. Straight home — the rule holds for the whole year. Slate. Slate, young man."

"Teacher, will we fight each other someday," said the knife boy, hopefully.

"You will find out on the day you intend to lose. — Not today. Out, out."

At last bell, the teacher saw them out the way he had seen them in.

"Same time tomorrow, new boys and girls."

"Yes, Teacher," they chorused, ragtag and loud.

"And — class of the grass years," Lin Ximeng added, dry as chalk. "The story says the grass endures. The story, if you have not heard it, does not lie."

Lan Shen, passing under the lintel, looked back once, and smiled. "Good start," Lin Ximeng said mildly after him.

Outside, Gou Dong fell into step beside him.

"Same direction," he announced. "I know. I have been watching which way you go."

"Two-door," said Lan Shen, "four past yours, if the butter shop is any direction."

"There IS a butter shop," said Gou Dong, with sudden intensity — and that was the beginning of several years.

---

Home was home at first bell's end, as fathers on the lane had both said. School-noise still in his ears, past the market, past the spirit station. Grass this morning, grass tonight.

At his own table his mother asked how it was, and his father, home by lamplight, looked up from oiling a customer's something-or-other and said: "Well."

"Well," Lan Shen said. "The class is fifteen. There is a boy with a knife who is going to call himself the Knife God Douluo." His mother smiled; she had been six once too. "And we learned the names of the roads. There is one of them where the federation pays a person without asking him to fight."

"I like that road," his mother said instantly.

"So does your ma," said his father, from the oilcloth.

"There is also one where the federation pays for everything else but you break the doors," said Lan Shen, thoughtfully. "Or get broken against them."

"That road," his mother said, "can be its own reward, for boys who deserve it less than mine."

"Eat," his mother said, setting his evening bowl down. "A schoolboy eats his school supper. There is a new rule, as of today."

"A new rule," Lan Shen repeated, and picked up his spoon obediently.

"Yes. The rule is: tell us everything." She sat down opposite him, elbows on the table. "Nine years is too long to hold in your head alone. Every evening, you empty the day onto this table, and we help you carry it back."

"Everything," he said, and thought briefly of gold near a grass-root that was not his to report, and then of the class, the knife, the snake, the teacher, the free years — "is a lot."

"Begin with the knife boy," she said, smiling.

His father set the oil-cloth down. "There is."

"There is," Lan Shen agreed, and they smiled small father-son smiles, and left the jar with the singing lid exactly where it was.

Two lamps lit as the early dark came down — two Lan names over two low doors, two schoolboys asleep before the lamps went out. One had eaten like a storm.

It was the end of the first day of nine years of school. The year remained unspent.

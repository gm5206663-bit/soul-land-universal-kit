At the corner where their ways split, Tang Ziran gave the third morning- crouch of the week.

"You look tired in it," he said.

"The collar's fine, Dad."

"I meant the boy. Those tables they feed you at — your mother wants a full accounting. Seven bowls, she counted, from your report. In writing."

"In *writing*, Dad." Wulin's ears went a little red, at the memory of the chant. "It wasn't seven."

"Then give it me honest."

"...Eight." He stood straighter, as if admitting to a rank. "Dad — the soul power burns it. The teacher's man said so. Burns fuel."

"Then burn it with all your effort," said Tang Ziran, patting him clean for the last time. "Today the teacher learns what walks in when a Tang Wulin puts his mind to a sitting."

The day had been too long, and too brave, to come out of clean.

Tang Wulin walked out of the gates, shoulders doing a hard sum: be upset, or be upset and smile about it. The soul power in his chest was real. That was the good news. Everything else about the day had been arranged to show him how far from enough it was.

The other students had made their opinion of his martial soul clear. What he had not known going in was the arithmetic of attention. In the meditation hour the teacher had gone from child to child; the visits to his desk ran shortest. Not unkind, never unkind. Teacher Lin Ximeng had crouched by him with the same mild eyes as anywhere. He had said the same true things about breath and the thread. And he had stayed the length of a wingbeat before moving on. The worst soul in the room needed the least explaining.

*Is my soul really that bad.*

The grass had three words' dignity. He lifted his chin and gave it a fourth: *No.*

Dad had said it — and Dad's sayings held weight. They held him at the repair bench all day; they could hold a son. Success was almost all effort, Dad said; talent the candle beside it. If his soul asked more of him than of a knife boy — well. More effort cost no money, and his house had never once run out of it.

By the time the gates' next street passed under him, the shoulders were doing the smiling version. His father's son recovered that way, from the ground up.

---

The soul-master lunch was told of by the ordinary students the way flat country tells of mountains.

It was true. The academy fed the class without limit, and fed it well.

"Take the fish as well, hero," the same serving woman had called after him through the hatch, loud enough for the whole serving line to join in the awe. "It is the soul-master cart today, they fried it proper — apprentices get the fish until it walks out the door. Next tray's on the federation." The official reason was fuel. The students at the hall's tall windows observed a plainer one: a federation paying for nine free years likes its future Soul Masters fed.

He had gone back for a fourth helping of rice when the laughter started.

"Back again, hero." The serving woman slid the ladle, used to wonders. "Yours is the second tray this week. Eat hearty."

"Smith's right, ma'am," said Wulin, earnestly.

"Fill him, he's hollow," called the next boy in line, to a clatter of spoons.

By the seventh, the table of fifteen had a new word for him.

"Rice Bucket," the knife boy declared, with the satisfaction of a boy naming a street. "Rank three soul, rank six bowls."

"That eats more than my whole house," said someone, awed.

"He is saving it in his legs," said the cauldron girl, "Cooks know. Look at him. That is not fat, that is *stores*."

Wulin had chewed, swallowed, and gone back for an eighth. The food was good and free; the laughter travelled with him each trip, fond by then — the way fools in a hall laugh at a comet for coming back on schedule.

"Six," chanted someone. "Wait for it." "SEVEN." "Heroic," said the cauldron girl, solemnly, hand over her heart.

"Do you feel the grass growing," asked a voice at the wheat end of the hall.

"With fuel," said Tang Wulin, unbothered, past a mouthful, and it was so unexpected from the grass boy that the laugh went twice round the tables.

He had not minded, much. It was hard to mind the sound of people counting when you were the number.

"Dad," he had thought, with hazy family pride, "would be so angry about the waste he is going to assume is happening at home."

---

The day had started with Gou Dong at the desk beside his, whispering urgent news of the district a moment before the Bell.

"Two-door." An elbow. "My ma packed me an egg. An EGG, boy. For attentiveness at the two-day school."

"Attentiveness," said Lan Shen, neutrally.

"The garden snake pays attention to the small things," said Gou Dong, with dignity, and produced half the egg under the slate.

"You are mad from not sleeping," said Lan Shen.

"Eggs are worth not sleeping for," said Gou Dong, with the injured pride of the chronic egg-holder. "Also, my uncle says your road goes past the temple corner same as his. So you can walk with me home this afternoon, if you are walking."

"I walk with you to the temple corner," said Lan Shen, judiciously, as if a contract had been read aloud in court.

Two tables over, at the seat he had made his own by being quiet, Lan Shen counted too. Not bowls — money.

*The federation feeds the fifteen without limit.* He set the line against the column in his book-head, working through a single modest bowl. *This is value.* A growing child eating at somebody else's cookfire, every school day for nine years, without a receipt. He did not know the price of a fed boy in this city; his mother did, down to the coin; and that much came off the house's books until the year he turned sixteen.

He ate slowly. Around the corner of his eye sat a wonder of engineering: the boy two tables over, back with the eighth bowl. *Rice Bucket. Rank three. Grass. Eats like a forge.* He filed it with the other morning's note and did not try to solve it. Contradictions that size were not his business, yet.

"One more bowl, Bucket."

"Hungry," said Tang Wulin, muffled, to general scandalised delight.

"You will eat the academy's whole third quarter."

"Ask it if it minds," said the knife boy, wedged now into the cheer like any cost-artist watching a rival perform. "Ask it."

Lan Shen finished his one refill, set the spoon level across the bowl the way his father set tools level at day's end, and resisted the eighth warm bowl's siren smell with the calm of a man whose attention was on ledgers the canteen could not smell.

The afternoon meditation hour was its own arithmetic, which the teacher kept: thirty small bends of the back, thirty minutes of breath, and the visits.

Teacher Lin Ximeng was kind about it, Lan Shen saw, from the wall side, eyes inward half the time. Kindness and efficiency made the same list in that hour. The boy two rows inland — the proud one — got crouched-to the least. And the teacher's look, resting a fraction longer on that small bent back, did arithmetic Lan Shen had seen under other suns: *this one will not repay the most sitting.*

Two rows inland, the proud one spoke small when the teacher came round the last time.

"Teacher Lin." It did not come up as a complaint, because nothing about the grass boy knew how. "I cannot seem to catch the thread as fast as the others. Is it that I am doing it wrong."

Lin Ximeng crouched, as at every desk that hour, and looked at him levelly. "You are doing everything right, Wulin." A mild pause. "Slower is not wronger. The thread does not mark time for anyone." He tapped the desk once, stood, and said to the small bent back: "It marks effort."

At the front, lin the hour's last minute, he addressed the rows again, brushing the chalk from his hands in one neat, daily motion.

"This part does not change because school's out," he said. "Six, seven, eight — the years don't hear the bell either. Sit at home, the fifteen of you. Ten breaths, when you begin to be tired. Hold the thread as it comes. You do it because it is free, and because the rank-ten door is nearer from sitting than from wishing. Home. Home sits too."

He kept his own breath even and did not judge the man. He had a soul worth a jar lid next to some. He of all people in that room knew how the world priced sitting.

---

At the temple corner, before the lanes parted, Gou Dong claimed his title.

"Two-door man," he said, with exhausted satisfaction, "school means not only medallions but also *conversations of mediation*. I held two bowls at lunch. TWO. There was also a boy at our table who ate eight. EIGHT."

"I know," said Lan Shen. "I was sitting beside you while you were counting the bowls. You put flour on your slate."

"Only someone who — " Gou Dong halted. "I did not. That was lunch-mess."

"The slate keeps proof," said Lan Shen. "Mind your own books. To-morrow, then."

The bell let the school out into a generous late sun. The weather had been unusually good; the afternoon's gold lay warm on the lane like a blanket set out to air. Tang Wulin came through the gates planning mercies on the home kitchen. The hour after the eighth bowl had begun, as hours now did, with hunger again.

*Home,* he thought. *Mom keeps the small pot on the stove back.*

And then the sun gave him something else to look at.

It struck down the lane and found, squatting near the roadside's long grass, a smear of silver.

A little girl was squatted by the road. Slightly shorter than him, ragged, her face a summer's worth of dust. She had the watchful stillness of a stray cat. Her hair, short-cut, was not grey, not ash, not anything brown or black or fair he had a name for. It was *silver* — true bright silver — and the sunlight came off it sharp as off standing water.

She felt his looking, and her head turned.

It was the eyes that rooted him. They were big — Mom had always said his own were big, and people said it all the time, so he was allowed to know big eyes when he met them. These were bigger in strangeness than in size. The iris, clear as dropped glass, was *purple*. Amethyst, the way a gem-stall paints the word. Long lashes shaded them like grass over a spring. Even down the whole lane he could feel the stillness in them. Something in the stillness said *lost*. Something else, farther down, said nothing at all.

Two pairs of eyes stayed together one slow breath, both too young for most of what was meant. Then the girl's huge purple eyes slid off him, bewildered gentle, to the ground again.

---

That was when the other feet arrived.

Three of them. Older boys, ragged and shiny-eyed — the look boys wear when the town has declined them a job. They had seen the silver too, of course; silver was visible a street away, "Well," said the tallest. "X

"Other continent," one of the others judged, with the fake authority of the half-informed. "Has to be. They fetch, yknow, that type — they *fetch*, in the lower market."

"The dealers down the riverside would like that hair," said the tallest, and his mouth unfolded its lazy grin. "And those eyes."

He bent at the squatting girl like a man bending to pick up dropped money.

"Little girl. Where's your mom and dad at."

"Just ask it where the rich house is," muttered one of the others. "The house is what we are after."

"Hair *is* the house," said the tallest, not turning.

She did not look up; her head shook slowly, no.

"Hungry, then." The smile went warmer, the way markets warm. "Big brother knows a place. Good things to eat. You come with big brother, hey."

The head-shake came again, sharper this time, fast as a cat escaping a grasp.

"Come on now." The tallest one tried the soft version, leaning closer, voice dropped for the lane. "Poor little thing sitting here in the dust. Smart little thing, with hair like that. The men I know would want to *meet* you sobad. Silver's a lucky colour, hey. Could be a new life for you inside a good house. Just a walk. What do you say."

Nothing came up out of her except her breath, quicker.

"Leave her alone," said his own friend, smallest of the three, uncertain. And still not a word out of her. Still she did not rise, as if the roadside's long grass were a spot somebody she missed terribly had told her not to leave.

The tallest one straightened and sent a look around to his friends, the sort of look that has already spent the money.

Then he reached down and took her arm, and hauled the girl up bodily off the ground, and the others closed their shoulders into a loose ring facing outward the way boys do around dice.

The scream, when it came up out of the girl's huge bewilderment, was small and sharp and went straight up the warm lane like a frightened bird.

---

"What do you think you're doing."

The voice was childish, and so full of fury that the three of them flinched before they saw whose it was — and when they saw, they straightened, because the owner of the fury was a small pretty boy with dusty black eyes, who did not come up to the tallest one's waist.

Tang Wulin had crossed the lane without knowing he had decided to. Dad's voice in his head held the head up; the scream stood in his ears like a struck nail. *Villains* had come out of him before the sentence-thought; then the public thing:

"What you're doing is wrong."

A pause, a beat of pure surprise up on the lazy grin. A leg came out, careless as kicking a can. Tang Wulin flew two meters and came down covered in dust.

"Little brat," said the one who had kicked him. "You want to meddle."

Tang Wulin rolled, found his feet, and ran right back and planted himself in front of the ring of shoulders again. His nose had begun to bleed, a thin line of it, and his palms were scraped, and the dust stuck to his face; and he stood anyway.

"Villains," he said, "do not end well, where I come from." He lifted his right hand, and the lane-light dimmed on a ring of faint blue. The grass appeared in his palm, wavering gently, as inoffensive as grass. "I am a Soul Master," he said. The thin voice did not wobble. It was six years and rank three, and it did not wobble. "You let the little girl go."

The tallest one did not move for a good three breaths.

A dagger slid into his hand in the third breath — but he did not step in behind it, the way someone means it. His friends were already tugging his elbows; under the lazy grin something had come unstuck. The word *Soul Master* does arithmetic in ragged boys' heads too. A child of six who will not step off, in broad afternoon, with witnesses, with a ring of blue light in his palm — that is not a nobody. Nobodies go quiet down the river, but a soul-power child is in the file: the government's record, the Spirit Pagoda's record. Files that stand up, walk, and come looking for what happened to their entries.

"That is a ceremony-child," hissed one, low. "Look at the grass in his hand. They write those down, Goro. They write them DOWN."

"I know what they write," said the tallest. "Damn bad luck," he said then, from between the lazy grin and the arithmetic, and the ring of shoulders opened, and they dropped the girl where she stood, and went away fast down the warm lane, not looking at the bystanders, not looking at anything.

"They are still here," whispered a boy with a hoop, across the lane, very reasonably, to nobody in particular. "All afternoon. Saw them clear as day, those three."

"Ssh," said the grandmother, fear still in it even after the going. "Not loud on the street."

The girl stumbled and sat down hard in the roadside dust.

---

Tang Wulin let the blue light out of his palm and crouched beside her.

"Don't be scared," he said. "They are gone. I am here. I am a man."

The absurd six-year-old gallantry of it hung in the warm air between his scraped palms and her scruff of silver. Then she raised her head properly, up close, and the big purple eyes went to his.

Within the purple, up close, a faint mist seemed to coil — thin, far back, like smoke in a deep bottle. He felt, without knowing what he felt, that *she was more than the dust*; then his chest hurt, light-headed, until he forgot to know it.

"Don't cry," he said, with authority, precisely as if she were rebelling when she did. "The villains have run off. My mom always says: whoever bullies you when the adults are not watching, you count them as half-points. — You can stand, is it all right. Your knee is scuffed; my nose is scuffed. It is the fashion on this side of the lane today."

The silver head hung another breath. Then it nodded, once, very small. That was, he supposed, the whole conversation most days there had been.

"My name is Tang Wulin." The ring of day stood still around them. "What is yours."

The girl stared at him. Her mouth worked; whatever lived where her words should be was not used to being asked anything.

"My name," she said at last, and her voice was so prettier than the rest of her current condition that even the afternoon road paused, "is Na'er."

---

A hundred and some paces up the lane, pressed flat against a market wall, another pair of eyes had heard the scream and the answer of it, and watched.

Lan Shen had been walking the same road a quarter-minute behind, half a hold on his slate,; and the commotion found him the way it had found the other boy — only the other boy was *in it*, and he was not. By the time he saw round the market stall's corner, the ring of older shoulders was already a closed door facing outward. And inside it, between one pair of legs, he could see the very tip of the silver below them — the same smear of silver the afternoon's gold had struck and caught on, half-handled by them, the head turning, lost.

His feet began moving toward it, the way they would; his body remembered what his head did not: he was six, short, unforeseen, and could do three things, none of them *fight*.

*The record,* said the voice in his book-head, before any other thought got room. He was in the thick of the knowledge before deciding whether he should have it: *all of us in that class wake in the official file. The grabbed girl is not in it. The boy planting himself inside the ring — he is.* And so, somehow, are you. It came from street-knowledge and the delinquents' own hesitation; or from the deep place where grown men keep watch over roads they no longer walk. The watcher's arithmetic, firing: that hesitation was the *file* settling. Quiet paperwork that turns out to wear armour.

And so, instead of flinging six-year-old daylight at the taller boys, he turned at the market-wall corner and ran five strides to the baker's closed shutter, and began banging on its boards with both fists and shouting the phrase every shop in this street knew meant come quickly:

"Bread-oven up, is the law," he banged out. "Bread-oven up. Is. The. Law."

Behind the boards there came a crash and a cursory baritone: "If this is a truancy, boy, I will have your skin for a flour sack."

"Not truancy, sir — older boys have a girl on the lane." He hit the boards again. "They are stealing her walk. Law."

By the time old Gao's shutter banged open and his flour-pale face came out of it, the tall boys were spilling down the lane away, the smaller boy was licking a bleeding nose and standing over the little silver-haired girl in the dust with heroic small gallantry. And the afternoon stood still and warm above them again as if nothing in the world had ever been threatened.

Old Gao came out wiping his hands on a rag. "Law, is it. What rubbed itself on the law."

"Older boys," said Lan Shen, breathing hard. "A little girl, lost, down the road. They are gone now. Bit the record."

"Bit the record." Old Gao looked down the lane at what was left — a small boy dusting a scraped forearm, a loose shape of street figures. "Good." He gave Lan Shen a purposeful eye. "The ants had the cake already; but a hand that slaps for the gong is good practice. Tell the young lady two doors down to lamp twice if it's back, mind — your house stands on this shop's corner. My shutter hears everything between here and the river."

He went back in and came out again with a heel of bread under his arm.

"For unsettled boys," Old Gao said, holding it out. "Rules of the street, boy. Tell your mother Old Gao said the alarm was sounded properly, if she asks you what took the long way home."

"She will not ask," said Lan Shen, taking the bread with a small bow of the sort he had seen his father give customers. "But I will tell her anyway."

"Then tell her the gong-hand is welcome to bread on slack days," said Old Gao, "on account it hears faster than some grown men I could name. Away with you."

Lan Shen walked past the spot a minute later with his slate tight under one arm and the bread under the other, and measured the distance the matter had been settled at: three breaths. Three breaths of the delinquents' smiles; three breaths of the child's grass. One afternoon's-length of federal paper.

*I am in that record,* he thought, on the walk; and the thought had two edges. One of them is shelter. The other edge is that they *file you*. You, measured, on their books, at rank one — nothing worth keeping, and kept. He filed the thought like a man there had already been a line ruled for it in his book-head, in a hand not his own.

Two doors down, by the washing line strung across the two courtyards, he could see Diminutive Tang (the Rice Bucket — the lane would not stop knowing) come limping cheerfully home, scraped forearm, counted dust, the small silver-haired girl walking at his heel the way a stray walks behind a dropped scrap.

Behind them in the lowered gold stayed no one but Lan Shen, who finished the walk home alone, thinking about records and armour and what *rank one* buys you in the hot lanes of sleepy governments.

---

The lamps were lit by the time the lane had the evening on. At the Shen table, the small bowl stood ready, and the rule of the household, two days old and as rooted as if it had been written on the mantel for a century, cleared its throat.

"Everything," his mother said, coming to sit opposite with her elbows in her apron. "Empty the day."

Lan Shen knew how to empty a day. He had watched his father do it over lamp-oil for as long as he had been counting. What he had was not practice in editing for a mother.

"Fifteen children," he said. "We sat and breathed. The canteen fed the whole class without counting, and the boy two doors down ate eight bowls and they named him for it. On the road home some older boys tried to pick up a little girl who was lost, because her hair is silver and she never said anything. They went away when they remembered the government writes down the names of the children who have soul power."

He had constructed the last sentence out of fear and pride and three breaths.

"And they left," his mother said, quickly, needing the order of it. "They took nothing. They did not touch you."

"They never came near me," said Lan Shen, truthfully on both counts, with the unrelated facts aligned neatly. "From very far I saw them go. The boy with the rank three told them, and they knew their names are written down, and they went. The baker said I did the right thing, banging his shutter."

His father set his own bowl down a little more carefully than usual as he laid the accent on the one bit of it that was new to the house. "Writing the names," he said slowly, "means keeping the names."

"Protecting them," said his mother, and it came out not as a question, and his father nodded at her anyway, because she had found the hearth-side of it first.

"And the dark ones." Lan Shen looked up with the question he had kept through the evening meal in his head. "When the crystal stays dark. The books keep them too. Do the books *check* on them. Or only on the written kind."

"The books are books." His father tipped his head. "They are better about the bright names. But they never forget the dark ones either. The books," he said, with a mechanic's respect for ledgers, "keep everyone, in order, near the candle. It is men who do the sorting after."

Lan Shen turned that once inside his mind and saw the mechanism. "Men do the sorting. Good."

"Soul-power children," his father said, with the weight of a man repeating a fact to keep it steady on the table, "are watched by the Federation and by the spirit hall alike. Burned into their books at the crystal."

"When I was your age," he said, after a moment sorting himself, "the crystal stayed dark for me. They write the dark too, mind. My name is in those books with a line through it. A machine's neighbour that never was a machine's kin." He shrugged in the way of a man letting an old shoe down gently. "You got written the other way, boy. Plain ink. That is not nothing, even if it only sits there and reads you back."

"It stands there and reads us back," said Lan Shen, testing the shape of it. "All fifteen of us. Rank one to five."

"From the first rank like everyone else," his father said, and it came out even, as if the arithmetic of the room were common-sensed. "The books do not rank you, son. The world does that second, outside the books, where it is ruder."

He looked at Lan Shen. "You are in those books, son. You were entered on Awakening Day, and you stand written now, wherever the books go."

*That,' thought Lan Shen, 'was what they were touching on the road, and did not know to name.* And the edge of the fact worked on him quietly both ways, like a two-faced coin being turned in some pocket of his mind. "Rank one too," he said. "Written."

"Rank everything," said his father, gently. "The books write everyone who stands in that light, whichever rank arrives."

"I sat the whole hour without slouching," said Lan Shen, closing out his part of the emptying. "The teacher clocked it. My one reliable advantage in this township, and I intend to defend it."

"Good." His mother, who had spent the afternoon picturing the whole list of things a boy alone with a slate might meet on a walk, exhaled at last. "Then they will also be writing down the little girl, when she is found by her people."

The small warm room went on breathing around them — the same thought in three different mouths: *if* she was found by her people, if there were people at all to find.

After the lamps, on the low bed, Lan Shen lay awake a little longer with his palm open on the dark quilt. He called the grass, as he had made a habit of, and watched it rise in the black, to lie against his hand, trembling.

*Rank one.* He could feel the thread's small answer to his sitting, faint tonight, a body's handful of what a rank-five boy called *gains*. He had his road; the federation fed the school; the books kept the rank-one among the burnished ranks higher. Silver, he thought, for a single glance's fraction, without letting his eyes know why. *Silver hair, going out of the lane through a door that was not ours.*

Behind them the silver flash settled lower along the light, and nothing else answered to it. The lane of the sleeping government closed itself for lamplight one more time.

The year kept holding its breath. First day done. The road, winter at both ends, was begun.

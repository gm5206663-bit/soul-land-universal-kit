His day started the way it always started: awake before the lane, legs folded on the sleeping mat, eyes shut, hunting the warm thread.

The thread was there. It was always there, thin as the first sip off a kettle, and today it came exactly as slowly as every other day he had bothered measuring, which was every day he had been alive enough to bother. *Another one banked.* He chalked it to himself the way you chalk a game score nobody else knows is being kept, and got up satisfied, because a streak you never miss never argues with you.

The grass was grass. It would take its thousand mornings at grass speed — and no, he did not count the thousand as a complaint; the streak was the game, and games you can't lose are only dull if you're losing them.

Some boys got thunder. He had been issued weather. Fair enough.

"Lan Shen." His mother's voice came with the scrape of the morning fire. "Pail, before your father wakes up thirsty and poetic."

"On it," he said, and was.

At the well, the rope went down and the morning game went live. Rules, self-made: ear only, no peeking, count what the lane tells you before the lane knows it's telling. Today's opening sound came from two ropes over — some uncle's bucket rattling, some auntie's slipper scuff — and under all of it, the lovely ordinary rhythm of a street that hadn't been told anything was happening today, because nothing was.

Not yet.

"Race you back!" That was Dubi, four houses down, five years old and twice his weight in opinions, hefting his own little pail like a general. "Last one home fills the other's tomorrow!"

"You're on." Lan Shen braced, took the sprint-burst start, and lost convincingly — it turned out a race about speed was won by sloshing, and Lan Shen refused, on craft grounds, to slosh. Dubi whooped away down the lane without much prize left to slosh, which Lan Shen decided, generously, was none of his concern.

Lan Shen set his own pail down at his doorstep with not one drop gone. His knuckles ached politely. His grip had not once opened. *I lost the race,* he noted, filing it, *and won the water.* There had to be a moral in that somewhere, and he declined, on principle, to write it down.

"Breakfast!" his mother called, and the day took its proper shape.

---

Now, a word about the trade the whole street already knew, since the whole street was about to remember it.

Casting and forging are not brothers. They are barely cousins. Casting pours metal into a mold and lets machinery grind it to shape after — which suits most of the metal in the world, and most of the world's work. Forging is one man and one hammer from first strike to last, and men who trade in it say the metal is alive — grain in it, veins in it, a temper like a mule's, and no machine yet built that can feel any of that coming back up the haft. Which is why, on a continent full of engines, every component those engines trust with lives was born under some blacksmith's arm. And why a blacksmith of the first rank collects a standing — and a fee — that any Master of souls would recognize as family.

Once, the boys of the world split their dreaming down two roads: the ones who would master souls, the ones who would master machines. The roads still exist. Only the machines grew.

Which brings the afternoon to a small face at a side door, rolling his shoulders like a man twice his size, walking in.

---

The side room had been reset while he slept. Where the riddled lump had cooled, a fresh bar lay waiting. Dull, honest, forearm-long.

"Same as yesterday," Mang Tian said. His voice had weather in it, none of it wind. "Strike until I rest you. I watch today." He took the stool. He took the stillness with him. He became furniture with eyes.

Wulin picked up the hammers. Five kilograms apiece. His arms supplied an opinion about yesterday, which he ignored, the way one ignores a small dog that has opinions.

He set his feet and swung.

*Direct,* the man on the stool catalogued. *Strong.* Each blow landed square. Each blow also came home. The bar shuddered, the handles translated the shudder, and the boy's own soft wrists — without a whisper of technique in them to soften it — drank the recoil whole. Nothing loosened. Nothing flowed off. The body was paying for every strike twice and had not been told there was a second price.

So for him there was no second price. There was the work.

Fifty.

Sweat arrived early and settled in like a tenant. Eighty. Breath started arriving in gusts. A hundred — and here the two days finally told on each other, because a hundred today rode on arms yesterday had ruined, and the arithmetic was ugly.

*One more,* the boy thought. *It's a test. Men pass tests. Papa said. One more.*

His shoulders begged. His vision began wetting at the corners. The rhythm staggered once — recovered — staggered twice — held.

*One more. One more.*

One hundred and fifty.

The room tilted gently to the left, which was curious, then to the right, which was alarming, and then the floor was heading for his face at a reasonable walking pace and two large hands took him by the shoulders out of the air.

"Enough," said Mang Tian. "That's enough of that."

The boy hung in the man's grip like washing on a line, not yet aware that letting go would have meant floor. The hammers stayed up, forgotten, then lowered, halfway.

The smith held him steady until the bench finished wobbling under no one. Then he took the boy's hands and turned them over.

The palms had gone somewhere past red. Skin sat raised where no six-year-old's skin had any business being raised. The wrists had swollen plump. Up the pushed sleeves, the forearms stood near double their honest measure, hot to a glance.

Mang Tian looked at all of it for a while, in his own privacy.

Everyone priced genius wrong, he thought, watching the boy try to remember how feet worked. At six, a thousand swings made the papers of the imagination — the kind of prodigy passed hand to hand over wells. He had watched a thousand yesterday and reserved his verdict. What nobody priced was the *shape of the stopping*: strength was trainable — anyone knew that, any arm could be poured full of it over a span of years; this other thing, this refusal arriving *before* the mind was old enough to build a case for it — this either came with the boy or it didn't come. Everything else a master could subcontract to time.

He said none of it aloud. What he said was: "You two did right by this one. I'm taking him as my disciple."

Behind the inner door, where they had been let in quietly moments earlier and had kept quieter still, Tang Ziran drew the breath of a man standing in a doorway between hungers, and Lang Yue's hand found the shelf-edge beside her and held it there.

"Master Mang Tian," she managed, and her face did several things at once, and none of them disgraced her.

"Every day," the smith said, already past it, already shelving tomorrow. "Same hour. Fed before he comes — a boy can't swing on an empty pot." From a shelf organized by some private law of ruin he took down a small dark bottle and put it into her hand, closing her fingers over it — his first gift to the household, and like all his sentences, exactly as long as it needed to be. "Warm it between the palms first. Thin coat, on the arms, after supper. Few days running."

"I'll see it done," Lang Yue said.

The boy on the stool drank water one sip at a time, and grinned at nothing, and did not understand he had just been priced at his true value. Which was fine. Six-year-olds shouldn't have to sit through appraisal.

---

The lane had its own machinery for moving news, and it ran on doorsteps.

They were halfway home, boy held upright between them like a nap that had learned to walk, when they passed the Lan house and Lang Yue stopped. Su Wan was out front with her mending basket.

"Su Wan." Lang Yue lifted the small dark bottle, a little helplessly. "From the smith's shelf. For his arms. I just— is it the honest kind?"

Su Wan put down her needle long enough to give the bottle one professional look. "That's the working salve. Smith's own shelf, and his shelf keeps honest goods. Rub it in long and low, don't dab at it." She glanced at the boy swaying between them, puffed arms, foolish proud grin, and softened in a way she only ever allowed on doorsteps. "And feed him double tonight. Working boys burn food faster than wood."

"Double," Lang Yue repeated, like a prayer someone had helpfully shortened. "Thank you."

The small procession moved on, and Su Wan watched it go, and then went back to hem number eight, which needed her more than history did.

---

At the Tang table, history was taking the form of rice.

It began with the hand. Lang Yue had spent the whole walk home not asking — a mother can carry a question the way she carries water, carefully, without looking at it — and inside the door she finally took her son's hand to draw him in, and felt the wrongness of the palm, and the question came out of her as if pulled.

"Heavens—" The bottle, the walk, the arms. "He did this to you?"

"Ma— it doesn't even hurt!" Wulin rallied with the bright desperation of a small boy who has located the exact wrong thing to say, and leaned in anyway: "Uncle Mang Tian didn't *do* anything, Ma. It was a test, that's all — and I passed it! Look how strong I am now!" His voice pitched up. "Don't cry, huh? It's the achievement thing! The one Papa always talks about!"

"I'm happy," Lang Yue said, crying. "I'm just so happy, sweetheart."

"Then why—"

"I'm happy," she repeated, in the tone that meant the subject was closed to cross-examination, and ruffled his hair, and went to the stove before her face could add anything.

Na'er had been watching from beside the table, hands wrapped in the hem of her borrowed dress, feet bare on the cooling clay. Now she tugged the empty stool out with both hands and sat, and studied him with frank interest as he lowered himself, wincing decorously.

A great rememberance hit him; he straightened. "Hey — I passed, did I tell you? Uncle Mang Tian's training me to forge now. I'll earn money. Real money, from real forging!" His voice dropped to planning volume, which in six-year-olds is louder than talking. "First I'll save for a spirit soul, that's the big account—" the fingers of his good hand enumerated the beans of it "—and then you'll be on the rolls. For the good eating. I'll earn, you eat. That's the plan."

"Mm." Na'er nodded seriously. Somewhere behind her eyes a shutter slid a sliver, briefly; it slid back. Her face found polish again.

"Na'er." He'd been holding something for days. "You really don't remember about before? Your family? Anything?"

The girl's gaze went somewhere soft and unlit. "Nothing before. Only my name," she said. The rest came out small, in a way it hadn't since the door had opened for her the first time. "Big Brother Lin — is Na'er stupid?"

*Big Brother Lin,* like that was a thing one simply said.

Wulin's answer came out faster than any adult could have manufactured it. "Don't be silly. Smartest girl here." Then, because she was still looking at him like the floor of the sea, he did the thing no one had scripted: he listed at her the assets as he currently owned them. "Look — here's how it is. This is your house, Na'er. That's your mama. That's your papa. And me, I'm your big brother. That's the rule now."

He was six. The rule was perfect because he could not yet imagine a world in which rules like that did not enforce themselves.

Na'er looked at him for a long moment, and then her face did a thing it had not done once since the street had given her to them: it smiled. Small, startled at itself, gone sweet a heartbeat too late to be called small.

"Wow," Wulin breathed, with an awe he had not budgeted for. He pointed at her mouth with his chin, helpfully, since his arms were on strike. "That. That's a really pretty smile you've got. Keep doing that one."

Then he improvised bravely onward. "Then I'll tell you a secret, okay? I'll work super hard. Practice forging. Save up. Be the strongest Soul Master anyone in this street ever saw—" it was a big claim, delivered with total sincerity, the sole currency of six "—and then nobody bothers you. Ever. That's what big brothers are for. Promise."

"Mm," said Na'er, and this time there was no shutter anywhere in it. "Promise."

---

When Tang Ziran came through the door, supper was already steaming and the household had arranged itself.

"Zi Ran." Lang Yue did not raise her voice one degree, and the temperature of the room changed anyway. "With me a moment." To the children: "Eat first. Go on."

Her husband went with her, a man who had not survived this many years of marriage by standing on the wrong side of a summoning. The bedroom door shut. A door. Doing the oldest job of doors, the one that predates houses: this side, rice and children; that side, whatever it is parents keep in the vault marked *later*.

At the table, neither child wasted a blink listening for what the door was standing in front of. The door gave them nothing, which was, honestly, the greatest kindness a door had done anyone all week.

Na'er's spoon stilled. She had been tracking Wulin for some minutes with the grave attentiveness of birds noticing bread — watching him transport rice to his mouth on arms that wobbled at the joints like a tent in gusts, his face doing brave things badly.

"Big brother," she said, crisp. "What's wrong with you?"

"Arms," Wulin admitted into his bowl, defeated by his own heroics. "From the test. They won't—" he demonstrated. They would not. "They won't come up. Ma said the salve's after."

The girl blinked, processing this engineering problem on behalf of a universe she had agreed to join. Then she took up her own spoon with the solemnity of it, and announced the solution like naming a new law of physics.

"Then I'll feed you."

"Really?" The face of a boy whose daily miracles had begun compounding. "Yes. Yes! Okay!"

The spoon journeyed out, navigated, wobbled, arrived. One mouthful went mostly into the boy. The next one mostly did too. It was, by any adult standard, rescue under sail in heavy weather. It was, by the standard of ages six and five-and-a-half, seamanship.

"More?"

"Mm!"

Scoop. Wobble. Deliver. The room let them, utterly pleased with itself. One six-year-old kept a five-and-a-half-year-old expertly busy keeping him alive bite by bite, and at the window-ledge the lamp-flame leaned its cheek in to watch; the light on the small table had, without anyone authorizing it, gotten gentler.

"Na'er," Wulin said around the fifth mouthful, full of rice and honest awe, "you're the best."

"Mm," said Na'er. "Eat."

---

Across the lane, the oil light in the Lan kitchen was doing its own evening — which involved, at this point, a full week of an extremely quiet siege ending in total victory for the besiegers.

"Whole bowl," Su Wan said. She produced, as agreed, the red bowl of peas and set it on the low bench in front of her son, who aligned himself before it like a man arrives at a construction site he owns. "Two coins as well." Two round flat federations appeared at his elbow, laid down precisely, the way she laid down everything. "Wages from the workbox. Week's work, counted. And here are the terms for what's next, since you will ask and then pretend you're not asking—" her boy had already begun to ask "—tonight, the whole bowl, shelled clean. Tomorrow, again, twice that. Do it neat."

"And then?" said the boy, thumbs already in a pod, saying it *entirely* innocently, for form's sake.

"And then," said Su Wan, "we discuss the spoon." She raised one finger off the spoon in question, which lay with the stirring things and had lately become, somehow, an inheritance asset. "A spoon of your own. On the shelf. With the name of its owner on the knowing of it. Greens stay yours as well, and the fat jar."

"Done," said Lan Shen, sealing it. "Tomorrow the egg is mine."

"Tomorrow we discuss the egg," said his mother, and smiled with the mouth's one corner only, which in her was a bell rung.

He held the two coins to the candle flame one at a time, as ceremony required. The federation's blunt minting, no romance in the metal, plain buyers' metal — and yet. The first coins on this planet with his name on the ledger line. He put them in his tin and the tin back under the bench-board and his mind was already elsewhere elaborate — pepper, salt, scallion money, the rock sugar jar at the corner shop that stood exactly thigh-height to him and exactly the height of his ambition. The egg tomorrow, the oil, the sound it would make— "Mother," he said, pausing, because curiosity did run hotter in him than scheming, attribution be damned, "the hammering today. It ended early."

"Mm." Su Wan fed the fire. "You noticed."

"I notice *everything*," said the boy, with enormous dignity, meaning it, missing the dry look he got for it.

"The lane says the smith is keeping the Tang boy. Daily now, at that same hour." She set her pot. "His mother came by with a bottle for the sore arms. There will be sore arms, daily, for a while."

*So the door wanted that,* the boy thought mildly, somewhere at the back of his head, in the drawer where he kept everything too new to have a file-name: *didn't want the longest arm or the biggest splash. Wanted the one who doesn't say stop.* It went in the drawer, unlabeled, with everything else about the ways the world decided things when nobody was watching it decide. Interesting. He'd come back to it in some year when it was more directly his business —

"Boy." His mother was smiling with both corners now, dangerous. "Your peas are escaping."

His peas were, in fact, everywhere. He rounded them up, guilty, pleased.

At the table his father reviewed his private totals for the day the way some men smoke: silently, in one direction. Over rice he said, without a preface, into the general air: "The smith doesn't take boys."

"He took this one," Su Wan said.

"Mm." Lan He ate. The single syllable stood in the steam for a moment, carrying, evidently, whole shipping manifests' worth of opinion, priced not to be read by six-year-olds. A pause. "Good." Which for him was a speech, and the household treated it as such, and moved on.

The peas gave way, eventually, wholly, without further casualties. And later, mat-bound once more in the dark before sleep, he took the day's second sip of the warm thread — same thin length, same satisfactory nothing-new — and chalked the streak to the day, and was asleep mid-tally, content.

The egg he had not yet cooked stood, in the boy's private theater, already golden — the best egg on the lane, in the only kitchen along it that had agreed, on candle-sworn terms, to let him ruin it first.

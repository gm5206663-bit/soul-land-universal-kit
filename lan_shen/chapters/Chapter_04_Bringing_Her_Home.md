The name sat between them in the warm, aftermath air like a coin left on a table for whoever wanted it next. Tang Wulin picked it up.

"Na'er." He said it back as if it had a shape worth testing, then grinned through the nose-bleed crust and the dust. "That is a good name. Your voice is even nicer than the hair."

The girl let herself be set on her feet. She did not take back her hand. From where she stood, half a lane below him, she could only see the side of his face. Red from the fight still, and rosy: the black eyes large, the lashes long, looking down at her. A lost small thing on a road had become, suddenly, the day's most proper errand. She watched him like that one breath longer than a beggar is inclined to.

"Have you got someone to go home to," he asked her, now that the small-out had been settled. "Where does a person with a name like Na'er live."

The shake came slowly, and the dust hid how deep it ran — but the stomach is less considerate than the face of a beggar. *Gu gu,* it said, trenchantly, into the warm lane-pause. Over the wreck of dirt her skin bloomed a dim, ill-managed blush.

"Hungry," Wulin said, translating, with great authority, as if this were a language he had spoken all summers — and perhaps, today, he had. "Come on then. If your mom and dad are nowhere to be found — you'll come home to mine. My mom makes the best food on this whole block," he said, and did not lower his voice, because nobody was left in the lane to hear. He took her hand, firmer this time, and the silver head bowed after him, and the girl, unresisting, followed him down the road toward the district of the low common roofs.

He talked the way walking boys do when there is somebody obliged to hold their hand, which is to say, without stopping.

"Did you get lost from a market? You can tell me, I already fought for you once. I'm reliable." Nothing. "You can't be from *our* district, I'd have seen the hair." Nothing. Did she know what a Soul Master was? The girl trekked on, shoes cuffing the road-ends of ruts, and said not a thing. It was gruesomely clear to Wulin, from long labours reversed that day, that he was the one doing the small talk. "You don't have to talk," he decided for her, generous. "Mum will ask at the office. Their books do it anyway."

The sun went with them at the height where shadows get long and kind. Nobody stopped them. A man towing a crate of bean curd looked twice, looked at the girl's hair, and filed the look under *not today's concern*. Two aunts stringing peppers over a door watched the small procession pass: the boy with the dirtied school back, the smaller silver girl at the end of his hand like a kite returned to its spool. The aunts waited until the corner. Then they agreed that district affairs were, by custom, shared by all — but not out loud.

"That's the millinery-door. Past it is my lane." Wulin pointed down the end of the road, enterprisefully, since silence invited corner-by-corner narration. He paused for a proper reply, got none, and forged on. He explained, in the tone of inducting a fellow citizen, that she could learn their house by its lamps, as his mother taught — two small ones under the roof. Then he laid out the household's constitution, in open lecture, while the sun did its rounds of the street. Mum decided everything practical. Dad carried the lantern and said yes to everything practical — two practical voices against none, so mind the manners. Bowls were rotated. Smaller household members ate more by the name of growth: Mum calls that arithmetic; Wulin calls it politics; he was sponsoring hers. The house commanded two mutton nights a week. Anything said to Wulin stayed strictly archived inside Wulin, unless a window stood open.

"Mom — I'm *home*."

"Hush, lower your voice." The door of the little house was already opening; the entire lane, if it did not know the boy's school-hours by now, was not deserving of seasonal tenure. "The neighbours do not need—" Lang Yue's words ran quietly out when her eyes came down. "Lin Lin. You are covered in dirt. How was—Lin Lin, what *happened*."

While he was still selecting from a list of ways to retell the afternoon, his mother's whole face changed. She drew him inside with one swift, efficient pull and shut the door.

"Listen," she said, crouching to look at him level — the level that means a fire has been lit behind the hearth. "Who did this. Did you—" Her eye caught, then, the half of the problem awake in the doorway-light — all rags and silver, watching with enormous purple eyes. A beggar-child, here, in her house, smaller than her own. "Ah. Hello," said Lang Yue, instantly, in a register entirely other.

Wulin told it in order — the three tall boys doing their valuables- thinking on the road; the pulling at the girl's arm; the way the tall one had meant the market-voice. And because he was a boy honest with his mother, he told her what she already knew: he hadn't gone alone; the whole street had seen it.

"But Dad said, before, boys need to be brave against the villains," said Tang Wulin, heated again in the telling. "I am a Soul Master. They can't hurt me, Mom. I said that, and then they ran."

"They did, did they." His mother stopped in the look — not rebuking, but overloaded in some way he could not read, standing guard with her whole body between the door and the two children. "Lin Lin. Listen to me. What those boys carry between them is a market. Things get bought from a lane at dusk and sold where lamps do not reach, and crying does not get them back — ask any mother here. The mothers all know. We pretend we do not, so the children do not." She touched his face, to look at where the blood had dried. "You stood your ground, and you are right. And tomorrow you are not going to. A man does not oblige the world twice."

"I will oblige it thirty times," said Wulin.

"Yes — that is the part that frightens me." Lang Yue pressed her mouth flat, watching the set of her son's chin. He was not wrong — that was the worst of it. Correctness is half the fear. She visibly wrote her reproach out into an order. "And from now on you tell me everything you see on a road; all the tall boys you see; you tell me AT the door, not an hour after in the retelling."

"Did you keep your head down, after?"

"I kept it right in the middle," said Wulin. "Theirs was the looking down at the grass."

"That is not what keeping it down means." She pinched the bridge of her nose. "Lin Lin — you cannot out-chin a market. A market is not adversaries, it is arithmetic: a silver-haired foundling is countable, a mother's silver is countable, and they always have more. Tomorrow you walk in the middle of the road with whoever is walking; you are brave under a lamp first and by yourself second."

"What if I see somebody's market again."

"Then you shout — from under every lamp there is — and you run for the nearest grown man BEFORE the bravery starts," said his mother. " Bravery starts with backup. THAT is how it starts, mister Soul Master."

"It starts with grass, actually," said Wulin. "Backup was supposed to be the grass."

"The grass is laminated *rain*. Call it backup when it is. And anyway—" She rose and clapped the dust off her hands. She rose and clapped the dust off her hands. "Wash. Wash, and do not speak of it anywhere else yet, either of you. Shoes off. Into the wash-room, before the mud dries for good."

Five-and-six-year-old children have no courtesy of nakedness that needs rules — in those small years a mother can wash two dirty monkeys at once and the world isn't large enough to disapprove. Only a good-hearted mother raises a good-hearted son, and good-hearted mothers tend to finish the job. Clothes came off. Two school bags were left outside the wash-room door. The dust went out with the basin water in whorls. Na'er watched it done once on Wulin before submitting to the swirl the way kittens have to — one exact flinch, then tolerance, then tolerance in places.

"Why are we not the same," wondered Wulin, honest as a prayer, looking down.

Lang Yue only laughed, without an answer. Swiftly as the dirt lifted, and brought up clear out of herself, Na'er chose, pale as first milk, to duck behind the mother's hip in a wash of bashfulness.

"She is shy," observed Wulin.

"She is a *girl*," said his mother, setting the towel over the girl's head whole and rubbing vigorously. "You will be seven before you find out what different means."

"Did you use to wash ME like this too."

"Boy, I use to wash you like this twice daily, and you submitted four times worse than she does," said Lang Yue. "Hold still, little one. They don't teach this at the academies. Nobody scores a mark for making a child warm."

Wulin found her face when she looked out from under the towel, and blinked. In the afternoon's dust she had been *a girl*, one of many. Washed, the dark was gone and she was all fairness. Clear-toned as the small porcelain cups his mother kept for festival guests. That same kind of fragility that was somehow never weak at all. His shirt on her, cuffs turned twice, sat wrong in the way borrowed things sit palaces-full of right. And off her, with the scrubbing, came a faint clean smell the washing-room, for all its soap, had not been making until that hour.

"Let's go eat," said Wulin, brilliantly, satisfied with his own work.

There were biscuits, and milk, because dinner came late in that house. Na'er did not speak; she ate. And this, told around the lane in the years after, was the truest small thing about the girl: silent she was, but nobody could ever say she trifled with a biscuit. They went down rapids- fast, seated close and careful, with a tidy fear of nobody.

"Have some of the milk," said Lang Yue, and the girl drained it half, considered the mug once from nearer, and drank nothing more until the plate gave her speaking rights.

"There," said Wulin, offering his thirds, pushing his plate across the table and through her hesitation, "have it. I ate so much at noon. I could sleep until breakfast on it." He said it with the unsmiling professionalism of a small tradesman selling what is already a gift.

"Mm," said his mother. "And what are her names going to be, at this table, before the office tells her real ones."

"Five-and-a-half. We established. It is ex-ecut-ive."

"FINE — but the name-deals stay outdoor, Mr Big Brother," said his mother. "While she is in trust we say LITTLE ONE."

"Come on, little one! Buckets of food left."

"She can reach for herself, Lin Lin."

"Her arm is smaller than mine. Things are far past her arm."

"Far past her arm is also where a mother puts things when the plate's whole road is speaking to her hand." But she let him push his thirds across regardless. Correctness ran in the boy today like weather.

The girl looked at the biscuits. She shook her head carefully once, twice, the way you negotiate with a demon. Then, the enticement having very much won the negotiation, the biscuit concluded itself in her small hands. It was eaten at the same tidal pace, but with an added slow dawn of a smile on the last mouthful.

Lang Yue sat down by the corner of the table and asked her gentle trio, as if of sparrows: where had she come from. The child shook her head. Could her family be fetched. Another shake. Was there a home anywhere at all. Shake.

"How old are you, little one."

"…five and a half," the girl let it out at last, in a small, shingled voice, hugely out of counting for a first spoken answer.

"Then I am your big brother." Wulin turned to face her wholly, grinning through the crumbs. "I am six, you are five-and-a-half. It is official. Five-and-a-half is my sister from today."

"No marriage deals, no name deals," said his mother, rapping his plate once with a knuckle to send the boasting down safer channels. "She may have a family out there looking, counting the same cups backwards. We'll take the girl to the Administrative Office tonight. Lin Lin stays — he has sick-face from the afternoon. Somebody keeps the house. They file missing little ones there; somebody out there will have her written. We only ask."

"Dad is going too, right," said Wulin, who knew a lantern-night when he saw one. "The office row is far."

"Your father walks in any minute, and he will like doing that duty as much as you would like coming along — which is to say, not in the least, either of you." His mother gathered the cups. "Over the stove, Mr Big Brother — there is rice for our bowl, so there is rice for *her* bowl."

"Dad takes the lantern. Right?" Wulin was already halfway down from the stool, angling to be needed, as was correct on lantern-nights.

"Nobody needs a boy with sick-face," said his mother.

"I healed it. It went already."

"It went where I can still prove it." The door opened on cue, the father's knuckle first. There is a commerce among arriving men, and he transacted it: one look at Lang Yue's forehead, one at the small silver stranger clutching a biscuit in a boy's outsized shirt — everything of the afternoon there was to know. His decided answer was to take up the lantern from the hook before anything was asked.

The small procession left into the dusk: mother first, silver middle, the household's lantern hovering behind them with a father at its handle. Two doors down, behind the crooked door-curtain of the Lan house, a rank-one boy watched them go at the pace a lantern walks. In that order. He thought nothing clever at all. He only counted: three. Three heads where this morning there had been two.

His mother had seen the procession before he had — the millinery door kept a better post at this hour than the curtain — and she was already down their two steps, sleeves pushed up, the way a lane-mother goes out for the business of lanes.

"Ho — Tang-family. Something's gotten small and silver among you."

"Something has," said Lang Yue over her shoulder, not stopping, because a procession like that does not stop, and all three of them knew why lantern-hour was chosen for it. "Out of the road. Homeless, presently. The office will tell me otherwise if it likes."

"Then — blessings; Wan's debt to your stove, whatever comes of the registry," said Su Wan, stepping back. "Bring the little one for bread some day, like the boys."

"'Bring the little one for bread' — say it again tomorrow if there is a tomorrow of her," said Lang Yue. She waved without turning; and the lantern, and mother, and silver middle went around the millinery corner and out of the lane's keeping for the evening.

"Street goes out at lantern-hour all the same," observed his mother, lowering the curtain. "Tables, schoolboy."

"I was watching responsibly," said Lan Shen.

"Watching is always responsible," said his mother. "It is the one free institution. Was she holding somebody's hand?"

"Her mother's hand," said Lan Shen, who had not missed the alleging at once. "Which is also its own answer."

"Yes, the girl did well to get herself mothered." His mother set the scene down with the cups, whole, untumbled. "Wash your hands with the same zeal you watched."

Tang Wulin, in the emptied room he had been left to mind — house-keeper by sick-face and appointment — walked about with the megalomania of a small man made responsible for an entire residence.

He drowned the small fire down to serious coals. He set the girl's borrowed cup beside his own, on the shelf, handles aligned, because the big brother appointment involved table wares. He considered the door bar, then didn't bar it, because Mum had said nothing about barring it, and a boy who bars what isn't told him runs the danger of being wrong in writing. He hopped a still stretched-out leg like the school-master had shown them. He sat. He unsat.

"No thief can get in," he announced to the room and the cup-shelf, on duty, lights to stay ON, rice to stay cooked — the household register recited aloud, item over item, the way his father weighed wagers. "The lamp is a watchman. The coal is a watchman. And *I* am the watchman watching the watchmen." He pointed at the girl's cup in the row, gratified at the solidarity of crockery. She would come back, through the books, with a name. Then he meant to sign her in at the academy too; schools fed a person, schools needed a person. "Tonight you are a FIND," he confirmed to the cup, rapping the shelf once under its authority, "and the cupboard does not give finds back." The room gave its answer in the walls' way: the tick of cooled pot-metal, and no mouse at all. It was the sort of voice a boy could call agreement.

He hopped the leg again and submitted to the stool.

Evening ran on the lane's roofs like somebody skimming fat off a pot.

Meditation wanted stillness — the schoolman's algorithm, second lesson from one day of school. Quiet the room inside the head. Sense one's own inner space and the nature sharing it, one breath for both. And do not go chasing the second half of that instruction, boy, until the first has warmed at all. That day at school, before the bell, his attempts had slid about like wet shoes. At home the small lamp was steady, the room was his, and the only sound was the street settling down in increments.

The first try wandered off after its own arithmetic: Millet's dog; six bowls at lunch; whether the girl had walked the road since morning. He had to begin the sitting again from the stool. The second try lost all its balance when his foot sent the leg a sleep-message; the way his father knocked an awning-post that's gone however slightly loose, he almost beat it honestly too with a length of fist. But the lamplight sat still under his eyelids and something in the quiet pulled him somewhere the Adam's marks of a room couldn't be minded. He sat again. He steadied the mind the way you steady a bowl of water: bowl first, then the water.

At length he came to the warm depths — the still place where the day's grass had seeds. Hold the mind wed to it, or it wanders off soldiers- and-shells, as minds will. He held.

Something opened out — small at first and then wide, as if the field of all things had agreed, for one boy's evening, to sit down with him. Grass was everywhere in the world, and all of it willing to be seen. Blades in the roofs' seams. Blades in the park's lean rows. Blades along the road he had walked a silver girl home on. Every single one of them was thin itself to breaking and yet refused. An old, entire crop stood in his knowing: the year's worth of it had gone down in the dry weather and come up back of it anyway, as if the wind that thins the world were always, immediately after, obliged to pay it back. It lay quietly in front of him, the whole map of it. Breathing slightly. Reachable at any point. Fit for a boy to swim in up to the chin on the first night.

The room was empty around him and the lamp stayed perfectly still. It was not the whole world that came to answer a six-year-old meditation; only, easily, softly, twice over, the grass.

There was nobody he recognised in it. That would not have occurred to him to look for. And there was nobody in it at all. Only meadows in the shape of a town, and a town in the shape of meadows — glad, in a quiet register, to be counted by somebody other than the records.

Two doors down, dusk-length, the Lan table at its emptying, Lan Shen reported in due form and in order of importance. Arithmetic lessons had lived up to the star. Meditation done. And the last strange matter of all.

"The neighbour across the lane," said Lan Shen, "walked out at lantern- hour with a little girl. Five years, or so. Silver hair, very dirty before, very quiet; his mother took them past the millinery-door. I suppose they went to the office that keeps the register."

"Hm. To the register." His father lay aside the evening's reading — letters came to that table the way the weather comes to a window — and turned the thought the way he tested pen-nibs. "That is the right turn to take her. That is what you do with found — you take it to what is kept."

"Which office at this hour reads the file?" asked his mother, putting the nose of a turnip-pot out on the sill to cool.

"There is one lit on the night-roster. It has the missing-names desk." His father reached for a small packet left by the door-post, the evening's commission — a letter, sealed imperfectly, paid for at the afternoon's lane-door by a soldier out by the docks. "The same as reading is kept for at all hours. This one you can hear too, schoolboy — it is an innocent one."

Unfinished dinner gone to cooling like the turnips, the man read it aloud, plainly and gently, as letters in that house were always read — to the room, without doctoring a worn-out sailor's news. The writer had been ten years at sea, three docks behind the sun; he wrote to his sister that the war was some other country's and would remain so; that he had malaria gotten over. That he would be home in the spring, for the spring was when ships' contracts wore out; and that she was not to fix him up against her neighbour's cousin at the miller's, because the thought of eating somebody's grain-savings for a hundred years made him _sea-sick incontinent._ Signed: *Idiot brother.*

"The miller's cousin will be spared," adjudged his mother.

"Very sparing indeed," said his father, counting the letter's two coins into the household cup already half-eyed by tomorrow's salt. "That is some sailor's sentence; illness and all, he kept his words. Was kept." He paused at the table. "Which is the one thing about what gets written down. Whoever wrote her might never have meant to keep her — but if she's lodged in the books, the books are holding her fairer than people do. Paper is made of whoever wrote; one page where a tired clerk skipped a mouth he couldn't pay for, and the record's honesty outlives the clerk. Find the tired clerk and you find the foundling."

"Can a child be missing from the books, truly," said his mother.

"Truly. Books are honest long after men are." His father tapped the table. "Tonight the girl has two mothers worth the name. That is enough; the paper will solve itself."

"Lan Tang." His mother, as if still in doubt about the number, mixing the houses' names up in her old way. "The girl was at Diminutive Tang's mother's arm, you mean."

"At her arm," said Lan Shen. "They walked in that order, like ducklings."

"Which desk is Na'er's, at the register," said Lan Shen, in the tone of one keeping the ruler straight. "The book-side, or the walked-away side."

"Both," said his father. "Every question with a girl is two desks, which is why registers are fat and clerks are tired. Now — winter's count before the lamp is out. What is twelve tenants of bean curd and eight of wine."

"Twenty of tables," said Lan Shen.

"Seven wagons, nine gone in the night."

"A minus-two of shame for somebody."

"Four-and-a-half plates of this household," said his mother, "divided equally."

"One and a half," said Lan Shen, "and the pot-age left over for the cat-less cat somebody keeps not buying."

"A boy *and* an abacus," adjudged his mother, all justice done. " The lane is full of mothers tonight —all their geese finding each other." She reached across and straightened his school collar flat against tomorrow. "To bed, to bed, schoolboy. We must not oversleep school, which is tonight's biggest danger by far."

It was the same hour, and the air stood cool on the boards in both houses. Tang Wulin sat in his turned-out window-lane with the world set softly out for swimming. Lan Shen, on the low bed under the quilt, did the evening's scheduled work. Breath counted. Air held. The sharpened air brushed for one hearthbeat down the lane's shoulder-moors, duly noting, as his program required, what came in. Nothing new came in. The grass at his feet was itself; the meadow-sense of the district was its own; whatever fields the neighbour was wading tonight did not land on his doorstep, not one blade of them.

He filed the dusk, instead, in the dull, useful column his father kept for houses' ordinary labor. Chickens played in the square. Two households quarreled over the awning-pegs. A sailor's sister was spared the miller's cousin. A child was found and taken, arm-in-arm, to the office. The lamp burned its minute. Somewhere under it — though he was no watchman, and poetry was a luxury class he could scarcely have named as his own thought — the silver from the afternoon rested washed at a cheek, down the lane, where a lamp was kept.

Silver, he thought. *Silver was staying.*

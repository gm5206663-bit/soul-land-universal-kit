"In, then," said the forge.

The boy went in. The hall took him the way a pocket takes a button. Everywhere the light had to pick a path between things. Leaning sheets of metal. Bins of bent bright pieces. Long bars stacked like a collapsed fence. Components of some larger intention, every one of them. Most clearly belonged to soul machines, though nobody here had labelled anything for six-year-olds. It was chaos, but a working chaos. A blacksmith's memory, arranged by hand. Defensible by hand.

The smells signed the building's papers for it. Metal first, and second, and third. Then charcoal with its blunt patience. Then water that had just been quarrelled with. Farther in, something breathed — a banked fire, not a dead one, coughing low in its sleep like a dog too old to bark at friends. Above a quench barrel, steam stood without hurry. The great anvil squatted in the middle of it all like the oldest animal in the world, face scarred with a lifetime of saying yes to other people's ideas.

Mang Tian walked as if the mess were a field he had planted. He went deep into the building. Wulin followed in the wake of him. At the back stood a second room, smaller and cleaner, with one thing in it that made no sense at all.

A workbench, built for a boy.

It stood barely a finger's height over the top of his head. In this grown realm of tall tools, a single bench had been cut down to a child's measure. Somebody had done that on purpose. That somebody was not going to mention it. Mang Tian turned between the bench and the boy, and folded his arms.

"Tell me, boy. What do you know of forging?"

Wulin's head moved once, side to side. Nothing to offer. He had never learned the words for this place.

"Then hear the arrangement once, because I do not like saying arrangements twice. I did not ask for an apprentice. You are small for it — too small, in my honest looking. Your father asked, and for your father's sake I agreed to look. That is all this is. If my looking says no, then home you go, and I will not have snivelling in my doorway afterward. Plain?"

"No crying, Uncle Mang Tian," said the boy, seconded by his chin.

Which was the correct answer. It was also, in the smith's accounting, the entire design. A man cannot decline his friend's son outright. Not in a town where friendship keeps a face. So a craftsman declines him with a hammer instead. Politely. At arm's length. The iron does the refusing for him. The test had been built to be failed, and only the metal did not know it.

"Here." Mang Tian brought him to a table of solid metal, half a metre tall. On it sat a round lump the colour of good sense. Under the table's edge, set into its face, a small soul-machine screen waited, dark. "That machine credits a strike only when the strike lands with enough behind it. Light taps buy nothing. I set the standard into it myself, so it cannot be sweet-talked."

"Sweet-talked by who, Uncle?"

"By anybody. Boys included." The smith put a knuckle on the lump, once. "You take a hammer in each hand. You put a thousand good blows into this, with everything you have behind every one. The counter keeps the honesty between us. Finish, and I will explain what forging is. Fail—" the beard agreed with itself "—and don't be in this doorway tomorrow."

The boy looked from the lump to the screen and back. A thousand was a number he knew from sums. Sums sat still while you did them.

"Questions?" said the smith.

"One, Uncle. Is the lump special metal?"

"Ordinary metal. Only the blows are graded."

That suited the boy's whole idea of the world. He said so with his whole face, but aloud he only said: "Then I'll grade well."

Mang Tian walked off to the outer room and his components. The door of his attention shut behind him. The click of it was audible only to the boy.

Two hammers lay waiting in a crate. Their handles were smooth-worn wood, a third of a metre of it. Their heads were solid-looking cylinders, half a foot long, ten centimetres across, black at the faces from years of yes. Wulin lifted one in each hand — and blinked at them, astonished.

Not heavy. Not nearly what they looked.

*Hollow,* his head decided at once, pleased with the smith underneath it all. The fierce uncle had roared like a festival villain and then handed him stage hammers. Props for a lesson about courage. He was wrong about both halves of that idea, as six-year-olds so often are about adults. The hammers were solid, forged honest and whole. Five kilograms apiece. No small weight for grown arms across an hour. No kindness at all across a thousand swings. And the uncle had not been kind. He had been thorough.

The boy set his feet where his feet happened to be. He raised the right hammer and brought it down.

BANG — and the screen woke, and calmly reported: one.

The left followed. BANG. Two.

Well. This wasn't so hard.

The first twenty blows taught his wrists a new country. Shock came back up the handles at him, every time, like a door slammed from the other side. His knuckles rang with it. His elbows learned to be hinges instead of sticks. And the table, to its credit, never once remarked on the wobble. It stood there and accepted his best approximation of a blacksmith, which is more than can be said for most of the world's furniture.

By fifty he had stopped thinking about the hammers as furniture too. He found the pair of them a rhythm. Right and left. Right and left. The metal table stood under him like the world's most patient animal. The numbers climbed. The work was warm. At first the work was almost companionable, a conversation of two words, his and the iron's, over and over.

At a hundred he was sweating.

At three hundred his arms were sore. The complaint renewed itself with every lift. The rhythm began to cost something to keep. Somewhere beyond the door of the little room, the smith's presence made an occasional small noise. Metal deciding against metal. Never once footsteps coming to check. The test was the test; a craftsman does not peer at bread through the oven glass.

At five hundred the soreness had become a hot ache with its own opinions. The skin of his forearms flushed a silly red. He discovered that teeth can hold a rhythm when arms want to drop it. He also discovered that sweat follows the forearms' lead, and finds the palms, and makes a question of the grip. He answered the question by squeezing harder. *Persevere.* His father's word, carried in the teeth. He kept going.

Because there was a list. It lived behind his eyes, where the tired could not smudge it. Learn the thing, however hands learn things. Earn the coin the smith pays for real work. Stack the coins against the soul-money until the soul-money exists. See Mum's face at the first coin. See Dad's face pretending he had not planned it years ago. And one entry more, further down, in fresher ink: become somebody too strong for any market of tall boys ever again to count a little sister as merchandise.

BANG, said the iron. Indifferent. Exact. The list held. He held.

At seven hundred his arms went somewhere. Not empty, exactly. Past empty. They were a numbness that had stopped reporting in. Each lift arrived from far away, like an order from a government he no longer trusted. The tempo sagged. The hammerheads hung at the top of every swing a heartbeat too long. Sweat came off him the way it comes off roofs in rain. His uniform had stopped being clothing and become weather.

The world made itself small to help him. By now it held exactly one coin of metal, two handles, and a number. There was no room in it for the forge's older, grander furniture. No room for the smith in the next room. No room, even, for the terms of the arrangement — the smith would say what he would say. The lump waited for the next blow. The next blow arrived. That was the whole of government, in the country of seven hundred.

And then, at the end of everything sensible, his spine went strange.

A numbness woke at the small of his back. Except that numbness is the wrong word for it. It travelled. It laddered the little bones one by one, and it carried a current with it. An inward shudder that had no business in a tired boy. Wherever it visited, the ache came loose, like a knot giving up. His shoulders unclenched. The right hammer came down lighter. Not a little lighter. Decisively lighter, as if the iron had quietly renegotiated.

The last three hundred went easier than the first.

In the same thin half of the afternoon, on a lane merely nearby, a different discipline was at its repetitions.

Lan Shen had two pails and the well-rope and a standard. Grip low on the bail. Shoulders level. Breath in fours. Pull on the four. Set on the four. Walk so the water forgets it is water. The well's mothers had long since stopped finding it strange. He was the boy who carried water like it was sums. Some lane children are made of errands. This one was made of practice, which is errands that have been promoted.

He was three trips in when the sound reached the rope.

Small metal, ringing. Not near, not far. The forge quarter's voice, thinned by distance to a wire. It rode the still part of the afternoon the way voices ride water. He had heard it since yesterday. Today, midway down a pull, his counting habit engaged it without permission. The way a hand engages a rope.

Right-left. Right-left. Somebody was swinging in twos. Even. Unhurried. Almost mechanical — except machines do not breathe, and after a while, faintly, this one did. A hitch. A gather. A going-on. Lan Shen stood hold of the bail. He let the breath-count double as a metronome. He ticked the blows against it, four and four and four, a slate with no slate in it.

He was a boy built of calibrations. His own arms, hauling water at the four-count, gave out at a number he knew. An honest, low, six-year-old number. Whoever stood on the other end of this sound went past that number without visible interest. And kept going. And kept going.

*Not a man, then. Or a very bored machine,* the tally boy offered. He corrected himself immediately, for overconfidence. Insufficient data. File it.

Then the rhythm dragged.

The twos came slower. Each arrived behind a small parliament of delay. Something in Lan Shen's chest knew the shape of that delay the way you know a neighbour's cough. *Exhaustion, as performed by a grip.* So strangers tired too. The finding pleased him, in a fair-minded way. The world was level after all.

And then the sound did the thing he would still be walking with after the lamps.

It gathered. The hitch at the top of each swing shortened, then vanished. The blows came quicker. Easier-quicker. A porter whose load had lightened mid-hill. Not the frantic scurry of somebody finishing. A change in the work itself. It hurried calmly toward some inward finish, struck the last of its even twos, and stopped.

The well dripped. The afternoon resumed its official version.

"Boy," called the third mother, from her stoop. "You have been holding that rope half an hour. Either propose to it or haul."

"Pulled and hauled, auntie," said Lan Shen. He completed the trip correctly. Pails level, water unspilled, discipline unembarrassed.

"Mm. And what did the abacus count today, that it forgot its buckets?"

"Five hundred and some, auntie. Of somebody else's."

"Counting other people's noise," said the third mother, "is free, so a poor boy may have all he likes."

He took the correction the way he took water: level. But the slate in his head stayed open the whole way home. From his first tick to the stop, more than five hundred blows. And the sound had been going before he started counting. And at the end of five hundred — at least five hundred — it had *sped up*.

He had no box for that part. He filed it, as was his custom with unfileables, under a blank. *Whoever.*

---

"One thousand!" the boy crowed, in the forge, as the two numbers met.

The hammers came down out of his hands by gravity's kind permission. He stood shaking over the table, regarding his extremities with distant interest. The palms had swollen out of their shape. The two arms were puffed and burning a deep, impossible red. Everything ached past the reach of description. And underneath it all, unreasonably, he was *invigorated*. Wide awake inside the pain, as if the work had fed him something.

The numbness with the current in it was still travelling. Slow cycles, up and down the neighbourhood of his spine. Every cycle left him speechless in a new way. What the boy did not see — could not have seen, bent over his own ruin and grinning — stayed polite about it, under the stuck shirt. There, in time with every cycle, a dim pattern of golden veins surfaced on his body. Delicate and definite as frost on a pane. It sank again. It surfaced. It was seen by no one at all.

It took five minutes for his breathing to come downstairs from the hammering.

In the outer room Mang Tian had received the last count through the walls. The way other men receive weather. He came in with none of it on his face, because craftsmen carry bewilderment the way they carry burns, and looked at the counter before he looked at the boy.

"Finished, then?" said the smith.

"A thousand, Uncle Mang Tian."

"The machine says so, or you say so?"

"Both of us say so," said Wulin, "but the machine said it first."

Mang Tian looked at the soul-machine screen. It read what it read. Then he looked at his wristwatch, the way a man looks at a witness he suspects of lying.

Half an hour.

He had assembled that counter with his own hands. Partly so that no child's imagination could invoice him for work not done. It did not flatter. It did not round. It did not cooperate with hope. A thousand it said, and a thousand it had heard. The arithmetic presented itself regardless, the way bills present themselves. Five kilograms to a hammer. Truly — he had weighed them himself, being a smith. A thousand blows in half an hour would leave a journeyman's arms hanging like old rope. The journeyman would be proud of the rope. Half an hour. Six years old.

Somewhere in the smith's chest a word got up and walked around. An apprentice with nothing to do. He declined to hire it.

He found the boy still standing over his swollen hands. Half ruined. Entirely lit.

"Again," said Mang Tian, heavily. "Hammer for me. Until I say to stop."

"Yes, Uncle Mang Tian." Said in a voice made entirely of elbows.

So the boy, whose arms had just been pronounced dead by every reasonable authority, picked the hammers back up. The smith stood where the light was honest, and watched him swing. There was no technique in it. None. No craft of shoulder. No borrowed lever of hip. No understanding anywhere in it at all. The boy was pounding that lump with pure strength alone. Strength arriving from somewhere form had never been consulted about. And it was *enough*. Truly, measurably, screen-credit enough. On every single blow.

When the boy's rhythm dipped, Mang Tian took up his tongs and turned the lump over. A craftsman reads iron the way clerks read letters. The pound-marks sat in the metal in rows of honest dents. Deep at the start. Shallower in the middle country, where the count had sagged. Then deep again at the end. Deeper after seven hundred than before three hundred, if the tongs' own measure could be believed. Metal does not flatter. Metal had apparently been lied to about what six-year-olds are.

Mang Tian watched it for a long count of his own.

The word in his chest stood up again. This time, he let it.

*Genius,* it said, in the clothes of a question, and the forge went on with its afternoon.

Down the lane, the Shen household's supper arithmetic had its usual problems and one guest of honour.

"Beans," said Su Wan, putting the sack on the table with the knife beside it. "Two coins' worth of work, if a person's hands are quick. The basket balanced today. Barely. Yesterday's two are still outstanding in the world."

"Noted," said her son, taking stool and knife. Column three, reporting for duty in the person of a six-year-old with a ledger heart.

"Your well-mothers," said Su Wan, "have concluded the quarter's new sound. The smith by the millinery road has taken an apprentice. A boy your age, they say. Mothers watched his own mother walk him in after noon-tide. Tang's boy. The one with the little silver sister, and the gate everyone is so fond of." She measured him with her eyes while saying it. In case the information needed darning where it touched him.

"Your well-mothers," said Lan Shen, "watch a great deal, for people with buckets."

"Buckets are how watching gets paid for," said Su Wan. "A full bucket buys a full view. That is the entire economics of the well."

"Then the economics are sound," said her son, "because they saw correctly. At least the timing."

"The timing you know how, little abacus?"

"I was at the well at noon-tide," he said. "I counted what the well counted."

She left him that, then, as mothers leave a knot alone when the boy's own fingers are in it. The beans shed their skins under his knife in small green votes. Su Wan took up her parcel-work — a neighbour's sleeves to let, two coins if the seams behaved — and the kitchen settled into its evening company of two small trades.

"The basket balanced today," she said, after a while, "because the market forgot to be proud of onions. It will remember by week's end."

"Then we buy onions against the remembering," said Lan Shen.

"We buy nothing until the remembering is priced wrong," said his mother, which was correct, and which he entered, and which was why the household, poor as it was, had never once been surprised by a market.

*The same boy who ate seven bowls,* one part of his head offered, helpfully. *Exhibit: hands, unremarkable. Appetite, legendary. Grass, as bad as mine.* He filed the offer under *plausible, unproven* and went on peeling. Beans do not care who hammers. But the timing of it ticked in him, neat as the well's drip. Noon-tide was when the counting had begun. Five hundred, then. At least five hundred. By a boy whose arms, on Lan Shen's own calibration, should have folded before the threes.

Doubtless strong boys existed. Doubtless the world was full of them. The part that would not sit still was smaller, and had no appetite at all. At the end, the sound had sped up.

"Meditation," said his mother, in the tone of a woman announcing soup.

He went and sat. Column one kept its books without romance. The sip arrived. The same sip. The size of yesterday's. Drawn at the speed of grass being grass, everywhere in the district, on its own schedule, for its own reasons. He logged it without complaint. The grass did not know about tests. The grass did not know about counters that credit the sufficient blow. He envied it almost nothing. That was the evening's honest finding.

And later, under lamplight, his father ruled the day's journal with the fine nib. The boy spread his grass-flat sheet beside him, and did column four aloud. Sums belong on slate.

"The door for ordinary sons was always arithmetic. Coin in, door moves. I have seen that door work. It works for soup." The nib scratched. His father did not look up, which was his way of listening with the whole head. "But today the quarter listened at a different door. A machine with a standard in it. It said yes a hundred times, and kept saying yes. That is the part I have no box for. Instruments exist that tell a boy in one afternoon what he *is*. Nobody has ever held one up to me."

'"Who holds the screen for the rank-one?"' said Lan He, writing it down verbatim. The journal had acquired a taste for the boy's columns. "Sign it, and the thought becomes law."

"It is not a thought yet," said Lan Shen. "It is an outstanding amount."

"Outstanding amounts are still entered," said his father. "That is the first law of ledgers."

"Then enter it in pencil," said the boy. "Somebody's arms found money's other door today. Or my counting wants auditing. The evidence is five hundred of somebody else's."

"And you," said Lan He, not unkindly, over the ruled line of the journal's edge. "If somebody held such a screen up to you — what would it count?"

"Rates," said the boy, without needing to think. "It would get bored."

"Bored screens are still screens," said his father. "A thing that tells the truth slowly is still telling the truth."

"A sip a day," said Lan Shen. "Forever, if the books are allowed. The grass does not file with anyone."

"Then neither do we," said Lan He. "We simply keep the books it does not file."

His father wrote his own line before the ink thinned. *The new sound dragged where drags are honest, and then did what apprentice-sounds do not do: it quickened.* He sanded it, and closed the book on the two of them together. The pen-work man and the sum-work boy.

That night two children fell asleep at the same hour, within the sound of the same water. One lay ruined and bright. Arms like overproofed loaves. He had failed upward past a machine's idea of six years old. One lay with his rate intact and his boxes in rows. He had counted the whole event second-hand, and understood the least of anyone — except, perhaps, the order of the milestones. The lane's lamps counted nothing. The lamps were for seeing by. *Who holds the screen for the rank-one,* the boy had asked. And only the reader, so far, has seen what surfaces when a tired boy's spine goes strange. Dim as frost. In time with every cycle. Under a stuck shirt. In a room that no screen on the continent was watching.

It was not the same meditation twice.

The lamplight did the same sway; the board under him did the same creak. And past both of those — past the stillness he'd rehearsed at school — the field came to him first tonight. Blades in the roofs' seams. Blades shoulder-deep at the lane's edges. Blades threading the parks in rows lean and glad. The world put its head down into him like a cat that had decided, and would not be talked out of it.

He heard voices. Not words; nothing so arranged. Bits and pieces, rising everywhere at once, incomprehensible like the sound a market makes two streets away. Cheerless to nobody's ears and yet cheering whoever sits in it. The grass is the gentlest thing that lives, and nothing on the continent guts a shaft of weather like the gentlest thing. It had out-waited entire kinds of old monsters: he could feel that, feeling it. The steadiness of something that has been on the ground since the ground got names.

And slow as water through cloth, something arrived. Small touches: not temperature, not smell — and still, obediently, everywhere. They seeped through skin and breath into the weak stream of his soul power, as if the world were fed to him a sip at a time. It was not quick. It was not much. It was stable, and it would not stop. And he understood, without owning the word for it, that slowness which does not stop is the fastest thing there is.

He woke from it cool-headed and calm as if some many-armed thing had wrapped him gently for the length of an hour. The window had gone indigo. The room was colder. And someone was sitting opposite him, starting again out of his own thinking as Wulin surfaced.

"Dad! How come you're home this early?"

"Early!" Tang Ziran laughed, and his laugh had a half-buried edge in it that evening carried here sometimes from somewhere else. "It is full night, son. I see somebody has learned to meditate, and ahead of time. Hm — the Red Mountain Academy's schooling is properly good, then."

"When I was sitting just now, it was like — little things kept falling into me. From everywhere. And then my soul power grew, like drinking." Wulin looked up, bright and exact. "Does that count as learning it, Dad?"

Tang Ziran was startled within his heart. In one day? One boy, one day, and already tasting what meditation finally is? That was not instruction, whatever the academy claimed; men teach the pose of it for years before anyone tastes the thing itself. *He grasped it, that quickly. That sort of comprehension — originally, he himself…*

The sentence, started inside him at a run, stopped as his son's eyes hit it. It stopped there. He left it lying in the road where it fell, as some things are left. And he opened a second, different sentence over the top of it, in his fullest father-light.

"You have it, son. You've learned to meditate — your comprehension is a credit to both of us." And he meant it entirely; praise was a habit he guarded like bridles, since childhood from confidence was made. "If one day people doubt your grass, they will not doubting your aptitude."

"Also there was grass everywhere in the district, and none of it cared about doubting," said Wulin solemnly, as if filing a secret report to relief. His father saw that his whole boy came home alight. Good. Very good. And then, because it was time, Tang Ziran set a small thing on the table between them, where the lamp was a witness.

"Son. I want to ask you about this afternoon."

Wulin pulled his sentences back into order and stood his own ground — not loudly, with care. "You said men must be brave against bad men, Dad. And also I didn't fight the students at school today when they laughed at my grass. I was obedient."

"Both of those are true, and neither's the thing I'm asking." Tang Ziran folded his hands like he did over sums. "Being brave is not wrong. But bravery needs thinking beside it, or it costs the wrong people. When your own strength plainly cannot carry somebody out of something, calling for help is still saving them — and running in all alone is only recklessness wearing a brave coat. Luck was the thing standing beside you today, son, not courage."

Wulin contemplated it. The lamp put the arithmetic shadows on his face. And here is the shape under all his memory of this evening: rules went in easily with the boy, not because he was soft, but because he was honest. He always took the right thing as if it were delivery for him.

"I was wrong, Dad."

It was true and quickly said, and so his father smiled again. A boy who takes a mistake quietly is already holding the larger one.

"Good. Then from now on, you'll ask for help first, yes? At a shout's distance there are always people."

"Dad —" something arrived in Wulin's face, and paused there, being counted. "Dad, that was today — a boy ran for the baker, when the three tall ones had us surrounded. A boy from school. He ran wrong, past us, for the law. The baker came, and the tall ones went."

Tang Ziran took that in the thoughtful way a man takes in weather of unusual ferocity from far off. The boy had not been untended, then. The afternoon's roads had held more hands than his own son knew. He did not say this aloud; but the lesson, in the warmth of the room, found itself already illustrated, a day old and wearing a face.

"There you are. Help was yours the moment somebody was asked. And a man, son — a man does not make that kind of error twice."

"_Hn._" Wulin nodded.

"And how was the girl, in all the telling?" said Tang Ziran, moving to the better part of it with the better part of his smile. "Turn six, and already you're bringing home beauties from the roadside. What'll you be like at twenty? Saved a beauty too, no less. Both our ancestors gone white with pride."

"No, Dad, be serious. Has Na'er's family come here looking — is any news?"

Tang Ziran let the smile get smaller, genuinely, because the answer had an end-curve to it. "Your mother took her down to the Administrative Office — walked the gray books till nightfall at the missing-children desk. There's nothing. No missing girl of her years nor her hair, nowhere in the file."

"Nothing?"

There was the big silence of the office stamped all over the evening, the kind of negative that's still news. Government and Pagoda kept records of the soul-power children; that much was general knowledge. Whatever this silver girl was under those eyes, she was plainly nothing so formal. Five and a half — too small to be written in the soul registers. And yet, a girl that age is known to somebody. Everywhere she should be named. Nowhere she was.

"So what becomes of her next?"

"You tell me, son. What do you want done?"

"She can stay with us," said Wulin immediately, arms wide on the table, in the bright daylight of its being obvious. "We have room if I move my jars. Really, Dad."

"What — live at our house?" His father's eyebrows went where eyebrows go when the household-ministry proposes a new citizen. "Child, your mother and I could not be the house of two children. Bread is bread, and the mouths here are…"

"I can look after her at home," said Wulin, burning ahead of him. "After school. And at school too. I can look after her everywhere."

"At school too, is it," said Tang Ziran, and had to turn away for his water and was smiling anyway, drowned against the cup.

The dinner bell-cry of the household was Lang Yue calling the three of them exactly once. Mother-manner: all at once, or she stops cooking. And the table had four places, like a prophecy.

Na'er was sitting already where she'd been put: docile as a broom, silver bright upon the dark wood, only her eyes moving, plate to plate. The little noises of six o'clock went on: bowls, stools, the small talk of where the spoon had gotten to. Wulin took his place beside her with the public majesty of a personal bodyguard. Behind his eyes there had not yet been time to decide what dinner itself would cost him.

Lang Yue did it for him — with the ease of somebody who has rehearsed a hard thing into soft phrasing since the lantern came home.

"Lin Lin. We couldn't find her papers anywhere. For now, there is the district orphanage, it has a dormitory and a roof. We would take her over in the morning, and they—"

"No." Tang Wulin stood up so fast the stool barely had time to object. He put himself bodily between his mother and Na'er, arms spread out like the man at a fire-station door. "Don't you dare send her to that orphanage! Why can't she just stay here? Mom — at worst I'll just eat a little less from now on."

He said it with the full-blooded sincerity of a boy who had that day set, at noon, the district's lifetime eating record in the Soul Masters' Class.

Lang Yue and Tang Ziran looked at each other above him. Some families have a small language. Theirs had this one: the quick glance, with its two items — "the gesture is not nothing" and "we are so poor already" — passing between husband and mother with courier-to-courier efficiency.

Na'er, behind the small human wall, raised her head.

The boy was not big. His arms didn't reach from her to anything. The whole theatre of him, standing between her and the largest question of her evening, was one medium-boned boy. He had nevertheless declared, an hour ago, that she was his sister. She had looked at so many shoes today. Big shoes, angry shoes, kind-mean shoes. And now, behind a boy in small shoes, she became aware of the first crisp stones of tears, coming out of the rock she had thought herself to be. She did not make a sound. The draft from the window had her lashes; that was all.

"Lin Lin." Lang Yue picked up her apron-edge and her ruling together, in her decisive manner. "You cannot decide this in place of her family. It is her life — you can't be allowed to even pretend to have a say in it. You have to ask Na'er. Does she want to stay, if we ask the office's permission? Or would she rather go to the orphanage? You ask HER, and she says."

"Well —" Wulin turned fully round and bent to the girl's table-height as to a very small minister. "Na'er. Stay here, with us. My mom cooks — well, you came in on the proof yourself. My dad is very nice, I never saw him beat anybody. You can live together with me, in my room, even, there's this divider we can make. And I will protect you. Since I was very little I wished for exactly one younger sister, and you need a place where big brothers live, quite honestly." He ran out of meteorological words and only hugged his ribs, expectant. "You could — yes? Please? Say you'll stay here — be my little sister, say so."

Na'er looked up, slowly, from her soup.

The house held its bookkeeping silence. Lang Yue mentally turned a page; Tang Ziran had, for demonstration, spread his hands; the lamp ticked.

"…_En._"

She nodded. It was the first time the silver head had moved all by itself with a yes in it. And every adult at the table understood, one breath late, what they'd been given out of the whole day.

"HA!" Tang Wulin made the noise a cannon makes when there's a six-year-old at the touch-hole, and danced once in a small circle, soup slopping, the very picture of self-dethroned management. "I have a little sister. I have a sister. She said EN."

"Eat," said his mother dryly, the way mothers knight heroes. "Big protectors eat. Nobody protects anything on fasting."

"And the office," said his father, "in the morning, we register it properly—you start collecting a person at the register as soon as they exist, daughter or foundling or lost property." He said it with the clerk's relish of the duty, for his only real religion was sunlight decently kept.

What happened over the next half-hour became a standing report of that part of Glorybound. Neither child could be controlled toward hunger — hunger was what was in fact in charge tonight. They ate the dinner. Then the seconds. Then the bowl kept warm "for your father's lunch tomorrow," and then, respectably, biscuits. Between them the two consumed more than a week's quiet arithmetic of family food. Lang Yue watched with her soup-spoon slow at her chin, the household tally running out from under her. Na'er, so small she could have been overlooked at a festival, emptied plates at the same appalling clip as her new brother.

"Some children's stomachs are barrel-based," said Tang Ziran protectively, when his wife glanced at him with entire discussions in her eyes.

"Her too," was the answer. "HER too, is the difficult one for me."

*(Held — unexplained.)*

The house had two rooms — the adults', and Wulin's. So the girl got the wash-room-end of the boy's room. A divider of mismatched board was knocked together after dinner by son and father: one wall, one door, keyhole-light between two little beds. Through the key panel, while they worked, Tang Ziran and Lang Yue came and looked twice at the small sleeping silver head. She had already got rid of the lamplight. She was asleep so fast her face seemed to leave the air mid-thought.

"Bed," whispered Tang Ziran, to the builders of children's partitions of the republic of house.

"Dad. I'm going to go meditate, actually."

And this, quietly, was the night's signature. Tang Wulin took his place at the headboard again, hands on his knees. Not because the school set assignments, not to be ahead of anything. Something in the grass had made available to him a world. But one floorboard away, through a wall he and his father had in fact just knocked together with nails, something newer and warmer than any of it was breathing itself asleep.

His soul power grew at the speed of grass. Stable, unmoving, drinking — because the boy using it had finally found out what it was *for*.

*A little sister to protect.*

On the other side of the district, in the only house where boys were bantered into bed with arithmetic, the day's market had been lamb and cabbage. Lan Shen stood with a book held upside down, recounting the coin basket the way his mother had taught. Delicate severity is the name of a boy who does all of a thing and not half of it.

"The visitor applicants applied today," he said, with the propriety of a recorder. "Two doors from the street. The Tang daughter, that was."

"'Daughter,' will she be called in the ledger already?" His mother at the washroom corner set sleeves to soak and raised her chin. "Or does the office make its own mind's column for arrival-day?"

"It doesn't have a column for yesterday arrival," said his father from behind the work of his pens, because registrars were a species he knew. "It has one column for ITS OWN formal only. Her yesterday will be written anyway — such is paper; paper is patient."

"Two coins short tonight when I wash," said his mother — none at all alarmed; that too was read during. Her counting was enough: winter porridge held its arithmetic for the winter as always. "To bed, to bed, and dream two less grass circles, schoolboy."

He went. And under the quilt, before sleep, he did the thing the program was actually for.

There were four columns in his head. One: soul power, sipped at grass-rate — time alone governs. Two: the body — staff-weight later; now grip, breath, the pail-walk; every six-year-old body is a loan against the man it becomes. Three: the table — mother's kitchen, where peeling fast becomes a trade; two coins tonight, short, noted. And four: the question.

The question was the only one of the four that kept him. It went like slate-sums: doors to being paid come in two kinds — money's, and speed's. Somewhere there is a door of the second kind, fitted for children with nothing but a grass soul and a rate. The books are very quiet about it at five years.

*—is that door real? Am I already climbing at door-pace? Or am I only a boy with a column that feels like pace?*

He had numbers for none of it yet. The program existed so that one day he would have the numbers, and this, and not the glamour of holding his breath until the lane went soft at the edges, was why there was a program at all.

So the rank-one boy went to bed. The district darkened. Around the lane the weekly-lamp hours swung their slow, lane-sized o'clock. And the tally of the day closed: something small had been signed-in at houses that have no columns for it. A pipe of somebody's meditation sang steadily upward, like a tap that does not know its purpose yet.

The second day of classes came bright and blustery. The previous day's meditation was still standing inside Wulin when he woke — a warm, entirely proprietary temperature. Everything the grass was felt a day's amount nearer: a gulping, shy-but-frank friendship.

Meditation was not every day of commoners' schooling. The fifteen split between soul-days and scripture-days like tides, and today was scripture. 

"No. 'Reg-een'. No verbs with your names — unless the NAME is a verb," objected the chalk's commander, dragging the afternoon back from other futures. Tang Wulin earned the word "good" twice from that commander — scripture praise cost nothing, and went somewhere.

At noon the knife boy did the shout again. Titles pass house-to-house in that class; the Rice Bucket took the third of his queued portions.

Standing with his own tin in the line's middle, two heads from Wulin's, Lan Shen did the conversion arithmetic for all markets.

"That bowl is proper rice at our table," his voice said matter-of-courses past the noise, not low, addressed in general.

The knife boy had his theory why two grass boys chose to share rather than ruin each other against the school's gravity. "Second boy," he said, suspicious, mouth half full: "You have someone in for the calories?"

"The kilo of food in the leaf-paper," said Lan Shen mildly, "at our house has a price on it. If the brown-haired boy gets enough of them in one lunch to beat his own strength, the basket man loses in the long run. But then he eats the right-half anyway." He looked at Wulin, not lifting his voice further: "I sit next to him. He ate seven bowls *and stayed awake — as ceremony performance, it comes under dramatics*."

"Wulin," said Wulin, gratefully included by the nearest mouth. Then, with school instincts as good as Mother could ask of any: "You sit next *to me*. There's hardly anybody else at our island. Don't make it sounds like a favour, you know — people will flee the premium desk."

"Suits," said Lan Shen.

The knife boy, soothed — since nothing in this asked forbidding, and a sorting that happened without him was mildly interesting — chose to let it stand with the blunt generosity of the self-replenished. "...Rice Bucket," he decreed, doubtfully, for the record.

"Good," said Wulin happily, and consciously — knowing precisely which habit this perpetuated in front of which boy, grinning around his spoon the arithmetical constancy of buckets.

The noon sun kept its ledger over the academy yard. Amid the scraping of bowls and the low bidding-war of conversation, this was what passed between the two grass boys: nothing. Nothing more had to. The broad change was already made: tomorrow's noon would find them at the same island, by invitation that needed no stamp.

When the scripture-hour gong rolled the fifteen out against a three-o'clock wind, Wulin crossed the yard with his slate under his arm. He walked home the long street's current. The third half-dark was rising when he turned into his lane. And Tang Ziran was waiting on the doorstep of the house.

"Wait a moment, son." His father patted the chair beside him. The hands were a clerk's at rest. The voice put on the other-room calm that mothers had used the night before. The house around them was so very nearly at the day's hour that the settle of the new divider could be heard deciding where it would hold the boy's room.

"You have chosen the road of the soul master. As your father I shall back you — but you should see the full map of what that road costs, at the academy and inside these walls." His father let the teacup set the meeting's pace: the nightly arithmetic language took the room's middle like honor at meals. "When your soul power reaches the door at rank ten, the growing stalls without a soul ring for you. In ancient days, hunters went out to take rings from the year-bones of slain beasts. Now there is better engineering through the Spirit Pagoda — indeed the highest-tier ones can further develop themselves, and stand in the place of a ring outright. But common folk like us —" he raised his cup, as if toasting the exact distance between small coin and dreams "— common folk only acquire by purchase."

"How much does purchasing cost, Dad?"

Under his fingers Tang Ziran frowned at the region of mathematics that does the worst of the world's work. "Your mother and I can lay nearly nothing aside. When you stand near that door, even the price of the LOWEST grade of soul will strain us, son — whatever our cooking is worth." He held up two fingers, patient, loving, honest. "The city-registers name only two ways into the Pagoda's vault. First: be the student they wait for — the genius with cultivation leap by leap ahead of the race — and your first soul comes granted, free, their name on you forever. Second: money."

"And mine is which road, Dad — first road or second?"

"Bluesilver grass is not the sort that counters dip receipts for, son." Said with love, the arithmetic did not change by being loved. "And yet that is one thing. It is not every thing." And now Tang Ziran placed his smallest, oldest plan on the table. It made the sound of a coin lifted out from under the tongue of a house. "Do you remember Uncle Mang Tian? His craft is not written in any academy book. From tomorrow, when your scripture or soul class lets out, two hours at his workshop — daily, three months. Try it. If at the end you can finish some small everyday thing on your own, even a pot that won't leak — he'll pay you a beginner's coin, for work done. Will you accept?"

"The coin from hammering counts toward the soul, Dad? Truly counts?"

"It counts as nothing else counts. And—" he lifted one finger exactly "—if, at the end of three months, you find that the hammer does not care for your hands, you have lost only the three months and another vat of charcoal. Try."

"I can do it!" said Wulin, with the standing-up sincerity of last night — evidently, not a last-night-only device. His father looked at him, half-warm, half-ruched. No further speech had been laid out. So he ruffled his son's hair one time exactly, and went out to count the salt like a man inspecting garrisoned soldiers.

Late that night, a drop fell and settled on Tang Ziran's shoulder; men warm the beds their tears fall through. He turned, and found his wife standing small against the door-hinge. He recognized at once the approach of a thing she had never done at him in all the years of this house: contradiction.

"He is six. He cannot serve his own jar of vinegar without wobbling it. If we went thinner at meals — if we BOTH went thinner —" the arithmetic was being rehearsed in every version, against him, at the hinge: "I would rather stack the weakenings on my own arms, before I let the smithy to Lin Lin."

"The smith is the better weakening." Tang Ziran kept his voice level and soft; he had always known where to put hardness, and it was never at her eyes. "It is Mang Tian's fire, not a stranger's. Craft tempers before fists do. Lin Lin has the grain for it — and afterward, whatever happens, he has a carried trade and coin of a carrying kind. His whole—" He stopped. Some part of the pause was older than their marriage. "I am afraid, Wan — I am afraid that one day, we might…."

The sentence did not finish.

And so, even in the low oven of the room, watching her own worry married to both his, Lang Yue did not hurry him to finish it. Some doors are best let close twice — once on mouth, once on ear.

---

By lamplight the third morning rolled out tranquil as the one before, and the fourth-guiding voice said the school bell was something you could fall in the wake of forever. The night before, meditation had treated his soul like a kettle set to one side of the fire. He had felt it warm. The cooling grass carried him under in the way of natural sleep.

At next day's noon the academy gate shook out its children with the usual brassy racket. Shouts, loose satchels, the pavement-market of boys let out for home.

"Well," said the knife boy, detonating on the pavement, "your MUM came for YOU in broad day —"

"Hers comes for her too — probably —" a hair too late to reverse, Wulin saw the return-route closed; he surrendered his slate to her and the grin was that of a boy presented whole to whoever they pleased.

His mother lost visibly at fighting what mothers fight at gates — and kissed his face over and over, as if stamps could be given him against the day.

"It is not as if they can laugh away twenty furlongs of us, either way." Lang Yue took his sleeve, serene, and turned her shopping basket to keep the little spider-red of the sun off his collar. "Somebody tells me hammering a kettle is slow work the first days. So this part we walk together first — I told you, son, you will earn, I only promised to be on hand for the first coin's appearance."

Her son — red with the effort of decent embarrassment, kindled anyway underneath. Fell in step beside her, across the long cobbles where market-steam sorted itself into vats of evening. Twenty minutes of unremarkable city went under them. Then the walls breathed another smell altogether: metal, honest heat, the tide of the clink going out, on, out.

Then the signboard said it: **Mang Tian's Workshop** — three deep-cut words over a door that stepped down into dark. And before the eyes could decide about any of it, the deep voice inside announced the rest of their arrival for them:

"Younger sister has arrived," said the forge, in the way a bell says a clanging thing without asking anyone's opinion of it.

Against the coke-glow, when the eyes had earned their bearings: a middle-aged man of height, dark-skinned and full-bearded, fierce by nature and not by cultivation. Hammer at wrist, ash at cuff — exactly such a blacksmith as a boy invents before meeting a real one. The voice held a little buzz in the boy's stomach he would never tell anybody about.

"Uncle Mang Tian," said Wulin, with all the politeness of the seriousness to come.

"En." The smith considered the top of his head as one considers a billet, expressionlessly. Then, manner full of meant errands: "Younger sister should return for the boy in two hours. We will have him in shape by then. Men on work should waste nothing."

"Brother Mang Tian —" and Lang Yue paused at the threshold. One more sentence held good-breath behind her lip. Then she caught herself, swallowed whatever it was, and was walking away already. It was the gritted farewell mothers perform at the door-frames of other people's institutions.

Two hours of hammer and tongs, ash and water, forearm of muscle. Wulin knew, even by the lamplit finish of them, what was taught when a boy raised his arms: arms are not what hammering practises. It is the children of boys, ten years out, who strike true.

By full dark both were home. And down the lane, a certain father of pen-work paused mid-nib at a sound never heard on his street toward the forge side: echo after measured echo of small metal, struck, lessoning. He set it in the night's journal in his best fine hand:

*New sound in the quarter. Hammering. Not urgent, not slack. Somebody is being apprenticed to the days.*

And the boy beside him took down the hour over his own grass-flat sheet of sums. Later he dreamed the way grass dreams — being walked on, un-ringing, in a colder, heavier forest.

Silver was staying. Iron was arriving. The lane had already decided where everything belonged.

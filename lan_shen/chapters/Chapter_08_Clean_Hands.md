# Chapter Eight: Clean Hands

```
◆ STATUS — Chapter 8: years 0–0
```

Lan Shen woke before the rooster. He had built that habit on purpose.

The sip came first. Warm thread on the tongue, the same thin length, nothing new in it. He chalked the day on the inside of the cupboard door, where his mother would not look and his father would not care. Five marks now. He had started it as a game. It had stayed a game, and that was the only way he could have kept it at all.

Breakfast was already on the table when he came through the kitchen. His father sat at the end of the bench with a bowl in both hands. He still had the scales in his head, judging the porridge by its weight. His mother stood at the stove with her back to the room. She spoke to the pot without turning round.

"You chalked my cupboard again."

"It's my cupboard door," said Lan Shen. "It's the inside of it."

"It's the inside of my cupboard, and it's my chalk." She tapped the spoon twice on the rim and set it down. "Five. Good. Keep counting, and I'll count the chalk."

His father made a small sound into his porridge. It might have been agreement. Lan Shen took his bowl to the window and ate standing up. That was not allowed. Nobody mentioned it, which was the same as allowed.

Outside, the lane was still grey. The pump at the corner had its own sound at this hour. A cough, then a long steady pour. Somebody had already been at it.

Dubi was there when he arrived. He stood on an upturned bucket with his pail on his head, lecturing the pump on its manners.

"It's slow," Dubi said, to nobody, with great authority. "It's slow because it's old."

"It's the same pump," said Lan Shen.

"Then it's old and it's the same."

Tang Wulin came round the corner at a run. His pail swung. His mouth was already open. He did not slow at the pump. He set the pail under the spout, took the handle in both hands, and worked it. Up and down. Up and down. The water came in a bright rope and filled the pail to the rim. He did not spill a drop. He did not so much as squint.

"I'm not even tired," he said. He lifted the pail one-handed, then the other, to show them. "Look. Look, Lin Lin. Not even a little."

Lan Shen looked. The boy's palms were clean. There was no red on them anywhere. Not even the pink that usually sat at the base of the fingers after a morning of work. Yesterday at dusk they had been raw and swollen. His mother had been crying over them in the lane. Lan Shen had watched the ointment go on. He had seen the wound, and the tears, and the small brave face that came after.

"They were red last night," he said.

"Last night was last night." Wulin shrugged the pail onto his hip and started back. "Cold water fixes things."

"Cold water doesn't fix palms."

"It does if you're me." He grinned, then looked a little surprised at his own grin, and went on carrying.

Dubi got down off his bucket. He stood looking at the pail with the bitterness of a boy who had lost a race to that pail the week before. "He's cheating," Dubi said. "He's got a bigger arm."

"He has the same arm," said Lan Shen.

"Then he's cheating with the same arm."

The Tang gate opened behind them. Tang Ziran came out with a coat over one arm and his son's school bag in the other hand. He stopped on the step, the way a man stops when he has walked into a room he meant to leave. He did not look at the pail. He looked at his son's hands.

He came down the step and took the nearest hand by the wrist. He turned it palm up and ran his thumb across the base of the fingers. Then he turned the other hand over and did the same, slower. He checked it the way a man checks a tool he has been told is broken, and finds it is not. He said nothing for long enough that Wulin started to fidget.

"Dad. It's fine. It doesn't even hurt."

"Mm." Tang Ziran let the hand go. "Don't swing that pail with the same grip as yesterday."

"I'm not, Dad."

"Good." He held the coat out. Wulin shrugged into it. The two of them went up the lane toward the school road, the bag bumping between them. Tang Ziran looked back once from the corner, at nothing in particular. Then he was gone.

Lan Shen stood by the pump a moment longer than he needed to. A grown man had looked at a child's hands the way you look at a sum that does not come out. He did not know what the sum was. He filed the look with the other things he could not yet check. Then he went to walk to school.

---

The school road was loud with other children and their mothers. The boys fell into step the way they always did. Wulin took the outside. Lan Shen took the wall side, with his hands in his sleeves.

"Today we learn about martial souls," Wulin said. He said it the way some boys said they were going to the sweet stall. "Teacher says every person has one. Everybody. Even the old ones who can't walk."

"Everybody has one," said Lan Shen.

"Na'er gets hers next year. Awakening Day. She's going to be six, and then she'll be a proper martial soul person. She'll have to get up early like me." He kicked a pebble ahead of them. "I'll show her the grass. I'll show her how to hold it."

"Next year," said Lan Shen.

"Yes. Next year." Wulin stopped kicking and looked at him sideways. "Remind me to meditate when I get home. I forgot last night. I was too tired. My arms were so tired."

"You forgot yesterday, too."

"That's why you have to remind me. That's your job, Lin Lin. You're the clever one."

The lesson was in the big room with the high windows. Lin Ximeng walked the rows the way she always did. She went slowly, with her hands behind her back. She talked about martial souls as things that came up out of a person in a single moment and did not ask permission. A soul could be a beast, or a plant, or a weapon. It could be strong or weak. Nobody in the room could choose it.

She drew a stalk of grass on the board in four strokes. Then she rubbed it out with the side of her hand, as if it had been an accident.

Wulin leaned across the gap between the desks while the rubbing was still going on. "Uncle Mang Tian says my arm is bigger than his hammer," he whispered, delighted.

"He said the hammer is bigger," said Lan Shen, out of the side of his mouth.

"That's what I said."

"You said the arm."

Wulin considered this with his whole face. He decided it did not matter. He sat back with the smug look of a boy who has been given a bigger hammer in his imagination.

Lan Shen kept his eyes on the board and his hands on the desk. He had decided long ago that forty minutes of not looking at anything was a skill like any other. He practised it as hard as he practised the sip.

Lin Ximeng's walk paused by the back row. It did not stop. It went on to the window, where she looked out at the yard for a while, with her own hands behind her back.

At the school gate Wulin remembered his promise. He remembered it the way a boy remembers a thing only once it is too late to do it.

"Meditate," Lan Shen said, before he could be asked.

"You're the worst reminder in the whole lane," said Wulin happily. He ran off toward his own street with his bag thumping. He did not look back. He did not need to. Lan Shen could see him in his mind. He was sitting cross-legged on the mat with his eyes shut and his face screwed up with effort. He would forget the whole thing before the first breath was done.

---

The workshop was four streets east. It had a shabby front and a hand-painted board that had faded to a rumour. Lan Shen's hour came after the school bell, and he kept to it the way he kept to the sip. Mang Tian did not greet him. He pointed at the anvil with the end of his tongs and went back to the fire.

The hammer weighed what it had weighed yesterday, and the day before that. The arms that lifted it were not the same arms. He did not say that out loud. He lifted, and he brought the hammer down where the tongs told him to. The iron rang back up through his wrists and into the bones of his forearms, and it stayed there. He counted the strokes in the old way, under his breath. He did not look at the number on the wall.

On the fortieth stroke Mang Tian's voice came from the fire without turning round. "Show."

Lan Shen put the hammer down and held out his hands. The palms were swollen. The skin at the base of the fingers had gone the colour of a cooked plum. There was a shine on it that he did not like the look of. It did not hurt more than a bad morning did.

Mang Tian looked over his shoulder. He looked for exactly as long as Tang Ziran had looked at his son's hands at the pump, and no longer. His face did nothing at all.

"Swollen," said Mang Tian. "Good. That's the iron talking. Cold water when you get home, not hot. Don't tell your mother the colour."

"She can see the colour," said Lan Shen.

"Then she'll ask. Don't tell her the number." He turned back to the fire. "It's loud, then."

"It's loud."

"Then stop."

Lan Shen picked the hammer up again. "Not yet," he said.

Mang Tian made a sound in his chest. It could have been a laugh, in a house where laughing was rationed. He did not say anything else. The tongs went back into the coals and the hour went on. Somewhere in the next thirty strokes the iron stopped being loud and became only heavy. That was a different thing. Lan Shen had no name for it yet.

---

The Lan shop smelt of dried leaves and hot metal. In other words, it smelt of Lan He's afternoon. The counter faced the street. The scales sat on it like a small grey animal that had been told to keep still. Su Wan was at the far end, mending a sleeve in the light from the door. She did not look up when the bell rang.

"Lang Yue," she said. "Sit down before you fall down."

Lang Yue did not sit. She stood at the counter in her good blue coat, with her hair tied back very neatly. She had tied it the way a woman ties her hair when she has been awake all night thinking. Her eyes were red at the rims. She had done something about the redness, a cold cloth probably, but it had not finished its work.

"Tell me you know the noodle street," she said. "The stall on the corner by the well. The tubs behind it."

"I know it." Su Wan put the needle through the cloth and left it there. "The old man with the broth. He pays on time and shouts about it."

"They want a washer. Six to eleven." Lang Yue's voice was steady. Her hands were not. "Eleven federation coins a day, and a bowl of whatever is left in the pot."

"Eleven, and a bowl." Su Wan thought about it. Her face did the thing it did when she was adding up someone else's bill and disliking the total. "You'll have wet hands from morning to night."

"I know."

"You'll want the working salve for the cold. Not the sweet stuff."

"I know that too."

Lan He came out of the back room, wiping his fingers on a rag. He was a quiet man. He noticed a great deal. He looked at Lang Yue's face for exactly as long as it took to be polite. Then he looked at the scales.

"Mang Tian," Su Wan said, to nobody in particular. She used the tone of a woman making a point she had been waiting to make. "Six stars on his name-board. And in all the years I have known him he has never once said a polite word to anyone."

"He was polite to Wulin," said Lang Yue.

"He was polite to a hammer." Su Wan bit off her thread. "Lin Lin is in there every afternoon, and that man is not going to be nice to him. That is why he will be good. Mark it."

Lang Yue made a sound that was almost a laugh. Then it was not a laugh. She turned to the shelf behind the counter and pretended to read the labels.

Su Wan put down her sleeve and reached under the counter. She came up with a small stoneware jar, sealed with wax. She set it on the wood between them with the flat of her hand.

"New batch," she said. "A bit more wax in it than the old one. For the washing. Rub it in after you dry, not before. Not while your hands are wet, or you'll only wash it off again."

"Su Wan, I can't pay for—"

"Then don't. It's a jar." Su Wan went back to her needle. "Put it in your bag before the boy sees it and asks for a spoon."

Lan He reached over without a word. He weighed the jar on the small brass pan, read the number, and wrote it on a slip of paper in his neat square hand. He tucked the slip under the jar's lid. It was the first thing in the afternoon that did not make Lang Yue look away.

Lang Yue put the jar in her bag. Then she looked over at Lan Shen, who sat on the low bench by the door with his hands folded in his lap. Her face softened in a way he had not expected.

"Lin Lin," she said. "Tell me how your hands are today."

"Clean," said Lan Shen.

"Clean, you say." She smiled, a real one this time, small and tired. "Wulin says the medicine is very good. He says Dad smeared some on his arms last night, when he was sleeping."

Lan Shen looked at the scales. The scales did not say anything.

"Did he," said Lan Shen.

"Mm." Lang Yue tucked a loose strand of hair behind her ear. "We decided after supper. Monday I start at the noodle street. It's only washing. It isn't anything."

"The noodle street has the best broth on the lane," said Lan Shen.

"That's what everyone says." She laughed properly then. She touched his hair once, lightly, the way you would touch a shelf to check it was still there. "Thank you, Su Wan. Thank you, Lan He."

Lan He nodded without looking up from the scales. Su Wan lifted her needle a finger's width. "Go home, Lang Yue. Eat something. You look like a wet cat."

The bell rang behind her. The shop went quiet. Lan Shen could hear the scales settling, one grain at a time, into their own opinion.

---

Supper was rice, greens, and a piece of fish that had been argued over and then given to the boy. His mother set the bowl down in front of him and tapped it once with the spoon.

"Whole bowl tonight," she said. "Twice tomorrow. Then the spoon goes on the shelf with your name on it, and you can look at it."

"That's not a fair term."

"It's the term I have." She sat down at last, with her own bowl, and her knees pressed against the table's leg. "Eat, Lin Lin."

He ate. The fish was good. His father ate with the quiet concentration of a man who had weighed every grain of rice before it reached his mouth, and then said, without looking up, "Good."

That was the whole of the conversation. It was enough to be going on with.

---

His room was at the back of the house. Its window looked across a gap no wider than two arm-lengths, at the back wall of the Tang house. The gap held a water butt, a drain, and a line of washing that had been left out too long. At night the gap was dark. The windows on either side of it were not.

He sat on the sill with the sip still warm in his mouth. He tried to work out how many mornings it was until next year's Awakening Day. He could not hold the number. It was too far out, and it kept slipping. He let it slip, and watched the gap instead.

The lane had gone quiet. Somewhere down the street a dog objected to a cart, then gave up.

Across the gap, a light came on low in a back room of the Tang house. It was not a candle. It was smaller, with a steady edge, and it moved. It slid along a wall, then stopped, then came down toward a bed that Lan Shen could not see. A shape knelt beside the bed. The shape was broad at the shoulders. It bent forward and stayed bent for a while. Then it straightened, and the light went out.

Lan Shen watched all of it, the way he watched anything he was not supposed to watch. He did not work out what it was. He noticed that he had not worked it out. The not-working-out was a small cold thing under his ribs. He let it sit there and did not poke at it.

Down in the kitchen his mother said something low to his father. His father said one word back. A pot lid settled with a tap.

Lan Shen closed the shutter on the gap. He chalked nothing, because the day was already marked. He lay down on the mat with his hands open at his sides.

On Monday, a woman would stand at a noodle-street tub with her hands in cold water and a jar in her bag. On the other side of the gap, a boy would wake with clean palms and no memory of why they should have been otherwise. A father would keep his own counsel, and his lamp, a little later than the lane was used to.

The lane kept all of it. It did not yet know which part would matter.

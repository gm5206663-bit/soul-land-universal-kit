# Chapter 7: Room 205

> **Canon reference:** Soul Land 3, novel ch 34–37 ("Reporting In" / "Roommates" / "Fight" / "Punishment") — the senior sister at the sign-in desk; the form; the sneer about a grass and the old stories of a royal line; the metal placard; the bus he rode alone; the father's parting words; the academy's two halves; class five; room 205 and its four beds; Zhou Zhangxi and Yun Xiao; the bag shaken out on the floor; **the blanket with the little sister's embroidered flower** and "my little sister embroidered that"; the punch with no soul power in it; the icy youth in black who kicks the scattered things aside; the fight in the narrow room; **Bind**; a yellow ring and a golden dagger that could not cut the grass; the director Long Hengxu and his verdicts; the repair bill; and the private word at the end about the boy from the Xie family
> **Timeline:** the reporting day · Eastsea Academy, first grade, class five · Tang Wulin, 9, rank 12

---

The man at the academy gate had looked at him twice before letting him through, and Wulin had understood the second look perfectly, because he had been getting it for a month. In one hand he held the metal placard the senior sister had given him at the sign-in table, and in the other the strap of a rucksack holding everything he had brought from home. The placard said he belonged here. The rest of him — small coat, plain shoes, and the address of a forge — did not say it so clearly.

The sign-in table had been under a canvas shade, at the bottom of the walk from the bus stop, and the senior student behind it had been the first stranger in Eastsea to look at him and smile.

"Hello, elder sister," Wulin had said, the line he had practiced on the bus. "My name is Tang Wulin. I'm here to report."

"Liu Yuxin. First grade, advanced division, and this year's receiving duty, so for today I'm your senior sister." She had slid the form over. "Fill that out, then give me the recommendation from your elementary school."

He had filled it out in his careful blank-ink hand. She had read it upside down as he went, the way people who have read ten thousand forms read them, and then she had read the last two lines of it out loud.

"Tang Wulin. Nine years old. Red Mountain Elementary, Glorybound City. Rank twelve." Her pen had stopped. "Rank *twelve*."

"Yes, Senior Sister."

"With a Blue Silver Grass martial soul."

"Yes, Senior Sister."

She had looked at the form, and then at him, and something in her face had gone through arithmetic and come out on his side. "Then whoever wrote your recommendation did it honestly, for once." She had stamped the paper and handed it back with the small metal placard. "Keep the placard around your neck until you're inside, and hold onto it besides. Our academy takes Blue Silver Grass, little brother. Anyone who tells you different has never watched a soul master grow up."

Behind her, a senior boy in the advanced division's grey had watched all of it with his mouth crooked. "Yuxin. You're gilding the boy because he has a pretty face. A grass is a grass. Whatever the old stories say about a royal line in this world, this one's got a rucksack and no parents, and he'll be in the worst class by the end of the week."

Liu Yuxin had turned her head a quarter of the way, which was apparently the whole of what she needed to do, because the boy looked away first. "Don't mind him," she had said to Wulin. "He's frightened of everything new. Here is the truth, since you're new here and no one's told you yet. The martial soul mattered most in the world ten thousand years ago. It matters less every year. What matters at this academy is your soul power, your head, and your hands. We build mecha here, little brother. A mecha doesn't care what shape your spirit is. Now go on the bus — the academy is twenty minutes out."

The bus had been half full already, and Wulin had found out what kind of half it was. Rows of students his age sat with a parent on either side or a trunk between their knees. He took the free seat behind the driver and hugged his arms and watched the city roll. Shops, crossings, more shops, everything moving fast enough that the street felt like one machine with a thousand pieces and no smith in charge of it. The envy came and went through him in one clean stroke, and he let it, the way you let a rain pass. His mother had stood on the platform until the train took her away. His father had told him to go and see the city for himself.

And the night before he left, his father had come up to the room where the packing was done and sat on the bed's edge, and had said the other thing — the one that had not gone on the paper. Wulin had been carrying it since the train.

"Lin Lin. In this world, the only person you can completely trust and rely on is yourself. No matter what setback you meet, or what bad affair finds you, ask yourself first: was this because you weren't strong enough yet?" He had looked at his son a long moment. "I don't say it to frighten you. I say it because I've spent years being careful without being strong, and I would like the next Tang in this house to be a different arrangement."

Wulin had not known what to say to that, so he had nodded. He thought about it now, hugging his arms on a bus in a strange city, and the thinking settled him the way rain settles dust.

The bus turned through a high gate, and the wall went by, and the whole city's noise dropped behind it like a door closing. Broad road, cut through the campus, paved in plain limestone. Trees on both sides. Somewhere ahead, a building rose twelve floors over a courtyard, and beyond it, further off to the west, another group of buildings stood with a different, quieter weight to them.

That was the split of the place, and Wulin had it from Liu Yuxin before she left him at the door. The west side belonged to the advanced division: a tenth of the wall, and everything that mattered, a place you tested into and did not get given. The rest — the long lawns, the limestone road, the twelve-floor building with its classes on the lower floors for the newest students — was the intermediate academy, where he had been sent, and where he was, as of this morning, a first-grader in class five.

Class five. Liu Yuxin had said it kindly, which mattered; the sneering boy at the table had said it unkindly, which did not, or should not. Wulin took his key and his two free uniform sets from the office, said thank you twice, and went looking for the dormitory.

His rucksack held clothes and paper. The two grey rings on his wrists held every tool he owned: the heavy-silver pair he had made with his own hands, and — packed because his teacher had sent word the week before he left — the old tungsten pair as well. *A new city's metal will eat a good pair of hammers before it teaches you anything,* the note had said. *Bring both.*

Room 205 was on the second floor. The corridor outside it was in the condition a corridor reaches when two hundred new students and their parents are all arriving at once, and the room itself, when he found it, had its door open and two occupants already in it.

Two bunk beds, along the walls, with four beds between them. Two square desks. Four chairs. Two cabinets. One lamp hanging from the ceiling in the middle of the room. That was the list, and Wulin — who had spent three years reading job orders — read it in one pass, and then read the two boys who were standing in the middle of it.

The one on the left bunk was bigger than him by half a head and thicker through, with short hair and eyes that sat a little too far out of his face, which gave him a look of always being about to take something. The one on the right was thin, with a small nose already wearing a pair of glasses, and a book open in his hand, and the general air of a person who preferred to be in a library and had ended up in a barracks.

"Hello," Wulin said. "My name is Tang Wulin. I just arrived."

The thin one nodded. "Yun Xiao. Civilian family, no soul-master line behind me. I'm the experiment in this room." He said it like a joke he had made before.

The big one rolled off his bunk, stood up, and looked Wulin up and down deliberately, from the coat to the shoes, and found in both the exact thing the sneering boy at the gate had found. "Newcomer," he said. "Clean the room first."

The broom stood in the corner. The rag lay folded on the table with a washbowl beside it. Wulin looked at the bowl, and then at the big one — whose name he did not have yet, and who had not offered it — and his hands decided before the rest of him had finished deciding. He picked up the bowl.

"Fine," he said. "Where's the water?"

Yun Xiao's eyebrows went up a fraction above the rims of the glasses. The big one — Zhou Zhangxi, which Wulin would learn in ten minutes' time, from a label on the cabinet — laughed and rolled back onto his bunk, satisfied with the world.

Wulin went out with the bowl. Down the corridor, left at the stairs, a wheelless tap in the alcove where the mops were kept, and back. It took him four minutes, and in those four minutes the room changed.

He heard it before he saw it, all the way down the corridor: the particular sound of things being shaken out of a cloth bag onto a floor. He came around the door frame with the washbowl full in both hands and stopped.

His rucksack was open on the top bunk, and the floor was a scatter of everything a household sends with a boy: two shirts, the good coat, the wool socks, the sewing kit, the small packet of food. And the quilt. His mother's quilt from the house, the one that had gone everywhere in the family and been mended twice, with the small flower worked into one corner in purple thread on a silver field.

Zhou Zhangxi was standing over the mess with Wulin's quilt in his fist, holding it up for Yun Xiao's inspection. "Look at this. Look. Does your mother still stitch flowers on things? Mine stopped years ago. What is he, a beggar with a garden?" He shook it. "Little crooked flower. Looks like a five-year-old did it."

"It's not crooked," Wulin said.

His voice came out strange, and both of them heard it. He set the washbowl down on the table — his hands were being very careful with it, and something in the carefulness made Yun Xiao set his book down as well. "It wasn't made by my mother. My little sister embroidered that flower. She sat at the table and did it while I was reading, and she did the petals first and then went around the outside, and she got the middle wrong twice and nobody told her, and then she gave it to me at the station when the train came in." He crossed the room. "Give it back."

Zhou Zhangxi looked at him over the top of the quilt, and discovered — as boys discover things, at full size and no earlier — that he had made a category error. He had read a small coat and a plain face and a bowl carried without argument. He had not read the hands, which had worked three years at a forge bench, or the eyes, which at that exact moment were not nine years old at all.

"Give it back," said Wulin, "or I'll take it."

"Take it off me, garden boy," said Zhou Zhangxi, who had a Titan Ape for a martial soul and had never lost a fight to anyone his own age in his life, and who believed, with the total confidence of the physically large, that he could say such a thing and remain standing.

Afterward, Yun Xiao would tell it in three parts, and he told them accurately. First: the quilt came up by one corner. Second: Wulin crossed the last two feet and punched, in the chest, once, with his right hand, with no soul power in it anywhere — no ring, no skill, nothing but a boy's arm and the three years underneath it, and the punch went off like something a forge would be asked to explain. Third: Zhou Zhangxi left the room without using the door, straight through the window, from the second floor of the dormitory of Eastsea Academy, trailing a scream and a section of curtain.

Wulin stood in the frame of the broken window, breathing through his nose, and did not understand yet, the way you don't understand a price until you're handed the bill. Yun Xiao got up off his bunk in a hurry and then could not decide where to put himself, and settled on the far corner, book on the floor, a brand-new respect on his face that he was too smart to voice.

The quilt was in Wulin's hands. He turned it over, and the flower was dirty but not torn. He dusted it with his palm very carefully, twice, and folded it once, and set it on the bunk, and did not get a single breath in before the door filled with somebody else.

This one did not look nine. That was the first thing. He came in wearing black, worn loose, cuffs pushed up. He had the face of a boy who had already decided that everyone in a room was furniture: fair skin, long lashes, deep green eyes. The eyes found the mess on the floor and did not bother to find the people. Something about the way he stood in the doorway said that his word in this room was about to be the last one said in it.

He saw the scattered clothes in front of the wardrobe he had picked for himself, and kicked them aside with the side of his foot, and walked across the room to his bunk.

"Get out of the way," he said, without looking up.

It should be said plainly that Wulin had planned to let it go. His temper had already been spent once that afternoon, the bill was already coming. He had his mouth open to move aside, politely.

Then the flat of the icy boy's boot, finishing its sweep across the floor, took the quilt's corner off the bunk and dragged it, with the flower, into the dust.

"It's true," Wulin said, in a voice that made Yun Xiao flinch, and flinch hard, which is how the room knew the fight had started. "You city people are all rotten."

The fight that followed was short and taught him things, and every one of the things it taught him cost.

He charged. The boy in black watched him come the way a cat watches a curtain, and then he was not there. He was half a step to the side; his elbow crossed Wulin's back; his foot was already placed where Wulin's momentum was going to arrive. Wulin went down hard, and got up, and charged again. This time the boy did not sidestep. He jumped, twisted, and came down on Wulin's back with a whirling kick that put him into the floor and left the boy standing on the bunk, light as a bad thought.

Twice down, in the time it takes to tell it. This was what an agility-trained soul master's body was, and Wulin's body had no answer to it, and both of them now knew that with the room's witness watching.

Then the boy jumped down onto the bunk's edge, and his heel came to rest on the folded quilt, on the flower, and Wulin stopped thinking.

Light came off him: a ring's light, rising around his feet — yellow, one ring, whole and unashamed, turning slowly. And every blade of grass in the room answered it at once. Blue Silver Grass came up out of the floorboards, out of the dust, out of the spaces behind the cabinets nobody had cleaned since the building went up. There were so many strands that the room's air went from furniture to forest in a breath. The lamps overhead cast every wall in a green that moved.

The boy in black moved faster than a person should. His right hand came up with a golden light in it, brighter than the ring under Wulin's feet. A short golden dagger chopped down through the thickest of the strands, twice, with a sound like a cleaver meeting a rope. It was a good attack. In any room in the federation, that attack cuts a boy's grass and ends a fight.

It did not cut the grass.

The room watched the dagger rebound and the strands close over it, and the boy in black looked, for the first time since walking through the door, at something other than furniture. Then Bind finished what the boy had started. The grass went over him like a wave going over a stone — both arms crossed and pinned to his ribs, the dagger caught point-down in the weave an inch from his own forearm, one leg lifted off balance and held there. His yellow ring burned over the floor, and the dagger's light flooded the strands, and the grass — breathing the way it always breathed, slow and green and entirely unexcited — did not let go by so much as a leaf-width.

In a fight between one boy's yellow ring and another boy's yellow ring, the whole business comes down to what the two of them are made of. The dagger's master was brighter and faster. The grass was older, and answered to a boy whose grip had been weighed that summer at five hundred kilograms and change, and it bound not the room but the entire floor of it, and it did not know how to be cut.

The boy in black, for the first time, made a sound — a short, enraged, child's sound of a person discovering that the universe has an edict he was not informed of — and fought the bind with everything the academy would later call exceptional. It was not enough. It was not, in fact, close. And when the boy's struggle reached its peak, the binder let him come forward the length of one hand's width, and a fist came through the gap and met the handsome face at the end of it.

*Bang.*

Wulin released the Bind as the punch landed — his own choice, made at the last split of the moment. He would understand later, as he learned his own skill better, that it was one of the choices that mattered: a control skill does not let go of a flying thing by accident. The room let go of the boy in black all at once. He left it the way Zhou Zhangxi had, except that Zhou Zhangxi had found a window and this one found the corridor wall. The whole second floor heard the sound of the discovery.

The fight was over. It had taken, by the room's clock, under a minute.

---

Fifty minutes later, four boys stood in a row in front of Director Long Hengxu's desk, under a ceiling lamp that seemed to be on the director's side of the conversation.

The director himself was a man built out of straight lines and long patience, and his face did the thing where a small frown does all the work of a lecture. He heard the account of the fight from Yun Xiao, who delivered it like a scholar reading sources aloud — accurate, complete, and with a carefully neutral face that fooled no one about where he had stood. The director asked two questions of Zhou Zhangxi and one of Yun Xiao, and none of Wulin at all, which Wulin only understood later was itself a finding.

"Summarized," Long Hengxu said, "this tragedy was triggered over a quilt."

Nobody in the row of four disagreed, which was, in its way, worse.

"Zhou Zhangxi. Titan Ape martial soul, rank eleven, and a boy of your size has no business losing a fight to anyone in this room, let alone to a coat that weighs what he does." The director's voice was cold and even. "Your martial soul would have put you in class three on looks alone; your rank put you in class five. As of today, your conduct has asked to be filed with it. You stay in class five. Permanently. Be grateful the file is the only place this goes."

Zhou Zhangxi's face worked, and his mouth opened, and he looked at the director, and at Wulin, and at the director again, and then shut it, with a lesson visibly filing itself somewhere behind his eyes.

"Yun Xiao. Spectators are participants who have chosen a side called nothing. Class five."

"Director, I was reading—"

"You were reading on a bed in a room where two students tore a wall down, which means you were reading fast." The director's gaze moved. "You. I'll want your name."

"Tang Wulin, Director."

"Tang Wulin, class five, and stayed there by choice of martial soul, not conduct. You were provoked, and the account says you gave the provocateur one warning before you gave him the window, which is more warning than most of my staff get. So: no punishment on your record. The fight itself, the wall, the window, the bed frame, and the corridor plaster I have already walked past — those are another matter. The academy does not pay for what students break. The costs will be given to you this evening, and you will pay them back, in full, at whatever pace the office allows, beginning when the office tells you. I don't care where the money comes from. It cannot come from your parents without a letter from them, because I refuse to teach a lesson to a mother."

"He told me to ask myself if I was strong enough," Wulin said, before he could stop himself. "So — the money should be mine too. I have some. And I'll be earning more." He stopped. "Sir."

The director looked at him for two full seconds, which for a director is eleven minutes. "That is the correct answer to a question I did not ask," he said. "All four of you are dismissed. The next fight goes on a record that follows you to the advanced division's door, and the advanced division does not open for boys with fight-scars on their file. Consider that the price of this one's being free. Out."

They went out into the corridor in the particular silence of boys who have been sorted. And behind them, in the office, Long Hengxu stood at his window a moment, looking down into the courtyard. Then he spoke to the secretary who had been writing all of it down.

"Give me Tang Wulin's file. The full one."

"Director, he's a Blue Silver Grass plant system with a class-five assignment—"

"Blue Silver Grass." Long Hengxu turned from the window. "A blue silver grass that put a boy through a second-floor window with one hand and no soul power, and then a boy from the Xie family through a corridor wall with one held skill. One of those students in that room had a golden dagger and a yellow ring at nine, which in this city means a family and a tutor and a schedule. The other one owns a quilt and sells nothing, and wears a pair of dull grey rings on his wrists, and I would like to know what a boy from a forge town keeps in them." He sat back down. "Cultivating a trash martial soul to rank twelve at nine years old is not a curiosity, and I am not curious. I am careful. Fetch the file, and keep the matter quiet."

---

Room 205, by evening, had found its new shape, which is how a room survives four boys.

The window was a hole covered by a sheet of canvas the groundskeeper had nailed up, and the cold of it made the room several degrees more honest than it had been that morning. Wulin's things were on the top bunk, folded, the quilt on the outside of the pile where it could be seen. Zhou Zhangxi had said nothing to anyone all evening, and when his bunk's springs creaked he turned his face to the wall. Yun Xiao had rearranged the desk into two territories and was reading with the fixed attention of a man pretending the day had not occurred.

The boy in black's cheek was swollen to the exact size and dignity of a steamed bun. He had put a cold cloth on it earlier with the skill of someone who had been knocked down before, which was itself a piece of information about the whole affair. That evening he walked into the room, glanced at Yun Xiao's bottom bunk for the length of one breath, and pointed up.

Yun Xiao looked at him over the rims of his glasses. Then he smiled — the smile of a scholar reprieving himself from a thesis — gathered his book, his blanket, and his dignity, and moved to the upper bunk without a single word.

The boy in black sat on the lower bunk, dismissed the room, and lay back. On the cabinet by his head, where the dormitory office had left it the way they left them for every student, lay the new roster card for room 205, four names handwritten in a clerk's dull hand.

*ZHOU ZHANGXI · YUN XIAO · TANG WULIN · XIE XIE.*

Wulin read it twice, flat on his back on the top bunk with his hands behind his head. The name was pleasant and strange, and it itched at some door in his memory: a crowd, a shoulder, a shout in a station. The itch went away the way itches do. The boy on the platform that day had worn blue from collar to boots and said nothing. The boy on the bunk below him wore black and had said two words to him total, both of them before the fight. City boys, it turned out, looked alike. It was another thing Eastsea had taught him today, and he filed it with the rest.

Under everything, down where a hundred years of grass kept the accounts, the ledger noticed.

The day had gone in as one entry and would not break into smaller ones, and the grass let it be a single rough stone in its store, because some days arrive as one stone. It had spent the afternoon in the floor and the air of a room full of strangers, and it had learned what a boy's anger was like from inside the weather of him. The anger came up like a forge's heat, all at once, with no preamble. The ring rode it. The grass had covered an entire room because the boy had asked — and the boy had not measured the asking first. The flower, in the grass's accounting, was not a decoration and not a quilt. The flower was a thread of one person carried by another, and strangers had dropped it into the dust twice in one day. Both times, the boy had stopped being a small animal and started being a stone wall.

People do that for threads, the grass recorded, in its patient season-script. It had spent its century thinking of the surface as hazard and weather. A thing that a boy would tear down a wall for changed what the word *surface* was worth.

The letter home, Wulin wrote that night by the desk lamp, on paper the office had given him, in his careful blank-ink hand. It told his parents: the academy was large; the senior sister at the desk had been kind; he was in class five; the dormitory room had four beds and he had the top bunk. It told them: he had found the guild's address and would report inside the month. It told them the truth of the other thing too, in the plain way the house kept numbers. He wrote that he had had a fight on the first day. He wrote that the fight had been provoked and that the academy had ruled it so. There was a repair bill, and he would pay it himself out of the coast shop's wage, on the office's own schedule, and they were not to send money or worry. The reason he was telling them at all was the reason they had raised him: families keep honest ledgers.

At the end, on the last line, he added the sentence he had promised, and then crossed out the line and wrote the promise *under* it, where it could not be mistaken for closing politeness.

*The white stone is coming. The shore is out past the school, and I will find it — not bought, picked up from the actual sea.*

He read it over, folded it twice, and put his name on the outside. Through the canvas over the window, the city hummed: three million people, all awake, none of them aware that Room 205 had this afternoon acquired a grass with a hundred years in it, and a boy who knew exactly what his hands were worth.

Below him, the boy named Xie Xie lay still on his bunk and was not asleep. His eyes were open in the dark, fixed on the underside of the bunk above him, and what was in them was not hatred. It was a man in a family workshop being told that the number he had been given for himself was wrong.

Above, on the top bunk, Wulin fell asleep the way he always fell asleep, all at once.

*Grow where you're planted,* the grass said to itself, in the only language it had, and got to work on the new ground.

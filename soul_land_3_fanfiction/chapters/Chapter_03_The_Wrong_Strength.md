# Chapter 3: The Wrong Strength

> **Canon reference:** Soul Land 3, Red Mountain forge arc — the forging failures that began with the first spirit ring; the strength test at nine (canon readout 483 / 543 kg); Mang Tian's attempt to snap the fused Blue Silver strand and the four-ring reveal beneath it (Earth Hammer; first skill Tenacity); the door to thousand refinement and the heavy silver (the first refinement itself stays for ch 4); Na'er's adoption — canon event, licensed here to its own timing window and receipted in the chapter's coverage file
> **Timeline:** two weeks after the school's measure · Tang Wulin, 9, rank 12 · Glorybound City

---

Before dawn, the boy did the thing the house did not talk about.

It had started as a scratch on the third morning after the school's measure, and it had become a habit by the tenth: he woke ahead of the house, sat on the edge of his bed, rolled one sleeve back to the elbow, and laid his open palm flat on the desk. Then he waited. The answer always came the same way. Warmth rose through the thinnest skin of the wrist first, like a flat stone that had been lying in the sun, and under the warmth came the small, steady pressure — the feeling of a finger pressing down on a blanket to find the body underneath it. He pressed back with his mind the way you press a question into a wall. The pressure stayed, patient, and gave nothing that could be written down.

The neighbors had a word for mornings like this. They called it the tickle-press, and they meant a child playing with a pet.

The boy pressed his palm flat, and the grass answered from where it lived — a place he could not point to, under his own thoughts, the way a floor is under a room. No words. There had never been words. But something in the answer had changed lately. It had the quality of a plant leaning toward a window: not a message, only a direction. Toward the ring hand. Toward the forge, and the days when the hammer rang.

He tucked the sleeve back down over the fading lines at his wrist — three faint gold threads, quiet as a watermark — and went to breakfast.

At the table, his mother set two bowls instead of one and said, in the voice she used for weather, "Cold's come. The school's furnace won't be lit until the end of the month, so if your hands numb in class, you rub them under your coat like everyone else's children. I don't want the teacher writing me letters about a boy who sits with his hands on a lamp."

"I'll sit on my hands," Wulin said.

"You'll eat," Lang Yue said, and put the egg between his bowl and his elbow like a pawn placed on a board.

Outside, the street had grown a new season. It was not the cold that told him. It was the eyes. Twice on the walk to the forge, a stranger's gaze went to his right wrist and stopped there a half-beat longer than a glance should stop — the sleeve covered nothing that could be seen, but the whole city had been told what it was covering. The clerk at the grain stand, who had known him for years, said, "The Tang boy," in a tone that had changed somewhere in the last month, the way a price changes; and then, being a decent man, he pretended he hadn't said it that way. Wulin walked on with his hands in his pockets and his shoulders making the shape his father's shoulders made when a customer asked about a debt too loudly.

Two weeks. The story had walked the district twice by then and crossed the canal once, growing a new detail at every stall. The school's recommendation letter sat on the shelf at home, sealed for the season ahead. And in the forge district, on a lane where the coal smoke kept the cold at shoulder height, the day's work was already stacked and waiting, the way it was every morning.

He turned in at the smell of hot iron, and the bell over the shop door sang the small song it always sang, and the day began.

---

The first blank spoiled before the shop had properly warmed.

It was honest work — a gate-brace, plain iron, the sort of piece Mang Tian handed to a trusted apprentice and forgot about. Wulin took it through a clean heat, brought it out amber, set it on the anvil, and struck his ordinary first blow. The hammer went in too deep. Not by much; by a thumb's width of excess pressure, the amount another smith might have written off to a bad heat. He frowned, reheated, and struck again with less. The second blow left the metal long and thin in a way that told him the truth even before he measured it. Over-struck. Then over-corrected, which was worse: he struck soft, the work barely moved, the heat fell out of it, and the blank came off the anvil with a folded seam down its spine like a bad healing.

He looked at it for the time it took to count ten. Then he walked to the scrap hook and hung it, and went back to the rack for another billet.

Mang Tian said nothing from the grinding wheel. That was his way; the wheel made its low, patient note and he let the note do the talking.

The second blank spoiled at the bending frame — same iron, same draw, and the piece bent past its line into a shape no gate would hang. The third spoiled differently again: it survived the hammer and died on the punch, splitting precisely along the line where the boy's hand had been half a shade too sure.

Three billets. He hung them all on the scrap hook, in a row, so that he would have to look at them.

At the midday break, Mang Tian walked over, took the three pieces down one at a time, and turned them in his big hands. He did not look at Wulin. He looked at the metal the way a physician looks at a tongue.

"Amber at the first," he said, "and you struck it like a man driving a nail. This one you struck like a man afraid to wake a baby. And this one — " he tapped the split — "this one you were angry, and you struck for the middle of the metal instead of the middle of the work. Three mistakes, and not one of them is in your eye. Your eye's still good. Your eye has never been the trouble."

"I'll take the burn marks off the ledger," Wulin said. "The blanks come out of my pay."

"Where else would they come from?" Mang Tian put the pieces on the bench between them, side by side, like evidence. "You drew the ring two weeks ago."

"Yes."

"So." The master wiped his hands and looked at him at last, and there was nothing cruel in it and nothing soft either; it was the look he gave a kettle of metal that had begun to behave in a new way. "Since then you've worked the same as before, and the work has come out worse. Do you know what I see?"

Wulin shook his head.

"I see a boy on the road where every smith in this city has walked, only you've come to it at nine instead of seventeen. The forge gave you hands, boy. And something gave you more hands than the forge did."

He let that sit. Over at the wall, above the quenching trough, the old count-screen still hung where it had hung for three years — the practice board from the day Wulin had first been allowed into the shop, its frame scratched, its face cloudy, the screen that had counted, in glowing numerals, a thousand strikes from a six-year-old's hammer. Mang Tian's eyes went to it, and Wulin's followed.

"We measured your hands when you were small," the master said, "and they didn't measure enough. So we built them up. Turned out they built themselves up faster than I was ever told. That's the trouble. It isn't the metal. It isn't your eye. It isn't even the strength. It's that your strength grew up, and your method stayed nine years old."

He went back to the wheel, and the wheel's note resumed, and it was settled and not settled at the same time, which was the shape of most of the answers in that shop.

"Tomorrow, before the first bell," Mang Tian said over his shoulder. "Bring your hands. All of them."

---

The shop kept an old pressing frame in the back room, under a canvas, next to a pile of warped practice plates. It had come from a mecha yard years ago, bought secondhand for the price of scrap, and the old smiths of the lane used it as a borrowing library uses a dictionary: once in a long while, when a number truly had to be known.

Mang Tian pulled the canvas off, oiled the two flat pads, and set the gauge between them. He checked the needle against a known weight — a stack of iron plates the oldest apprentices used to settle arguments — and then he stood back and pointed at the left pad.

"Flat hand," he said. "Heel of the palm on the mark. Press like you mean it. You are not pressing the frame. You are pressing the world."

Wulin set his hand. The metal was cold — the back room never warmed — and he pressed the way he had learned as a small boy: through the floor, up the calves, along the back, out through the shoulder, down into the heel of the palm, everything going into one line.

The needle climbed, hesitated at a hundred and forty, and kept going.

He heard Mang Tian's breath change. The needle passed two hundred. It crossed three. Somewhere past four, the gold lines woke in his forearm — he felt them come the way you feel heat come up the handle of a ladle: faint first, then certain. They would be visible now, threading up under the skin, brighter where the effort ran. The needle trembled. He pressed harder, because pressing harder was the whole test, and the lines brightened in answer, and the frame creaked like a ship's plank.

The needle stopped at five hundred and seven.

"Again," Mang Tian said quietly. "Right hand."

The right hand read five hundred and sixty-one.

The master wrote both numbers in the shop's tall ledger, in the column where the boy's wages were recorded, and then he did something Wulin had never seen him do: he stood over the numbers with the pen still in his hand, and read them the way he would read a weight of precious metal, slowly, more than once.

At full effort, both of Wulin's arms had lit with the lines. They faded now, slow as cooling steel, and Mang Tian watched them fade, and Wulin watched Mang Tian watch, and neither of them said a word about them.

Then the master shut the ledger.

"Old frame reads a shade generous," he said. "Give it back six kilos a hand and it's still five hundred. Do you know what that means?"

"That I'm strong," Wulin said.

"That you're strong," Mang Tian agreed, "and that's the least interesting half. The interesting half is what you did not do. Most boys who crack a frame like this are farm lads, big in the bone. You are not big in the bone. You are nine years old and you weigh what a schoolboy weighs. You pressed through the floor and out of your palm, and the frame asked, before the needle even moved, what school taught you that line — and it was my school. Everything you are, you pushed through my method. That is why the blanks died. Not because the strength is wrong. Because you put four hundred kilos through a hundred-kilo door."

"What's the door?" Wulin asked.

"Your swing," Mang Tian said. "A swing lasts as long as it lasts and eats what it eats, and yours eats the wrong amount of you each time. You have grown past your own hands. Now." He hung the canvas back over the frame. "There's one more test, and it's not for your hands."

---

They went out into the shop proper, where the day's first heat was already in the forge mouth. Mang Tian put his back to the anvil and folded his arms.

"Show me the grass," he said.

Wulin released his martial soul. The blades came up from the floor by his boots — broad, straight, silver-pale along the edges the way the name promised, and threaded now, faint but certain, with gold along the rooting lines, thin as the veins in a leaf. He gathered one strand up into his hand and held it out, and the blade lay across his palm, lighter than it should have been, tougher than any boy's plant had a right to be.

Mang Tian unwrapped his fingers from his mug of tea and set the mug down.

He took the blade in both hands, wrapped it once around his fist the way a man tests a rope, planted his feet like a man at the bellows, and pulled.

Nothing happened. The strand did not part. It did not even sound like a living thing being hurt; it made no sound at all. It merely straightened, the way rope straightens under load, and filled his fists, and waited. Fine veins along its length glowed the least shade brighter where the strain ran.

The master tried again — a twist this time, working the fiber against itself the way you break a stubborn vice-grip of wire. The strand took the twist, lay smooth, and gave nothing.

Wulin watched his teacher's face, and then, for the first time in his life, he watched his teacher do something other than work.

Four rings came off Mang Tian's body.

The first was white, the second and third were yellow, and the last was purple — deep, quiet purple, the color of old river stone. They rose around him and turned slowly, and the shop, which had been the most ordinary room in the world a breath ago, was suddenly a room where a soul master stood.

Three spirit souls appeared along his shoulders. Wulin saw a pair of small hammers first, yellow-lit and exact, the size of the hammers a jeweler uses; then a broad, low bear the color of old oak, heavy-shouldered, standing at the man's left shoulder like a cousin who had come to watch. And around the master his martial soul stood a moment too — a hammer, big and plain, glowing a dim white, with deep brown veins running through its head like rings in a stump, the way old iron carries the history of its heats.

Earth Hammer. Wulin had known the word from books and had never once used it for the man who had mended the family's kettles for a decade.

"First soul skill," Mang Tian said, and the words were strange on him, the language of the pagoda and the examination hall, spoken in a coal-smoke shop by a man with iron dust in his beard. "Ten years, the book calls it. Tenacity."

Heat ran down his arms. The muscle stood up under his sleeves, and his hands grew — not hugely, just enough, the way a hammer that always fit suddenly fits better. He set his grip on the strand again, breathed out once, slowly, and pulled with everything he had ever been.

The strand straightened, and straightened, and went tight as a bowstring. In Wulin's palm the veins burned faint gold. And the grass — the small one standing quiet in the boy's soul-space, watching this day the way it watched everything, from under the floor of the boy — felt the whole strength of a trained man run along its blade. It understood, in its slow, seasonal way, that it was being weighed.

The blade did not part.

Mang Tian let go. He stepped back, flexed his hands twice, and looked at them as if they belonged to someone who had just been told a hard piece of news.

"I tried," he said at last, plainly. "That's the whole of it, boy. I tried a second time with the skill that makes my grip worth two of any other man's, and I did not break it. So hear it once, in the plainest words I own: I could not break it."

He said it without heat. He said it the way a smith reports that a rivet did not fail: because the report is useful, and the report has to be true.

"Yes, sir," Wulin said, and then, because a question had been standing in him since the first ring came off, "You're a soul master."

"Four rings," Mang Tian said. "Registered and rated and filed, which buys me nothing at the anvil. Holds my grip longer when the work is stiff — that's the honest sum of thirty years of it, and I've had no complaints."

"You never said."

"You never needed a teacher with rings," the master said. He picked his mug back up and drank, then added, in a tone that had a dry edge under it: "And the lane does enough bowing already to the people who wear their strength on their sleeves. I'd rather my boys bow to the work."

Wulin looked down at his own arms, where the faint gold was already settling back under the skin like a tide going out. Then his teacher, who did not know it, had been the third person alive to weigh that quiet inheritance — the first two being the pair of them who lived in it. Wulin tucked the blade away, and the grass stood out of sight in the young soul-space it now held, and filed the day's entry in its own wordless ledger: a man had spent the whole of his trained strength on one blade of grass and failed cleanly, and had then told the boy exactly, and had counted change.

---

"Now we fix the door," Mang Tian said.

He went to the locked drawer under the bench — a drawer Wulin had dusted around for three years and never once seen opened — and from it he took a wrap of oiled cloth. Inside the cloth was a single bar of metal: heavy, dead-grey, half the length of the boy's forearm and worth, Wulin guessed from the way it was carried, more than the family's month.

"Heavy silver," Mang Tian said. "I bought this the year you were born, against a day I couldn't name. It has sat here since. That's fifty days of your wages sitting in my hand, so you'll treat it like a grandmother."

"It's for the work," Wulin said, understanding already, with the front part of his mind, what the back part of his mind had been circling all afternoon.

"You have the strength for a grown man's work and the hands of an apprentice," his teacher said. "So we'll teach the hands the grown method. It's called thousand refinement. One thousand strikes on a single piece. Not one thousand blows, and not a lad hammering a fence-post — one thousand strikes with your soul power riding down the handle into the metal with every hit, holding every part of yourself in the iron while you work it, following the metal's grain the way you follow a good conversation. You aren't shaping a hot blank. You are going through a piece of metal with a fine comb, a thousand times, until what's left is what was always inside it."

"How long does it take?" Wulin asked.

"That depends on the smith," Mang Tian said. "An old master I apprenticed under did one in five hours. Most graders stop at the door of it and never walk through. Some men work at it for years, and one morning the metal turns around under their hands like a fish that has finally decided to be caught, and they'll swear their whole arm lit up. They call that the moment the metal answers." He laid the bar on the bench and set a cloth over it, smoothing the cloth flat with one pass of his palm, which was the most ceremony he had spent on anything since the day he first handed a six-year-old two small hammers and told him to strike a lump a thousand times.

"You'll start tomorrow, early. School first, then the forge; you'll sleep here on the long days if we run late, and I'll square it with your mother. Half wage while you're learning the method and full rate the day the work comes out clean. The spoiled iron on your hook comes off your pay like you said, and you'll melt it down for practice by your own hand, because a boy who wastes metal learns the price from the other end. Failure is allowed. Quitting is not. Those are the terms."

"And if it takes a year?"

"Then it takes a year," Mang Tian said equably, "and you'll be a year better. It's a good thing to want, boy. Want it struck, not wished."

They banked the forge together. The last thing the boy did before going home was look once at the bench where the heavy silver lay under its cloth, quiet as a sleeping animal, and at the count-screen on the wall behind it, cloudy and watchful with its one old thousand in it. He had the feeling — the specific feeling of finishing a page rather than a day — that a door he had been born too early for had just been opened by the one man in the city who had never once been impressed by anything he could not hit with a hammer.

---

He heard the trouble before he saw it.

The lane behind the dye-works cut a shortcut home when the forge ran late, and the dye-works never slept, so the lane was never dark and never quite empty. Four boys had a fifth pressed back against the drying frames — a small girl, silver-haired, one shoulder of her coat torn, standing absolutely still. She was not crying. She was not even pulling away. The biggest boy had her by the arm and was shaking a packet of something in her face — sweets, coins, Wulin couldn't tell and never found out; it didn't matter, then or later, because it was never about the packet.

Wulin walked into the middle of it the way his father walked into a dispute about a bill: without hurry and without stopping short.

"Hello," he said to the big one, in the tone of a person who has already seen the end of the conversation. "Whose is that?"

"Not yours," the boy said.

"No," Wulin agreed. "She's not anyone's. That's the thing you've got wrong. Let's fix it cheap." He held up his own empty hands, shoulders level, no ring, no grass; a slight, working-class nine-year-old in a soot-stained coat, standing between a stranger and four boys who were trying very hard to look like a crowd. "You want to be a story in this district? You'll be the boys who did a thing on Dye Lane and got written down for it. You want to be nobody? Let go and go home. The clerk at the grain stand knows my name now. It'll be known for a while. Which of those two do you want more?"

The big one pushed him. He took it on the shoulder — his grown hands stayed at his sides, which cost him something; his mother had questions about sleeve-height, and none of the answers were fists. He did not fall down, and the boy who had pushed him discovered the arithmetic of shoving a smith's apprentice, whose bag of bones was heavier than it looked. The grip on the girl's arm loosened a finger's width.

The girl moved then — quick, cold, precise. She turned inside the guard, took her own coat back from the frame where it was pinned, and stepped out of the circle to stand behind Wulin, all of it in the time a cat takes to be somewhere else. The four boys looked at each other, weighing the new awkwardness, and did what boys do when a scene has stopped paying: they swore, and they went.

Wulin turned around. Up close, she was small; smaller than him by a year or two, with short silver hair and pale amethyst eyes, and a face that gave no truth away for free. She had been in the district a few days, he learned later; no family had come asking; that was the whole of her situation, and the whole of it was enough to end the day's ordinary shape.

"What's your name?" he asked.

"Na'er," she said, and it was the first word she had chosen to spend on the evening, and she spent it carefully.

"I'm Wulin." He looked down the lane, at the wet street and the lamps going on, and then at her, doing the sums a boy of his house does before he has been taught the words for them. One more bowl. One more blanket. A roof that already leaked in two places. An extra pair of hands in a shop-house where nobody sat still anyway. "Do you have somewhere to be?"

"No," Na'er said.

"Then come and eat," said Wulin, "and we'll figure out the rest while the soup's hot."

---

The rest took the whole evening, because it had to go through the house.

Lang Yue listened to the story with her hands still in the dishwater, which was how she listened to everything that might cost money. Tang Ziran listened with his elbows on the table, and asked four questions, none of them about rent: whether the girl was hurt, whether anyone was looking for her, whether the boys would be a nuisance, and what the girl herself wanted. Na'er sat on the bench with her hands around a bowl she had not yet begun to eat, answering what she could and staying silent over what she couldn't. Then Tang Ziran asked her straight out — gently, the way a man asks a question he intends to stand behind — whether she would like to stay with them, on her own account and not because anyone had decided it for her. She looked around the small kitchen at the three of them, and the answer she gave was the same as the word she had spent in the lane. It was one word.

"Yes."

"You'll take the truckle bed and the corner of my room," Wulin said, already moving, because boys of this neighborhood do not get to be careful with happiness; they only get to make room for it. "It's got the good window. You'll hear the forge bells if the wind's east, but only for a minute, and I'll show you where the water butt is so you don't have to ask twice."

"It's a good window," Na'er agreed, gravely, as if the window had been part of the negotiations and she did not want to seem ungrateful.

That was the joke that broke the evening open. Lang Yue laughed at last — the short, surprised laugh of a woman who had just discovered she was rising to something. She got up and began to make up the bed, and Tang Ziran quietly pulled a second share of everything out of the pantry and put it on the table without announcing it. Wulin carried Na'er's one small bundle of clothes upstairs and laid it on the truckle bed, then came back down and sat with the family.

He looked at his own hands on the table then, with the faint gold gone quiet again under the skin, the hands that had read five hundred and seven and five hundred and sixty-one on the old frame that morning.

They had grown big enough to break a bar of iron. They had not been able to do the small thing sitting in front of them — carry somebody safely to a warm place — until today, and today they had. The day went quiet around that fact the way a shop goes quiet when a long piece of work finally sets.

---

Late that night, the grass kept its own ledger.

It lay in the boy's soul-space, and the boy slept, both hands loose at his sides, the ring turning its slow small sun beneath the skin of the right wrist, and across the room the new small breathing moved up and down in the dark, not like a pattern it knew. It had counted boys before, in its hundred forest years, but never this close, and never this one's own house.

It filed the day in the only way it had, which was not numbers but weather. A morning of wrong strength and ruined iron; a cold gauge that had asked the boy for everything and written down all of it. A trained man's whole weight spent on one blade and returned as a straight green line; a door named for the morning, with a bar of grey metal asleep under a cloth on the other side of it. And now, a second small heartbeat in the room, silver-haired and careful with its words, no larger a thing of the world than a bird on a windowsill, and about as easy to scare off.

The grass reached a root-hair to the edge of the bond, the way a farmer moves down a fence line, checking posts. The boy's lines were quiet. The ring turned. Tomorrow held a thousand strikes, and tomorrow after that another thousand, and the boy was still nine, and the method was coming to him as slowly and as surely as new wood comes to an old house.

It had waited ninety-nine years for weather worth standing in. It could wait for one boy's hands to learn what had been put into them.

It could wait.

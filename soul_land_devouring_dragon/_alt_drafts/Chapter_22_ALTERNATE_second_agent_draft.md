# Chapter 22: The Deep Forest Proper

He came down off the pass's north lip into trees that had no end to them.

The first mile was the wet stone and thin wood he had crossed into at gray light and eaten in. Then the ground went soft underfoot and the trees closed overhead, and after that there was no sky to be had anywhere. The light came down in slow yellow shafts through layers of leaf and branch, and under him the mold was so deep his feet sank into it past the ankle.

He stopped and stood a long time and let the new country come to him. What came first was the age of the place. The trunks around him were old past any counting. The bark was cracked and split like rock in a mountain, and the roots rose out of the ground higher than his shoulder and ran away into the dark. The whole smell of the place was leaves going back into leaves, season on season, for longer than the country he had left had been country at all.

What did not come was the running.

In the low country his weight went down through the ground ahead of him and everything that could go, went. He had earned that, year on year, and he had used it. Here, at the first drinking water he found, a blunt heavy kind of beast stood in the shallows feeding on the water plants no more than thirty paces off, and went on feeding. It raised its head once, looked at him with flat old eyes, and went back down to the plants. Above him the birds called from the crowns the way birds had called in his own country before he was born. The small brown kinds went about their work in the leaf-mold as though he were weather.

He slept that first night in the open, in the mold beside a root, with no hollow and no cover, because he wanted to be shown what the country would do about him. He woke whole to a gray light coming down in the same slow shafts, and lay there taking the measure of the place. This country had never learned him. It did not know it was supposed to be afraid.

---

He walked north for days and read as he went, and the reading took him a long time, because the deep forest proper wrote in a hand he had never had to learn.

There were no trails in it. In all his own country there had been something — the men's trail grassing over, the runs at the dry, the banks the grazers used, the prints of everything that had ever crossed. Here the ground went away under the trunks for mile after mile and showed nothing but leaf-fall and old wear, as if no creature had ever needed to go anywhere twice.

The men were not in it, either. He turned that over for a while before he trusted it. No stumps, no cut wood, no ash on any wind, no smell of iron or smoke in the whole country he walked that first season. The men had come into his border country for years and carried out the years of its beasts, and here, one pass to the south, there was no sign that any of them had ever been. Whatever the deep forest proper was, it had been that thing longer than men had been taking the years of beasts anywhere.

The country's own writing he found in pieces, one piece at a time.

On the third day he came to the high marks: three gouges set side by side in a trunk, above the place where his own standing head reached, old and healed gray, with the bark grown thick and knotted around them. He put his muzzle to them and got nothing — no smell, no fresh edge, no blood in the wood. The tree had taken the cutting in, closed it over, and kept on growing. Something enormous had stood where he was standing and put its claws into that trunk, and the years since then had been many.

On the fifth morning he walked up over a ridge of root and into a line he could not see and could not miss.

It lay across the ground from the root-ridge on his left away into the dark on his right, and it was not prey, and it was not water, and it was not the passing of any beast. It was a smell laid down on purpose, old and thin but steady, the way a thing is set somewhere in order to mean something later. He stood at it and felt the country on the far side of it with all of his sense at once, and the far side was thinner. Fewer small kinds. Less going on. The leaf-mold had not been cut up by feet in a long time.

He did not cross that day. He turned and went back down the way he had come, and he slept that night where the small kinds were.

---

The seams he learned the way he had learned the runs and the rock bars: because he had to eat.

Between the claimed grounds the country had laid itself out in strips. They were old ground under the thickest wood, bare of leaves in places, worn into faint paths by everything that had to go from one part of the forest to another and had no leave to cross the wide dark in between. No marks stood in them anywhere. They ran north the way a lane runs between two standing walls, and once he had found one, he found it again a day's walk north, and again after that, and after that he stopped being lost.

A beast could live in the strips. He did, for most of that first year. He ate small and he ate quick, and he learned where the strips ran wide enough to sleep in, and he learned to keep the wide dark on either side of him like a wall he did not touch. Twice he stood at the edge of a claimed ground with meat in it, close enough to smell it, and turned away and slept hungry. That was the price of a country that had been owned longer than he had been alive, and he paid it without complaint, and because he paid it well he began to believe that he had understood the place.

That belief was the year's whole mistake.

---

It came apart under the wide dark in the second summer, in a stand of giants where the strips ran out.

He had been walking north for days on old barish ground, and the strips had gone thin, and then they had gone into the wide dark and stopped. Nothing but trunks for as far as his sense reached in every direction. The small kinds were thin there too. He had eaten little for four days and the hunger had moved past patience into the thing that takes the reins, and somewhere in the middle of the fifth day he came to a line in the ground.

It was old. It had almost nothing left to it — a smear of a smell on a root, worn down by weather until it was less than the smell of the wood under it. He read it twice. The first reading said: a line, walked over years ago, let go since; nobody lives here. The second reading said the same thing. His hunger said it louder.

He crossed, and hunted, and killed.

The beast he took was a slow crown-feeder, heavy in the shoulder, graying around the muzzle, and the hunt went the way his hunts go when they go right: he came at it from the uphill side in the middle of its feeding, took it at the base of the neck, and held until it was done. The meat was deep and fat with the summer, and he ate his fill and then more than his fill, and the day's walking had been long and the kill had cost him little, and so he did a thing he had not done in years.

He slept on the kill ground.

He woke in the black middle of the night because the country had gone quiet in a way it had never been quiet around him before.

It happened in a long wave, coming from the north, and he felt it move: the small kinds of a whole country going silent one strip at a time, then the next strip, then the next, until silence had run south past him out of his hearing. The quiet passed over the wide dark and took everything with it. Nothing moved. Nothing called. Even the leaves overhead had nothing to say.

Then the ground spoke.

It was not a sound in the air. It came up through the mold and stood in his chest, a low note with weight behind it, as if the floor of the world had been struck once somewhere far off to the north. It rolled away and was gone, and the country stayed silent for a long time, and then, much later in the night, it came again — the same note, and nearer.

Nothing came. That was the thing he lay there learning, with the kill meat beside him and his belly full: the note was not a hunt and not a challenge, and nothing came, all night, and the being-left was worse than anything that could have walked out of the dark at him. He lay on claimed ground with the meat at his side, and he did not sleep, and would not, and could not.

At gray light he got up off the kill and walked.

---

The ground would not take him for three days.

He went south and west, back the way he had come, and every time he lay down the note was still in the world somewhere, and his body would not let go of him. He slept in pieces, an hour at a time, waking with the mold in his nostrils and his legs already trying to stand. The fourth day he stopped eating and stopped caring that he had stopped. He passed the half-eaten kill again in the dark and did not turn his head for it. He was thin at the ribs by the time he came back to the strips, and when he got there he lay down in the open on the worn ground of a strip and slept the whole of a day and a night.

He woke in the middle afternoon. He drank. He ate a small quick thing and kept it down. And then he did the thing he had done after every other mistake of his life, and went back to read the ground.

He found his own trail first, where he had come out. His line, going south, in the leaf-fall. And over it, alongside it, in it: another.

Something had come down his trail in the night, and it had done what he did. Where he had left steps, it had put its own steps — not over them, but in them, from one end of his line to the other. Where he had not left anything, it had left nothing either. He followed his own line back for the better part of a mile, reading, and it held all the way: his track and the other one walking it like a man walking the same road a day later, patient, unhurried, from the deep dark down to the place where he had slept.

At the sleeping place the steps came in close. Within two paces of the hollow where he had lain with his legs drawn under him, the ground was pressed, evenly, in a patch the length of a big beast, as though something large had stood there a while. Then the steps went the other way, off south, and the leaf-fall kept them for a hundred paces more and let them go.

There were no prints anywhere. Nothing broken, nothing turned, no claw, no pad, no weight driven into the ground at all. Only the leaves pressed, in the shape of feet, the way leaves are left when a great weight has been on them and lifted off.

He stood in his own sleeping hollow and understood the whole of it, and the understanding went through him cold from the jaw to the tail. He had walked across that country leaving almost nothing, and something bigger had walked across him leaving nothing at all. His own trick, done to him, and done better. And it had not hunted him. It had read his line the way he read a line, come to the end of it, stood over the beast at the end of it — and then gone away, because there was nothing in him worth the trouble of a print.

---

He kept the lessons of that summer the only way he kept anything: in the body, where they stayed.

Read every line before the hunger runs out. A line that is old is not a line that is dead. A full belly is a reason to move, not a reason to stop. And the wide dark between the strips is not empty — it is the country of things that do not need to show themselves to own a road.

The rest of the year he walked north in the strips and got fat again and slept light, and by the falling season the ground stopped carrying the note of that one bad night and he stopped waking to look for it.

---

The pull did not change. It had been under everything since the first year of the road, and a country that owned him as easily as that country did could not touch it: it ran north, and he went north with it, walking the seams it wanted him to walk.

The deep forest proper was bigger than he had had any way of knowing. He walked it a season and a season and a season and the trunks went on. At the end of the third summer he found a root-ridge high enough to stand above the leaf-mold and look, and the crowns went away from him to the end of seeing, rank on rank, with no break in them anywhere.

He was not the first of his kind to walk under those trees. On trunk after trunk as he went north, high above the reach of his standing head, he found the old gray gouges — three to a trunk, sometimes four, always the same size, always healed over, spread across a summer of walking as though something had gone this way once and written its going into the wood. The trees had kept those cuts a long time, and the beast that made them was nowhere, and there was no word for it anywhere in the country.

He read them each time he passed one, and got nothing out of them, and went on.

North, the strips ran into older ground still, where the trunks stood wider and the marks stood thicker, and the small kinds grew careful, and even the light came down slower. He walked in, one day at a time, reading at every step now and never at nothing. He was inside the deep forest proper, and it had let him in, and it had let him pay for it, and it had not yet once, in three years, shown him a single thing in the whole of itself that was afraid.

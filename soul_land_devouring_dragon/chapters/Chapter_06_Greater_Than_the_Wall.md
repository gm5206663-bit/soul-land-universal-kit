# Chapter 6: Greater Than the Wall

The second winter was not the first winter, and that was the whole of it.

The first winter had been a thing that fell on him and nearly killed him. The second winter he walked into like a den: he knew how the cold came up out of the ground before the sky showed any sign of it. He knew that the stone-runners wintered in the same cracks every year and how deep they went; he knew which side of a ridge held the warm and which held the killing wind. The white came, and he sat in the middle of it fed, and the white did not sit on him as it had before.

He was stronger than the season now. That was what mastery looked like. Nothing had to be paid, because everything had been paid.

And under the mastery, quiet and steady, sat the thing that would not sit down: the hunger that had no meal.

The country was still too small. It fed his body and it did nothing for the thing that grew as he grew, and it was growing again. He could feel it as a body feels a fever coming. The meals filled him and the sleeps restored him and nothing changed the shape at the bottom of him, which was saying more, greater, up.

The wall was nearer than it had been in the autumn. He could not see it any more than he had before. But he had begun to live beside it.

---

The long storm came in the second month of the white, and it was the worst weather of his life.

It lasted longer than a storm should last. Snow came sideways for days and the wind screamed through the high ground without stopping, and he lay in his rock-shelter with his body wrapped in its own heat and his whole world narrowed to the small dark space under the stone. He ate the last of what he had stored, and then the storm went on, and he did not hunt, and then he began fasting whether he wished to or not.

The fasting did something to him. That was the door.

On the fourth day of it he lay in the dark with the wind roaring and his belly gone quiet and cold, and the hunger in him turned over like a beast changing its position. It stopped saying eat. It started saying down.

Down was not the ground. Down was past it.

He lay in the rock's black inside and felt his own self reaching down — under his body, under the stone, under the mountain. The reaching was the new thing, the thing the pull had never done. It went out of him in a slow wide wash, like mist going out over a valley at dawn. The mountain's own old faintness was there. Stone and root and the deep places where the earth's own slow life sat banked and enormous and patient and had never once been looked at by anything — and the pull drank from it.

The mountain had lived for a long time. It was not alive as a beast is alive. But there was something in it that could be fed on, as thin as the air at the top of a peak and as old as the ground, and the new part of him found it and drank, and that was the crossing.

He did not know that yet. He only lay in the dark and felt it work in him, and the work went on for days.

What the body did in that time, no beast of his kind could have watched without shivering, because the body was not the body of a beast any more. It was a workshop. The layers of his back — the hide, the scales, the deep armor he had been growing for three years — had become too thin to hold what he was becoming, and the storm had starved him past arguing, and the change did not wait and did not care. It slowed itself. Somewhere in the singing distance, the thing in him that had been working since the egg took the change in hand as a smith takes iron off a fire. Too hot for the metal, so hold it. Draw it back, feed the layers first, let the layers thicken to what the becoming will need, and then let it through.

The crossing was not a moment. It was a season inside a storm.

He came out of the shelter at the end of it into a world so changed that he stood in the doorway looking at it as if he had opened the wrong eye.

The snow lay exactly as it had lain. Nothing about the mountain had moved. And he was greater than the wall.

---

He knew it in his feet first.

He took three steps out into the deep snow and stood still, because his feet had walked through it differently. He had walked the white for a season as a heavy thing that sank and had to lift and sink again; the economy of it had been written into his legs since the first winter. Now the legs answered a different question. His weight found the crust and stayed on it. He went up the slope above his shelter and came down again, and the going used half of him, and the other half was free.

The rest came in over the following days, one piece at a time.

He heard the country better. He saw the white with a new sharpness at the edges of things, where snow meets stone and stone meets water. And in the deep still hours he felt the far away as he had never felt it: the whole white country went up the scale of his sense like a page held to a fire, and he could read it clear out to the black ridge and the ravine country beyond it.

The thing in the ravine read as never before.

He lay on the warm rock on the third clear evening and let the reading go north, and the weight of the Black-Ravine Serpent came back at him complete. Not just the depth of it now. Years. He could feel years on the weight, actual years, an enormous stacked weight of them, and he could feel the appetite in it — an appetite the size of a country — and under both of them, thin and clear, singing quietly, the thread that ran from that blood to his own.

He lay there with the whole of it in him and for the first time in his life he did not go flat under the weight. He lay full length in the light of it. The roof of the world was still the roof of the world, but he could stand under it now and read it, and reading it did not crush him.

Then he got up and tried the voice.

It came up out of him in the evening cold with no warning and no plan, and it was not a cry. It was a roar, and the roar carried something in it that no mouth of his had ever made: the weight of his blood. It rolled out over the white country and broke against the far ridge and rolled back, and the snow on the rocks above him let go in small falls, and under the snow, in the buried cracks and the wintering hollows, everything that was alive went flat and still.

He held it as long as the breath held him, and then his voice cracked and gave out and he stood with his head down, chest heaving, tasting blood at the back of his throat. His whole body shook with the spending of it.

It did not come when he called for it. He tried twice more that month and got nothing but a thin flat hiss. The voice was not a thing he owned yet. But it was a thing he had done, and in the doing of it the whole white country had learned something it had not known before: there was a new weight in the mountains.

---

The sense came the same season, on a day of thin bright cold, and it changed the shape of the world more quietly than the voice had.

He was hunting in the low white below the ridge, deep in the still hours of a windless afternoon, when he picked up the line of a hare-kind in the snow. And he knew, before he had seen the hare or smelled it, more than one thing about it: it was young, and it was fast, and the life in it was thin and bright and barely any weight at all.

He lay in the snow with that knowledge sitting in him, and then he deliberately read the next thing his nose found — a fox-kind den under a snowbank — and it read the same way: young, bright, thin, quick. And then he turned his own reading back on his own body, in the middle of the cold morning, and felt himself.

Heavy. Slow. A deep, banked, mountain-big weight of years, stacked up inside his own chest.

He was reading years. The country had stopped being a map of smells and become a map of ages. He could feel the young of the country, all the thin bright quick lives, and the old ones, heavy and slow, and far away, burning low and deep, the ancient things that had been alive before the first tree of his valley came up.

The hunger that had no meal took that in like a long drink.

This was what his kind had been born to hunt with. Not the nose, and not the eyes: this. Everything that lived was a number of years in a body, and he could read the number as he read the wind. The ones that mattered — the heavy, oldest, deepest years, the years that answered the hunger — stood up out of the country like fires on a dark hillside.

He could read the country like a larder now, and the larder was organized by the one thing he wanted.

---

The outcast came for him at the end of the season, and it came at the worst of times, and it went worse for the outcast.

He was crossing the long white of the high break in the grey of the evening, going home fat from a meal, when the snow ahead of him burst and the thing was in the air.

It was a lone beast, a long-fanged kind, grey and scarred and thin from the winter, and it had been hunting him for days. He understood that in the half heartbeat he had to understand anything. It had picked the ground, and the ground it had picked was the place where the winter wind cut across the open slope — the one place on the whole break where a beast's nose was blind.

The fangs went for his throat.

The bite did not break him. That was the new thing, and the beast learned it in the same moment he did. It came down on his shoulder layered in the deep armor the crossing had finished, and its teeth skated and then closed and stuck shallow, and the shock of it went through the outcast's whole body and up into its eyes.

Then his jaws had it.

It fought as cornered things fight — teeth and claws and a lifetime of taking other beasts apart — and he took everything it had, and the red thing came up in him at the taste of blood and stood in his chest saying tear, tear the whole of it apart.

He put it under.

That was the second proof of the winter. The first had been the wall; this was the red thing, risen with all its strength against a beast that had come to eat him, and his choosing had been faster than it was. He killed the long-fang cleanly, held to the jaw-work, ate what he needed, and left the rest under the snow for the ravens, standing over the kill in the falling dark with the wind in the new crown of scales along his skull and the deep weight steady in his chest.

The white country read him differently after that. In the days that followed, the under-snow lives went deeper and farther out from his trails, and the foxes gave him ground, and the hare-kinds went flat in the open, and the country made a new space around him everywhere he walked.

He walked down to the deep pass at the end of that month and stood above the trail, at the place where the trap of the takers stood buried under the winter's snow.

The line and the posts were under the white, the bait long gone. The death waited, patient, as the men who set it waited, down-country behind their walls.

He stood above that buried trap in the falling snow and felt, clear as the thread to the ravine, the whole of what had changed in him.

The years he had crossed were his. He had taken them out of his own bones and his own fasting and his own storm, and no upright thing was ever going to carry them off with a line and two posts. If the takers wanted the years of him, they would have to come and take them from the whole of him, awake, in the open.

He was not the biggest thing on the mountain. He was not the oldest, or the sharpest, or the most patient.

But he had been a hatchling on a slope three winters ago, and the wall had stood over him all autumn like weather, and he had broken it open in a rock-shelter in a storm, and the mountain had not even noticed. The mountain would not notice for a long time yet.

He turned away from the pass and went up toward the high country with the snow coming down, and behind him his tracks — prints wider than any beast's in that country — filled with white and disappeared.

---

Two men came up the pass on the first mild day of the year, and the line they had left in the autumn was buried.

The younger one dug the post out with his hands and then with the hatchet, and pulled the wire up out of the drift, and found it slack and whole and empty.

"Bait's gone," he said. "Line's whole. No hair on it, no print within three lengths of the trail, nothing walked through."

"Nothing walked through it," said the older one. He stood with his hands on his hips and looked at the trail as a man looks at a door he has locked. "Then where did the bait go?"

"Birds."

"Birds," the old man said. He took the boy's shoulders and turned him to face the open ground under the wire, and the boy stood under his hands and looked at the unbroken snow for a while and then stopped saying anything at all.

"Write it in the book," said the old man.

"What do I write?"

"Line found whole, bait gone, ground under it unmarked, no beast named." The old man pulled the wire up out of the last of the drift and coiled it. "And the pass gave nothing all winter."

"Two winters on this trail," said the boy.

"Did anyone walk it from the other side?"

"Nobody walks the pass in the white," said the old man. "That is why we set a line up here and not down at the water. A trail that is used is a trail. This one is not used from the first snow to the thaw."

"Two winters, and hares and deer-echo for it, and nothing better." The old man hung the coil on the post and stood with his hand on it. "We give the pass one more winter and then the whole line moves south, where the market is. The lodge cuts our winter count either way, so there is no sense feeding a gate that opens onto nothing."

"Next winter we work the water line instead," said the boy.

"Next winter we work the water line, three days south of here, where the lodge is and the market is."

"Then why keep the pass at all?"

"Because the day the pass is the only open way through, the man who holds it says who walks," said the old man. "That is worth more than lights. That is why the lodge still pays for the wire."

"There was the light off the water, two winters back."

"There was," said the old man. "And the man who took it is dead in a village fight, and the light is somewhere in the low country in another man's chest, and I am standing in snow arguing with a boy about birds."

"What was it, then?"

"Ask the snow," said the old man. "I am done asking."

They went down the pass together in the mild afternoon with the wire over the boy's shoulder, and the hound walked ahead of them and read the trail, and found nothing, in every direction, the whole descent.

Behind them the snow stood deep over the trail, unbroken, printless, and holding everything it knew.

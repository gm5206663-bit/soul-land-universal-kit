# Chapter 11: The War of the Lists

He came down out of the high scree on the first still morning of the thaw, thin from the cold months and hungry in the honest way, and the ground he came down through was already waking. That was the rule of the winters he had lived — three of them now. The ground woke first. The snow on the low slopes went soft, then rotten. Water ran under it in the dark, and the moss under the water came green before the air believed in spring, and everything that ate and was eaten woke with it.

He went to the high break first, because the broad-antler wintered on it, and because a beast that has wintered well is a beast that has not yet learned to be careful.

The run was empty. He knew it before he reached the standing stone — no beast-smell on the path, no new rub on the stone where the antlers had worn it pale for years. And under the rot of the thaw, the other smell.

Blood. Days old. With man-smell laid over it.

He read the ground from the outside in, slow. Boots. Two sets of them, walking. Poles dragged. The beast had gone down in the moss at the mouth of the run and had not gone far. The blood was spread wide and dark, and the flies had found it and gone, and where the head had lain there was a shallow pressed place in the new green.

Above that place the ground held a color. He had seen a light stand in the air when a beast died. He had never seen a light sink into the ground and stay there a while — but this one had stayed: pale gold, thin, gone down into the earth like water into sand, and what was left was the color sitting in the moss. A hundred-year light. Not the deep light. The low fat of a winter, paid to other hands.

He had marked that beast at the turn of the year, and for one breath the grey stag's ground came back to him whole: the body opened in the snow at the second water, the two kneeling prints filling slowly with snow, the ring of deep purple carried off in their hands. The same country. The same season's work.

He stood a long time over the pale color in the moss, and did not move until the flies had gone by him twice. Then he did what he did with everything. He wrote it down.

He carried his list in his blood, not on paper. A line taken went into him and stayed, flat and hard, like a scar on the inside. Two lines now. The grey stag of the second water, taken at the turn of the year. And the broad-antler, taken in the same country, in the same season, at the hour he had kept for it.

He walked the ground around the kill twice more and read the rest of it. No second kill. No wasted meat. They had eaten what they wanted and left the rest for the ravens. They had not hurried. And they had come to this run at the hour the beast came to it, on a path no man-shaped thing had walked all winter.

He sat on his haunches in the new green and let it settle, and it settled into one plain thing: they read the ground. They read it as he read it — patiently, in signs — and from the signs they knew where a beast would be and when. They had read the broad-antler's habit and taken him on the strip of moss where he had eaten for years, without noise and without waste, and then they had gone.

And below, where the smoke-line had stood over the winter place all winter, there was no smoke any more. The winter place had been struck at the thaw, taken apart and carried down-country, and the going had a shape he had learned to read in the cold months: they had gone to work for the season, and work does not go home.

His list had two lines taken by other hands. Their list had lines on it he had never seen. The two lists sat over the same country, and the country was the thing they were both spending.

He made the first decision of the summer before he left the moss. He would not wait. Their patience was longer than his patience, and their hands never tired. He would take his own line first, in the days when their line was deep and far — and the line he chose was the thick-paw of the summer run.

He knew the thick-paw by its tracks: the wide paw-print in the soft ground, one blunt toe dragging, the bushes broken at the height of a tall beast's chest. It was slow and heavy and bad at running, it had fed well in the hot springs since the melt, and it fed on slopes a man could walk. A line that read the ground could read the thick-paw too. It was on both lists already. It only had to be taken.

---

For nine days he kept to the high ground and let the hunger work. Then he found what he had been waiting for: fresh sign of the hunting line, moving down-country of the giants on the long march, two days of walking away from the summer run.

He went after the thick-paw that same evening.

He found it in the last of the light, rooting in the fern at the edge of the run, and he did not go at it at once. He stilled himself and let the country settle. The hunt was a long slow thing done in short hard pieces — walk, stop, read, wait, and then the one piece that is all of it — and he had learned the pieces in three winters of the work.

The thick-paw went down the run at first light, as it did every morning. It was a heavy thing, all shoulder and neck, the head low and the tusks dirty from the rooting feed, and it moved with a rolling confidence that came from never having been chased by anything faster than itself.

He was faster. He let it take the length of the run ahead of him, because the run was cut with old gullies and thorn, and a beast that runs on bad ground breaks its own legs. He stayed high on the slope beside it, where the ground was clean and his own feet were sure, and he ran, and he shut everything else out.

The summer was thick and loud with spring, and it did not want him moving through it at speed. Twice the fern closed over the run and he lost the shape of the beast and had to take the high line again. Once a pair of low lives — some small pale thing and its young — turned their heads as he passed above them, and he went down into the fern at once and held the quiet until the two heads turned back and forgot him. The quiet cost him time. Time cost him ground. When he came up again, the thick-paw had gained the run's far bend — and had stopped running.

It had turned. It stood in the open with its head down and its tusks up and its weight set back on its haunches, because that was what the thick-paw did. It did not run well. It fought well.

He came at it low, from the side of the run where the bank was cut and the beast could not swing its tusks without losing its feet, and the short hard piece began.

The thick-paw came off the bank in a short grunting rush and caught him across the ribs with its shoulder and drove him into the fern. The breath went out of him and the world went white for a moment. He rolled with the blow, not against it — that is the whole of the art — and came up on his feet with the ground still swinging.

It came again, and he was ready for the shape of it. He took the second rush into his own shoulder and gave it ground, and the tusks grated along the hard plates under his hide and did not find the soft place. That was the whole of the second exchange: it had spent its weight, and he had spent only ground.

Then his head was past the tusks, and his jaw had the thick-paw behind the ear.

The hold went in. The thick-paw screamed and tore backwards, dragging him through the fern and the thorn, rolling, all its weight and fear up at once — and he held. The jaw's work was quiet work: a slow deeper set, muscle and bone, no hurry. The beast thrashed a long time, and then it was still.

He did not let go for a while after it was still. He had learned that too.

Then the two red things stood up over the meat. They always came at a kill, and he always felt both. The first was the boil: the bloodthirst of his kind, the old rampage that wants the meat taken whole and the body torn and the ground washed red — the thing that has killed more of his kind than any hunter. The second was quieter and worse in its way: the hunger, the red pull that had made him mark beasts and spend nights over them and lose the grey stag to his own delay. The boil and the hunger met over the thick-paw's body, and he stood over the meat with his jaw still holding and put the choosing between them, and the choosing said what it always said: the boil is yours and the hunger is yours, and neither of them is you.

He ate. The meal was good meat, and the deep sleep did not take him, because he did not let it. When the meat was down and the body was left, he used the oldest trick of the deep forest. He put his own scent down in the ground all around the place, a wide ring of it, and the ring said one plain thing to the country: a hunter holds this ground. The heavy kinds of the run would find the ring and move off it, and other beasts would keep away from him while the meat was on him. That was what the sign was for. It had always worked, because nothing in the forest reads a sign wrong.

He stood at the edge of the summer run in the last of the light. His sign stood in the ground behind him. His list was shorter by one line, and theirs the same. And he understood, for the first time, that his own trick was a thing said out loud in a country that now had listeners who could read.

---

At the winter place, the fire was out, and the poles were down, and the two of them were packing the last of it onto a back-frame while Old Qin walked the ground at the edge of the camp. He walked it as he walked every ground, with his hands behind him and his eyes on the moss, and at the far edge he stopped and called, and the two of them left the packing and came.

"Same ground," said Gao Tie. He crouched and looked at the print in the moss without touching it. "Same shape as the deep place. Same as the water, two years back. Day old, no more. Whatever it is, it ate here, and it walked off south."

"South is the water line," said Xiao An.

"South is the water line," Gao Tie said.

"A thing that signs and does not show itself," said Xiao An. She was younger than the other two and she had learned to read the ground fast, and she read it again now, the print in the moss and the drag of the kill beside it. "It takes what we take. It takes it a day after we set it out, every time, and it leaves its own sign in the ground after. Old Qin. It is not reading us. It does not know us at all. It never comes near the fires. But it knows the country, and it knows where a beast will stand, the same way we know."

"It leaves signs," said Old Qin. "Then it is a line on the list." He took the list out of his coat and set it flat on his knee, and took out the brush and ink from the small case, and wrote, and the writing was slow and careful, because the list was the work.

"One line," he said, when he had done it. "No name. The beast only, and the ground, and the season. A thing that signs and does not show."

"The old hunters of the north border say the deep forest has its own keepers," said Gao Tie. He said it for the record and not as a belief, the way a man sets down a story he has no use for.

"Then write it under what we know, and not under what we guess," said Old Qin. "What we have is sign, and a bank. Sign goes on the list."

"And what does the list do with it?" said Xiao An.

"When it is seen, the list goes to it," said Old Qin. He looked back down the country at the low breaks, and then at the two of them, and his voice had no heat in it, because the list never needed heat. "The stag's light is in my coat. It goes down the road with the rest of them; the tables buy depth for frames that have grown to hold it, and we buy the season's bread with the price. That is the work. That is all the work. But the beast is a line on the list now, and a line does not sit on the list forever. Nothing does."

He folded the list and put it away. It had the sound of a thing being decided.

"The old bull of the hot springs," he said. "His years stand low and deep, and the deep light is worth a year of the season's bread. We keep him on the list for the season, not for an hour. We take at the water's edge, where the ground is soft and the beast is slow. And if the beast comes while we are at it —" he looked at the two of them, one after the other, "— we pull back. The list does not buy a season of the border with the whole of a hunting line."

"And the hound?" said Gao Tie, because the hound had wintered badly and had come out of the cold thinner than it went in.

"The hound goes," said Old Qin. "The hound always goes."

"Then we walk the water line first, and the breaks after," said Gao Tie. "Same as the winter."

"Same as the winter," said Old Qin. "And Xiao An walks the tracks. She reads them faster than the hound does, and the hound does not argue."

"The hound never argues," said Xiao An.

---

He learned about the old bull from a hillside, without wanting to. It was the second week of the summer when he came down off the giants' shoulder and found the hot springs full of the heavy kinds, all of them fat and slow with the new feed. And one of them was different. The bull stood alone on a rise above the water, gone dark with the years, and when he read it at a distance his sense did not close on anything. The years of it did not read down into the ground as the old stag-kind's had. They stood low and deep and went down out of reach, like a shaft into a mountain, and what came back up was a weight.

He sat on the hillside a long time with his sense low and small in him. The thick-paw had been meat. This was a line — the deepest one the summer had to give — and it stood in his own reading as though it had been standing there since the melt, waiting to be seen.

He marked it that evening. He set his sign in the ground above the water where the old beast came to drink, and went back up the shoulder, and he did not take it that day, because the deep line is a season's work and not a day's. Behind him, down the long low country, two fires stood in the country of the giants.

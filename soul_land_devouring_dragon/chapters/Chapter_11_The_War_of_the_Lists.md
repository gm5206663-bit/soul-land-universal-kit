# Chapter 11: The War of the Lists

The thaw came up out of the ground. Snow on the low slopes went soft, then rotten. Water ran under it in the dark, and the moss under the water came green before the air believed in spring. He had lived three winters by then and he knew the rule of them: the ground woke first, and everything that ate and was eaten woke with it.

He came down out of the high scree on the first still morning, thin from the cold months and hungry in the honest way. He went to the high break first, because the broad-antler wintered on it, and because a beast that has wintered well is a beast that has not yet learned to be careful.

The run was empty. He knew it before he reached the standing stone. No beast-smell on the path. No new rub on the stone where the antlers had worn it pale for years. And under the rot of the thaw, the other smell.

Blood. Days old. With man-smell laid over it.

He read the ground from the outside in, slow. Boots. Two sets of them, walking. Poles dragged. The beast had gone down in the moss at the mouth of the run and had not gone far. The blood was spread wide and dark, and the flies had found it and gone, and where the head had lain there was a shallow pressed place in the new green.

Above that place the ground held a color. He had seen a light stand in the air when a beast died. He had never seen a light sink into ground and stay there a while. But it had stayed: pale gold, thin, gone down into the earth like water into sand, and what was left was the color sitting in the moss.

A hundred-year light. Not the deep light. The low fat of a winter, paid to other hands.

He had marked that beast at the turn of the year. It was the second line of his list, and he had kept it in the deep place through the snow, with the patience he had paid for in blood, and this was what the patience had bought him: the ground, the color, and the man-smell.

He stood a long time in the cold green. Then he did what he did with everything. He wrote it down.

He carried his list in his blood, not on paper. A line taken went into the deep place and stayed there, flat and hard, like a scar on the inside. Two lines now. The grey stag of the second water, taken at the turn of the year with its ring of deep purple carried off in their hands. And now the broad-antler, taken in the same country, in the same season, at the hour he had kept for it.

He walked the ground around the kill twice more and read the rest of it. No second kill. No wasted meat. They had eaten what they wanted and left the rest for the ravens. They had not hurried. And they had come to this run at the hour the beast came to it, on a path no man-shaped thing had walked all winter.

He sat down on his haunches in the new green and let that settle. They read the ground. That was the thing. They read it as he read it: patiently, in signs, and from the signs they knew where a beast would be and when. They had read the broad-antler's habit and taken him on the strip of moss where he had been eating for years. They had done it without noise and without waste, and then they had gone.

And below the smoke-line, which had stood over the winter place all winter, there was no smoke any more. The winter place had been struck at the thaw. They had taken it apart and carried it down-country, and the going had a shape he had learned to read in the cold months: they had gone to work for the season, and work does not go home.

His list had two lines taken by other hands. Their list had two lines on it that he had never seen. The two lists sat over the same country, and the country was the thing they were both spending.

He made the first decision of the summer before he left the moss. He would not wait. Their patience was longer than his patience, and their hands never tired. He would take his own line first, in the days when their line was deep and far, and the line he chose was the thick-paw of the summer run.

He knew the thick-paw by the tracks it left: the wide paw-print in the soft ground, one blunt toe dragging, the bushes broken at the height of a tall beast's chest. It was slow and heavy and bad at running, and it had fed well in the hot springs since the melt, and it fed on the same slopes a man could walk. A line that reads the ground could read the thick-paw too. It was on both lists already. It only had to be taken.

For nine days he kept to the high ground and let the hunger work. Then he found what he had been waiting for: fresh sign of the hunting line, moving down-country of the giants on the long march, two days of walking away from the summer run.

He went after the thick-paw that same evening. He found it in the last of the light, rooting in the fern at the edge of the run, and he did not go at it at once. He stilled himself and let the country settle. The hunt was a long slow thing done in short hard pieces, and he had learned the pieces in three winters of it: walk, stop, read, wait, then the one piece that is all of it.

The thick-paw went down the run at first light, as it did every morning. It was a heavy thing, all shoulder and neck, the head low and the tusks dirty from the rooting feed, and it moved with a rolling confidence that came from never having been chased by anything faster than itself.

He was faster. He let it get the length of the run ahead of him, because the run was cut with old gullies and thorn, and a beast that runs on bad ground breaks its own legs. He stayed high on the slope beside the run where the ground was clean and his own feet were sure, and he ran, and he shut everything else out.

The summer was thick and loud with spring and it did not want him moving through it at speed. Twice the fern closed over the run and he lost the shape of the beast and had to take the high line again. Once a pair of low lives — some small pale thing and its young — turned their heads as he passed above them, and he went down in the fern at once and held the quiet while the two heads turned back and forgot. The quiet cost him time. Time cost him ground. When he came up again the thick-paw had gained the run's far bend and was not running any more; it had turned.

It had turned, and it was standing in the open with its head down and its tusks up and its weight set back on its haunches, because that was what the thick-paw did. It did not run well. It fought well.

He came at it low, from the side of the run where the bank was cut and the beast could not swing the tusks without losing its feet, and the short hard piece began. The thick-paw came off the bank with a short grunting rush and caught him across the ribs with its shoulder and drove him into the fern, and the breath went out of him and the world went white for a moment. He rolled with it, not against it. That is the whole of the art: not against. He took the second rush into his own shoulder and gave it ground, and the tusks grated along the hard plates under his hide and did not find the soft place, and then his head was past the tusks and his jaw had the thick-paw behind the ear.

The hold went in. The thick-paw screamed and tore backwards, dragging him through the fern and the thorn, rolling, all its weight and fear come up at once, and he held. The jaw's work was quiet: a slow deeper set, muscle and bone, no hurry. The beast thrashed a long time and then was still.

He did not let go for a while after it was still. He had learned that too.

Then the two red things stood up over the meat. They always came at a kill, and he always felt both. The first was the boil: the bloodthirst of his kind, the old rampage that wants the meat taken whole and the body torn and the ground washed red, the thing that has killed more of his kind than any hunter. The second was quieter and worse in its way: the hunger. He had carried the hunger since the deep summer — the red pull that had made him mark beasts and spend nights over them and lose the grey stag to his own delay. The boil and the hunger met over the thick-paw's body, and he stood over the meat with his jaw still holding and put the choosing between the two of them, and the choosing said what it always said: the hunger is yours and the hunger is yours and neither of them is you.

He ate. The meal was good meat and the deep sleep did not take him, because he did not let it. When the meat was down and the body was left, he used the oldest trick of the deep forest. He took the kill and set it in the ground around the place: his own scent put down in a wide ring: a sign that says a thing. The heavy kinds of the run would find it and move off the ground he had marked. It would keep them away from this place. It would keep him safe from other beasts while the meat was on him.

He stood at the edge of the summer run in the last of the light. His sign stood in the ground behind him. His list was shorter by one line, and their list the same. And he understood, for the first time, that his own trick was a thing said out loud in a country that now had listeners who could read.

---

At the winter place, the fire was out, and the poles were down, and the two of them were packing the last of it onto a back-frame while Old Qin walked the ground at the edge of the camp. He walked it as he walked every ground, with his hands behind him and his eyes on the moss, and at the far edge he stopped and called, and the two of them left the packing and came.

"Same ground," said Gao Tie. He crouched and looked at the print in the moss without touching it. "Same shape as the deep place. Same as the water, two years back. Day old, no more. Whatever it is, it ate here, and it walked off south."

"South is the water line," said Xiao An.

"South is the water line," Gao Tie said.

"A thing that signs and does not show itself," said Xiao An. She was younger than the other two and she had learned to read the ground fast, and she read it again now, the print in the moss and the drag of the kill beside it. "It takes what we take. It takes it a day after we set it out, every time, and it leaves its own sign in the ground after. Old Qin. It is not reading us. It does not know us at all. It never comes near the fires. But it knows the country, and it knows where a beast will stand, the same way we know."

"It leaves signs," said Old Qin. "Then it is a line on the list." He took the list out of his coat and set it flat on his knee, and took out the brush and ink from the small case, and wrote, and the writing was slow and careful, because the list was the work.

"One line," he said, when he had done it. "No name. The beast only, and the ground, and the season. A thing that signs and does not show."

"The old hunters of the north border say the deep forest has its own keepers," said Gao Tie. He said it for the record and not as a belief, the way a man sets down a story he has no use for.

"Then write it under what we know, and not under what we guess," said Old Qin. "What we have is sign, and a bank. Sign goes on the list."

"And what does the list do with it?" said Xiao An.

"When it is seen, the list goes to it," said Old Qin. He looked back down the country at the low breaks, and then at the two of them, and his voice had no heat in it, because the list never needed heat. "The stag's light is in my coat. It goes down the road with the rest of them; the tables buy depth for frames that have grown to hold it, and we buy the season's bread with the price. That is the work. That is all the work. But the beast is a line on the list now, and a line does not sit on the list forever. Nothing does."

He folded the list and put it away. It had the sound of a thing being decided.

"The old bull of the hot springs," he said. "His years stand low and deep, and the deep light is worth a year of the season's bread. We keep him on the list for the season, not for an hour. We take at the water's edge, where the ground is soft and the beast is slow. And if the beast comes while we are at it —" he looked at the two of them, one after the other, "— we pull back. The list does not buy a season of the border with the whole of a hunting line."

"And the hound?" said Gao Tie, because the hound had wintered badly and had come out of the cold thinner than it went in.

"The hound goes," said Old Qin. "The hound always goes."

"Then we walk the water line first, and the breaks after," said Gao Tie. "Same as the winter."

"Same as the winter," said Old Qin. "And Xiao An walks the tracks. She reads them faster than the hound does, and the hound does not argue."

"The hound never argues," said Xiao An.

---

He learned about the old bull from a hillside, without wanting to. It was the second week of the summer when he came down off the giants' shoulder and found the hot springs full of the heavy kinds, all of them fat and slow with the new feed. And one of them was different. The bull stood alone on a rise above the water, and the old beast had gone dark with the years, and when he read it at a distance his sense did not close on anything. The years of it did not read down into the ground as the old stag-kind's had. They stood low and deep and went down out of reach, like a shaft into a mountain, and what came back up was a weight.

He sat on the hillside a long time with his sense low and small in him. The thick-paw had been meat. This was a line, and the line was the deepest one the summer had to give, and it was standing in his own reading as though it had been standing there since the melt, waiting to be seen.

He marked it that evening. He set his sign in the ground above the water where the old beast came to drink, and he went back up the shoulder, and he did not take it that day, because the deep line is a season's work and not a day's. Behind him, down the long low country, two fires stood in the country of the giants.

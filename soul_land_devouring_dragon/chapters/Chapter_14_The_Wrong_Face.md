# Chapter 14: The Wrong Face

The snow came early that year. It started under the ground, days before it started in the sky. He knew winter was close by the hot springs: more beasts came down to the warm water than in other years, the steam hung thicker over it, and the small creatures went into their holes and closed them behind. He left the high stone country and moved down to the edge of the deep forest, and made his winter den under a shelf of roots above a stream, with one way in and another way out.

The hunters moved north with the cold. They had set three camps on the high trails before the end of summer, and by the second month of winter their line had reached the edge of the deep forest where he hunted. From a ridge at dusk he studied their newest camp: a fresh kill left lying in the open, wire strung low across both trails, a blind built of poles and cut branches, and a spirit hound staked by the water with its nose toward the meat.

He did not go near it. He took his own hunting down onto the hard ground where tracks do not hold, and he ate small, and he watched the hunters work their way up the country like water finding a crack.

---

The danger came in behind his own kills. At the end of the second month he found tracks going back to a kill he had left as bait in the low country outside his range: a heavy beast he had eaten deep and left standing. Something big had come to it. The tracks were wider than his own by a hand's width. They had come up to the kill out of the south, walked once around it without hurrying, and left the meat untouched.

Beasts come to meat. That is what meat is for. Something had come to the kill, read what it was, and walked away from the food, and only one kind of creature does that: one that is hunting something bigger than a meal.

He found the tracks again a week later, and a third time, closer, on a trail he used to reach the hot springs. Something was following him. He had left kills to send the hound and the hunters away from his range, and he had taught a different hunter to follow him. The new one was heavy and lean and patient, and it had killed nothing he could find. His own trick had grown a second edge, and the second edge was pointed at him.

---

The hunters met it before he did, and he read the whole of it from the ground. He came down the long slope on the third morning after a hard night and stopped on the ridge. The camp below him was wrecked. The kill was scattered and half eaten. Both wires were down. The blind had been knocked apart and one pole leaned over with a long dark smear down the wood. The snow around the water was cut over with tracks: boots, and the wide pads he now knew, and one line of tracks that went away south without ever breaking into a run.

There was blood on the bank, and it was not the beast's. He read the ground for the better part of a day. The hunters had made their kill and set their watch and sat behind the poles as they had five times before. The beast came up the trail in the last of the night, crossed the wire without touching it, and walked to the meat as if the wire were drawn on the ground for it to read. Then the ground broke up: boots going out, the wide pads turning, something heavy dragged down the bank, and the hound's line snapped clean at the stake.

One man had gone down by the water. The others had carried him west toward their camp. He sat on the ridge a long time after that. None of it was his doing, and all of it had come out of him. He left the wrecked camp at dark and did not go back.

---

The hunters carried their dead west and made camp in a hollow of black stone, and the four of them sat at the fire with the fifth one wrapped in his coat. The older lodge man had been carried a day and a half and had not spoken since the water. A long tear ran down his leg and another across his ribs where the wide forepaw had come down, and the younger man had packed both with moss and bound them with the last of the spare line.

"He should have stayed behind the blind," said the younger lodge man.
"He did stay behind the blind," said Gao Tie. "It walked past the blind. It walked past the wire, past the meat, past all of it, and it came to the water where he was filling the skin, because that was the job I gave him."

The older lodge man spoke from the fire, and the whole camp turned to him. His eyes were open, and his voice came out low and level.
"Read it back to me," he said.
Old Qin took the list and read it: the water bank, the high trails, the low meadow, the fern country south of the giants' water, five camps, the kills left standing, the wire, the watch, four days.
"Now the new one," said the lodge man.
"A beast of the deep forest. Heavy, wide-tracked. Stood the wire and never touched it. Killed at the water and went south."
"Good," said the lodge man. "Put my name under the list, and the winter's pay to my sister's house."
"You will carry the list down yourself," said Gao Tie.
"I have read enough ground in my life to know what I am lying on," said the lodge man. He shut his eyes. "Write the beast. Leave the story."

Old Qin had the list open on his knee, and the brush lay in the ink beside him. He had not written a word.
"Write it," said the younger man. "A beast in the camp, a beast that kills. That is a line. That is the line you wanted."
"It is not the same line," said Xiao An.
She had come in from the ridge, where she had walked the tracks twice.
"It leaves signs and never shows itself," she said. "That is what is written on the keepers' line. Five camps, and not one print in the meat, and the hound never once smelled it, and the wire was never touched. This beast left prints everywhere it went. It left a man dead. It ate the meat it came for."
"Something came to the sign and killed for it," said Old Qin. "Find me the difference that matters to the list. The lodge asks one question: did we put a beast on the paper. This beast walked into a camp we set for the thing we have been hunting, killed the best hand we had on the mountain, and went south without hurrying. If I write the thing we have been hunting and leave this beast off the page, we go home with a blank line and a dead man."
"Then write two lines."
"Two lines for one beast is one line too many," said Old Qin. "What we are hunting is a beast. It reads, it kills, and it has been on our heavy beasts since the first water. Now it has a shape and a size, and a name can wait."
"Then the line goes south when the ground softens," said Gao Tie.
"At the first soft day," said Old Qin. "South on the wide tracks, with the wire off the camps and the hound on the front. If the beast on the paper is the thing we have been hunting, we will not know it until it is dead on the ground. If it is not, we come north again in the summer and we will have spent a season to learn one true thing."
"Which is?" said the younger man.
"That this country has more than one hunter in it," said Xiao An.
"It tore the hound's stake out of the ground," said Gao Tie. "That is the thing I keep coming back to. It walked past the wire, and it did not go round the blind. It went into the middle of the camp and did what it liked and it left."
"Then the paper carries the thing we can prove," said Old Qin.

He dipped the brush and wrote, and the writing went on for a while in the quiet. The wind came down the hollow over the stone and moved the coat.
"What did you put?" said the younger man.
"A beast of the deep forest," said Old Qin. "Heavy, pale-sided, wide-tracked. Stood the wire and never touched it. Killed at the water and went south. That is the beast of the keepers' line until another beast proves otherwise."
"And the other one?"
"The other one is a smell the hound will not leave, a shape on paper, and a bank at the water," said Old Qin. He shut the list. "If the two are the same creature, then we have written it. If they are not, then the one we have written will take us south, and it is a killer, and killing it is work we know how to do."
"And after the south?" said Xiao An.
"After the south, the season is spent, and we take the list down to the lodge and stand in front of the table with a beast on the paper and a light in the box." He put the brush away. "That is as close to the truth as this country lets a man stand."

Then Gao Tie got up and put more wood on, and the younger man went out to the hound. Xiao An stood where she was, with the tracks of two beasts in her head and one line on the paper.

---

He crossed the high ground above their camp two nights later, and that was when the hound smelled him for the second time. He had not meant to come near the camp: the hard ground above the hollow was the driest crossing in the west, the wind came off the black stone with no scent of men in it, and he had held the quiet since the first ridge.

Halfway across, the country below him changed. He felt it as he felt a trap. The small creatures of the hollow went still all at once, and the wind came up the slope carrying men and fire and wet wool, and under that, thin and cold, a hound's voice asking a question of the dark.

He went down into the lee of a stone and pulled the quiet down over himself to its last limit, and it cost him as it always cost him now. The hound worked up toward him, not baying and not running, walking with its nose low, stopping every so often to stand and smell the air. Once, near a stand of black pines, it gave the long thin whine that is not finding and not losing.

It had two smells in its nose. It had the wide-pad smell from the water, fresh and strong, and it had the old smell it had carried for an hour at the water in the summer. The two of them crossed on the same ground in the same night, and the hound could not make them one, and it could not leave either, and its whole body was the argument.

A man's voice came up the slope, flat and low, and called it in. The hound stood a while longer with its muzzle up and its ears working, and then it turned and went down, and the men took it into the camp, and the fire went on burning behind the stone.

He crossed the rest of the high ground at its western end, in the dark, without leaving a print worth reading. The kills he had left as bait were confusing their hound, and the confusion was protection he had never planned and could not count on twice.

---

The hunger asked its question at the turn of the cold, and a full belly could not answer it. He took a heavy beast in the second month of winter, out of the hot springs, an old slow creature of the outer forest that had come down to the warm water as they all did. The hunt took three days and the kill was clean, and he ate the deep parts first and then the rest over the next two days. He came up out of the long sleep stronger, with heavier blood, and the pull toward the ravine felt a little clearer. The hunger that had no meal sat up inside him the moment he was awake.

He tried to feed it. He took a second heavy beast and ate it down. He took a thin one. He ate the deep parts of three kills in eight days, and every meal went in and every meal was good, and the hunger never once changed its note.

It was not asking for meat. The meat lay in him warm and whole, and the hunger stood over it and asked for the same thing as always, and the thing was not meat and not sleep.

He lay on the rock above the hot springs one grey evening and reached out north, and the dark places were there, and the answer was the same: the deep years, the ground under the great beasts. The hunger had stopped being about any beast he could kill. He held it as a beast holds a current, and the holding did not end.

---

What he did with his voice at the end of that month, he did because of the tracks. He went south into the low country where he had left the bait kills and where the wide pads had gone. On the third day he found the tracks running east along a dry watercourse, and he followed them at a distance for half a day, and in the last of the light he saw the beast itself for the first time.

It stood in a killing ground of its own making, under a shelf of old rock. It was not as big as the great beasts of the deep forest. It was bigger than anything else he had ever hunted: pale along the sides, heavy in the shoulders, with a long scarred jaw and a low head. It stood over the opened body of a heavy beast and ate, and everything about it said that it had never once hurried in its life.

Its ears came forward before he had moved. The two of them looked at each other across the killing ground, and neither moved for a long moment. He felt for its age, and found it old, the years in it thick and black and deep, and strong in a way that had nothing to do with the size of its body.

Then it took one step toward him. He roared, and this time he kept it short.

The sound came up out of his chest heavy and low, one breath long and no more. It went into the air between them and one pace past the beast's jaw. It landed as a stone lands in deep water: no long roll out over the country, no snow coming off the rocks, no small creatures going flat in the valley.

The other beast stopped, one forefoot still forward and its head low, and the long jaw came open a little, and the feeling it had been making went still. For one breath the two of them stood in the same killing ground with one sound between them, and the sound was his kind's whole meaning, and it went into the other's ears four paces away and left nothing to answer.

Then the beast turned off the kill and walked south, unhurried, as it did everything, and did not look back. He stood over the dead beast and ate the deep parts and left the rest. By then his legs had gone soft under him and a dimness sat behind his eyes. The walk home took two days longer than it should have. The second day he spent lying in a stone shelter with the whole country sounding loud and near and too bright.

His voice had cost him breath and blood before. This time it cost him something more. For two days the country read too warmly, the way a bank reads after a flood goes off it, and he lay there and knew that the aiming had been the price. He came home to his den with the lesson whole. His voice was not a shout any more. It was a word, and a word said to a hunter is a word the hunter keeps. Somewhere south, in the pale beast with the scarred jaw, there now walked the sound of what he was.

---

The season turned with the hunters still in his country and a wrong beast on their paper. One camp was struck, and then another, and the hunters' tracks turned south where the wide pads had gone. He crossed above their last camp on the night before they left it: the meat in the open, the wire low on both trails, the poles and the cut branches, and the hound staked by the water with its nose to the east.

The hound lifted its head while he watched. It stood up on the end of its line and smelled the wind coming down off the rocks, and the long thin whine came up the slope to him once, and then the man at the fire called it and it lay down again.

He went on north in the dark, over the hard ground, toward his own range. The paper had a face on it now, and the face was wrong, and he had not put it there. What he had put there was a kill left in the open as bait, and the bait had brought a hunter of his own weight, and the hunter had taken the watch off his ground.

South was where the hunters were walking, with the wire and the blind and the paper and the boy on the hound, and south was where the two of them would meet. And he would be north, and free, and the freedom would have been bought with bait that had grown legs. He came down into his range at the grey end of the night, and went in under the roots, and slept.

# Chapter 14: The Wrong Face

The white came up out of the ground first, days before the sky agreed to anything, and he read the turn from the warm breaks. He moved down off the high stone into the fringe and made his winter hollow under a shelf of roots above a stream, one way in and another way out.

The takers moved north with the same cold. Their folds had stood three times up the deep trails in the falling of the green, and by the second month of the white their line had come up into the fringe where he did his winter hunting. He read the newest fold from a ridge at dusk and found it a small finished piece of work: a kill left standing in the open, the wire run low on both trails, poles and cut branches for the blind, and a hound staked by the water with its nose toward the meat.

He did not go near it. He took his own hunting down onto the hard ground where prints die, and he ate small, and he watched their line work its way up the country like water finding a crack.

---

The danger walked in behind his own kills. He found the first of the tracks at the end of the second month, going back to a lie he had left in the low outer country: a heavy beast taken in the open, the meat eaten deep and the rest left standing. The tracks were wider than his own by a hand's width, and they had come up to the kill out of the south, walked once around the whole of it without hurrying, and left the meat untouched.

Animals came to meat. The answering came to meat. Something had come to the word and read it and walked away from the meat itself, and only one kind of creature reads a word and leaves the food.

He saw the tracks again a week later, and a third time, closer, on a trail he used when he wanted the warm breaks. The beast was reading him. He had written signs to send one reader away and had taught a different reader to follow him, and the new reader was heavy and lean and patient, and it had killed nothing he could find. His own craft had grown a second edge, and the second edge was pointed at him.

---

The takers met the beast before he did, and he read the whole of it from the ground. He came down the long break on the third morning after a hard night and stopped on the rim, because the fold below him had been broken. The kill was scattered, half eaten and half trampled. Both wire lines were down. The blind had been knocked apart and one pole stood leaning with a long dark smear down the wood. The snow around the water was written over with tracks, some of them boots, some of them the wide pads he now knew, and one set that went away south in a long straight line without ever breaking into a run.

There was blood on the bank, and it was not the beast's. He read the ground for the better part of a day. Men had made their kill and set their watch and sat behind the poles as they had five times before. The beast had come up the trail in the last of the night, crossed the wire without ever touching it, and walked to the meat in the open as if the wire had been drawn on the ground for its reading. Then the ground broke up: the boot-prints going out, the wide pads turning, the drag of something heavy pulled down the bank, the hound's line snapped clean at the stake.

One man had gone down by the water. The others had carried him, by the look of the two lines of boots and how the snow lay under them, west along the trail toward their camp. He sat on the rim a long time after he had read it. None of it was his doing, and all of it had come out of him. He left the broken fold at dark and did not come back to it.

---

The line carried its dead west and set the camp in a hollow of black stone, and the four of them sat at the fire with the fifth one wrapped in his coat. The older lodge man had been carried a day and a half and had not spoken since the water. He had a long tear down his leg and another across his ribs where the wide forepaw had come down, and the younger man had packed both with moss and bound them with the last of the spare line.

"He should have stayed behind the blind," said the younger lodge man.

"He did stay behind the blind," said Gao Tie. "It walked past the blind. It walked past the wire, past the meat, past all of it, and it came to the water where he was filling the skin because that was the errand I gave him."

The older lodge man spoke from the fire, and the whole camp turned to him. He had his eyes open, and his voice came out low and level.

"Read it back to me," he said.

Old Qin took the list up and read it: the water bank, the high breaks, the low fold, the fern country south of the giants' water, the five folds, the kill left standing, the wire, the watch, the four days.
"Now the new one," said the lodge man.

"A beast of the deep fringe. Heavy, wide-tracked. Stood the wire and never touched it. Killed at the water and went south."

"Good," said the lodge man. "Put my name under the count, and the winter's pay to my sister's house."

"You will carry the count down yourself," said Gao Tie.

"I have read enough ground in my life to know what I am lying on," said the lodge man. He shut his eyes. "Write the beast. Leave the story."

Old Qin had the list out on his knee. He had opened it at the fire and had not written a word in it, and the brush lay in the ink beside him.

"Write it," said the younger man. "A beast in the fold, a beast that kills. That is a line. That is the line you wanted."

"It is not the same line," said Xiao An.

She had come in from the ridge where she had walked the tracks twice.
"It signs and does not show," she said. "That is what is written on the keepers' line. Five folds, and not one print in the meat, and the hound never once got a scent off it, and the wire was never touched. This beast left prints everywhere it went. It left a man. It ate the meat it came for."

"Something came to the sign and killed for it," said Old Qin. "Find me the difference that matters to the count. The lodge asks one question of a line: did it put a beast on the paper. This beast walked into a fold we set for the mark, killed the best hand we had on the mountain, and went south without hurrying. If I write the mark and leave the beast off the page, we go home with a blank line and a dead man."

"Then write two lines."

"Two lines for one beast is one line too many," said Old Qin. "The mark is a beast. It reads, it kills, and it has been on our heavy kinds since the first water. Now it has a shape and a size, and a name can wait."

"Then the line goes south when the ground softens," said Gao Tie.

"At the first soft day," said Old Qin. "South on the wide pads, with the wire off the folds and the hound on the front. If the beast of the paper is the mark itself, we will not know it until it is dead on the ground. If it is not, then we come north again in the green, and we will have spent a season to learn one true thing."

"Which is?" said the younger man.

"That this country has more than one reader in it," said Xiao An.

"It tore the hound's stake out of the ground," said Gao Tie. "That is the thing I keep coming back to. It walked past the wire, and it did not go round the blind. It went into the middle of the camp and did what it liked and it left."

"Then the paper carries the thing we can prove," said Old Qin.

He dipped the brush and wrote, and the writing went on for a while in the quiet. The wind came down the hollow over the stone and made the coat move.

"What did you put?" said the younger man.

"A beast of the deep fringe," said Old Qin. "Heavy, pale-sided, wide-tracked. Stood the wire and never touched it. Killed at the water and went south. That is the beast of the keepers' line until another beast proves otherwise."

"And the other one?"

"The other one is a scent the hound will not leave, a draft on paper, and a bank at the water," said Old Qin. He shut the list. "If the two are the same creature, then we have written it. If they are not, then the one we have written will take us south, and it is a killer, and killing it is work we know how to do."

"And after the south?" said Xiao An.

"After the south, the season is spent, and we take the count down to the lodge and stand in front of the table with a beast on the paper and a light in the box." He put the brush away. "That is as close to the truth as this country lets a man stand."

Then Gao Tie got up and put more wood on, and the younger man went out to the hound. Xiao An stood where she was, with the tracks of two beasts in her head and one line on the paper.

---

He crossed the high ground above their camp two nights later, and that was when the hound read him a second time. He had not meant to come near the camp: the hard ground above the hollow was the driest crossing in the west fringe, and the wind came off the black stone clean with no scent of men in it, and he had held the hush since the first ridge.

Halfway across, the country below him went wrong. He felt it the way he felt a trap: the small lives of the hollow went flat all at once, and the wind came up the slope with the smell of men and fire and wet wool on it, and under that smell, thin and cold, the line of a hound's voice asking a question of the dark.

He went down into the lee of a stone and pulled the hush down over himself to its last margin, and the stillness cost him as it always cost him now. The hound worked up toward him, not baying and not running, walking the ground with its nose low, and every so often stopping to stand and read the air. Once, near a stand of black pines, it gave the long thin whine that was not a cry of finding and not a cry of losing.

It had two lines in its nose. It had the wide-pad smell from the water, fresh and strong, and it had the old scent it had carried for an hour at the water in the green season. The two of them ran across each other on the same ground in the same night, and the hound could not make them one and could not leave the other, and its whole body was the argument.

A man's voice came up the slope, flat and low, and called it in. The hound stood a while longer with its muzzle up and its ears working, and then it turned and went down, and the men took it into the camp, and the fire went on burning behind the stone.

He crossed the rest of the high ground at its western end, in the dark, without a print worth reading. The lie he had left for their line was the thing that confused their hound, and the confusion was protection he had never planned and could not count on twice.

---

The want asked its question at the turn of the cold, and the fullness could not answer it. He took a heavy beast in the second month of the white, out of the warm breaks, an old slow creature of the fringe years that had come down to the hot water as they all did. The hunt was three days and the kill was clean, and he ate the deep parts first and then the whole of the rest over the next two days. He came up out of the deep sleep stronger in the body, and his blood ran heavier, and the thread toward the ravine sang a shade clearer. The hunger that had no meal sat up inside him the moment he was awake.

He tried to feed it. He took a second heavy beast and ate it down. He took a thin one. He ate the deep parts of three kills in eight days, and every meal went in and every meal was good, and the hunger never once changed its tone.

It was not asking for meat. The fullness lay in him warm and whole, and the hunger stood over it and said the same word as always, and the word was not meat and it was not sleep.

He lay on the rock above the warm breaks one grey evening and let the reading run north, and the standing was there, and the word was the same: the deep years, the ground under the standings. The want had stopped being about any beast he could kill. He held it as a beast holds a current, and the holding did not end.

---

What the voice did at the end of that month, it did because of the tracks. He had gone south, into the low fringe where the lie had been made and where the wide pads had turned and gone. He found the line of them on the third day, running east along a dry watercourse, and followed it at a distance for half a day, and in the last of the light he saw the beast itself for the first time.

It stood in a killing ground of its own making, under a shelf of old rock. It was not as big as the elders of the deep country. It was bigger than anything else he had ever hunted: pale along the sides, heavy in the forequarters, with a long scarred jaw and a low heavy head. It stood over the opened body of a beast of the heavy years and ate, and its whole manner said that it had never once in its life hurried.

Its ears came forward before he had moved. The reading went across the killing ground between the two of them, and neither of them moved for a long moment. He read its years. They were old and thick and black, and the beast was strong in a way that had nothing to do with the size of the body it lived in.

Then it took one step toward him. He called, and this time he called short.

The note came up out of his chest heavy and low and flat, spent the whole length of a breath and no more. He put it into the air between them and one length past the beast's jaw. It landed as a stone lands in deep water: no long roll out over the country, no snow coming off the rocks, no small lives going flat in the whole valley.

The other beast stopped, with one forepaw still forward and its head low, and the long scarred jaw came open a little, and the reading it had been making went still. For the length of a breath the two of them stood in the same killing ground with one sound between them, and the sound was his kind's whole word and it had gone into the other's ears at four lengths and left nothing to be answered.

Then the pale beast turned off the kill and walked south, unhurried, as it did everything, and did not look back. He stood over the killed beast and ate the deep parts of it and left the rest. By then his legs had gone soft under him and a dimness sat behind his eyes. The walk home took him two days longer than it should have, and the second day he spent lying in a stone shelter with the whole country sounding loud and near and wrongly bright.

The voice had cost him breath and blood before. This time it cost him something more: for two days the country read too warmly, as a bank reads after a flood goes off it, and he lay there and knew that the aim had been the price. He came home to his hollow on the fifth night with the lesson kept whole: the voice was not a shout any more. It was a word, and a word said to a reader is a word the reader keeps. Somewhere south, in the pale beast with the scarred jaw, there now walked the sound of what he was.

---

The season turned with the line still in his country and a wrong beast on their paper. One fold was struck, and then another, and the track of the men turned south where the wide pads had gone. He crossed above their last fold on the night before they struck it: the meat in the open, the wire low on both trails, the poles and the cut branches, the hound staked by the water with its nose to the east.

The hound lifted its head while he watched. It stood up on the end of its line and read the wind going down off the rocks, and the long thin whine came up the slope to him once, and then the man at the fire called it and it lay down again.

He went on north in the dark, over the hard ground, toward his own breaks. The line's paper had a face on it now, and the face was wrong, and he had not put it there. What he had put there was a word, left in the open for the answering, and the word had brought a hunter of his own weight, and the hunter had written the line's page for him and taken the watch off his ground.

South was where the men's line was walking, with the wire and the blind and the paper and the boy on the hound, and south was where the two of them would meet. And he would be north, and free, and the freedom would have been bought with a lie that had grown legs. He came down into his breaks at the grey end of the night, and went in under the roots, and slept.

# Chapter 22: The Deep Forest Proper

The forest north of the pass was not like the forest he was born in. It took him weeks to understand how different it was, because the difference was not in the trees. It was in how old everything was.

The trunks were wider than the ravine den was long. The leaves were so high and so thick that noon was like evening, and at night it never went fully dark. Between the trunks the ground was soft black soil, made of rotted wood and rotted leaves going down who knew how far, and it took his print everywhere and held his heat when he lay on it. The air smelled of wet and rot and growing, all the time. The smell he had known on the winds of his border his whole life was here the whole world.

The animals were old too. The small quick things of the floor were heavier and slower than their cousins outside, colored dark, not afraid of much. The moss lay over fallen giants in acres, and under the moss were more fallen giants. Everything had been dying and growing on top of itself for longer than he could imagine.

The strangest thing was the quiet underneath the noise. The forest was loud with birds and insects and water. But under all of it, all the time, was a pressure, faint and wide, as if the ground itself was listening. It never got louder and never went away. In his first weeks there, it made him nervous, and he could not say why.

He was nothing here. That was the change, and it took him most of a season to stop fighting it. Back in the low country, his blood had been a law: animals made their decisions at his smell. On the road, it had still counted for something. Here it counted for nothing at all. He was a young animal in an old country, and the forest did not know he existed.

The animals taught him how to live in it.

They went by roads. He found them in his second month: lines of smooth, polished ground running under the great trees, narrower than the river-road but the same idea. Some were made by the deer-kinds. Some were torn-up mud byways made by the rooting boar-kinds. The small quick things ran all of them at dusk and dawn, and the birds crossed low, and every animal in the forest used the same lines, all day, going where the food and water were.

For his whole first season he did what he had always done. He stayed off the roads, in the untracked dark between them, leaving no print and no pattern, hunting alone in the quiet places. He did everything right, by the old rules.

All that season, the forest had a strange feel he could not place. Rooms, he thought of it later, going quiet when he walked into them. He could never find the cause, because he was doing everything right.

Then, in the second month, over a kill in a fern hall, the cause found him.

It was a root-doe of the fat old kind, feeding alone in the ferns at midday, far off the road. He worked his approach for an hour — downwind, slow, in the shadow lines — and the take was clean. He was standing over her with the first hot mouthful in him when the fern hall went quiet.

It did not happen from the edges, as quiet does in an ordinary forest. It happened from the ground up. First the birds stopped. Then the insect noise, in a widening circle. And through the soles of his feet, in the roots under the moss, he felt something start to move toward him. Not a sound. Not a smell. Something paying attention.

He never saw what it was. The attention arrived like deep water arriving, slow and total, and where it stood, everything living around held its breath. It was interested in him. And he understood, with his whole body at once, what had been making the forest feel strange all season: the ground had been telling on him the whole time. The roots under that soft black soil carried his steps to the forest, every step of his slow hour, and the forest had been sending its quiet ahead of him for weeks, and now something had come to see what the quiet was about.

He did the hardest thing he had ever done on a kill. He left it. He backed away from the warm meat, low, and walked — not ran, because running tells everything you are running from something — out of the untracked dark, across the open ferns, to the nearest road. He stepped onto the polished ground, turned north, and went at the ordinary pace, in the open, like all the forest's other animals.

The attention followed him to the edge of the road and stopped there. He felt it stand at the line of smooth ground like a man standing at a door. It did not cross. Then, slowly, it drained back into the soil, and the birds came back behind him, first one, then all of them.

He walked for miles before his heart came down. Then he thought it through.

The road was the hiding place. That was the law of the deep forest, and it was the opposite of every law he had ever owned. Back home, a strange animal survived by leaving nothing: no print, no pattern, no road twice. Here, in a country where the ground repeated every step to whatever listened, there was no leaving nothing. There was only being ordinary, or being a question. The untracked dark was where questions lived. The road was where everybody was, and everybody together was beneath notice.

So he changed his whole way of living in one day. He walked the roads. He hunted as the road's other hunters hunted: the sick, the slow, the unlucky, taken at the edges at the ordinary hours, quickly, without anything special about the taking. When he wanted more than that, he went to the water.

The water could not be read by roots. That was the second law, and it was given to him rather than learned, because it was the oldest thing he owned: moving water made him invisible in the one country where nothing else could. He proved it the first evening he went looking. A fat old fish held in the slack water behind a fallen trunk, and he came down the bank at the drinking hour, plain as a drinking animal, and stood in the shallows, and the water carried his shape apart as it had always carried his sound. The fish never knew he was there. Nothing knew. For the first time since the fern hall, he was an animal that nothing anywhere was thinking about.

He took the fish in one hard lunge — the old ones fight, and this one used the current against him, and lost anyway — and stood in the noise eating it, with the water pushing at his sides like something that had always meant to protect him.

The seasons turned, and the forest fed him, and he grew.

The meals were old. The fish carried years in them, and the road's edge-kills were animals grown fat in a country where almost nothing ever died before its time. He ate, slept the deep sleep, and woke a little more of what he was becoming. His body lengthened. His scales came in darker, with the color of the soil in them. By the second wet season, the animals on the roads treated him like a local trouble — known, allowed for, a young heavy animal with a jaw that you kept half an eye on and did not worry about.

One thing grew in him that was not body. After the fern hall, he could feel the quiet coming. It arrived before its reason, as weather arrives: the floor's sounds thinning somewhere off ahead, the birds' pattern going wrong in a way he could never have described but never once mistook again. Many times in those seasons, he felt the forest go quiet over some other stranger, an hour before the stranger himself had any idea he was being noticed. It was a sense his old country had never needed and could never have taught him: the reading of a whole ground's attention. He had paid a kill for it. On the roads of the deep forest, it was worth a kill a month.

Twice, the roads themselves taught him the forest's manners.

Once, the road he was following bent. All of it, the whole polished line, swung wide around a patch of darker trees and went a full day's walk out of its way, for no reason his eyes could find. He took the bend. When every animal on a road goes around something, the something is not his business. Far off through those darker trees, in the black hour of one night, he saw a light standing on the land. It was not fire, and it did not move. He looked at it for exactly as long as he had once looked at the middle of the pass, which was not long, and walked on.

The other time, in the dry season, a wind came down out of the north that smelled of stone and snow and enormous distance. Every old animal on the roads stopped walking and stood facing it. He stood with them. He had no idea what they were hearing, and he understood that not knowing was the ordinary condition of the young, and he stood there until it passed.

The pull ran under all of it, north, the same as it had run under the pass. But here he came to know it better, because there was so much else to know it against. The roots repeated steps. The quiet came for strangers. The roads bent around sleepers. And under all of that, unnoticed by any of it, the pull ran like water under ice. The forest's whole machinery of attention never seemed to read it at all. It did not push. It did not hurry. It was the oldest patient thing in a country full of old patient things.

In the second dry season, on a spine of bare rock that the road climbed to get across, it changed.

He came up out of the trees with the road, onto open stone, and the country opened: the deep forest going on and on north, wave after wave of dark trees, into far haze. He stopped, because the pull had stopped being a direction. All his life it had been north, plain as hunger. Now, standing on the stone with the whole land in view, it leaned — a few points east of north, toward the far country where the haze sat lowest and the sky had a weight in it that his eyes kept calling mountains.

Not a swerve. A settle, like a current finding its channel. The road ahead still ran north. The pull did not follow the road anymore.

He stood on the stone a long time, reading the difference, with the wind out of the north carrying the stone-and-snow smell that had stopped the old animals. Then he came down off the spine and took the road north anyway, because roads were how this country allowed walking, and because the fern hall had taught him exactly what going his own way cost.

But he carried the difference with him and weighed it at every bend. Somewhere ahead, the road and the pull would come apart for good. When that day came, he would have to leave the ordinary and go back into the untracked dark, carrying everything he now knew about what that cost.

That was the road's next question, and the old, patient forest held the answer somewhere under its leaves.

He walked on north, on the road, at the ordinary pace, one of the forest's many ordinary troubles, and grew.

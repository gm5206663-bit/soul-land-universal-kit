# Chapter 16: The One Print

The heat came up the low country in the first weeks of summer, and the water went down.

The two streams that crossed the country went thin and slow, and by the middle of the season they were chains of pools with long flats of cracked mud between them. The fern stood as high as his shoulder and hid everything that moved. The low country was his range now, and he hunted it the length of it. On the fourth evening he took a heavy grazer at the lower pool: the slow kind, old and thin from the winter, come in out of the fern alone. He lay in the reeds above the bank and let her drink. She put her head down and drank a long time, and when she lifted it he came up out of the reeds at the shoulder and took her at the neck. The water broke around the two of them, and then it was quiet, and he stood in the shallows and ate the deep parts while the light went out of the sky.

That was how the summer went. The meat came down to the water in the evenings, the water held it there, and the low country gave him a meal every few days without a fight.

The nights taught him the water's hours, and he hunted by them. The slow kind came down in the last of the light and drank long. The small quick things came at dark, in and out in a breath. The pigs came at the black of the night, and the birds at first grey, working the drying edges for what the night had left. He took his share of all of it and slept the hot middle of the day away in the shadow of the shelf, with the flies walking on him, and woke to drink when the sun was going. He ate deep and slept the hot hours away in the shade of the stone shelf, and the flies worked at him, and the country steamed. He grew heavier as the summer went on. His kills he kept off the trail, and what he could not eat he dragged away into the fern and left to the crows. Nothing else heavy had walked the low country since the spring.

The hunger came up after every meal the same as always. Over a full belly, in the heat, on bare stone, it asked its question, and the question was never meat.

---

In the middle of the summer the smoke came back up the country.

The wind turned south on a grey morning and brought it: wood smoke, and men, and the wet wool smell, thin and far. He knew the smell. He had left it in the south at the thaw, and now it was walking north again, and it was going to cross ground that was his.

He read them before he saw them. On the second evening the smell was strong enough to have a direction in it, and on the third morning he lay high on the rim of the low country and watched them come up the low trail: four men and the hound. An old man walked in front, then one with the hound on a short line, then one who walked with his eyes on the ground, and then a last one with the packs.

He could have gone down onto the trail and been away over the ridge before they came level with him. He did not. The low country was his, and the men were walking through it to somewhere else, and a beast who moves in front of men is a beast they follow. So he lay on the rim and let them pass along his own trail, and when they camped that night at the lower pool, he kept his quiet over himself and did not go down for water.

He watched them the whole night from the fern above the camp: the fire, the four shapes around it, the hound lying by the water. The youngest of them did the camp work — water and wood, twice out to the fire and back — and the one with the hound sat a little apart with his hand on the collar. Near the middle of the evening the youngest one stood up and looked out at the dark for no reason, the way men do, and looked a long time at the fern above them without seeing it, and then sat back down and put more wood on. Near the middle of the night the hound got up and worked a slow half-circle out to the edge of the firelight with her nose low. He pulled his quiet down over himself to its last limit and lay without moving. She stood at the edge of the light a while with her whole body in the feel, and then she went back to the fire and lay down, and the men had not noticed her go. When the grey came up he was still there, and he drank at a stream higher up when the sun was high, and he went that night and the next without a kill.

In the morning the four men and the hound went north off his low ground and up onto the high ground, and he stayed on his own side and let them have the bare ridges.

He read their camp ground the day after they left it, the way he read every ground they touched. They had set the fire in the lee of the bank, close to the water, and slept in a row with their feet to it. The hound had been staked downwind of them, on a line short enough that she could reach the water and no further, and she had walked circles at the end of it most of the night, or lain and got up and lain again. There was a place under the bank where one of the men had knelt with a cup and drunk, and a place where the youngest had sat a long time by himself in the fern, facing nothing. And that was all of the use they had made of his country: a fire, a row of men, a hound at the end of a string.

---

Three days after their smell had gone off the high ground, he came up and read what they had done.

The dry ground showed him less than the wet ground would have, but men write loudly when they stop. He found their walked line along the ridge, four men and the hound in it, and he found the two places where they had stood a long time: the bank of the upper stream, and the bare pan in the middle of the high ground. Two of the men had gone down on their knees at the edge of the pan and had stayed down long enough to leave the shape of their knees in the dust, and the prints of their hands where they had leaned in over the rim. The hound had lain down once in the dust by the east edge, in the shape of a dog that had been waiting a while.

He walked round the pan himself, once, as they had. The hound's tracks went all round it too, and off the north edge onto the stone and back, and down to the stream and back, the same line twice. And in the middle of the pan his own print lay where he had made it, in the setting mud of the wet season, dried hard, cracks running through it, with the marks of men's knees in the dust beside it at the edge.

He stood over it a long time.

The wet season's pan had taken his shape and kept it all summer, the way the wet country writes down everything that walks it. He had crossed that pan once, in the first soft days, on his way up to the deep forest, and stood in the middle of it a while, looking south at the low country under its mist, and then gone on. One crossing was all it had taken. The mud had run out from under his feet while he stood, and the sun had dried the whole thing into one piece, and the piece had kept him.

He read the rest of it the way he read any ground. The hound had put her nose into the middle of the pan and stood there, and then worked both ends of the line the men wanted — off the north edge onto bare stone and back, and down the grass to the stream and back — and given it up. Two of the men had knelt at the rim and read his print with their eyes for as long as men read anything. And north of the pan, four stones had been set in a row on the ground, out in the open, where a man coming up the ridge would find them.

He looked at the stones from the outside and did not go near them. He did not know what men did with stones in a row, and he did not need to. He understood the shape of the thing well enough without the why of it: men had marked this place, and men mark a place in order to come back to it.

Twice now he had used the writing trick. He had left kills standing in the open with the meat untouched so that men who read ground would read the wrong thing, and the trick had worked, and the working had taught them. The men had learned to read his signs, and the hound had learned to hold his smell, and now the mud itself had learned to keep his shape and hold it up to them, ready-made, on the bare hill all season.

So he would not write for them again.

---

He did not touch the pan. A pan torn open would be one more thing to read: a place visited twice, once by a beast and once by whatever came after it and tore the ground. So he gave them nothing.

From that morning he took the long way. He crossed the streams at the rock bars, where the water runs over stone, and he walked the high ridges where there is nothing but stone underfoot, and he left the mud flats to the grazing kind. Twice that month he passed up a kill he could have taken, rather than cross a soft pan to reach it, and went a day hungry instead.

The second time was a young grazer at a soft flat below the first spring. It came down to drink at dusk and stood in the open with its head down, and there was a pan between him and it, three strides of cracked yellow mud, and the grazer was thin and quick and the light was going. He stood a long while at the edge of the stone and turned it over. Three strides, and meat for three days, and one print going and one print coming back. He went without, and the grazer drank and went away up the far bank without ever knowing he had been there, and he went and lay in the shade with his ribs showing the summer's lean.

He paid it.

The four stones stood where the men had set them, in a line at the north edge of the pan. He walked round that ground once more before he left it, and took the long way down to his own country, on stone.

---

The summer turned over after that, and the low country stayed his.

The meat kept coming down to the water, and he kept taking it at the bank, and dragging what he could not eat off the trail; and when the flies were at their worst he lay up in the shade for a day and a night and let the country work. He grew heavier still. The pull toward the north was there the same as always, under everything, far off, and the hunger stood up over every meal, and neither of them changed.

Twice he walked to the edge of the deep forest where the ground rises, and stood under the first of the great trees with the forest going away north in front of him, dark and full of years, and listened. The deep forest was the same as it had been every season he had stood there: the great beasts' business going on somewhere inside it, far off, patient, none of it about him. He did not go in.

When the light began to come up colder in the mornings, he crossed once more to the ridge above the high ground and looked at the pan from a long way off. The four stones stood in their row. The pan lay in the sun with its one print in the middle of it, and nobody had come back for it all summer, and nobody had touched it.

The wet would come again before long and run the mud soft. Whatever walked the pan after that would write its own line there. He had already chosen where he would walk when the soft ground came.

He went down off the ridge and back to his own country. The low water was low, and the fern was turning, and the meat was still coming down to drink. He took a heavy one that week and ate deep. The nights were getting longer. He knew where the cold would come from, and where he would put his winter den, and which of the trails he would keep and which he would let go.

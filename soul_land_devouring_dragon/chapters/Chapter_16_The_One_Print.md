# Chapter 16: The One Print

The heat came up the low country in the first weeks of summer, and the water went down. The two streams that crossed it went thin and slow, and by the middle of the month they were chains of pools with long flats of cracked mud between them. The fern stood as high as his shoulder and hid everything that moved.

The low country was his range now, and he hunted it the length of it. On the fourth evening he took a heavy grazer at the lower pool. She was one of the slow kind, old and thin from the winter, and she came in out of the fern alone. He lay in the reeds above the bank and let her drink. She put her head down and drank a long time, and when she lifted it he came up out of the reeds at the shoulder and took her at the neck. The water broke around the two of them, and then it was quiet, and he stood in the shallows and ate the deep parts while the light went out of the sky. Two crows came down and sat in a dead pine above the pan and waited for the rest.

That was how the summer went. The meat came down to the water in the evenings, the water held it there, and the low country gave him a meal every few days without a fight. He ate deep and slept the hot hours away in the shade of the stone shelf, and the flies worked at him, and the country steamed. He grew heavier as the summer went on. His kills he kept off the trail, and what he could not eat he dragged away into the fern, and the crows took the rest. Nothing else heavy had walked it since the spring.

The hunger came up after every meal the same as it always had. He would lie full on the warm stone with the taste of meat still in him, and the hunger would stand up and ask its question, and the question was never meat. He had stopped trying to answer it with kills. He carried it the way a heavy beast carries an ache, and it stayed, and it did not change.

---

In the middle of the summer the smoke came back up the country.

The wind turned south on a grey morning and brought it: wood smoke, and men, and the wet wool smell, thin and far. He knew the smell. He had left it in the south at the thaw, and now it was walking north again, and it was going to cross ground that was his.

He read them before he saw them. On the second evening the smell was strong enough to have a direction in it, and on the third morning he lay high on the rim of the low country and watched them come up the low trail: four men and the hound. An old man walked in front, then one with the hound on a short line, then one who walked with his eyes on the ground, and then a last one with the packs.

He could have gone down onto the trail and been away over the ridge before they came level with him. He did not. The low country was his, and the men were walking to somewhere else, and the summer had already taught him that a beast who moves in front of men is a beast they follow. So he lay on the rim and let them pass on his trail, and when they camped that night at the lower pool, he kept his quiet over himself and did not go down for water.

He watched them the whole night from the fern above the camp: the fire, the four shapes around it, the hound lying by the water. Near the middle of the night the hound got up and worked a slow half-circle out to the edge of the firelight with her nose low. He pulled his quiet down over himself to its last limit and lay without moving. She stood at the edge of the light a while with her whole body in the feel, and then she went back to the fire and lay down, and the men had not noticed her go. When the grey came up he was still there, and he drank at a stream higher up when the sun was high, and he went that night and the next without a kill.

In the morning the four men and the hound went north off his low ground and up onto the high ground, and he stayed on his own side and let them have the bare ridges.

---

The high ground in summer was stone, dry grass, and old pans of mud that the spring had left and the sun had baked hard. Water had to be carried up to it. Xiao An walked at the front with the hound on the long line, and Old Qin walked behind her, and the younger man carried the water and said what he thought of the ground, and Gao Tie said nothing at all.

"Three days," said the younger man. "She has shown us stone, moss, and one old crow kill. The lodge is paying for a summer walk over nothing."

"She is not pointing at kills," said Gao Tie. "If she wanted kills she would have us standing in the crow sign all day."

"Then what is she pointing at?"

"Whatever walks," said Gao Tie.

On the third morning the hound stopped at a pan of dried mud the size of a tent floor, put her nose down over the middle of it, and did not lift it again. Gao Tie came up and took the collar in both hands, and he did not pull.

Xiao An was already going down onto her knees at the edge of the pan. She brushed the dust away with the flat of her hand, once, and then again, and then she went still.

"Well," said Old Qin.

"Come and look," she said. "All of you."

The mud had kept the whole spring in it. The water that had run through it had left its lines, and the sun had dried the pan into one piece with cracks across it, and there in the middle of all of it was one print.

It was as long as her forearm from the wrist and a hand and a half across. Four toes in front, wide flat pads, no mark of claws anywhere. The weight had driven the mud up around the edges and run it out at the toes, and then the whole thing had dried where it stood, cracks and all.

"It stood in the wet, and it did not move," said Xiao An. "The mud ran out from under it while it was standing there, and then it dried with the beast in it. A beast walking through the spring mud leaves a roll in the print, and the roll is the walk. This one has no roll in it."

"Looking at what?" said the younger man.

She looked down the slope the way the print faced, down to where the low country lay under its own mist, and she was quiet for a moment.

"Looking south," she said. "In the wet season our fires were down there."

Nobody said anything for a while. Gao Tie held the hound, and the hound shook once against the collar and stood.

"Stay with what the ground says," said Old Qin. "What else does it say?"

"It says one print and no others," said Xiao An. "The pan is ten paces across and I have been over the whole of it on my knees, and there is this one. Off the pan the ground is stone in two directions and hard grass in the third."

"A beast that heavy cannot cross stone," said the younger man. "It must have left a track somewhere."

"It left one," said Gao Tie. "What does that tell you?"

The younger man looked at the pan for a while.

"That it does not want to be followed," he said.

"Is it the beast of the paper?" said Old Qin.

"No," said Xiao An. She sat back on her heels. "I walked the pale one's tracks for half a season in the south. The pale one signs everything it goes near: it comes in, it walks once around what it has killed, it goes away. And its pad is wider than this one, and flat all across. This beast is heavier in the front and narrower in the track. It has walked a whole spring and left one print."

Old Qin came to the edge of the pan and stood over it with his hands behind his back. Then he went down on one knee beside Xiao An and put his hand into the print, and his hand did not cover the pad.

"How old?" he said.

"The last of the wet," said Xiao An. "The mud cracked after it. The cracks run through the print the same as they run through the pan, and that is how I know it is a spring print and not an old one."

"Gao Tie," said Old Qin. "Let her cast. Where does the line die?"

Gao Tie slipped the collar. The hound went off the north edge of the pan onto the stone and worked it for half the morning and gave it up. Then she took the line back south, down the grass to the stream, and at the bank she put her nose into the water and stopped, and she would not cross.

"She does not cross running water," said Gao Tie. "She never has. The line goes into the stream and it stops there."

"Both ends of it stop," said the younger man. "Stone and water, and one print in the middle."

Old Qin walked twice around the pan. Then he stood at its edge with his hand on the list inside his coat.

"Say it plainly, all of you," he said. "One heavy beast walked the high ground in the wet season. It stood in this pan in one place and looked down the low country for a while. It left no track onto the stone and no track into the stream, and one print behind. Is that all of it?"

"That is all of it," said Xiao An.

"Then I have a place and a print, and no beast," said Old Qin. "Write it, the young man says. And if I write a beast out of one print with no face and no kill, next season the lodge pays for a hunt after a guess. So we will do this instead. When the wet comes back, this pan takes a print from anything that walks over it and dries the whole season into the mud. Next spring, before the mud sets, the two of you will be up here with the hound and the wire, and you will watch this pan."

"For how long?" said the younger man.

"Until the mud sets hard," said Old Qin, "or until something walks."

"And if it comes back in the dark and stands in the pan and is gone before morning?" said Xiao An. "We would be watching a floor of mud in a season when the thing that walks it does not want to be seen."

"Then in the morning the hound will have it," said Old Qin, "and we will walk it from this pan instead of from a guess. Mark the place, Gao Tie."

Gao Tie brought four stones up from the stream bank and set them in a row at the north edge of the pan, where a man coming up the ridge would find them. They walked two more days on the high ground and the hound found nothing else, and on the third morning the line went down off the ridges.

Xiao An was the last one off the ground. She stood at the four stones a while with her pack on her shoulder, looking back at the one print, and then she went down after the men.

"When the wet comes, I want the first watch," said the younger man, below her.

"You will have it," said Old Qin.

---

Three days after their smell had gone off the high ground, he came up and read what they had done.

The dry ground showed him less than the wet ground would, but men write loudly when they stop. He found their walked line along the ridge, four men and the hound in it, and he found the two places where they had stood a long time: the bank of the upper stream, where the hound had put her nose into the water and stopped, and the bare pan in the middle of the high ground. Two of the men had gone down on their knees at the edge of the pan, and had stayed down long enough to leave the shape of their knees in the dust.

He walked round the pan himself, once, as they had. The hound's tracks went all round it too, and off the north edge onto the stone and back, and down to the stream and back, the same line twice. And in the middle of the pan his own print lay where he had made it in the wet, dried hard, cracks running through it, with the marks of men's knees beside it at the edge.

He stood over it a long time.

Twice now he had used the writing trick. He had left kills standing in the open with the meat untouched, so that men who read ground would read the wrong thing, and the trick had worked, and the working had taught them. The men had learned to read his signs, and the hound had learned to hold his smell, and now the mud had learned to keep his shape. He was not going to write for them again.

He did not touch the pan. A pan torn open would be one more thing to read: a place visited twice, once by a beast and once by whatever came after it and tore the ground. So he gave them nothing. From that morning he took the long way. He crossed the streams at the rock bars, where the water runs over stone, and he walked the high ridges where there is nothing but stone underfoot, and he left the mud flats to the grazing kind. Twice that month he passed up a kill he could have taken, rather than cross a soft pan to reach it, and went a day hungry instead. He paid it.

The four stones stood where the men had set them, in a line at the north edge of the pan. He did not know the whole of what they said. But men set a thing down in the open and walk away from it only when they mean to come back to it, and nothing else in that country did that.

---

Somewhere south the pale beast went up and down its own ground as it always had. Once he found the wide pads at the far end of the low country, coming down to the water and going back, unhurried, and he did not follow them, and the beast did not come up. The roar he had put into it in the winter had not come back to him. The south was the same south.

His meals were the same meals, too. The hunger stood up after every kill the same as always, and the low country had nothing in it that could answer the question, and he carried it, and it stayed patient.

North of the high ground the deep forest stood as it had stood. The great beasts went up their country and down it as they had the season before, and the pull under them was the same pull and as far off. The barrier at the thousand years was where it had always been, and he did not feel it.

He walked on stone for the rest of the summer. The pan on the high ground lay in the sun and held its one print, and the four stones stood beside it, and the rain would come in the end and the pans would run soft again. Whatever walked the high ground in the wet would sign the mud for the men who meant to come back and read it, and he had already chosen where he would walk when they did.

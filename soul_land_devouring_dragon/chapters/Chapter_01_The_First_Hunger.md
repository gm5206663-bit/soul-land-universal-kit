# Chapter 1: The First Hunger

Douluo Continent. A wild mountain country, where no men lived.

On the slope of one of those mountains, in a hollow under a shoulder of stone, an egg lay in the cold ground. It had lain there a long time. Nothing watched over it. Nothing came for it.

That morning, the egg moved.

Inside it, the little Devouring Dragon had been a curled, blind thing for as long as there had been such a thing as him at all. The shell had been his whole world, a wall on every side. Now that wall was pressing in. A thin, cold wetness seeped through it from outside, and something in him that had waited a very long time decided it was done waiting.

He pushed. He did not choose to push. The push came up out of his back and his neck and his legs all together, like a cough coming up out of a chest, and the shell split near his head with a small, sharp sound.

Light came in through the split. It was white and thin, and it hurt, because his eyes had never seen anything at all. He did not know it was light. He only knew that the world on the far side of the shell was colder and louder than the world inside it.

He worked himself free, half pushing and half falling, and came down on his side on hard, wet ground, with the broken pieces of the shell under him and the wind moving over his skin.

The wind was the second thing he learned. It was cold, and it did not stop. His skin was all wrong for it — soft, pale, thin, no armor to it at all. He curled up into a ball, and his own body was the only warm thing anywhere in the world.

Something in him was already working. He had no word for it, and no thought about it, and he would not have a word for it for years. It went quietly about its business, the same business it had done inside the egg. It took the cold and pushed back against it with something that was not quite warmth and not quite skin — a slow thickening that would only show itself much later. It never stopped. It never slept. Every hard thing that ever happened to him after that day would be met by that slow answering. This was the one gift his blood gave him on the first morning of his life. He did not know it. It did not matter that he did not know it.

And he was hungry. That was the third thing, and it was the biggest. The hunger did not come and go as the wind did. It lived in him, low and steady, older than his eyes. It said one thing, and it never said anything else: eat.

So he turned his head and ate the shell.

The pieces were dry and sharp, and they cut the inside of his mouth. He ate them anyway, every piece he could reach. When they were down, he lay still and felt something of the shell stay inside him, a warmth spreading out through his belly and reaching, faintly, into his legs and his tail.

This was the first law of his kind that the world taught him, and he learned it alone, with nothing to teach him: what he ate became him. He did not understand it as a law. He felt it as warmth. But the law was there all the same, and everything that came later in his life would stand on it.

---

The mountain was not built for something as small as he was. He learned this in the first days, and he learned it the hard way.

His scales were pale and soft, and the cold went straight through them. His legs were thick and clumsy under a body too light for them. His tail was too long, and it got in his way every time he turned. His head was too heavy for his neck. He moved badly, like every newborn thing, and mostly downhill.

On the second day he fell off a rock shelf and lay at the bottom of it, stunned. Then he got up and went on. The hunger did not care about the fall. The hunger kept his time. It rose, and he obeyed it, and it went quiet for a while, and that quiet was the only rest he had.

He drank at a seep, where cold, clean water came out of the stone, and he learned to come back to it. He learned which rocks held the sun longest, and lay on them. He learned to keep to the low ground when the wind rose. He learned the slope by falling down it, over and over, until his legs understood what his eyes had already seen.

In this world, every beast was a soul beast, and a soul beast's strength lived in its years. A hundred years made a beast dangerous. A thousand years made it the lord of its ground. The very oldest, ten thousand years and more, were feared by everything, men included. The little Devouring Dragon knew none of this. He was days old. But the mountain fed even the things with no years.

Near the top of his hollow there was a seam of rock that held the sun all afternoon, and beetles worked in the grit of it. They were the smallest soul beasts of the mountain, too small to have years worth counting — slow, armored, and stupid. He learned to catch them. The first one died to a clumsy snap of his jaws, and he crunched it, shell and all. After that, he came back to the seam every warm afternoon and crouched in the grit with his nose down, waiting. It was the first hunting he ever did. He did not know it was hunting. He only knew that it worked.

Lower on the slope, where the stone went soft and wet, the seep ran down in a thin green line, and blue silver grass grew along it. Blue silver grass was the commonest soul beast in the whole world. It grew everywhere, in every ditch, a thin grass with a silver vein running down every blade, and it was worth nothing to anything that counted years. He ate some of it anyway, when the hunger was bad and there was nothing else. It tasted like wet stone. It kept him walking. That was enough.

There was also a broken pine, low on the slope, with a hole in its heart, and things nested in the hole. Small things. Things with beaks.

He learned about the beaks on the third evening, when hunger put his head into that hole without any caution at all. Something in the dark stabbed him above the eye, twice, fast. He pulled his head back, and the whole side of his face was suddenly hot and wet. He sat down in the fern and licked at the hurt until it stopped.

After that, he walked past the pine slowly. Every time. On the downhill side. He never put his head into that hole again, and he never needed to learn the reason twice. The mountain's first rule had cost him a little blood: the things that live in the dark can hurt you even when they are small. Most of a soul beast's rules are learned exactly like this — written in blood first, and understood afterward.

---

His first true hunt was for the stone-runners, and it taught him more than the beaks had.

The stone-runners lived in the talus, where the slope went wide. They were low, thick, quick things, the same color as the rock they lived in, with small eyes and a fatness about them that his hunger could feel from across the stones. They came out with the sun, sat up on their haunches, and looked around. They ran between the rocks along paths they had worn smooth themselves. To anything with patience, they were food walking.

He wanted one. He wanted one as badly as he had once wanted the shell.

The first try was a disaster. He went at the nearest runner straight, fast, and all at once, exactly as the hunger told him to. He was still two paces away when it saw him. It was gone into a crack between two stones before he reached the place where it had been sitting, and he hit the boulder behind it shoulder-first and lay there with his ears ringing.

He tried again that evening. The same thing happened. He tried again the next morning. The same thing, again.

A stone-runner is faster than a hatchling, on ground it has known since birth. A head-on charge, on the runner's own rocks, could not work. He did not think any of this out. He was days old; he could not think. But the failures went into him somewhere, and on the third morning he did not go at anything at all.

He lay down in the sun on the warm side of the talus, put his chin on his forelegs, and watched.

He watched for a whole day. He watched where the runners came out, and where they went back in. He watched which cracks were too narrow for a body his size. He watched which rocks they liked to sit on — the same three rocks, all morning, over and over. He watched one of them dig at the grit with its small hands, stand up, and look at nothing at all for a long time. None of them ever looked up. That was their one blindness, and it would be a long time before he learned what that blindness was worth.

Something in him settled while he watched. It was not that the hunger went away. The hunger stayed exactly where it always was, low and steady, saying eat. But the running-and-snapping part of him went quiet and lay down behind the watching part, and the quiet felt right, though he had no words for it and never looked at it directly. Much later, when he was older and far larger, he would understand that this was the first time the hunter in him won an argument against the hunger in him. On that morning, it was only a feeling, and the feeling was enough.

In the evening, he went down the talus on the downhill side, to the place where all the runners' paths came together between two boulders. He got low in the grit there, and waited.

It took a long time. The light went orange, and then gray. Twice, a runner came close, and both times the go-fast part of him jumped in his chest, and both times he held it down and lay still. Both runners turned aside at the last moment and went another way.

Then, in the last of the light, one came along the path in a hurry, and it did not see him until it was almost on top of him.

He came up out of the grit. It broke left — and he had watched them break left at that exact spot for two days, so he was already moving left before it moved. His jaws closed on it, and it was done.

The runner kicked twice and stopped. He stood over it with his whole body shaking, and the shaking was not fear. It was something else, something hot and simple, and it wanted more. Much later, he would learn to be careful of this thing in himself. On that evening, he only knew that he had won, and that the winning felt larger than the meal.

He ate it down to the bones and the pelt, and then he ate most of the bones. When the fullness came, the sleep came with it, and the sleep was the deepest thing he had ever felt. It came down on him like a landslide. He lay in the grit beside the bones of his first kill and slept through the night and most of the next day, and while he slept, the meat worked in him — the first law of his kind again: what he ate became him. His legs thickened. The first of his scales hardened, from pale to gray.

He woke bigger. He did not know that he was bigger. He only knew two things: that the world smelled stronger than it had, and that he was hungry again.

---

The great wings came back on the fifth day, and with them came the mountain's second rule.

He was on the warm rock above his hollow when the shadow crossed him. He looked up. A winged thing was crossing the valley, wide as three of him laid end to end, with a long neck and a calm, heavy certainty in every line of it — the certainty of great years. A small thing knows a large thing without being told. He knew.

It was not hunting him. It was not hunting anything. It was riding the warm air above the valley, as a leaf rides water, and the whole valley watched it, and nothing moved that did not have to.

Then the wings went down the far side of the valley, toward a stand of old trees, and something happened there that he saw only in pieces: a rush, a cracking of branches, a high, thin crying that stopped. When the wings came back up, they were carrying something.

He sat on his rock with his whole body still and his eyes wide, and something in him stood up and reached toward the wings.

This was the strangest thing that had ever happened to him. It was not fear. His fear was already there, flat and complete — the ordinary fear of a small thing under a big thing. This was underneath the fear, one layer lower, and it was a pull. It was the same hot, simple pull that had shaken him over the stone-runner, only aimed at something far taller. It said: I should have that. It said this about a creature that could have picked him up and carried him off without ever noticing he was there.

The pull rose until his legs trembled with it and his jaws ran wet. The wings passed over him, close enough that their wind pushed him flat against the rock, and nothing happened at all. They did not see him. They were not looking.

He stayed flat on that rock until dark.

This was the mountain's second rule, and it was harder than the first: what lives above the mountain is not food. Not yet. Not while the pull inside him could stand a small thing up at the edge of a rock, trembling, where the whole sky could see it. He never let the pull stand him up again. He would think about that rule for years afterward, in far worse places, with far better reasons. But the rule itself he learned completely, in one evening, and it never had to be taught twice.

---

After that, the days made their habit, and the habit was simple: hunger, hunt, feed, sleep, hunger. He grew. The first winter came at him slowly, as the cold had come through the shell — first uncomfortable, then hard. The stone-runners went fat, and then scarce. The beetles disappeared from the rock seam. The grass at the seep went brown. At night, the wind came down off the peaks with a smell like iron on it.

He was bigger now than the hatchling had been. The gray had come into his scales from nose to tail, and the muscle under them was real muscle, and he had learned to dig a stone-runner's crack out with his hands when the weather made the hunters lazy. He was still small. He knew that now, because he had the wings to measure himself against.

He was hungry all the time, and he did not die. Those were the two facts of his first season in the world, and they were enough.

Then, in the last gray days before the real cold, the wind brought him something new.

It came from beyond the shoulder of the mountain, from up past the pass, on a wind that had crossed snow to reach him. It was a thin smell, and it was not the smell of any beast, or any green thing, or any water. It was smoke, though he had no name for smoke and would not have one for years. Under the smoke there was a smell like wet iron, and a smell like the inside of a cave, and under both of those, faint and strange and impossible to place, the smell of something that walked on two legs.

He stood on the slope with his nose in the air for a long time.

Nothing in him had a shape for it. Nothing in his world had ever smelled like that. It was exactly the size of a fear, and yet it was not like any fear he had met, because fear comes with the thing that makes it, and this thing was far off and did not know about him at all. Somehow, that made it worse. He could not have explained why. He was not old enough to explain anything.

He did not go toward it. He did not run from it, either. He turned his back on the pass, went down the slope to his hollow, and slept badly. In the morning, the wind had turned, and the smell was gone.

He kept it anyway. He put it away in the same place he kept the beaks, and the wings, and the crack in the talus where the runners lived. There was nothing to do with it yet. There is nothing to do with a thing that far away. But he remembered it, and that was the whole of the danger: he was the kind of creature that remembers. This was his nature, as the slow thickening under his skin was his nature, and it would be both his wealth and his danger for the rest of his life.

The last of the warmth went out of the ground. The water at the seep froze at its edges at night, and the stone-runners went into their cracks for good. One evening he climbed the slope and stood looking down the long gray country — toward the side of the mountain where the smell had come from, and away from it, both at once.

The cold was coming. He did not know what winter was. He had never seen one.

He turned down into the hollow, out of the wind, and lay down with his head on his forelegs. The hunger said eat. There was nothing to eat. So he slept, and the first season of his life closed over him like water.

The winter would open it again. The winter had lessons of its own, and the first snow was already on its way down from the peaks.

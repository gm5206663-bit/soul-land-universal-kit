# Chapter 1: The First Hunger

The shell had been a wall for as long as he had been anything, and then it hurt.

That was the first thing: the wall pressing in from every side at once, water-tightness giving way to a thin, cold wet seeping in, and something in him that had been waiting a very long time deciding that it was done waiting. He pushed. The push was not a thing he chose. It came up out of his back and his neck and his legs together, like a cough coming up out of a chest, and the shell split somewhere near his head with a small sharp sound.

Light came through the split. It was white and thin and it hurt too, and he did not know it was light, only that the world beyond the shell was colder and louder than the world inside it.

He worked himself free, half pushing and half falling, and then he was lying on his side on hard wet ground with the pieces of the shell under him and the wind moving over his skin.

The wind was the second thing. It was cold and it did not stop, and his skin was wrong for it: soft, pale, thin, no armor to it at all. He curled up, and his own body was the only warm thing anywhere.

Something in him was already working. He had no word for it and no thought about it. Even then, in the first minutes, it was going quietly about its business as it had in the egg, taking the cold and pushing back with something that was not quite warmth and not quite skin, a slow thickening that would show only later. It never stopped. It never slept. Every hard thing that happened to him after that would be met by that slow answering.

He was hungry. That was the third thing, and it was the biggest.

The hunger did not arrive and leave like the wind. It lived in him, low and steady, older than his eyes. It was there before he had eyes to see the mountain with, and it would be there when his eyes were done. It said one word, always: eat.

So he turned his head and ate the shell.

The pieces were dry and sharp and they cut the inside of his mouth, and he ate them anyway, all of them he could reach, and then he lay still and felt something of the shell stay in him, a warmth that spread out through his belly and reached, faintly, into his legs and his tail. It was the first lesson the world gave him, and it was simple. What he ate became him.

---

The mountain was not built for something as small as he was.

He learned that in the first days. His scales were pale and soft and the cold went through them. His legs were thick and clumsy under a body too light for them. His tail was too long and got in the way when he turned, and his head was too heavy for his neck, so he moved as new things move: badly, and often downhill.

He fell off a rock shelf on the second day and lay at the bottom of it for a while, stunned, and then he got up and went on, because the hunger did not care about the fall.

The hunger kept his time. It rose and he obeyed it and it went quiet for a while, and the quiet was the only rest he had. He drank at a seep where the water came out of the stone cold and clean, and learned to come back to it. He learned where the sun lay longest on the rocks, and lay there. He learned to keep to the low ground when the wind got up.

He learned the slope as water learns it. By going down it many times.

There was a seam of rock near the top of his hollow that held the sun all afternoon, and beetles worked in the grit of it — the smallest soul beasts of the mountain, too small to have years worth counting, slow and armored and stupid. He learned to catch them. He got his first one with a clumsy snap of his jaws and crunched it shell and all, and the hunger said eat, and after that he went back to the seam every warm afternoon and crouched in the grit with his nose down, waiting.

There was a lower slope where the stone went soft and wet and the seep ran down it in a green thread, and blue silver grass grew along the thread. It was thin grass with a silver vein in every blade, the commonest soul beast of the whole world, and it was worth nothing to anything that counts years. He ate some of it anyway when the hunger was bad and there was nothing else. It tasted like wet stone. It kept him walking.

And there was a broken pine low on the slope with a hole in its heart, and things nested there. Small things with beaks.

He learned about the beaks on the third evening, when he put his head into the hole out of the plainest hunger he had ever felt. Something in the dark stabbed him above the eye, twice, fast, and he pulled his head back and the whole side of his face was suddenly hot and wet. He sat down in the fern and licked the hurt until it stopped, and after that he walked past the pine slowly, every time, on the downhill side.

That was the first rule the mountain taught him that he never once had to be taught again: the things that live in the dark can hurt you even when they are small.

---

The first true hunt was for the stone-runners.

They lived in the talus where the slope went wide — low, thick, quick things the color of the rock they lived in, with small eyes and a fatness about them that the hunger could feel from across the stones. They came out when the sun did and sat up on their haunches and looked around, and they ran between the rocks along paths they had worn smooth.

He wanted one. He wanted it as he had wanted the shell.

He went at the first one straight, fast, all at once, as the hunger told him to go. He was two lengths away when it saw him, and it was gone into a crack between two stones before he reached the spot, and he hit the boulder behind it shoulder-first and lay there with his ears ringing.

He tried again that evening. Same thing. Again the next morning.

The third morning he did not go at anything. He lay down in the sun on the warm side of the talus with his chin on his forelegs and watched.

He watched the runners for a whole day. He watched where they came out, and where they went back in, and which cracks were too narrow for a body his size, and which rocks they liked to sit on — the same three rocks, all morning, over and over. He watched one of them dig at the grit with its small hands and stand up and look at nothing at all for a long time.

Something in him settled while he watched. It was not that the hunger went away. It stayed exactly where it always was, low and steady, saying eat. But the running and snapping part of him went quiet and lay down behind the watching part, and the quiet felt like the right shape for the moment, though he had no words for it and never looked at it directly.

He lay on the warm rock until evening. Then he went down the talus on the downhill side, into the place where the runners' paths all came together between two boulders, and he got low in the grit there and waited.

It took a long time. The light went orange and then grey. Twice a runner came close and he felt the go-fast part of him jump in his chest, and both times he held it down and lay still, and both times the runner turned aside at the last moment and went another way.

Then one came along the path in the last of the light, hurrying, and it did not see him until it was almost on him. He came up out of the grit.

It broke left. He had watched them break left at that spot for two days. He was already moving left when it moved, and his jaws closed on it, and it was done.

The thing kicked twice and stopped. He stood over it with his whole body shaking, and the shaking was not fear. It was something else, hot and simple, and it wanted more.

He ate it down to the bones and the pelt and most of the bones.

When the fullness came, the sleep came with it, and the sleep was the deepest thing he had ever felt. It came down on him like stone out of the sky. He lay in the grit beside the bones of his first kill and slept through the night and most of the next day, and while he slept the meat worked in him, thickening his legs, hardening the first of his scales from pale to grey.

He woke bigger. He did not know that yet. He only knew that the world smelled stronger than it had, and that he was hungry again.

---

The great wings came back on the fifth day, and he learned the law of the mountain.

He was on the warm rock above his hollow when the shadow went over him. He looked up, and there was a winged thing crossing the valley, wide as three of him laid end to end, with a long neck and a stillness in how it held itself that he knew at once, as a small thing knows a large thing, without being told.

It was not hunting him. It was not hunting anything. It was riding the warm air above the valley as a leaf rides water, and the whole valley watched it, and nothing moved that did not have to.

Then the wings went down the far side of the valley to a stand of old trees, and something happened there that he saw only in pieces: a rush, a cracking of branches, a high thin crying that stopped. When the wings came back up, they carried something.

He sat on his rock with his whole body still and his eyes wide, and something in him stood up and reached toward the wings.

That was the strangest thing of his short life. It was not fear. His fear was already there, flat and complete, the fear of a small thing under a big thing. But under the fear, one layer lower, there was a pull. The same hot simple pull that had shaken him over the stone-runner, only taller. It said: I should have that. It said it about a creature that could have carried him off without noticing.

The pull rose until his legs were trembling with it and his jaws were wet, and the wings went by overhead, close enough that the wind of them pushed him flat against the rock, and nothing happened at all. They did not see him. They were not looking.

He stayed flat on the rock until dark.

That was the second rule, and it was harder than the first: what lives above the mountain is not food. Not yet. Not while the pull in him could make him stand up and shiver at the edge of a rock and be seen.

He never let the pull stand him up again. He would think about that rule for years afterward, in worse places, with better reasons.

---

The days made their habit after that.

Hunger, hunt, feed, sleep, hunger. The first winter came at him slowly — a cold that went from uncomfortable to hard, and the stone-runners going fat and then scarce, and the beetles gone from the rock seam, and the grass at the seep going brown, and the wind coming down off the peaks at night with a smell like iron on it.

He was bigger than the hatchling had been. The grey had come into his scales from nose to tail now, and the muscle under them was real muscle, and he had learned to dig out a stone-runner's crack with his hands when the weather made the hunters lazy. He was still small. He knew that now, because he had the wings to measure against.

He was hungry all the time, and he did not die, and those were the two small facts of his first season in the world.

Then, in the last grey days before the real cold, the wind brought him something and it was new.

It came from beyond the shoulder of the mountain, up beyond the pass, on a wind that had crossed snow to get to him. It was a thin smell, and it was not the smell of any beast or any green thing or any water. It was smoke, though he had no name for smoke. Under the smoke there was a smell like wet iron and a smell like the inside of a cave, and under both of those, faint and strange and impossible to place, the smell of something that walked on two legs.

He stood on the slope with his nose up for a long time.

Nothing in him had a shape for it. Nothing in his world had ever had it. It was exactly the size of a fear and it did not feel like any fear he had met yet, because fear came with the thing that made it, and this thing was far off and did not know about him at all. That made it worse, in a way he could not have explained to anyone.

He did not go toward it. He did not go away from it either. He turned his back on the pass and went down-slope to his hollow and slept badly, and in the morning the wind had turned and the smell was gone.

He kept it anyway. He put it in the same place he kept the beaks and the wings and the crack in the talus where the runners lived. There was nothing to do with it yet. There was nothing to do with a thing that far away.

But he remembered it, and that was the whole of the danger: he was the kind of creature that remembers.

When the last of the warmth went out of the ground, and the water at the seep froze at the edges at night, and the stone-runners went into their cracks for good, he climbed up the slope one evening and stood looking down the long grey country toward the smoke side of the mountain and away from it both.

The cold was coming. He did not know what winter was. He had never seen one.

He turned down into the hollow, out of the wind, and lay down with his head on his forelegs, and the hunger said eat, and there was nothing to eat, and so he slept.

That was the first season of the world.

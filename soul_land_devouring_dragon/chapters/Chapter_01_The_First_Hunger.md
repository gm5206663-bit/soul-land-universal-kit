# Chapter 1: The First Hunger

Before anything else there was the dark, and the tightness, and the hunger that was already there, because the hunger was the first thing of him, older than his eyes and older than his breath, and it was the hunger that broke the world.

And even in that first dark there had been more than hunger. Something had been at work in him from the first moment anything of him was, before the eyes, before the heart had its habit — at work quietly, never sleeping, never even waiting: knitting what was weak, deepening what was shallow, pressing shape into the smallness while the smallness grew. The egg had held him long, and it had held him well, because it had been built from within, and what came out of it that dawn on the cold scree of the mountain was not what his kind comes out of eggs as — hunger and flesh alone — but hunger and flesh with a FOUNDATION under them. He could not know it, and would not know it for a long time; but he was different from his own kind from the first breath, and the difference had been laid down in the dark, before the first breath.

The shell held him as long as it could. He did not know it as a shell; he knew it as everything, and everything had been getting smaller around him for as long as there had been anything of him to notice — the walls of it drawn close, the water in it spent, the tightness saying now in the only language there was, which was the language of his own body. He pushed against everything with what he had, and what he had was a head too big for him and a neck and the stubbornness of a hunger that had outgrown its house, and everything cracked, and the cold came in.

The cold was the second thing of him. It came against the wet of him and stood his newness up in a way that had no gentleness in it, and he worked himself out of the pieces of everything and lay where he had fallen, on the cold scree of a hollow in the mountain, while the sky he had never seen looked down at him without interest. Dawn was coming up behind the ridge. He did not know dawn either. He knew that the dark was thinning, and that the air moved, and that he was alone in a way he had no mind to measure, because there was no other smell like his own anywhere in the world, and he did not know enough yet to miss it.

He ate the pieces of everything, because they were there, and because the hunger said eat, and the hunger was the eldest thing in him and it was obeyed. That was the first eating, and it told him what eating was, which is to say it told him what he was, though he could not have heard it in words. The pieces went down, and did not merely stop the hunger; something of them stayed, a warmth that settled into his smallness and became a piece of him, and his blood knew what that meant even if nothing else of him did, the way blood knows. Eating was not ending. Eating was becoming.

Then the mountain had him.

---

He was small, and the mountain was not built for small things. His scales were new and soft over him, not the dark blue armor that would come but a pale thin skin of them that the cold got through; his limbs were thick and clumsy under him, built for a weight he did not have yet; his tail was too long for him and his head too big, and he moved the way newness moves, which is badly, and often downward. The hollow had kept the egg, a cup of scree under an overhang where the rain came in sideways and not straight, and he stayed in the hollow at first because the hollow was where he had begun, and beginning was the only reason he had for anything. The mountain set about making him fit to it from the first day, the way it sets about everything that means to stay: the rain came and went, and the new scales had closed over him harder than they came out of the shell, and the cold had stopped getting in where it had been getting in, and none of this was done by him or for him — it went on the way his heart went on, without consulting him, because that is the way his kind of growing grows.

The hunger kept his time. It came and it filled him and it drove him and it was paid, and then it came again, and the days were the spaces between. Rain came and lay on the mountain and made the scree smell like iron and deep cold, and he pressed into the driest of the hollow and endured it the way all small things endure weather, without complaint, because there was nothing to complain to. When it passed, the mountain steamed, and everything alive moved again, and so did the hunger.

He learned the slope the way water learns it, by going down it many times. There was a seam of rocks that held the sun when the sun came, and beetles — the smallest soul beasts of the slope, below the counting of years — worked in the grit of it, slow and armored and stupid, and the hunger said eat, and he ate them, crunching shell and all, because the hunger did not argue with shells. There was a seep of water down the slope where the stone went soft and green with blue silver grass — the commonest soul beast of the world, thin and silver-veined and worth nothing to anything that counts years — and he learned the taste of it, cold and full of small living things. There was a broken pine low on the slope with a hole in its heart where things nested, and he learned to pass it slowly, because the things that nested there had beaks, and beaks were one of the first words the mountain taught him.

The first true hunt was for the stone-runners.

They lived in the talus where the slope went wide — low, thick, quick things the color of the rock they lived in, with small eyes and a fatness about them that the hunger could feel from across the stones. They ran between the rocks in paths they had worn, and they came out when the sun did, and they sat up on their haunches and looked about them the way the eaten look about them before they are eaten, which is without believing it can happen.

He went at the first one the way the hunger told him to go, which was straight and fast and all at once, and the stone-runner was not there when he arrived. It was gone into the rocks like water, and his jaw came down on stone, and the pain of it went up into his head, and the slope was empty around him, and the hunger had nothing for him but itself.

A thing of his kind would have raged. It was in him to rage — he felt it rise, the red pull to tear and take and fill, the bigness of the want — and he stood in it for a breath with his claws in the grit. And then the rage went down again, because something in him was quieter than the rage, the way the rock is quieter than the wind, and what it said was wait. He did not decide it. It came into him the way cold comes into stone, all the way through, and he settled himself in the shadow of a rock where the sun made a door of light on the slope, and he was still.

There was something else in the hunting, too, and it had never been taught, because it had never needed teaching. A stillness fell over the small lives when he came near — a cowering of the little bones, a lowering of the small voices — as though the slope itself turned its voice down before him, and it was so in a way he did not know and did not need to know. He was a dragon, though the word had never come to him, and the low things of the world hear dragons in the blood, from before learning, and bow in the blood without knowing. His blood was young and his blood was thin, and the weight of it was a small weight yet, but it was there the way the dawn was there, and the eaten felt it, and it helped the hunger in its small uncounted ways.

The stillness was not in his kind. Stillness is a thing a beast learns when it has been taught by being empty, and his kind does not learn it; his kind takes, and takes, and is taken. But the quiet thing in him had the whole slope for a teacher, and the slope was patient, and he was new, and newness is the one time anything is allowed to be taught. He waited while the sun crossed the door of light. He waited while his own small heart went and went. And when the stone-runner came out of the rocks into the warmth, sure of its world the way the eaten are sure, he was already moving, and the mountain let him have it.

The eating of it was deep. The first prey is always deep, because the body has been waiting for it since before the breath began, and the warmth that came into him out of the stone-runner was more than warmth; it laid itself along him and became him, scale and claw and the strength in the thick legs, and the blood said the thing it had been saying since the shell — becoming, becoming — and the mountain had charged him for the lesson, and he had paid once, and the payment was the whole of the payment, and that was a thing he never once, all his life afterward, forgot how to do: pay once.

When the fullness came, the sleep came with it. It came down on him the way stone comes down out of the sky, without warning and without refusal, a deep black sleep that took him where he lay with the blood of the hunt still warm in him, and he did not know — nothing could have told him — that it was in the deep sleep that the mountain paid back what it had charged, and that he would not wake the same smallness that had gone down.

---

He woke colder, because the sun had moved, and bigger, because he had slept, and hungry again, because that was the law of him, and each of these was ordinary. What was not ordinary was the world.

The world had grown. That was the only way he had of knowing it, because he did not know that it was he who had changed. The slope below the hollow, which had been a blur of grays and greens, was a country now, with shapes in it and distances; the wind, which had been one thing, was many things, and each of them had a place it came from and a story; the far side of the valley, which had been a wall, had folds in it, and the folds had shadows, and the shadows had sizes. He stood at the mouth of the hollow and took all of it in with the newness of his eyes and ears, and did not marvel, because beasts do not marvel at their own growing; he only turned his head to the wind the way a thing turns that means to use it.

It was on that wind that the fear came to him for the first time.

It came before anything else of itself came — before sound, before shadow, before the great wings that came sailing down the valley on the morning air, wide as the world and quiet as death. Something in him went flat against the scree before he had any knowing of why, every new scale of him pressed to the rock, his smallness gathered under him like a thing hidden, and the great shadow crossed the slope and crossed him and went on down the valley, and nothing happened. That was the mercy of it, and the lesson of it was the same: that there were things above him the way he was above the beetles, and that the distance between them and him was not a distance he could argue with, and that the mountain was not making a threat. The mountain was teaching. Eat what is beneath you. Fear what is above you. It went into him the way all the mountain's teaching went in — not as a thought, because there was no room in him yet for thoughts, but as a shape, the way water keeps the shape of the stone it has been poured over. He kept that shape all his life, and it saved it.

The wings came back down the valley before the sun was high, and this time they did not sail. They folded, and fell, and the slope below the broken pine exploded in beaks and dust and the scream of the nesting things, and the great hunter took what it had come for and rose again, heavy and slow, and went up over the ridge with its prey. He watched it from the shadow of his rock, flat and still, and felt the fear, deep and right, and under the fear a pull, red and hot, that wanted to go after it and take the prey from it and kill it and eat it, and was bigger than the fear, and would have killed him, and he did not follow it, because the quiet thing in him held him to the rock, and the wings went over the ridge, and the dust settled, and the mountain went on.

That was the bloodthirst, and he had met it for the first time, and neither of them knew the other yet.

---

The days made their habit. The hunger came and was paid and came again; the rains came and were endured; the sun crossed its door of light and he waited in the shadow of it, and the slope gave him its stone-runners one at a time in the way the mountain gives everything, which is slowly, and in exchange for patience. He grew the way the days were growing him, which was on every side at once: the new scales darkening from pale to blue, the legs finding their thickness, the claws coming to their cold shine, the tail learning the balance it had been built for. The hollow grew too small for him without his noticing, the way a house grows too small while the one living in it is asleep.

It was in that time that the wind brought him the strangeness.

It came from beyond the shoulder of the mountain, down-slope of the far pass, on a wind that had changed its mind in the night, and it came into his world the way a stone comes into still water. Smoke. He knew fire by then, because the mountain had its own fires sometimes, far off in the dry season, and he knew smoke as the smell of heat eating wood. But this smoke was a tamed thing, small and kept and tended, and in it and under it and all around it were smells that had no place in his world at all. A sourness that was not any beast's. A sharpness that was cold the way nothing living is cold. A heaviness that moved through the other smells the way a stone moves through water, without belonging. And under all of it, carried thin and wrong, the smell of other hungrinesses that walked upright and killed with their hands and kept the heat in houses.

He had no shelf for it. Nothing in him had a shape for it, because nothing in his world had ever had it, and a beast's mind does what a beast's mind does with the shapeless, which is to feel it in the body and let the body decide. His body decided without him. The new scales along his neck stood up of themselves. He turned his back to that wind, and stood facing the forest, and the hair of his nature went and lay down in the direction of the trees, and he did not know why, and did not need to know why, because the mountain had already taught him the shape of the law: that some things are above you, and you fear them, and live.

He did not go toward it. He did not go away from it either, because a beast does not flee a direction; it only knows one. He went on with the slope and the stone-runners and the door of light, and the strangeness stayed where it was, beyond the shoulder of the mountain, small and kept and tended, and the world held both of them, the way the world holds everything, without saying which of them was the future.

---

When the cold season began to think about coming, and the stone-runners grew fat and slow and scarce, he did the thing his nature had been pointing him toward since the shell, which was to go deeper. Not far. Not yet. Down the slope and along the seam of rocks to where the scree ended and the trees began — the first trees of the great forest, standing at the mountain's hem like the first words of a story too long to hear the end of. He stood at the edge of them with the wind in his face and the dusk coming down the valley, small and blue-scaled and alone in a way that nothing else in the world was alone, because there was nothing else in the world that was what he was.

The dark came down, and the forest breathed in the dark, full of lives and hungers and laws, and he went in among the first of the trees and found a den under the roots of a fallen giant, and he lay down in it with the night moving around him, and the hunger asleep in him for the first time in a day, and he did not know what he was, and did not need to know what he was, because knowing what he was had never once been any beast's way of being what it was.

He knew where he was. He knew what to do. The mountain had taught him, and the mountain was still teaching, and the teaching was not finished, and he was made to learn.

That was enough for the first season of the world.

# Chapter 2: The Law of the Snow

The first snow came in the night.

He woke in his hollow with the cold on his back, and when he came out, the world had turned white. Snow lay over the whole slope, soft and deep, and when he stepped into it his legs went in to the knee, and the cold of it came up through the pale scales of his belly and stayed there.

The snow changed every rule the little Devouring Dragon had learned, and it changed them all at once. The slope he knew was gone under it. The rock seam was gone. The summer line of water at the seep was gone. The talus where his paths ran was a white field now, with the tops of the stones standing out of it. Nothing looked like anything. He stood in the middle of it with the hunger already up, and looked for one thing that made sense, and found nothing.

The hunger did not care that the world had changed. It said eat, and there was nothing to obey it with. That was the first day.

On the second day, he began learning the snow's own smell. This is what saved him.

A nose does not stop at a white field; it reads through. That was the first thing the cold taught him, and it taught it slowly, one failed hunt at a time. Snow had a smell of its own, thin and flat. Under that smell, if the wind was wrong or the day was old, everything buried was a secret, and he went hungry. But on a still morning, with the frost hard and the air clean, the snow carried smells upward, as water carries oil. He learned to stand with his nose up on those mornings and read what the snow had buried: a hare-kind sleeping under a drift, a fox-kind's line running down a gully, old blood two days cold where something had died. He learned all of this the same way he had learned the slope in summer — by being wrong about it, and paying for being wrong.

The stone-runners saved him. They did not leave the talus in the cold.

He found this out by lying beside a crack through most of one freezing afternoon, waiting for the hunger to answer a question he had not worked out yet. Nothing came out of the crack. But under his chin, through the rock and the snow over it, he could feel a slow, warm breathing. The stone-runners were down in their cracks for the winter, fat and deep and stupid, curled together.

He dug one out with his hands.

The digging took most of the afternoon and left his claws split and blunt. He got one runner out of its crack and ate it in two bites, and then he dug for the next crack, and the next. By the end of that day his claws had changed — thick and flat, made for digging — and something inside him had learned what digging was for, the same way his body had learned the hiding crouch in the summer. He did not think about any of it. It simply happened, day after day, as the cold made his belly want more. The mountain was teaching him. The mountain never explained a lesson. It only paid him when he was right, and let him hurt when he was wrong.

---

He saw men for the first time in the middle of the cold.

It was a late afternoon, the light going thin and gray, and he was up on the high rock above his hollow, where the wind had swept the stone bare. Far down the slope, near the pass trail, something was moving that was not a beast.

There were beasts there, and not-beasts. The beasts he knew at once for what they were: spirit hounds, soul beasts of the dog kind, kept and bred by men — though these were bigger and thicker than any he had ever met. They ran loose in the snow with their heads down and their cry going up in pieces, and they ran on lines, the whole pack working together, each hound reading its own trail.

Behind the hounds, walking upright on two legs, came the not-beasts.

Men. He flattened on the rock and watched them come down the pass trail. They were slow, and they were loud, and they were wrapped in the hides of other beasts, and they carried long, thin things over their shoulders. The gray light caught the ends of those things and turned them into points of fire.

The spirit hounds ran a deer-kind out of the lower woods, and he watched the whole thing happen.

The deer-kind broke from the treeline into the open snow. One of the upright things stopped walking, put the long thin thing against its shoulder, and there was a flat, hard sound, like a stick breaking. The deer-kind fell in the middle of its stride and did not get up.

It fell before the nearest hound had taken one step toward it. He lay on the rock with his jaws shut and his whole body still. There was no wound on the deer-kind that he could see at that distance. Nothing had touched it. And it was dead.

This was the lesson, and it went into him and stayed there for the rest of his life: the death those creatures carried had no length to it. It reached across the whole slope and took a life out of the middle of a stride. Distance did not protect. Nothing he had learned in his first season protected against it. He would never have to be taught this twice.

The hounds reached the deer-kind and stood around it, crying. The upright things came up and did things to it with their hands and their thin, bright blades. Then one of the hounds stopped.

One of the spirit hounds lifted its head.

The wind had turned. He felt it come up the slope against his face, and he saw the hound's head come up out of its work and turn toward the high ground where he lay, and he knew, absolutely and at once, that the wind was carrying his smell down to the dogs' noses. He was afraid. He did not move.

Running was the thing his body wanted, and his body was wrong. Running is a thing a hunter sees, and what a hunter sees, it follows. He pressed himself flat into the hollow of the rock and laid his whole weight along the stone, the same way he lay over the wettest mosses in the full sun of summer, and the wind went over him and down the slope. Below, the hound stood with its nose up and its ears forward, asking the air a question.

The wind kept going. It carried his smell down the slope — and then it shifted, rolled, and took his smell past the hound and away into the empty country to the west. The question went unanswered.

The hound stood a while longer with its nose up. Then it dropped its head and went back to the meat. The upright things finished their work and took the deer-kind away on a drag of wood, and the snow slope stood empty and quiet under the gray sky. He lay on his rock until the light was gone.

Men. That was the rule of the place, though he had no words for it. Men, and their kept spirit hounds, and the death that had no length. It changed the map of his mountain more than the snow had.

---

The lights came out on the dark evenings after that.

They stood beyond the shoulder of the mountain, down the pass side, small and orange, and they did not move. He could see them from the high rock on clear nights — three of them, sometimes four, in a row. And every time he saw them, the red thing inside him stood up and reached toward them.

That was the second thing he learned that winter. There was a hunger in him that pointed. It pointed at the fires without knowing what fires were. The pull came up through his chest and his legs, hot and simple, and it said: go down, take, eat. It was bigger than the hunger of any empty day. It was the biggest thing in him.

But there was a line, and he did not cross it.

He stood at the treeline on the coldest evening, with the lights hanging below him in the dark and the pull standing up in him, and he did not go. He stood there until his legs were shaking and the smell of smoke was in his throat. Then he turned back, went up to his hollow, and lay down. The pull stayed with him all night, the way an old wound stays with a body.

Why did he not go? He did not know. There had been no fear in it, and there had been no thinking in it. Some part of him, older and slower than the pull, had looked at the whole shape of his short life and said: not yet. And the pull could not bend that part of him. That night was the first night he carried the red thing without using it. His body remembered how, and it would remember for the rest of his life.

---

The offal brought it to a head.

Late in the cold, hunger drove him down toward the pass trail, where he had not gone since the deer-kind fell. He found a kill there, or what was left of one: bones, a hide, and a red mess of gut and scraps that the men had left on the snow when they went.

He ate it. Carrion was carrion, and the hunger was real. But while he ate, he kept his nose working, because for his kind, eating and reading are both halves of a meal. Through the smell of the dead thing, he caught something else.

Beast-smell. Strong, and fresh, laid over the offal by something that had been at the same meat very recently.

He got his head up out of the offal, jaws red. The smell came from the rocks above the trail, and as he read it, the rocks moved.

A cat came off them. It was gray and big and thick-furred, made heavy for the cold, and it came down the rocks at him without a sound. It was on him before the go-fast part of him had finished waking. Claws went across his back and opened the hide there in four long lines. Teeth found the loose skin behind his shoulder and tore it. The two of them went over into the snow together, the cat came up on top, and it was faster than anything he had ever met.

He had never fought anything. That was the true trouble, and it is worth saying plainly. The stone-runners ran. The beetles crunched. In his whole life, nothing had ever fought him back. The hill-cat was his first real enemy, and it was better than the little Devouring Dragon at everything except one thing.

He was heavier than he looked. And he had a jaw.

The cat raked him twice more, opening his flank and his shoulder, and he took both of them, because taking them was the only way to get close. He gave ground. He rolled with the blows instead of into them, as a leaf goes with the water, and the cat took the ground and came for his throat, as its kind always does.

When the throat came, his jaws closed on the back of the cat's neck.

Then it was simple. The cat fought as a beast fights when its whole life is suddenly held in one place, and he held on, and the holding was quiet and long. The snow went red under them both. After a while, the cat's strength came apart into a long shivering, and stopped.

He let go and stood over it. His legs were shaking. His own blood was running down his flank into the snow. Then he did the other thing, the thing his kind does first and never thinks about.

He ate.

He ate the hill-cat down to the bones and the hide and the cold of it, and the fullness came up over the hunger — and this time, the red thing came with it. The boil. The old rage of his kind. The thing that wants the whole world torn and eaten, and that would kill him one day for wanting it. He stood over the bones with the red thing standing in him, and his blood on him, and the lights of the men burning down in the dark below, and for a while he did not know what he was going to do.

Then the sleep came down on him, and the red thing went under with it. He slept in the open snow beside the bones of the cat — through the night, and the next day, and another night. When he woke, the world was louder and nearer than before. The wound-lines on his back had closed into four pale scars that would stay as long as he lived, and the scales over his shoulders had gone from gray to a deeper gray that light did not go through.

He was bigger. The cold was less. And he could feel, faintly and without knowing how he knew, that his nose had grown sharper again.

That was the winter: a white country, a louder world, and the first enemy that ever tested him — carried in the scars behind his jaw, where the fur never grew right again.

---

Late in the season, in the waits between storms, he found the lodge empty.

He came on it from upwind, following a smoke-smell that had gone cold and strange, and he found the timber-and-stone place standing in the snow at the head of a little valley, with its door open and its inside dark. He went around it twice, at a distance, reading. Fire-ash, days old. The smell of men, thick inside, fading at the edges. No sound at all.

He went closer. Snow had drifted over the doorway. The fire-stones inside were gray and dead, and the smell of the men who had built the place hung inside it so heavy that the whole lodge smelled like a wound.

Men had been here, and men had gone. The shelter they built was empty, and it stayed empty, and something in him stood at the threshold and read that, and did not go in.

The empty lodge taught him what men were, better than the kill on the trail had. They were not like the badgers that bolt, or the cats that come back. They built a place and lived in it, and then the whole breathing of them went out of it at once — they went somewhere, together, on some errand he could not see, and the errand took all of them. When men go, they go as a season ends: all at once, and everything at the same time.

He left the lodge standing empty behind him and went back up the slope. Men breathe like a season. That was the keep, and he kept it whole: they come, and they go, and their going is bigger than anything he could read.

The storms thinned after that. There was a day of dripping, and a night when the cold came loose, and then a morning when a whole slope of the snow had gone gray and soft, and the water under it was moving.

He stood on the warm rock that afternoon, in the sun — thin, hungry, and bigger than he had been when the snow came — and watched the mountain start giving itself back. The first season had taught him water, hunger, wind, and stone. The first winter had taught him snow, men, and the red thing inside himself. He climbed down into his hollow with the sun on his back and the smell of wet ground rising up the slope, and something in him — the deepest thing, the thing that had been counting his life without ever saying a number — sat up and said one word, low and firm:

More.

---

At the lodge, the fire had burned down to coals. Two of the men sat at it with their boots off and their hands out to the heat.

"The deer runs are thinning," said the older one. He had a knife and a stick, and he was working the stick down to a point, slow, a shaving at a time.

"They thin every year," said the other. "That is what winter does to deer."

"I mean the runs are thinning for us." The old one turned the stick and looked at the point. "There is a hole somewhere in the lower woods that is taking more than we take. Three clean kills this month, and on two of them so little meat was left that the ravens had been first."

"Cat?"

"Cat eats where it kills. This moves."

"Bear moves."

"Not in the low woods, and not this season." The old man turned the stick once more. "You go out with the hound tomorrow, and you read the ground where the third kill was. Read it before noon, when the light is flat."

The other man said nothing for a while. The fire spat.

The door came open, and the third one came in with a hound behind him, and the cold came in with both of them. He shut the door with his heel and stood over the fire with his hands out.

"Snow on the pass," he said. "To the knee, and deeper above the third turn."

"To the knee," said the old one, "and a month of it yet to come."

"The trap held?"

"The line held. The dogs read it three times, and there was nothing in it."

"Then the pass is finished for the year," said the old one. "On paper it is open another month. On the ground, it is finished tomorrow. The ring-light is what the buyers want now, and the buyers pay for ring-light, not for pelts."

"Pelts are pelts," said the younger one at the fire.

"Pelts are pelts," the old one said. "And the boy could have taken a light off a hare-kind years since. He went up the pass with his father's bow instead, like his father."

"He is going out with the light next season."

"He is going out with his bow," said the old one. "The light comes when the ground gives it, and the ground is giving less every year. That is a thing the young ones have not noticed yet."

"And if the thing in the lower woods comes up to the pass?"

"Then it comes up." The old one turned the stick once more, and threw it on the fire. "And we will hear about it from the dogs, and the dogs will decide what we do. That is what we keep them for."

The dogs were asleep in the straw by the door in a heap. One of them lifted its head at the sound of its own name, and put it down again.

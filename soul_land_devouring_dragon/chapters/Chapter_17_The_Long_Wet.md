# Chapter 17: The Long Wet

The rain came up the country in the middle of the spring, and after the first week it did not stop for more than a day at a time.

The streams came up first and then went over their banks, and the low water spread out of them across the flats until the whole country south of the fern edge was one brown sheet with the tops of bushes standing out of it. The meat came up out of it at the start. The slow heavy kind that could not swim well pushed up onto the ridges, and he took two of them on the second and third days, easy, without a fight, standing in water to his chest to do it, and eating on the high ground with the flood running past below him in the dark. Then the water spread further, and the meat went out of the country: the slow kind went north toward the dry ridges, and what was left in the low land was water and birds.

He moved up onto the stone. His old den under the shelf of roots was under the flood, and he went back to the ravine den in the outer forest, up on high rock with the stream running past the mouth of it. He stood in the mouth of it on the first night and looked out at his own country under the water. Rain came off the lip of the den in one long line, and the flood smelled of wet earth and drowned grass. He was hungry inside a week.

The flood brought him one gift before it finished rising. A day after he moved to the rock a heavy grazer came down the current, dead and turning, drowned somewhere up in the low country and carried along like a log. He went into the water for it where the stream ran wide over a shelf, caught it in the current and worked it out onto the stone. It was old and the flood had been at it, but it was meat, and it had cost him no print at all — the water had written it, and the water would take its own writing back. He ate there on the bare rock in the rain for two days, with the dark water going past and the crows standing off in the pines.

---

He learned the rain's hours.

The wet country writes down everything. Mud, soft moss, the flats — even the hard grass went soft enough to take a print, and a print that stays is a thing men can carry away in their heads. He worked it out in the first weeks: in the heaviest rain, nothing keeps. He crossed the open ground only in the middle of a downpour, and he stood and watched his own print go. The rain filled the pad first, then the toes, and then the edges slumped. Inside the hour there was a shallow place in the mud the size of his foot, and no more shape to it than a puddle. Nothing to read.

So he took his hunting routes along the rock bars, where the streams ran over flat stone, and slept on stone, and drank where the water came off ledges rather than from the banks.

It cost him. Hungry as he was, he passed up two kills in the first month of it, both of them in soft ground, and once he stood over a stranded beast on a mud island and did not take it and went away upstream. He could not eat a meal and unmake the print he had made getting to it. So he learned where to stand and how to wait, and he ate what came to the rock, which was little. The cold water ran off him all day. His ribs came up under the hide again for the first time since his first winter, and he slept light.

The ravine den was a good place for a wet like that one. It sat high on the rock with its mouth looking down the stream, and the water ran past below the lip of it and covered the sound of anything moving in the country. He slept with his back to the stone wall and his head toward the mouth, and the stream talked all night, and the rain came and went outside like something walking around the hill. Two of the small kind lived in the rocks above the den that season: a stone-runner and a fat grey burrower. They got used to him, or as near to it as their kind ever comes. By the end of the wet they went about their business on the same shelf while he lay in the mouth of the den with one eye on the whole of it.

The hunger used the thin weeks. It came up in him after every mouthful, and on the days he ate nothing it came up anyway, and asked its question. The question was not meat. He lay in the mouth of the ravine den in the wet dark with the water going past, and the pull toward the deep forest was there the same as always, far off, under the great beasts, and the answer was the same as always: not yet, and not food.

Twice in that month he saw their smoke off to the north, on the high ground above his old low country. It hung low and grey in the rain and did not move. He knew the place it came from without going near it, and he did not go near the high ground, not once, and when the wind came off it he took his water lower down.

---

A light came over the high ground near the end of the wet, on a night when the rain let up for an hour before the next band of it came through.

He was on the rim of the high ground, hunting the dry edges above the flood, when is came up over the northern lip of the country: a light, small and white, standing up off the ground where no fire should be. He went still and read the air. No smoke belonged to it. It did not move the way a fire moves; it stood, one long thread of light in the rain-glare, up in the middle of the high ground where the ground was bare and the watching country was.

He knew what a light rising and standing meant. He had seen it done in his first years, to the quick slender beast in the fern country, and twice again at the wet breaks and the high fern, both times read from the ground after. When a beast dies with its light still in it, the light stands up out of the body and burns where it stands, and men with their iron and their boxes come and take it away. He had never watched one stand from the beginning, the whole length of it, before this night.

He lay on the rim and watched it. It stood in the rain for a while, one clean line of light in all that grey, and it did not move and it did not shorten. Then it was gone — not faded and not fallen, just gone, the way lights go when the men's work reaches them. After it went out he stayed a long while in the fern with the rain starting again on his back. He worked at the thing the way he worked at a track. Something up there had died and left its light standing. The men had been close enough to take it. There was nothing else to know about it, and he would not go and look.

He went down to his rock and slept.

---

He read the whole of it from the ground ten days after they left.

The wet was going out by then and the rock was drying in patches, and he came down off the ravine rim on the stone side and read the place from outside first. The wire was gone from both sides. The lean-to was gone, and the poles' holes in the moss were the only marks where it had been. The fire ring was scattered. There were boots all over the grass side, and drag-marks, and then, out on the rock past the edge of the pan, cut bones, picked at by crows and rained on for a week.

He walked out to the bones and read them. Heavy, slow kind, young, of the low water. One of the herd that the flood had driven up onto the high ground, just as it had driven him. It had come up through the wet country and gone into the men's wire in the dark and died there.

He read the kill the wire had made. The beast had come in from the low side and hit the wire low, and gone round the pan once, fast, dragging the stake, and the men had worked it half a night with their poles. It was all there in the ground if a body took the time: the circle, the drag, the deep pressed places where it went down and got up again, the boot marks closing in when it stopped. It had come up through the wet country and gone into the men's wire in the dark and died there. The bones were cut the way men cut what they will carry, and the hide was gone, and the skull had been left where the crows could get at it. Whatever light that body had carried had stood up over this rock, some wet night while he lay hungry in his den, and the men had taken it down the country with the rest.

Then he walked the pan, and the pan had one thing to say, and it said it in every line it had. The season lay written across it: the hound's circles round the edges, the poles' holes, the boots, and the great smeared track of an ox that had come up out of the low water and died there. He walked the whole pan twice, and in the middle of all of it there was not one print of his own.

A whole wet, soft as the country would ever be, and the page had written everybody who walked on it except him.

He stood at the north edge of the pan a while, a good way off from the four stones. He understood the trick now, whole; not the why of the stones, but the length of the thing. He had kept his body out of his own country's writing for a year and a half of seasons, and the price of it was the lean weeks in the ravine and the kills passed up and the ribs under the hide. He paid it again that morning, standing on wet stone, and did not go near the mud.

---

The wet broke in the last days of the season. The water went down off the flats, the streams came back inside their banks, and the low country came out from under the flood brown and soft and rich, with the drowned grass lying flat and the new shoots already coming through it.

He went down for the first time in two months and drank at the stream's edge where the water was still quick, and then he hunted. The first kill after the flood was an old grazer standing in the shallows where the flats were coming out: no fight and no long stalk, just the bank and the shoulder and the water closing over the two of them. The water was still high and quick everywhere, and his print in the shallows filled and went before he had finished eating. He ate deep for the first time in a season, and the hunger stood up over the full belly and asked its question, the same as ever.

The water was going down. The low country was coming back, and the meat with it, and the road between the two countries would be open again before the warm season.

He slept that night on bare rock, well back from the mud, with his country under him and the deep forest standing north in the dark, and the pull under the great beasts where it had always been.

In the morning he crossed to the ridge above the pan one last time before the season turned. The four stones stood in their row. The pan held its page, with the ox written across it and nothing else, and the crows were working at the cut bones on the rock. Then he went down to the low country, where the work was, and left the high ground to its stones.

The spring was coming up the whole country by then. The road between the two countries was open again, and the low water was clear and quick where it had been brown and slow. He went down the long slope on the second morning after the break and put his nose into the new grass at the edge of the fern. Shoots, small feet, old scent washing out of the ground. The country turned over from one year to the next, the way it had turned every year of his life.

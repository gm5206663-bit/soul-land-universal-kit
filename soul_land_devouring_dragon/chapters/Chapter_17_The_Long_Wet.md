# Chapter 17: The Long Wet

The rain came up the country in the middle of the spring, and after the first week it did not stop for more than a day at a time. The three of them went up onto the high ground in it. Xiao An walked in front with the hound on the line, Gao Tie came behind her with the wire and the poles on his back, and the younger man carried the packs and said what he thought of the weather. The rock was slick all the way up and the dry grass lay flat and grey under the water. When they came out on the pan, the four stones were standing in a row where they had been set, and the whole floor of the pan was soft.

"First thing, before it bakes," said Gao Tie.

He set the wire low across the two ways onto the pan, the stone side and the grass side, and staked it down tight. Then they built a lean-to against the shelf of old rock, poles first and cut branches over them, with the mouth of it turned away from the wind. Nobody had to be told to keep off the mud. By the second evening the pan lay open and soft and blank under the rain, and the three of them sat in the lean-to and watched it.

---

That was the week he left the low country. The two streams came up first and then went over their banks, and the low water spread out of them across the flats until the whole country south of the fern edge was one brown sheet with the tops of bushes standing out of it. The meat came up out of it at the start. The slow heavy kind that could not swim well pushed up onto the ridges, and he took two of them on the second and third days, easy, without a fight, standing in water to his chest to do it. Then the water spread further, and the meat went out of the country: the slow kind went north, and what was left in the low land was water and birds.

He moved up onto the stone. His old den under the shelf of roots was under the flood, and he went back to the ravine den in the outer forest, up on high rock with the stream running past the mouth of it. He stood in the mouth of it on the first night and looked out at his own country under the water. Rain came off the lip of the den in one long line, and the flood smelled of wet earth and drowned grass. He was hungry inside a week.

---

On the high ground the days were all the same. Rain in the morning, rain at noon, rain at dark. The pan kept its blank face. The hound worked round it every day, and the rain took her tracks out of the mud faster than she could make them, which she did not like; on the third day she went to the middle of the pan, put her nose down where the one print had been, and stood there with her tail low. There was nothing there. The wet had taken the print early in the season, as it takes everything.

"Nothing," said Xiao An.

"There has never been anything," said the younger man. He was sitting in the mouth of the lean-to with his coat over his head. "We are three days up here, and the pan is a soup bowl."

"Four days."

"Four days," he said, "and the lodge is paying by the day, and the season is a season. It rained harder last year and we were warm."

"We were south last year," said Gao Tie. "The south has trees."

"So we watch a bowl of soup."

"We watch what walks in it," said Xiao An. "The mud is doing the work. If anything heavy crosses this pan this spring, we will know its weight and the shape of its foot, and the shape does not lie."

"If anything comes."

"Yes," said Xiao An. "If."

The young man put his head back down. The hound came in under the branches and shook herself over all three of them and lay down against Gao Tie's leg, and Gao Tie put his coat over her without saying anything. She was slower than she had been. He had never said a word about it, and the other two had watched it all winter and said nothing either.

---

He learned the rain's hours.

The wet country writes down everything. Mud, soft moss, the flats, even the hard grass went soft enough to take a print, and a print that stays is a thing men can carry away in their heads. He worked it out in the first weeks: in the heaviest rain, nothing keeps. He crossed the open ground only in the middle of a downpour, and he stood and watched his own print go. The rain filled the pad first, then the toes, and then the edges slumped. Inside the hour there was a shallow place in the mud the size of his foot, and no more shape to it than a puddle. Nothing to read. Then he took his hunting routes along the rock bars, where the streams ran over flat stone, and slept on stone, and drank where the water came off ledges rather than from the banks.

It cost him. Hungry as he was, he passed up two kills in the first month of it, both of them in soft ground, and once he stood over a stranded beast on a mud island and did not take it and went away upstream. He could not eat a meal and unmake the print he had made getting to it. So he learned where to stand and how to wait, and he ate what came to the rock, which was little. The cold water ran off him all day. His ribs came up under the hide again for the first time since his first winter, and he slept light.

The hunger used the thin weeks. It came up in him after every mouthful, and sometimes on the days he ate nothing it came up anyway, and asked its question. The question was not meat. He lay in the mouth of the ravine den in the wet dark with the water going past, and the pull toward the deep forest was there the same as always, far off, under the great beasts, and the answer was the same as always: not yet, and not food.

Twice in that month he saw their smoke off to the north, on the high ground above his old low country. It hung low and grey in the rain and did not move, and he knew the place it came from without going near it: the marked pan, the four stones, the men come back to read what the country wrote. They wanted the country's writing, and the country wrote everything that walked it, and now the men sat beside the page all season. He did not go near the high ground, not once, and when the wind came off it he took his water lower down.

---

Two days before the wet broke, the wire took something. It happened at the black hour of the night, and it came to all three of them through the ground before it came through the air: a shock in the wire, a sound of wood pulling, and then a heavy body going round and round in the dark twenty paces away. The hound was up before any of them, standing at the end of her short line with her whole body shaking one long note, and Gao Tie had his hand on her collar and his eyes on nothing.

"Round the pan," he said. "It is in the wire over the grass side."

"It sounds big," said the younger man. He had the iron in his hands. "Let me—"

"Put that down," said Gao Tie. "Iron in the dark in the rain gets one of us. Get the long poles and get behind it and stay out of its feet."

They worked the thing half a night. It was too dark to see more than the shape of it, wet and turning, and it was strong: once it came down off the wire and went across the corner of the pan, and they saw the size of it against the sky. Then it went back into the wire and stayed, and it tired. By the end of the night it stood still with its front legs in the wire, its head down, and blew.

When the grey came they saw it properly: a young ox of the low country, one of the heavy slow kind, drowned out of the flats like everything else. Its coat was plastered flat and its horns were short and pale, and it stood in the wire with its sides heaving and its head turned to the pan, and it looked at them as a beast looks at men when it has run out of everything.

"Ah," said the younger man, and lowered the iron.

Nobody spoke for a while. The rain came down on all of it.

"It is not what the wire was set for," said Xiao An.

"No," said Gao Tie. "But it came up onto the pan, and it has been through the middle of the page, and we cannot leave it in the wire." He looked at the ox as at a problem in a snare. "Kill it clean and take the meat up, or cut it loose and watch it go."

"Cut it loose," said the younger man. "It is young. It is—"

"It is meat," said Gao Tie. "It is also a soul beast in our wire in a season when the lodge is paying for three mouths on a rock. Cut it loose and it goes north and starves. Kill it and we eat."

The younger man looked at the ox and looked at the pan and said nothing. Xiao An walked round to the animal's head and stood where it could see her, and stood there a while.

"Kill it clean," she said.

The younger man did it with the iron, once, at the base of the skull, ten paces out on the grass side so the pan was not touched. The ox went down on its knees first and then over on its side, and it was still, and the three of them stood in the rain and looked at it. Then the light came.

It rose out of the ox's body slow and straight as smoke on a still day, and it stood over the animal, over its ribs, and it was white and thin, the plain white of a young thing, and it burned steady in the rain and did not go out and did not move away. The younger man took his cap off.

"That is a wet's whole watching," he said.

"That is the watch feeding us," said Gao Tie. He took the box out of the packs and went over and stood beside the body and opened the lid, and the white light leaned in, and the lid shut on it. He put the box back in the packs and put the packs under the lean-to.

They dragged the ox off the grass side and butchered it on the rock. They got more meat than the three of them could eat and cut the rest in strips and hung it over the fire, and the rain wet it and dried it and wet it again. The work took most of a day and nobody talked much. The hound ate the offal and lay in the lean-to, and the pan lay beside them all through it, blank under the rain.

---

Old Qin came up in the last week of the wet with fresh salt, and he looked like a man who had been walking in water for a year. He stood in front of the pan and did not sit down.

"Show me the season," he said.

Xiao An walked him through it from the pan out. The pan's floor, soft then setting, then set: the hound's lines round the edges, the poles' holes along the grass side, three sets of boots. The drag-mark of the ox, the place it had gone into the wire, the place it had stood, the dark stain that was mostly rain by now, and the four stones at the north edge, exactly as they had been set. Nothing else. One season, and every mark on the page belonged to the watch or to an ox.

"There is no heavy track but the ox's," said Old Qin.

"There is no heavy track but the ox's," said Xiao An.

"And the beast of the one print?"

"It did not walk here this season," said Xiao An. "The country was as soft as it will ever be, and it did not walk here."

"That is a true thing, and I can write it," said Old Qin. He had his list out and the brush in his hand, and he wrote on it for a while, standing up, with the rain running off the edge of his hat onto the paper. "The watch held the pan through the wet. One young ox of the low water taken in the wire, drowned out of the flats, its light boxed and going down to the tables. The heavy beast of the one print did not cross the high ground in the wet."

"And the other line? The thing that signs and does not show?"

"Nothing on it and nothing off it," said Old Qin. "The paper does not gain a line for a season of rain." He wrote again, shorter. "But this: the low water will draw down through the summer and everything that walks this country will have to come to it and drink. When the flats come out, we watch the water."

"The summer is the wrong season for the high ground," said the younger man.

"Which is why we will not be on the high ground," said Old Qin.

They struck the watch the next day. The wire came up off the stone side and the grass side and went back into the packs. The lean-to came down, branches first, then poles, and the holes were filled and the fire ring scattered and the whole watch ground put back to rock as well as three people could put a camp back to rock. The hound got up slow and shook herself and went to stand by Gao Tie's leg. The four stones stayed where they were, in a row, at the north edge of the pan.

"You will come up early in the summer," Old Qin said to the younger man, on the way down. "The ox's meat and the light will be accounted for, so your season is on the books. Do not put a line on the paper out of wanting one."

"I did not put a line on the paper," said the younger man.

"You thought about it for two days," said Old Qin. "I saw it in your walk when I came up the trail. So did she."

---

He read their watch ground ten days after they left it. The wet was going out by then and the rock was drying in patches, and he came down off the ravine rim on the stone side and read the whole place from outside first. The wire was gone from both sides. The lean-to was gone, and the poles' holes in the moss were the only marks where it had been. The fire ring was scattered. There were boots all over the grass side, and drag-marks, and the deep pressed place where something heavy had gone into the wire and turned, and the cut bones of an ox out on the rock where the crows were working. And in the middle of the pan, baked thin by the first dry days, the season lay written: the hound's circles, the poles, the boots, the great smeared track of the ox coming up and going nowhere, and the men's own feet at the edge where they had stood and looked at their page.

No print of his. Not one. Whole season, soft as the country would ever be, and the page had written everybody who walked on it except him.

He stood at the north edge a while, beside the four stones. He still did not know what men did with stones in a row, and he did not need to. He understood the shape of it well enough now: men marked a place so they could come back to it, and they had come back, and the country wrote for them, and it wrote nothing about him. The trick had a price, and he had paid it all wet: his own body out of his own country's writing.

He went down off the high ground on the stone, and took his first kill in the drawn water that same evening, an old grazer standing in the shallows where the flats were coming out. He ate deep for the first time in a season, and the hunger stood up in him over a full belly and asked its question, the same as ever. The water was going down. The low country was coming back, and next summer the men would come down to the water with their wire and their hound and sit beside it. He slept that night on bare rock, well back from the mud.

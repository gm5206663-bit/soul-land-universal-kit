# Chapter 15: The Hound's Third Time

He woke with water moving under the ground.

It had run in the dark below the den for three nights, and that was how he knew the winter had broken. He came out onto a slope where the snow had gone grey and soft, and the air smelled of wet stone and roots, and the stream below the den was running loud for the first time since the autumn. Out in the low country the ice was going out of the water, and it went with a sound like a herd crossing a long way off.

He walked his own range for two days. The camps on his ground were down. At the first one he found the fire ring under new water with grey ash around it, the blind's cut poles lying in the pine needles where the men had thrown them, and a bald patch where the snow was beaten flat. The wire was off both trails. Nothing of them was left in his country but the smell, and the smell was going out of it day by day.

On the second evening he stood on the high ridge and looked south. The low country lay under its own mist, and down near the edge of the fern there was still one thin line of smoke.

The hunters were in the south yet, on the ground where the wide pads ran. He had left their paper a face that was not his, and the face had walked them south, and the smoke said they were still walking it. Below that smoke the pale beast went up and down the low country as it always had, unhurried, and the roar he had spent on it in the autumn was still somewhere in that heavy old body.

The hunger stood up in him when he was full, as it did now at the end of every meal, and asked the same question it had asked at the turn of the cold. He had stopped trying to answer it with meat. He put it behind him the way a heavy beast puts an ache behind a shoulder, and it stayed there, patient, and did not go.

He did not go down while the smoke stood. He hunted the hard ground at the top of his range and waited. The snow went off the low slopes, the streams came up, and on the fourth day the smoke was gone.

---

In the low country the watch had stood four days below the shelf of old rock, on the low trail where the wide pads crossed and where the pale beast had left its own kills. The bait beast lay in the open forty paces out, cut down the belly, its meat untouched. The wire ran low across both trails at the height of a man's chest. The blind stood up off the ground on poles, and the hound was staked by the water.

On the fourth morning the younger man came up from the water with his coat wet to the waist and dropped an armload of dead fern by the fire.

"If it does not come today, the crows get the whole beast," he said. "Four days, and the meat is going grey."

"The meat is not going grey," said Gao Tie. "The crows are getting fat. Sit down."

"We killed a good beast and left it standing in the open," said the younger man. "That is a trick I have only ever seen one thing use."

"That is where we learned it," said Old Qin. He had the list open on his knee, and the brush lay in the ink beside it, and he had written nothing on the list since the camp at the black stone. "Sit down and watch the meat."

Xiao An came in off the low trail at midday with the mud to her knees. She had walked the wide crossing twice and read what the night had left on it, and there was nothing new there. She took her place behind the blind, and the four of them waited, and the low country did the small work it did every day.

It was the hound that saw it first.

She got up off her paws without a sound and stood at the end of her line with her head up and her whole body pointed down the low trail. The hair came up along her back. Gao Tie put his hand on the iron and left it lying across his knees.

"Out of the water," he said, very low. "Coming up."

Nobody moved. The waiting went on long enough for the younger man to start breathing through his mouth. Then the beast came up the low trail out of the mist with the light behind it, and it was bigger than the tracks had said.

Heavy in the shoulder, pale down the sides, with a long jaw scarred along one edge. It came up the trail at a walk and did not stop at the wire. It stepped over the wire as if the wire were a line drawn on the ground for it to read, and then it stopped four paces short of the meat, and it stood with its head low and looked at the blind.

It saw them. Nothing about the low old head said otherwise. It looked at the poles and the cut branches as a man reads a page, and it did not hurry.

"Still," said Old Qin, hardly moving his mouth. "Nobody puts a hand on anything. Count it."

"Heavier in the shoulder than the tracks said," said Xiao An, as low. "The jaw is scarred on the near side. It has been in fights."

"It read the wire from the water," said Gao Tie.

"Let it eat," said Old Qin.

The beast ate. It took the deep parts first, slow and steady, watching the blind between bites. Nothing in that country had ever made it run, and it knew it. Twice it stopped with its head up and the meat hanging from its jaw, and stood, and went back to eating.

Then the hound left the trail.

She came up the line to the end of it, and she did the thing Gao Tie had never once seen her do in eleven years of hunts. She turned her back on the beast. She walked four paces off the line toward the low water, onto a patch of ground where the snow had gone and the moss was torn, and she stood over it with her nose low and one forefoot up, and her whole body pointed at that ground, and she did not move again.

The younger man whispered her name. Gao Tie did not answer. He was looking from the beast to the hound and back, and his face had gone flat and still, the face of a man counting something twice.

The beast ate until it had taken what it wanted. It stood over the meat with its head up and looked at the blind one last time, and then it walked off south, back down the trail it had come by, past the wire, unhurried, and did not look back.

The younger man was the first one up. He went out to the end of the line and took the hound by the collar and pulled her off the ground, and she came, because she always came, and then she walked straight back and stood over it again.

"She has lost her nose," he said. "She looked at that thing and it went through her, and now she is standing on some smell she likes."

"Say that to her face," said Gao Tie.

"I am saying it to you."

Xiao An walked out and went over the patch of ground twice, once on her feet and once on her knees, and came back with the wet on her hands.

"There is nothing to see," she said. "No print, no blood, no hair. The ice came off it last week. Whatever she has found, it is under the moss."

"It is the beast of the line," said the younger man. "We saw it with our own eyes. Heavy, pale-sided, it walked the wire and never touched it, it killed at a camp in the winter. Write it and let us go home."

"The beast of the line leaves its kills standing and never shows itself," said Xiao An. "That beast showed itself. It ate meat it did not kill, in the middle of the day, with four of us behind the poles. And look at her. She is standing on the one patch of ground in this country that the meat never touched."

Gao Tie had gone out to stand beside the hound.

"At the water in the summer she told us there was a beast where we could see nothing," he said. "We came up the bank with the iron and there was nothing there. That was the first time. On the high ground in the cold she had it again and could not hold it. That was the second. This is the third, and she has left a living beast to point at it." He looked back at Old Qin. "I have never known her to lie."

"Then one of the beasts is standing on nothing," said the younger man. "A smell is not a beast."

Old Qin had walked out to the torn moss while they argued. He stood over it with his hands behind his back for a long time. Then he came back and took up the list.

"The list carries what the ground can prove," he said. "The ground under her feet proves nothing to me. She smells something, and I believe she smells it, and I cannot write a smell." He looked down the low trail, south, where the wide pads went. "So the paper keeps the beast we saw. It stood our wire and ate our meat in the open in the day, and that is a true thing, and true things go on the paper."

"And the other one?" said Xiao An.

"The other one is a smell in a good hound's nose," said Old Qin. "When the snow goes off the high trails, we come back north. If she points again, I will walk where she points before I write a single line."

"You will walk on nothing," said the younger man.

"Then I will have walked," said Old Qin.

---

They struck the watch at the end of that week. The wire came up off both trails and went into the packs. The blind came down pole by pole. The bait went to the crows, which had earned it.

On the last night Old Qin wrote under the list.

"What goes down?" said the younger man.

"The beast of the low country," said Old Qin. "Heavy, pale-sided, wide-tracked. It stood our wire and never touched it, and it ate our meat in the open in the middle of the day. Seen and counted, with the place under it." He wrote on for a while. "And under that, the old man of the lodge, killed at the water in the second winter, and his winter's pay to his sister's house."

"And the light in the box?"

"There is no light this season."

"Then we carry down a paper with nothing under it."

"We carry down a paper with a beast on it, and the account of how the season went, and we stand in front of the table and say it all," said Old Qin. "The lodge asked for a beast on the paper. Nobody asked us to write a beast we do not believe in."

Xiao An said nothing. She sat with her back to the fire, looking east across the dark at the patch of ground the hound had chosen. The hound lay at her feet with its eyes open and its head turned that way.

"When the summer comes up the country," said Old Qin, "you will walk the high ground where she points. In front of me."

"Yes," said Xiao An.

---

When the smoke had been gone four days he came down into the low country.

He found the watch ground in the middle of the afternoon and walked it twice. The men had killed a heavy beast of the low country and left it standing in the open. The pale beast had come up out of the south and eaten it. The men had watched the whole of it and gone down the country. He read that much in one walk: the wire gone, the blind gone, the fire ring cold under new water, the rest of the meat left to the crows. Their smell was already coming apart in the wet ground. The old beast's tracks went south down the low water, heavy and unhurried, days old, and none of them had turned back up.

One patch of ground by the water stopped him. The moss was torn and worn through in a circle, and the tracks of the hound were all over it: standing, turning, coming back to it, with the boots of a man coming twice to lead her away. He could not read what she had found there. Whatever it was had no print and no blood, and she had stood over it long enough to wear the moss out.

He took the low country.

It was open ground. No camp stood on it now, and no fire would stand on it again before the next winter at the earliest. No other heavy beast had walked it since the autumn. The thaw was bringing the meat down to the new water, the slow heavy kind that had wintered in the fern. The low country was the road, too: everything that came up out of the men's country into the deep forest came up through it, and everything that went down out of the deep forest went down it. Whatever walked the road first saw everything else that used it. So he took it. He walked its two waters and the fern edge over two days and put his own quiet over the ground.

On the third evening he took the first meat on it. A heavy grazer came down to the low water at dusk, alone, one of the slow kind that had come out of the winter thin. He let it drink. Then he came up out of the reeds below the bank and took it at the shoulder and it went down in the shallow water. It was not a fight. He ate the deep parts standing in the water and dragged the rest back off the trail into the fern, and went downwind, and slept with his back against a stone.

He stayed on the low ground for the rest of the thaw, and the meat came down to the new water and he took it as it came. The men did not come back up the country. Their smoke stayed down, and the paper went down with them, carrying a beast that was not him, and under that a season with no light in the box.

The hunger was the same. It stood up at the end of every meal and asked its question, and he carried it, patient, and it did not change its note.

Far off above the fern the deep forest kept its own business. The great beasts' country had not changed all season. The pull toward the ground under them was the same pull it had been in the autumn, far off, unhurried. Nothing up there had moved for him, and he had not moved toward it.

The hound went down the country with the men. It had smelled him three times now — once at the water in the summer, once on the high ground in the cold, and once in the low country with a beast eating in the open in front of it — and nothing about him was written anywhere.

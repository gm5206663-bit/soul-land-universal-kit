# Chapter 15: The Hound's Third Time

He woke with water moving under the ground.

It had run in the dark below the den for three nights, and that was how he knew the winter had broken. He came out onto a slope where the snow had gone grey and soft, and the air smelled of wet stone and roots, and the stream below the den was running loud for the first time since the autumn. Out in the low country the ice was going out of the water, and it went with a sound like a herd crossing a long way off.

He walked his own range for two days and read what the winter had left. The camps on his ground were down. At the first one he found the fire ring under new water with grey ash around it, and the cut poles of the blind lying in the pine needles where they had been thrown, and a bald patch where the snow had been beaten flat. The wire was off both trails. Nothing of the men was left in his country but the smell, and the smell was going out of it day by day.

He worked the high stone for the two days after that, because the thaw had opened it. The springs came up warm out of the rock in the middle of the snow, and the small beasts of the slope had come out of their holes to sit in the steam: beetles first, then the stone-runners, then a thin fox-kind that looked at him once and did not come back. He ate beetles off the warm rock, which was not hunting but was food, and he drank where the water came out of the ground already warm, and lay in the steam with the sun on him and let the winter come out of his hide. On the second evening he took a young stone-runner up on the scree, sitting at the mouth of its hole with its cheeks full, and it was one mouthful and he ate it and went on.

On the second evening he stood on the high ridge and looked south. The low country lay under its own mist, and down near the edge of the fern there was one thin line of smoke. The men were south yet, on the ground where the wide pads ran. He watched the smoke until dark, and he did not go down while it stood. He slept that night on the ridge with the smoke smell in the wind. In the morning the wind had turned and there was nothing to smell but his own country coming awake.

---

The smoke was gone on the fourth day, and he went south into the ground he had not walked since the autumn.

He did not go for the men. He went because he had spent one whole roar on a heavy beast in the winter, and a thing like that leaves a debt in a country, and he wanted to read what the debt had become. Two days of walking brought him down the long slope and along the low water, and on the third day he found the place where the men had kept their watch.

It was a fine open bit of ground above a pool, where the low trail crossed the wide pads, and the men had worked it hard. The wire was gone from both trails, and the blind was down, and the fire ring was cold under a shawl of wet ash. But the ground told him more than that. Men had walked it for days; the same two pairs of boots went in circles he could follow; and by the water there was a patch of torn moss, worn through in a circle no body of that size makes by standing once.

He went over that patch twice. The hound of the men had stood there a long time. Her tracks were all over it, pointed and re-pointed, and a man's boots had come to her twice and led her off, and she had come back to it both times. Whatever she had found there, it did not have a print. The moss was torn, and the mud under it was old, and there was nothing in the whole patch for a nose that was not a hound's to take hold of.

He made his own circle of that ground, wide, and left it the way he had come.

He crossed his own trails once on the way north, the ones he had used all winter on the hard ground, and read them the way he read everything. Nothing had walked them but him. Whatever the men had learned in the south, they had not brought it up onto his range, and the wide pads had not come north either. He went along the trails to the top of the range and stood a while at the edge of the deep forest, where the ground rises, and looked north into the trees, and then went back down.

---

He took the low country a day at a time after that, and there was a lot to take.

The two waters were the country's whole business, and he learned them the way he had learned the high ground in his first year. The upper water ran down out of the stone country and cut a soft valley through the fern, with pools at the bends and gravel bars between them. The lower water came up out of the men's country far south and ran flat and brown between low banks, and the two of them joined at the bottom of the country and went south together. Everything that drank there drank between the two, and everything that crossed the country crossed between them, and there was no way from the deep forest down to the south country that did not pass through his ground.

It was open ground. No camp stood on it, and no fire would stand on it again before the next winter at the earliest. No other heavy beast had walked it since the autumn. The thaw was bringing the meat down to the new water, the slow heavy kind that had wintered in the fern, and the low country was the road. Everything that came up out of the men's country into the deep forest came up through it, and everything that went down out of the deep forest went down it. Whatever walked the road first saw everything else that used it.

So he took it. He walked its two waters and its fern edge over two days and put his own quiet over the ground, and on the third evening he took the first meat on it. The kill was a heavy grazer at the lower pool: an old one, alone, thin from the winter, down at the last of the light. He came down to the pool against the wind and lay in the reeds above the bank to watch her. She was one of the slow kind, old, thin from the winter, and she stood in the shallows with her head up for a long time before she drank, the way the old ones do when they have heard a thing or two in their lives. He did not move. The reeds stood still around him and the water talked at the bank, and he let all of that be the only sound in the country.

She put her head down and drank. He came up out of the reeds at the shoulder and took her at the neck in the first stride, and the water broke around the both of them, and she went down and was still. He stood in the shallows and ate the deep parts while the light went out of the sky. Two crows came down and sat in a dead pine above the pan and waited for the rest. He dragged the rest well off the trail and left it to them.

The hunger came up when he was full, as it did at the end of every meal, and asked the same question it had asked all winter. He had stopped trying to answer it with meat. He carried it, and it stayed, and it did not change.

---

On the fifth day he went further south, because he wanted the pale beast's ground read before the season turned.

He found the wide pads at the far end of the low country, at a pool below a shelf of old rock, and he read them a long time with his nose down. The big one had come down to drink in the last days of the winter and gone back up onto its own ground, unhurried, the same walk it had used all its life. Its track was wider than his own by a hand's width. The age was in the print itself, in how little the toes spread: a beast that had stopped hurrying long before he was hatched.

He stood over the prints with the sun going down and read the size and the years out of them. The beast had come down to drink and stood a while at the pool, and then gone back up onto its own ground the same unhurried way it did everything. The tracks of one animal are a road it has spent its life building, and the road in these said the same thing at every step: this is a beast that has never once run.

He did not follow them, and he did not roar. The sound he had spent on that beast in the winter walked somewhere behind those tracks, in the meat of it, and the roar had not come back to him and would not. It was not a debt. It was a thing said once between two of his kind, and it would stand as said.

---

He turned back north before the light went, and he crossed above the place where the men had last camped on the southern ground, on the high dry crossing, and that was when he saw the hound for the third time.

She was on a short line at their fire, and she was working the ground the way she worked everything, in slow arcs, nose down. Halfway across her arc she stopped. She went down into the moss with her whole head and stood there, and then she walked a few paces to her left and did it again. Then she stood still, tail low and body pointed. The man with her stood up and came to her and looked over the same patch of moss, and there was nothing there to see. He looked a long time. Then he put a hand on her collar and spoke to her and led her back toward the fire, and she went, and she looked back at the moss twice.

He knew the ground she had been standing on. He had lain in that moss for the better part of a night in the summer, with his quiet drawn over himself to its last limit, and the hound had climbed the slope toward him in the dark and lost him in it, and the men had called her down.

She had his old smell in her nose and she could not make it a thing a man could see, any more than she could make a scent into a track. And the man had come to her and stood over her patch and looked, and had found nothing, and had taken her away from it.

He crossed the rest of the high ground at its western end, in the dark, without leaving a print worth reading.

---

South was where the men walked now, with the wire and the blind and their paper, and their hound had put his smell on the ground twice in one year and could not spend it. North was the deep forest, and the great beasts' country, and the pull he had carried since the first summer, and the sound of his own voice standing somewhere in a heavy body to the south.

The low country lay between the two, and the low country was his, and he walked it now the way he walked his own range — not reading it any more, only using it.

He came down into it at the grey end of the night, to the fern and the new water and the road, and he went along it at a walk, and the small kind heard him and went quiet and let him pass, the way they did when the country was working right.

The springs were giving off their steam in the cold of the morning. The beasts of the low country were at the warm water: the slow kind, the small kind, the birds, crowded in together with their heads down, drinking. He smelled it all from the top of the slope. For a while he only breathed. The country was waking up all around him, and none of it knew he was ten strides above it.

The whole low country lay under his nose like a map. The waters. The road. The springs. The high ground at his back, and the deep forest standing north.

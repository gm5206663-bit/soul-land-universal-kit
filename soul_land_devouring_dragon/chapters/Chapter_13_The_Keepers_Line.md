# Chapter 13: The Keepers' Line

The bank had learned him. He came back down the water line on the fourth day, working the low country for the heavy kind, and he read the place from the high fern before he went near it.

The mud at the crossing lay churned and dry, and the bull's bones were picked and scattered where the ravens had worked them, and the boots of the takers had gone down to the shallows and back, hard-edged now in the sun. Under all of it, standing in the ground like a stain the rain had not touched, his own word lay over four days of bank.

The small lives had not come back. The runs under the bank came up short of the water and turned, and the burrowing birds had moved their ground a length up-country. A bank that had been a place where the country went about its small business with its head down was now a place where everything kept the far edge of what it knew.

He paid the debt down through the falling of the green. The hunt came back to him a length at a time. On the second morning he took a hare-kind off the end of its run with nothing in the fern lifting its head, and on the fourth he took a young boar-kind out of the low wallows and held the hush over the whole of the meal. And that was when he read the shape of what he had made. Every beast eaten down, every rest left, every place he had let the answering come to: all of it wrote him into the country in a hand any reader could learn. He had been signing his ground since the first season, and he had never once had a reader on it.

Three mornings later he had his answer. He came down out of the high fern into a fold he had hunted since the deep country took him, a bowl of soft green with a wallow in the low side and two game trails running through it, and he stopped on the rim because the fold had been worked by other hands.

The beast lay in the open grass with its hide opened in a long line down the belly. It was heavy, one of the fringe years, and its meat lay whole in the sun. Its years were gone and its light was gone, and the smell stood in the fold clean: the light had been taken by someone standing at the kill. A wire line ran low across each of the two trails, set at the height of a chest. Upwind of the wallow, off the ground on a frame of poles, stood a blind of cut branches with the smell of men in it, and down by the spring a spirit hound lay staked on a short line with its muzzle toward the open grass. In the middle of the afternoon a man came out to the hound and looked over the fold for a while, and went back into the blind.

Nothing hunted. Everything waited.

He lay on the rim through the grey end of the day. The takers had made a kill of their own and taken the light out of it and left the meat standing in the open with a watch over it, which was his own trick turned around: the kill left as a word, the word calling the answering, and the man behind the poles writing down whatever came.

He did not eat. He had never eaten a husk and had no plans to start. A snare catches one animal and teaches a man nothing, so this line had not been set to catch. He gave the fold up that night, without haste, and crossed the ridge east.

---

Three days up the country from the water, the line made its kill. They took the old beast of the fringe years at a wallow in the last of the light, with an arrow out of the dark trees and a run of two hundred lengths after it.

Old Qin did the work at the head with the short knife. He opened the hide and set his hands in where the light sits in a beast, and the light came up pale and slow over the body. Xiao An knelt on the moss beside the kill with the box open and held it there until the light was in and the lid was shut.

"Pale," said Gao Tie. He crouched by the body and turned the empty hide back with two fingers. "A young beast would have given more."

"A young beast is not worth a watch," said Old Qin. He wiped the knife and stood up and looked at what they had made: the opened hide, the meat whole, the great body left lying in the open. "Leave the meat. The meat is not for us this time."

Xiao An came up from the moss with the box against her chest.
"Both trails?" she said.

"Both," said Gao Tie. "Set at the chest, anchored at the ends, low enough to hold a bull."

"Then the thing that reads this fold will read the wire. It has read everything else we have put down."

"Then let it read," said Old Qin. "A snare catches one animal, and we have caught animals all our lives. I want the ground it comes over, and the hour it comes in, and the manner of its eating and the manner of its leaving. I want the mark of the thing that signs and does not show, written by its own feet on ground I chose for it."

Gao Tie ran the last of the wire through his hands and set the anchor post true with the flat of his boot.
"Once, at the water, I had a hound pointing into thirty lengths of open fern with nothing in it to see," he said. "I am done with that. The next time she points, I want to put a mark on the ground and go and look at it after."

"Then we hold the watch," said Old Qin. "Four days here. The ground is soft at the wallow, so anything that comes will print. If nothing comes, we carry the kill a day's walk and set again, and hold four days, all the way up toward the giants' water and the breaks beyond."

"And if it comes while we hold the watch?" said Xiao An.

"Then we do not shoot."

"Not shoot."

"Not shoot," said Old Qin. He put the list down flat on his knee and took out the brush and the ink. "Nobody in this line has ever shot first and learned second. If it comes to the meat and we are behind the blind with the hound staked, the three of us sit still and count. The size of it. The manner of its eating. Whether it stands and eats like a beast or eats and looks. How much meat, and which parts, and where it leaves the rest."

"And the hound?" said Gao Tie.

"The hound stays staked," said Old Qin. "If it comes, she will read it before we do. This time the scent stays in her nose and we keep hold of her collar, and nobody lets go of anything."

The younger lodge man came up from the spring with the water.
"What do we put on the paper for a name?" he said. "The men at the lodge will ask."

Old Qin wrote for a while. The brush moved slowly, as it always did, and the evening went down over the kill-ground while he wrote.

"One line," he said at last. "The mark on the deep kills. The ground it has been read on: the water bank, the high breaks, the low fold, and the fern country south of the giants' water. Count taken: thin light, fringe years. Next: the watch, four days at the wallow, then the kill a day's walk north and four more days."

"And the name," said the younger man.

"The keepers' line," said Old Qin.

"Keepers?"

"The old hunters of the north border say the deep country has its own keepers," said Gao Tie.

"I do not know what keeps the deep country," said Old Qin. "I know that something in it has been feeding on our heavy kinds for two seasons and has never once shown itself, and that is not a beast's way. Or it is one beast that walks soft, and then we will find its feet. Either way the line is the keepers' line, and it is a line, and it waits to be filled in."

He shut the list and put it away, and the four of them went down to the camp under the trees and left the kill standing in the open grass with the wire across both trails. Behind them the hound got up, walked to the end of her stake, and stood with her muzzle up, reading the wind off the high ground.

---

He found the second kill four days later, from the high fern, in the grey before the last light: a heavy beast opened down the belly in the open grass, the meat whole, the light gone, two lengths of wire low across the two trails, the blind up on its poles, and a hound staked short by the water. He lay on the rim through the night and watched, and this time he did not need the day to read it. The kill had been carried a day's walk north, which meant the watch was not on any one fold. The takers were working the country as he worked it, a length at a time, and behind every length they left a meal laid out and a man sitting behind a blind, and the meal said come and the man wrote down whatever came.

They had not come to eat him or to take his years. They had come to read him.

He spent the next days making the country lie. It was the oldest craft he had and he had never once used it against a reader. He took a heavy beast in the far south of his range, in a fold he did not mean to come back to, ate the deep parts at the kill and left the rest standing, with the still-wet hide on it and the bones broken open, and he made the whole thing as loud as a kill can be made. He did that twice in the south, and once again in the low outer country, on a wallow the men had worked two seasons back. Then he kept to the hard ground and the stone and the high fern, and he hunted small and quiet while the season turned, and he learned the feel of being a country's secret instead of its sign.

The small lives of his own ground came back to him all the same. On the fifth morning of that run he came down a stone trail and a hare-kind sat at the end of its run and did not go flat, and then did, too late, and made its run. The country was forgetting him a little every day, and the forgetting was the only cure for what he had spent at the water.

---

He went north once, at the green's falling, and he spent the voice at the edge of the deep place. The edge was where the giants' country stopped and the world changed its mind about trees. He walked up to it in three days over ground he knew, and on the fourth morning he came out into a country where the trunks stood far apart and the ground under him had no trails in it at all. Nothing that made trails lived in that country. The great ones did not make trails; they stood.

He read the standings as he always did, as absences, wide and patient and unlit, the places where his sense went out and came back with nothing. Three of them lay within the reach of his reading that morning. He walked between their grounds all day, up one side of a ground and down the other, and nothing stirred and nothing was staged for him and nothing was hidden from him.

At the last of the light he came to a rise of bare stone above a long valley, and beyond the valley the country went up into unreadable ground. He stood on the stone and called. It came short and low, because the summer's work had made it that: one thick note instead of the long tearing roar, and the note went out from the stone over the valley and into the unreadable country like a stone dropped into deep water.

The country went quiet in answer, and it was not the quiet he knew. Nothing went flat, and no small bodies ran, and no ground was given. The country simply stopped being busy, and the silence that came back over the stone was the silence of being read. The deep country had taken his word and read it, and now it knew the shape of him the way he knew the shape of the standings: a thing with no name and a weight that could be measured. Nothing answered, and the standings did not stir. He spent the voice once and once only in the whole falling of that green, and came down out of the deep place with the cost paid. The kill-word wrote him into the country for readers who read with their feet, and his voice wrote him in for readers who read with their depth.

---

The want woke on one of the grey days at the turn of the season, and it did not wake as it used to. He was lying on the warm stone above the warm breaks, fed and easy, with the old bull's years settled into his bones, and one grey afternoon the want stood up in him and looked north. In the old days it had always come up hot. It had spent hunts before they began and hurried him up a gorge with the hush broken behind him, and he had lost a stag to it in the second winter.

This was not hot. The want stood in him quiet and heavy, and it did not want the next meal or the next heavy beast, and it cared nothing for how long the waiting took. It wanted the deep years. It wanted the ground under the standings, and it wanted it as the mountain wanted weather: without hurry, without end.

He held it, and the holding was like standing in a river. He had done it through the green's height and through the fight at the water, and the carrying was the difference between a beast with an appetite and an appetite with a beast. But he kept the shape of what he had read in it, and he kept it carefully: the flaw was growing up, and it had learned to wait.

---

The green turned. The kills had been carried three times up the deep country, and three times they had stood four days in the open with a hound staked down by the water and a man behind the poles, and by the turn of the season their line had reached the low end of his own breaks. He was not going to give it a face this season. A line without a face is free, and he had spent the whole falling of the green keeping his face off it, hunting small on hard ground and writing his words in countries he did not mean to sleep in. The wall at the thousand years stood somewhere far off in the middle of all of it, as far as it had ever been, and he did not feel it. He went down off the rocks at dark and crossed the hard ground toward his own breaks, and somewhere far down-country, past the giants' trees and the camp and the wire, a hound that had held his scent for one hour lifted its head off its paws, and read the wind, and found nothing.

# Chapter 13: The Keepers' Line

The bank had learned him. He came back down to the water on the fourth day, hunting the heavy beasts of the low country, and he studied the place from the high ferns before he went near it.

The mud at the crossing was churned and dry. The bull's bones lay scattered where the ravens had picked them, and the hunters' boots had gone down to the shallows and back, hard-edged now in the sun. Under all of it, standing in the ground like a stain the rain had not touched, lay four days of his own killing. He had killed the bull there, in silence, and the ground remembered it.

The small creatures had not come back. The burrows under the bank came up short of the water and turned away, and the burrowing birds had moved a little upstream. The bank used to be a place where the country went about its small business with its head down. Now everything there kept to the far edge of what it knew.

He paid for it through the end of summer. His hunting came back to him a little at a time. On the second morning he took a hare at the end of its run and nothing in the ferns lifted its head at him, and on the fourth morning he took a young boar in the low wallows and held the quiet over the whole meal. That was when he understood what he had been doing all along. Every beast he ate, every rest he left behind, wrote his name into the country in a hand anything could learn to read. He had been leaving signs on his ground since his first summer, and no one had ever read them.

Three mornings later someone did. He came down out of the high ferns to a meadow he had hunted since he was young, soft grass with a wallow on the low side and two game trails through it, and he stopped on the ridge because the meadow had been used by other hands.

A dead beast lay in the open grass, cut open down the belly. It was heavy, a full-grown one, and its meat was untouched. Its light was gone, taken by a man standing over the kill. A wire ran low across each of the two trails, set at the height of a chest. Upwind of the wallow, off the ground on a frame of poles, sat a blind of cut branches that smelled of men, and down by the spring a spirit hound lay staked on a short line with its muzzle toward the meat. In the middle of the afternoon a man came out to the hound, looked over the meadow for a while, and went back into the blind.

Nothing hunted. Everything waited.

He lay on the ridge through the grey end of the day and read what he saw. The hunters had made a kill of their own and taken its light and left the meat standing with a watch over it. It was his own trick turned around: the kill was the bait, the bait brought beasts in, and the man behind the poles wrote down whatever came.

He did not eat. He had never eaten a kill that another hunter had made and left for him, and he did not start now. A snare catches one animal and teaches a hunter nothing, so the wire was not there to catch him. He gave the meadow up that night, without hurrying, and crossed east over the ridge.

---

Three days up-country from the water, the hunters made their kill. They took an old, heavy beast at a wallow in the last of the light, with an arrow out of the dark trees and a run of two hundred paces after it.

Old Qin did the work at the head with the short knife. He opened the hide and put his hands in where a beast's light sits, and the light came up pale and slow over the body. Xiao An knelt on the moss with the box open and held it there until the light was in and the lid was shut.

"Pale," said Gao Tie. He crouched by the body and turned the empty hide back with two fingers. "A young beast would have given more."

"A young beast is not worth a watch," said Old Qin. He wiped the knife and stood up and looked at what they had made: the opened hide, the meat whole, the body lying in the open. "Leave the meat. The meat is not for us this time."

Xiao An came up from the moss with the box against her chest.
"Both trails?" she said.
"Both," said Gao Tie. "Set at the chest, anchored at the ends, low enough to hold a bull."
"Then the thing that reads this meadow will read the wire. It has read everything else we have put down."
"Then let it read," said Old Qin. "A snare catches one animal, and we have caught animals all our lives. I want the ground it walks over, the hour it comes, how it eats, and how it leaves. I want the beast that leaves signs and never shows itself to write its own print on ground I chose for it."

Gao Tie ran the last of the wire through his hands and set the anchor post true with the flat of his boot.
"Once, at the water, I had a hound pointing into thirty paces of open fern with nothing in it to see," he said. "I am done with that. The next time she points, I want to mark the ground and go and look at it after."
"Then we hold the watch," said Old Qin. "Four days here. The ground is soft at the wallow, so anything that comes will print. If nothing comes, we carry the kill a day's walk and set again and hold four days there, all the way up toward the giants' water."
"And if it comes while we hold the watch?" said Xiao An.
"Then we do not shoot."
"Not shoot."
"Not shoot," said Old Qin. He put the list down flat on his knee and took out the brush and the ink. "Nobody in this line has ever shot first and learned second. If it comes to the meat and we are behind the blind with the hound staked, the three of us sit still and count. How big it is. How it eats. Whether it stands and eats like a beast or eats and watches. How much meat it takes, and which parts, and where it leaves the rest."
"And the hound?" said Gao Tie.
"The hound stays staked," said Old Qin. "If it comes, she will smell it before we do. This time we keep hold of her collar, and nobody lets go of anything."

The younger lodge man came up from the spring with water.
"What do we put on the paper for a name?" he said. "The men at the lodge will ask."

Old Qin wrote for a while, the brush moving slowly as it always did, and the light went down over the kill-ground while he wrote.
"One line," he said at last. "Something that leaves the deep kills standing. Where it has been read: the water bank, the high breaks, the low meadow, and the fern country south of the giants' water. What we took today: one thin light, an old beast. Next: the watch here, four days, then the kill carried a day's walk north and four more days."
"And the name," said the younger man.
"The keepers' line," said Old Qin.
"Keepers?"
"The old hunters of the north border say the deep country has its own keepers," said Gao Tie.
"I do not know what keeps the deep country," said Old Qin. "I know that something in it has been feeding on our heavy beasts for two summers and has never once shown itself. So we keep looking until we find it, and the line is the keepers' line, and it has one name on it: the beast that leaves signs and never shows itself."

He shut the list and put it away, and the four of them went down to the camp under the trees, leaving the kill standing in the open with the wire across both trails. Behind them the hound got up, walked to the end of her line, and stood with her muzzle up, smelling the wind off the high ground.

---

He found the second kill four days later, in the grey before dark: another heavy beast cut open in the open grass, the meat untouched, two wires low across the two trails, the blind up on its poles, and the hound staked short by the water. He lay on the ridge through the night and watched, and this time he did not need a day to understand it. The kill had been carried a day's walk north, which meant the watch was not fixed on one meadow. The hunters were working their way up the country the same way he hunted, a step at a time, and behind every step they left bait and a hidden man with paper.

They had not come to eat him or to take his years. They had come to find out what he was.

He spent the next days making the country lie. It was his oldest trick and he had never once used it against men. He took a heavy beast far to the south, in a meadow he never meant to visit again, ate the deep parts at the kill and left the rest standing, the wet hide still on it and the bones broken open, as loud as a kill can be made. He did that twice in the south, and once more in the low country outside his range, at a wallow the men had used two summers back. Then he kept to the hard ground and the stone and the high ferns, and he hunted small and quiet while the season turned, and he learned what it was like to be a rumour instead of a sign.

The small creatures came back to him all the same. On the fifth morning of that run he came down a stone trail and a hare sat at the end of its run and did not go flat, and then did, too late, and ran. The country was forgetting him a little every day, and the forgetting was the only cure for what he had spent at the water.

---

He went north once, at the end of summer, and he used his voice at the edge of the deep forest. The edge was where the giants' trees stopped and the world changed its mind about trees. He walked there in three days over ground he knew, and on the fourth morning he came out into country where the trunks stood far apart, the light fell whole, and the ground under him had no trails at all. Nothing that made trails lived there. The great beasts did not make trails. They stood.

He felt for them as he always did and found nothing: wide, patient, unlit places where his sense went out and came back empty. Three of them stood within his reach that morning. He walked between their grounds all day, up one side of a ground and down the other, and nothing stirred and nothing was set for him and nothing hid from him.

At the last of the light he came to a bare stone rise above a long valley, and beyond the valley the country climbed into ground he could not feel at all. He stood on the stone and roared. It came short and low, because the summer's work had made it that way: one thick note instead of a long tearing bellow, and it went out over the valley like a stone dropped into deep water.

The country went quiet in answer, and it was not the silence he knew. Nothing ran, nothing hid, and no ground was given up. The country simply stopped being busy, like a room when someone walks in who can read. Whatever lived out there had heard him and taken his measure: a thing with no name and a weight that could be counted. Nothing answered. The great beasts did not stir.

He spent his voice once and once only that summer, and came down out of the deep forest knowing the price. The kills he left standing wrote his name for anything that reads with its feet, and his roar wrote it for anything that reads with its depth. He could not take either of them back.

---

The hunger woke on one of the grey days at the turn of the season, and it did not wake the way it used to. He was lying on the warm stone above the hot springs, fed and easy, with the old bull's years settled into his bones, and one grey afternoon the hunger stood up in him and looked north. In the old days it had always come up hot. It had spent hunts before they began and hurried him up a gorge with the quiet broken behind him, and he had lost a stag to it in his second winter.

This was not hot. The hunger stood in him quiet and heavy. It did not want the next meal or the next heavy beast, and it did not care how long the waiting took. It wanted the deep years. It wanted the ground under the great beasts, and it wanted it the way a mountain wants weather: without hurry and without end.

He held it as a beast holds a current, and the holding did not end. He had done it through the whole summer and through the fight at the water, and the carrying was the difference between a beast with an appetite and an appetite with a beast. But he kept what he had learned from it, and he kept it carefully: the hunger was growing up, and it had learned to wait.

---

The summer turned. The hunters had carried their kill three times up into the deep forest, and three times they had watched it for four days with a hound staked by the water and a man behind the poles, and by the end of the season their line had reached the low end of his own range. He was not going to show them his face this year. A hunt with no face on it is blind, and he had spent the whole season keeping his face off it, hunting small on hard ground and writing his signs in countries he did not sleep in. The barrier at the thousand years stood far off in the middle of it all, as far as it had ever been, and he did not feel it. He went down off the rocks at dark and crossed the hard ground toward his own range, and somewhere far down-country, past the giants' trees and the hunters' camp and the wire, a hound that had once smelled him for one hour lifted its head off its paws, smelled the wind, and found nothing.

# Chapter 23: The Parting

For a long time the road and the pull both went north, and the difference between them was small enough to ignore.

That was the problem. The road kept to the wide valleys and the low passes, where the water was and the food was, the ground that feet had chosen for good reasons. The pull did not care about any of that. Every day it leaned a little more toward the east, toward higher, stonier country, as a smell leans when the wind changes. He walked on the road and told himself the difference was small, and every day that was less true.

He also knew why he stayed, and it was not a good reason. The road was easy. He knew the animals on it and their hours. He was allowed there. He was a local hunter that the road's animals had grown up around, the same as they had grown up around the stream. After a whole childhood of being nothing anywhere, being somebody — even a small, ordinary somebody — was warm, and warm things are hard to walk out of.

His last full day on the road, he did not know it was his last. It was a long, gold evening. Deer crossed the path ahead of him in a loose line, mothers and that season's young, fat from the summer, and none of them so much as broke step at the sight of him lying off the side. He was furniture. That evening he hunted the ordinary way, at the ordinary hour: an old animal feeding apart from the others, taken quickly, before its trouble could start. He ate what he needed, left the rest for the small things, and slept full and easy between the roots of a giant tree.

The pull ran under all of it the whole time, quiet and straight and patient. Somewhere in the black middle of that night, his feet decided. In the gray morning he let them be right.

The road dropped into a stream valley that day and crossed the water at a wide shallow ford. On the far side it went on north, climbing gently toward the next low pass. The stream itself came down out of the east, cold and quick.

He stood in the ford for a long time with the water pushing at his legs, and let the two directions show themselves. North, the road climbed out of the valley in easy stages toward the next low pass, and he could see the line of it going on: valley, pass, valley, pass, into the far haze. Upstream, the water came down out of close, dark, stony country with no path anywhere on it. The road was the soft world. The stream was the hard one, and the pull ran up it.

A boar of the road kinds was drinking downstream. It raised its head and looked across at him — a flat, measuring look, an animal that knew him — and went back to drinking. That was the whole goodbye. He turned upstream, and the road went on being the road without him.

He went up the stream in the stream. Where the water ran over rock he walked in it, letting the noise cover his sound and the current push his smell apart. Where the banks were soft root-ground he kept to the stones. He ate in the water. The first evening he went looking for it, the stream slowed around the trunk of a fallen giant, and in the slack water behind it a fat old fish held its place, working the current with no effort at all. He came down the bank at the hour the drinking animals came, plain and unhurried, and stood in the shallows like one of them, and let the water take his shape apart as it had always taken his sound. The fish did not know he was there. Nothing did. He took it in one short lunge — the old ones are all jaw and opinion, and this one swung the current against him, and still it was over in three heartbeats — and he stood in the noise and ate it, with the water pushing at his sides.

Other evenings it was drinking animals that came down right into the reach where he lay, and never noticed him. The water said nothing about him to anything. He slept on the gravel bars and the mid-stream rocks, where no roots could carry his steps to the forest.

On the second night a long, low swimming animal came up the dark pool below his gravel bar and hunted fish there for an hour. It worked the water ten lengths away from a sleeping thing that could have killed it in two moves, and it did not care, because the water did not say he was there. He lay with his flank to the current and slept as a nobody again, the first time since the ford.

The forest never noticed any of it. The quiet never came near him. Days went by like that, and he understood that the parting had been real.

Then the stream betrayed him. Another stream came in from the north and he took it, and for a day it was good water, quick and loud. On the second day the walls closed in. The stream narrowed into a gorge, the gorge dropped, and the water gathered itself and went into the dark under a shelf of rock. It did not come out. There was a sound like a den swallowing, and then only dripping walls.

He stood at the edge of the sunken place and read the country ahead, and it was bad. Between him and the next water — he could smell it, thinner and colder, somewhere beyond a stretch of forest — lay a reach of old deep trees on soft dark ground, thick with roots. Ground that would repeat every step he took to whatever was listening. A day of it. Maybe more.

He went in at gray light, when the night animals were settling and the day ones had not yet come out, because those were the quietest hours the forest had. He chose a line that kept to higher ground between the root-hollows, where the soil was thinner and there was more old leaf-fall to walk on. It did not matter as much as he wanted it to. Every fourth or fifth step, his weight went down through something soft and dark that took his print and held it, and he knew the ground was writing him as he walked, and he kept walking anyway, because the smell of the next water was ahead of him and there was no other way to it.

He had crossed maybe half of it when he felt the quiet coming. The ground's small sounds thinned out somewhere ahead of him and a little to the east. He could not have said how he knew. It was a thing his body had learned at the fern hall, months before, and it never once failed him after that: a whole forest going quiet has a feel, wide and wrong, like weather arriving.

He ran out of time for choices. There was a hollow between two of the great root-butresses, deep in moss. He got into it, pressed himself down into the cold damp, and slowed his breathing to the smallest fire that would keep his legs alive. No print, no pattern, no movement. The old tricks, learned on a mountain far south of here, while men still hunted him.

Something came through the ground toward him.

It was not the pass's sleeper. The pass had been one great weight in one place, reading him in passing, minding its own business. This thing walked. He could feel its steps in his spine — slow, heavy, spaced out, the steps of something that owned the ground it was on. Where it passed, the whole floor of the forest held its breath. It came near enough that the moss between the roots trembled. It had a smell, faint through the wet: deep stone, like a cave breathing.

It stopped somewhere off to his left, and stood.

It stood for a long time. He knew what it was doing, because he had done it himself over a hundred kills: listening to the quiet, asking it where the strange thing was.

The red thing rose in him while it stood. It came up his neck and sat in his jaw, old and hot, and every line in his body said turn and answer the attention. There was no river here to carry it away. He spent it himself, slow breath by slow breath, holding himself still by nothing but his own will, until the heat burned down to nothing and there was nothing left of him but the quiet he was making.

The steps went on. North and east, deeper into the root-country, fading into the ground until the moss began to talk again, first in one place, then everywhere, and the birds came back. He lay until full dark, and longer. Then he got up, crossed the last of the bad ground under the stars, and came down before dawn to the colder water he had smelled. He got into it and let it hold him while his heart remembered how to be ordinary.

After that the land began to climb. The water ran straighter and faster. Stone showed up in the stream beds, then took them over. The great trees fell back — first into stands, then into single bent trees growing out of cracks — and the sky came in over the water in long bright pieces. The nights got cold with a new cold, dry and thin. He learned to sleep where the day's heat was still held: flat stones near the loud water, and beds of dry moss on the south faces. He was climbing, and his body knew it before his eyes confirmed it. The air itself was changing weight, the same change he had felt once before, going the other direction, down off the mountain he was born on.

The stream went up in a chain of small waterfalls, white thread after white thread down gray steps of rock. He climbed with it, wet through and easy, taking the fat fish out of the black pools one after another. The falls were stairs and the pools were pantries, and the noise of the water hid him the whole way up.

One gray morning, the haze that his eyes had been calling mountains stopped being haze.

It was stone. Ranges of it, running north and east into more ranges, blue-gray, with snow on the high shoulders even in the warm season. The wind that came down from them was the wind that had stopped every old animal on the walked roads: stone and snow and enormous distance. And the pull did not lean anymore. It pointed, straight at the highest of the snow peaks, like a smell riding a straight wind.

He came out onto a bare shoulder of rock to see it whole. Behind him, the deep forest lay like a dark sea. Somewhere in it, roads he would never walk again were being walked by everybody, and everything ordinary was going on without him. He thought about that and found it did not hurt. He was a stranger again, in a country of strangers, one young heavy animal with a jaw. That was the old way of being his. It still fit.

On the shoulder he took his first meal of the mountain country: one of the sure-footed gray animals of the high rocks, run down in the open in plain light — no quiet to read him here, no roots to tell on him, just the long chase and the take. Its years were thin and cold and clean, like the water it drank.

The road was behind him. The pull was ahead. Between the two, for the first time since the shell, there was nothing at all standing in his way but the world.

He drank from the thin water, and went up.

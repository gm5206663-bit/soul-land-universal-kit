# Chapter 3: The Ring of Light

The thaw took the snow off the mountain in three weeks, and when the snow was gone, the slope was not the slope any more. It was his.

That was the difference the winter had made, and he felt it the first morning he came down through the wet fern with the sun on his back. The rock seam was his seam. The seep was his seep. The talus where the stone-runners lived was his larder, and when he walked the paths between the cracks, the runners did not sit up and look at him any more.

They went still. Then they went down.

Something in them knew him before his shadow came across them. It was the same knowing he had seen in the deer-kind on the open snow, and it was new to be on the giving end of it. The low lives of the slope feared him now. Not because he was big — he was still small. Because of something in him that they could smell, and that he could not smell at all.

He put that fact away and went to the broken pine.

The nesters were still in the hole in the pine's heart. He walked up to the pine in the open, downhill side and all, and put his head into the hole. There was a flurry in the dark, and something stabbed at his eye — and this time it did not reach. His face had grown too hard for beaks, and he was too big for the doorway.

He took the nest and ate what was in it, the small things first and then the eggs. Afterwards he sat against the pine in the sun with his claws buried in the soft, rotten wood, and felt nothing at all about it. He had come up this slope a hatchling with a punctured face. He was going down it as something the pine was afraid of. One winter had done that; one tree could tell the whole story of it.

---

The pull had been waiting for the thaw all winter.

It came on the first truly warm morning, thin and far, from the low country south of his slope. It was not a smell and not a sound. It was a direction. Something down there was the right shape for him, and had been the right shape all winter, and the cold had only been hiding it.

So he went — farther than he had ever been.

The slope fell away below him into a valley of deep, mixed green. The trees were bigger than his trees, and the ground between them held more tracks than he could hold in his head. And as the little Devouring Dragon came down among them, the country opened in him.

He could not have described it. It was like warm water around a body that has been cold for a long time. His blood — thin, young, and mostly silent until now — sat up in him and answered the forest. He walked on with his head up and something that was not hunger and not fear standing along the whole length of his spine. The forest was the country of his kind. He had not known there was such a thing; his blood knew, and had always known.

The forest knew him too; by the afternoon he could not miss it. The low lives of the valleys — the ground-birds, the small burrowers, the pale things in the fern — went quiet as he passed, and not the ordinary quiet of a passing beast. In one old hollow he came on a fox-kind family at their den, and the whole family lay down flat in the fern, ears back, eyes on him. They did not run, and they did not move, until he was gone from their country.

He was not big, and he was not old. But he walked through the low country like weather, and it lay down before him, and he did not yet have the learning to wonder why. Before dark he made his den in a bank of old roots above a stream, in the shape his kind always makes: one way in, which he did not use, and one way out, which he did.

---

The bristle-back was the first thing that fought him for the valley.

He found it at the stream bend in the morning: a low, thick, rooting beast with its snout in the mud and needles along its back. It was not afraid of him — it looked straight at him and went on rooting, and this dull, fat thing had read him better than the foxes had.

So he watched it, all morning, the way he had once watched the stone-runners.

It was slow. It had short legs and a heavy shoulder, and it tore roots out of the bank with its snout and ate them. And every time anything came near it — a bird, a hare, a lizard-kind — it swung its head, the needles stood up with a wet tearing sound, and the thing that had come near went away.

The trick was the same trick the stone-runners had taught him. Be patient. Take the right moment.

He came at it from behind and above, off the bank, at the exact moment its whole snout was buried in the streamside mud. His jaws went onto its neck.

The needles came up, and he learned about the needles in the first heartbeat. They raked his muzzle and the side of his face when the beast humped and twisted under him, and they went through his lip and the soft skin under his jaw. The pain made his grip loosen for half a breath, and he felt the strength of the bristle-back come up into his jaws like the strength of a tree.

After that, it was only holding. The beast hurled itself against the bank, rolled, and drove its needles into him. He got his forelegs around it and set his weight, and the two of them went down the bank into the stream together. The water came up red around his jaws. He held through the heaving and the tearing, set the hold deeper each time the beast screamed, and the screaming went on for a long time out in the stream, where no other beast could hear it.

When it stopped, he dragged the bristle-back up the bank and ate it. And this meal was different from every meal before it.

The meat was heavier than any meat he had ever taken, and it was not just meat. When he swallowed it, everything in it that had been alive went down with it. He felt the bristle-back's life go into him — the years of it, the strength of it, the rooting and the digging and the going down into the cold. His blood drank all of it in, as dry ground drinks rain. And he understood, with a feeling that went through his whole body like a shiver, that the beasts of the forest were meat that fed more than the belly.

Then he felt something else, and it made him stop eating with his head down.

The air around the kill was not empty any more. He was drawing on it. A thin, warm thing was coming off the whole stream bend and going into him, through hide and scales and blood — the same thing the mountain air had done in the hungry weeks of winter, except that now it moved because he was pulling it.

He did not make it happen. It had always been in him; the winter had laid it down, the months of eating had thickened it, and now it came out whole, at his first real meal. His kind had a word for what he was doing. Every beast of his line, down all the years, had done the same, and none of them had ever needed to say it. He had no word at all — only the pull, the warmth, and the meat.

He ate until he could not eat any more. Then the deep sleep took him where he lay, and it worked on him longer than any sleep had worked before. He woke to a world with more in it.

It took him most of a morning to understand what had changed: it was not his eyes, and it was not his nose. Everything he looked at had a second thing in it now, as a rock has a warm side and a cold side. Some lives glowed. The beetles on the rock had a faint, flat look to them. The stone-runners, up on their haunches watching him, had something small and bright inside them. The bristle-back's meat had left a warm, banked coal low in his own belly, and he could almost see it when he turned his mind to it.

He could begin to tell the small lives from the great beasts. He could not read it yet. He could only feel the difference. But he had felt it, and that was a door opening.

---

The men came down the fern country eleven days later, and that day gave him the worst lesson of the spring.

He had been working the slopes above the fern country for a week, and he was up on a root-wall above the long grass when the fern country went loud. The change came at him across the whole valley at once: the hounds' cry in the middle distance, the flat, hard voices of the upright things, and under both of them a noise he had never heard — a fast rustling that ran through the fern like water going downhill.

A slender thing burst out of the fern at the bottom of his wall.

It was quick. He had never seen a beast run like that — low to the ground, all legs and speed, its coat the color of the fern, its whole body made for going. It gained on the hounds at every length.

He had seen the death that has no length take a deer-kind in mid-stride, on the snow. He knew what was coming, and he could do nothing about it.

The death came. The flat, hard sound, once, from the far trees — and the slender beast went down in the middle of its run, at full speed, slid a length through the summer, and did not move.

The upright things came out of the trees and walked across the fern to the body at their own pace. It was the walk of men who already know how a thing ends. There were two of them. The old one had the long, thin thing over his shoulder; the young one carried a short, bright blade and a pack. The hounds came up and stood around the body with their heads down, respectful. Then the old one crouched down and did something the little Devouring Dragon did not understand.

He watched from the root-wall without breathing.

Something rose out of the slender beast. It was not blood. Blood sinks, and spreads, and goes dark, and he had seen blood a hundred times. This came up out of the body as a light: a ring of light, thin and bright and pale gold, turning slowly in the air above the beast's back, hanging in the warm air like a thing being offered.

In this world, the years a soul beast has lived are real. They live in the beast's body the whole of its life, and when the body dies, the years come out of it all at once, as a ring of light. A ring of that kind is the greatest treasure of the hunt. Men are not born with the years of beasts in them — but they can take a ring in, and wear it, and grow strong on it. This is the oldest bargain of the hunt between men and soul beasts, and it is why men hunt.

He knew none of that. He saw only what he saw: the ring turning in the air, and the young man coming forward and kneeling down over the body, and the ring going into him.

The light went down out of the air and into the young man's body, as a splash of water goes into cloth. For one moment, the whole of the young one's frame stood lit from the inside with a pale gold glow. Then it was gone. The young man stood up with his hands shaking and his face shining, and the old one put a hand on his shoulder and said something short and hard, and the young one answered it with a laugh.

The body on the ground was just a body after that.

He lay on his root-wall for a long time after the men had gone with their hounds and their kill, and the thing he had just seen stayed open in him like a wound. They had not taken the meat of the beast at all; they had left most of it. What the men had come the whole length of the fern country to take — what they carried away with their hounds and their long, flat death — was the light. The years.

They take the years. That was the thing. Everything that had ever been hunted and eaten on his mountain was eaten for the belly. But these creatures hunted for the light that lived inside a beast's body, and when they had it, they carried the light away inside themselves.

He came down off the root-wall at dusk, when he was sure they were gone, and stood over the body. The slender beast was still warm. Its eyes were open, and its legs were folded as they had folded when it went down. And the pale light that had stood in it — the warm, banked coal he could now see in every living thing, when he tried — was gone out of it entirely. Where the beast's years had stood, there was a flat, gray nothing, like water where the spring has stopped.

He sat down beside it and looked at it for a long time. Then he ate it. There was no more in it now than in the beetles of the rock seam: the meat was meat, and the years were gone. He ate what was there, left the rest for the ravens, and walked back up the valley understanding two things. The men of the mountain were hunting a different thing than food. And the thing they were hunting, some part of him had always known, was the true meat of the world.

And there was one more thing, and it stayed with him longer than all the rest.

When the ring had risen out of the beast and the young man had knelt beneath it, the red thing in him — the one that wanted to go down to the lights and tear and take — had come up so hard that his vision went narrow. He had lain on the root-wall with his whole body trembling, at the wrong end of the valley, tasting the light in the air as he tasted meat. For the first time in his life, he had wanted to eat the years of a creature that was already dead and already in other hands.

The red thing was deeper than hunger. He knew that now. It was not hunger at all. It was what his blood was for.

He held it with the whole of the winter he had in him, and it did not calm. He lay on the ledge and let it stand up in his chest until the last of the men's smell went off the fern country. Then he went down to the ground, put his nose in the cold fern, and waited for the heaviness to drain out of his legs, as it drains out of a body after a long run.

He walked back to his roots-house in the dark and lay down, and the pale light stood over the emptied body in his memory all night. He was of the forest now, and the forest had told him its worst secret. Men did not only kill the beasts of the mountain. They took the years out of them and wore the years themselves. And one day, because the pull was pulling him, the years would be his. Behind his patience and his little jaws there stood a long line of other creatures who had wanted exactly the same thing, and the men who were better at it than any of them.

Then the deep sleep came for him, and for a while the world was quiet.

---

They had the fire going before the light went. The two of them ate sitting on their heels, with the ring-beast's haunch turning over the coals.

"It went in clean," said the young one. He kept turning his left hand over, looking at it. "The whole light. It went in at the palm and it went up my arm and it sat down in my chest."

"It will sit there," said the old one. He was a generation older, and he took his meat carefully, because he had been on this line long enough to know what a good day was worth. "You will feel it tomorrow, when you carry the pack up the fern slope."

"Will I be stronger?"

"You will be heavier," said the old one. "And you will be warm in the mornings before the fire is lit. That is what a first light gives a man. Strength comes later, and it comes with work."

The young man put his hand down and looked at the beast instead. The great gray carcass lay on the moss with the hide cut down the belly where the old man had done the work, and the meat left lying where it fell.

"Four this season," he said. "Four lights. One for each of us, one for the man at the water, one over for the lodge."

"The lodge takes the pelts," said the old one. "We take the lights. That is how the line has always worked, and the reason it works that way is that the line is ours."

"The lodge says the ground is theirs."

"The lodge says a great many things," said the old one. "The lodge sits in a warm room and counts what we carry down. Eat. Then sleep. Tomorrow we walk the fern country east, and I want the tracks read before noon."

"What is in the fern country east?"

"Three beasts with more years on them than this one," said the old one. "Two cows and a bull. Or there were, three weeks ago, when I walked it. The bull has been in the same break two winters."

"And the light off a bull that age?"

"Bigger than this one, if the ground gives it," said the old one. "And you do not take it with your hand out. You take it with a line, a bow, and eleven hours of standing still."

"Eleven hours of standing still."

"Eleven hours. You will learn to stand." The old one lifted his head and read the sky for a moment. "It will rain before morning."

"Then it rains on the kill-ground."

"Then the trail reads clean behind us," said the old one, "and the ravens do the rest of the work. Where is the hound?"

"At the camp, with my brother."

"Good. He is not to walk a kill-ground for three days, or he will eat the hide off it and we carry nothing down."

"And the bow?"

"Strung and dry," said the young one.

They ate. The young man turned his hand over one more time in the firelight and then put it away in his sleeve, and the old man watched the small gold light move under the skin of his wrist and said nothing about it, because the boy had walked twelve seasons of lines to get it. He banked the coals and lay down on his side of the fire, and the fern country stood around them in the dark, going about its business.

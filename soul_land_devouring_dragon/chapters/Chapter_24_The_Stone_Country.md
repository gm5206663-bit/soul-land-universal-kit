# Chapter 24: The Stone Country

The change came slowly. First the trees grew farther apart. Then the streams ran faster and colder. One morning he walked out of the last of them, and the ground under his feet was stone, and it stayed stone all day.

There was nowhere to hide. The forest behind him had been dark and full of cover. Here the land was gray and open, the grass grew in thin patches, and the wind blew all the time, cold and loud. He could see a long way in every direction, and anything on the stone could be seen a long way off.

That first day he also saw the bird. It was very high up, turning in slow circles over the mountain wall ahead. He watched it until his neck ached. It never flapped, and it never came down. He saw it again the next day, and every day after that. He never saw it catch anything. It just turned, and turned, and waited.

There were three kinds of animals on the stone, and he learned from all of them.

The first kind looked like the goat-antelopes of the low country, but heavier, gray like the rock, with short thick legs. They lived in herds on the steep grass shelves. They never hid from anything. They fed in the open with their heads up, spread far apart, and while some of them ate, others watched. When one lifted its head, the ones next to it kept eating. The watching went around the herd all day like water going around stones.

He tried to stalk them the old way and it did not work. There was no dark to move through and no cover to break his shape. On his second try, the whole herd was looking at him before he was halfway across the open ground, and they simply walked up the shelf out of reach and stood there. They did not even run. They knew he could not follow them where they were going.

So he stopped stalking and started reading the ground instead. The stone was not flat. It had dead streambeds cut into it, low places, long ridges, and in the late afternoon every rock threw a long shadow to the east. That was the only cover the country had, and it was enough.

He killed one of them on the sixth day. The herd was feeding along a shelf with a dry streambed below it. The wind was loud that afternoon, and the wind's noise covered his steps. He went down into the streambed a good distance away and walked along it until he was below the west end of the herd. Then he waited for the light. When the sun got low, every animal's shadow stretched out long and broad beside it, and one old female was feeding apart from the others, right at the edge, with her shadow and a boulder's shadow lying together in one dark patch. He crossed the last open stretch inside the moments when the nearest watchers' heads were down, came up over the edge of the streambed, and took her back down into it with him.

The herd stood in a circle for a while and then went back to feeding. Nothing had chased them. The bird far above turned and turned and did not come down. He ate in the streambed with the wind going over him, and he had learned the first rule of the stone country: the ground itself was the only hiding place, and the light was on his side if he was patient.

The second kind was small and lived in the loose rock. Scree, the piles of broken stone that sat on the steep slopes, was full of them, and each pile was like a small town. They were quick, fat, and loud. The first time he hunted near one of their towns, a single sharp cry went up, then another, then the whole slope was calling, and the noise carried farther than any sound he had ever made. Everything on the mountain for a mile around knew something was moving there.

After that he stopped hunting near them. Instead he walked his routes in the quiet spaces between their towns, and he learned to use them. If the scree on a slope was silent, nothing strange was on that slope. If one town suddenly began to call, something was there, and it was not him. They were the mountain's alarm system, and he let them work for him.

The third kind was the bird. He never learned its rules, because it never showed them. It turned in its circles, high up, every day, and every living thing on the stone went about its business underneath it without looking up. He looked up. He had spent too much of his life being the watched thing not to recognize watching when he saw it. But it never came down on him, and he could not reach it, so the two of them let each other be.

The cold came early on the mountain. The first hard night taught him what he was worth in the open. He walked until his legs got stiff, and the wind went through him as if he had no scales at all. Just before light he found a patch of low ground behind a boulder where some dry lichen grew, and the difference between that spot and the windward stone was the difference between two countries. He lay there and slept through the morning, and after that he stopped thinking of warmth as something that came with time. Warmth was a place. The lichen beds held the day's heat for an hour after dark. The low side of a boulder was good, the high side was nothing. And the flat stones just above a loud stream stayed warm all night, because the water never froze and gave its heat back to the rock. He learned where those places were, as he had once learned where the safe kills were, and he moved between them as the season turned.

Food was thin. The goat-kind was all leg and wind, and the scree animals were hardly a mouthful. He ate, and hunted again, and ate, and he did not get fat as the low country had once made him fat. The deep sleeps came slower. He got thinner, and his growth slowed down, and at first this troubled him, and then it did not. His scales came in tighter. His wind got longer. The soft weight the forest had put on him burned away through the cold weeks, and what was left under it was a harder animal that needed less and noticed more.

In the third season he made his one real mistake on the stone.

There was a valley he had to cross, running up toward the wall. He could take the long way around, over open ground where the bird could see him the whole time, or he could go straight across the head of the valley, over a wide slope of loose scree that had no town on it. He took the straight line. It was the choice of an animal that owns the dark, and the mountain corrected him the moment his weight came onto the slope.

The stone began to move under his feet. It did not slide all at once. First it stirred, a long slow shifting that ran down the slope ahead of him, and then the whole face of it was running, a river of loose rock with him in the middle. The noise was enormous. He could do nothing but ride it. He kept his feet by some instinct he did not know he had, and the slide carried him down the slope and dumped him out at the bottom onto a field of freshly settled stone.

Before the noise had even died away, the bird turned and came down the wind.

He did the only thing the country allowed. He lay down flat on the stones he had ridden in on, gray on gray, and made himself into a thing the slide had made — one long shape among many. The bird came down and circled low over him. He could see it clearly now: the width of it, the fingers at the ends of its wings, the head turning from side to side. The red thing rose in him at the nearness of it, the old heat, the urge to turn and answer the attention with his one answer. There was no river here to carry it away. There was only bare stone and daylight. He breathed it out, slow breath by slow breath, into the rock under him, and held still, and after a long time the heat was gone and he was only an animal lying down.

The bird circled him for the better part of the morning. Then it went back up into its height. He lay where he was until the light went, and moved in the dark to one of the warm places. The crossing cost him a night of cold and hunger, because every scree town in the valley had heard the slide and knew exactly where the noise had come from.

He never took the straight line again. On the stone, a mistake was not a moment that passed. The noise ran downhill. The alarm ran from slope to slope. The bird remembered which valleys had things happening in them. The mountain kept accounts, and he walked like an animal that knew it.

The pull went across the stone country like a line drawn on a map, straight toward the highest of the snow peaks. But the wall of the mountains did not open where he wanted it to open. Range stood behind range, and the peak he was pulled toward sat far back in the middle of it all. What the mountain offered instead were the passes — the low places where the ranges had never joined, gaps worn by wind and by the feet of everything that had ever crossed here.

He found the first pass by reading the ground. A path's worth of polished stone ran up a seam in the wall. There was dung on it, old and new. There were bones at the bottom of one steep stretch, from a crossing that had gone badly for somebody, a long time ago. Anything that wanted over this wall used the passes, and had used them forever.

He went up with a herd of the goat-kind. They were moving to the high seams for the season, and he joined them at the bottom of the path in the gray light of morning, at their edge, at their pace, one more heavy animal walking the road. The wind through the notch at the top was a standing wall of noise, and it blew his smell out behind him where nothing ahead could read it. They went up together, single file in the narrow places. The bird turned far above them all and came down on none of them.

On the far side, the next country opened out: more stone, then more ranges behind it, going blue into the distance, and the snow peak one wall nearer than it had been. Clouds were already building on its shoulder, the first storm of the winter coming in.

He left the herd at the bottom of the seam without either side doing anything about it. Below him, in a hollow of the new stone, a spring came out of the rock, warm. Steam hung over its stones in the cold air, and green grew around it, the only green in sight. He denned there through the first hard snows of the far side. The warm stone under him, the snow over everything, the streams going quiet as they iced — he slept the mountain's winter the same as the mountain's own animals: low, slow, patient.

The pull did not sleep. It ran under the snow the whole winter, as it had run under everything else, straight toward the snow peak. It did not push him and it did not hurry him. It had never once asked him for anything.

When the snow went, and the passes opened again, he would go on.

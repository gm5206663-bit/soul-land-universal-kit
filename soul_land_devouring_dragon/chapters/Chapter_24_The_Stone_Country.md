# Chapter 24: The Stone Country

The mountains did not begin. The forest thinned, the streams grew loud and quick, and one morning the ground under his feet was simply stone, and stayed stone, and the world opened into a cold light with nothing in it to hide behind.

It was the opposite country. The deep forest proper had been dark, rooted, speaking — a ground that repeated every step to whatever listened. The stone country said nothing, and hid nothing, and saw everything. Distance was the ruler here. A beast walking a bare shoulder could be read from a ridge away off, and the wind ran loud and constant, and the sky stood enormous over the gray, and there was no dark to lie in and no water deep enough to be absent from. After a lifetime of being nothing, he had walked out of the one country where nothing was possible into another one, and the trick of surviving it was not the trick he had brought.

The high kinds taught him. They had lived on the rule of distance for ten thousand generations, and everything about them was a lesson in being seen and living anyway.

The gray sure-footed kind — the mountain's own meat, that he had first taken on the shoulder of stone below the wall — did not hide at all. It fed in the open, in herds, on the sparse green shelves, and it lived by watching: heads up, spaced wide, every beast a sentinel, the whole herd taking its sleep in shifts with the sun. It could not be stalked across open stone, and it knew it. What it could not do was watch every gully at once. So his hunting became a study of edges: the seam-lines of the ground, the dead streambeds, the long shadows of the late light — the one cover the country allowed was its own shape, and he learned to read it as he had once read the forest's quiet.

He took his first gray-kind that way, in the late light of a cold-clear day, and the taking was the country's whole lesson in one act. The herd fed on a shelf across a dead streambed, spaced wide as always, six heads coming up in turns while the rest fed. He went down into the streambed a ridge away and walked it to them under the sound of the wind, a moving stone among stones. He rose out of it only inside their own shadows — the long light throwing each beast's shade out broad beside it, his shape lying inside the shade of a boulder at the herd's west edge. The straggler was an old one, feeding apart, at the seam where the shelf fell off. The sentinels were up when he moved — up and looking at wind and shadow and distance, the country's ordinary alarms. He crossed the last open ground inside the gap between one head's lifting and the next, and took the old one into the dead streambed with him. The herd stood its circle for a hundred breaths, and then went back to feeding, because nothing else had happened. Distance had not seen him. The mountain's own shape had hidden him in plain light. He ate in the streambed with the wind over him, and the high watcher turned far above the shelf all that evening and descended from nothing.

The scree colonists — small, quick, alarm-voiced, living in the loose rock in warring towns — taught him the country's other rule. They were food, the small change of the high ground, and they were also its bells. Nothing moved near a scree town without a voice going up, a single sharp note that ran along the whole slope, town to town, farther than any sound he had ever made. The first week, he hunted them and set every slope talking. After, he mapped their towns as he had once mapped the watchers' camps, and walked his lines between them, and let their alarm be his sentry instead of his enemy: a scree that was quiet meant the ground near it was empty.

And over everything, patient in the updrafts, hung the high watcher. He saw it the first day, a shape turning in the cold sky above the wall, and he saw it every day after, and he never once saw it hunt. It turned, and turned, and turned, at a height that made it nothing and a patience that made it everything, and every living thing on the stone spent its whole life beneath that turning without ever looking up. He looked up. He had been the watched thing too long not to know one when he saw it.

---

The cold season came early in the stone country, and it came as the country's own voice: the wind.

In the forest, weather had been something the trees took for him. Here it came down off the high snows with nothing between, and the first hard night taught him what he was worth in the open. He walked all night, stiffening, and learned the first law of the stone: warmth was a place, not a time. The lichen beds held the sun's heat an hour past dark. The lee of a boulder was a different country from the windward face. And the low stone above the loud streams stayed above freezing while everything else went hard — the water's own warmth, breathed out all night. He learned the map of warm places as he had once learned the map of safe kills, and the map became his dens, moving with the seasons.

The meals were thin. That was the country's bargain, and it took him a season to stop fighting it. A beast of his weight, built for the fat lowlands, walked the stone country at a cost, and the stone country did not pay fat. The gray kind was all leg and wind. The scree towns were handfuls. He ate, and hunted again, and ate, and the deep sleeps came slower and thinner, and his growth slowed to the mountain's own pace — and when he thought about it, lying in the lee of a boulder with the wind going over, he found he did not mind. The low country had made him big. The stone country, he came to feel, was making him something else, and the cold was the tool of it. His scales tightened. His wind lengthened. The fat of the forest burned off him through the cold months and left, under it, a harder and quieter animal.

The wrong he did in the stone country, he did in the third season, and the ground itself did it with him.

There was a valley to cross, running up toward the wall, and the map of it offered two lines: the long walk around, on open stone, under the turning watcher, or the straight line across the head of the valley — a wide slope of loose scree, unmarked, unnerving in a way he had not yet learned to read. He took the straight line. It was the low country's choice, the choice of a beast who owns the dark, and the mountain corrected him the moment his weight came onto the slope.

The stone began to move.

It did not slide all at once. It woke under him, a long stirring that ran down the slope ahead of his feet, and then it was running, a river of gray rock with him in the middle of it, and the noise of it went up the valley like a flood announcing itself to the whole country. He rode it. There was nothing else to do. He kept his feet by instinct he did not know he had, and the slide carried him down and spat him out at the bottom, whole, on a field of new-set stone — and above him, before the noise had even finished dying, the watcher turned and came down the wind.

He did the only thing the country allowed. He lay down on the stone he had ridden, flat, in plain sight, and became a thing the slide had made: a long gray shape among gray shapes, nothing new, nothing worth a second turning. The watcher came down the wind and turned above him, close enough now that he could see it whole — the width of it, the fingers at the wing-ends, the head turning, turning — and the red thing rose in him at the nearness of it, and there was no river and no moss, only bare stone and light.

He spent it into the stone. Breath by breath, into the ground, under the turning of the thing above him, until the boil was gone and he was only meat at rest. The watcher turned over him for a long time. Then it went up, unsatisfied, back into its patience, and he lay until the light went, and moved in the dark to the map of warm places, and paid for the crossing with a night of cold and hunger and the whole valley knowing where he was.

He never took the straight line again. It went into him beside the fern hall and the bad ground: in the stone country, a mistake was not a moment — it was a mark. The noise ran downhill; the alarm ran slope to slope; the watcher remembered the valleys where things happened. The forest had had a memory of quiet. The mountain had a memory of noise. He walked accordingly, and lived.

---

The pull crossed the stone country like a plumb line, and the wall of the world grew ahead of him, and the question of his walking became the question of the passes.

The pull pointed at the highest whiteness — had pointed, straight as a smell on a straight wind, since the shoulder of stone below. But walking was not pointing. The wall did not open where he wanted it to open. It stood, range behind range, blue-gray and white-shouldered, and the high whiteness he was pulled toward stood far back in it, at a depth of stone that no straight line could touch. What the mountain offered instead were the passes: the low seams where the ranges had failed to join, wind-scoured, often high, always walked — because whatever crossed this wall, crossed it at the seams, and had done so forever. He found the first of them by the oldest evidence there is: the ground told him. A path's worth of polished stone, a warmth of dung old and new, the bones of a crossing done badly, all running up a seam toward a notch in the wall.

He stood at the foot of it in the late light and read the country above with everything the road and the forest had taught him. The pass was a road again — the walked-road law, come back at the top of the world. Whoever used it would be seen using it, by everything that lived on the use of it. The seams were the mountain's rivers; the users were its fish; and he knew from a hundred streams how a fish lived.

He took the pass in the first hard cold of the stone country's winter, at gray light, in the company of the gray kind — a herd of them moving up to the high seams for the season, spaced and watchful, and one young heavy shape with a jaw among them, at their edge, at their pace, ordinary. The wind in the notch was a standing wall of noise, better cover than any forest, and his smell went nowhere the wind did not want it. They crossed together, and the high watcher turned far over them all and descended from none of them. On the far side of the notch the whole next country opened: more stone, and more ranges beyond it going blue to the edge of seeing, and the highest snows he was pulled toward standing one wall nearer, its shoulder carrying the first storm of the winter like a shawl of smoke.

He left the herd at the seam's foot, and they were no more sorry than the root-boar had been. Below him on the far side, in a hollow of the new stone, a spring ran out of the rock, warm, steaming faintly in the cold, its stones green with a life the mountain did not allow anywhere else. He denned there through the first hard snows of the far side — the warm ground under him, the snow silence over everything, the loud streams icing toward quiet — and slept the mountain's winter the way the mountain's own did: low, slow, patient. Under the snow the pull ran as it had run under everything: straight as a plumb line toward the high snows, asking nothing, hurrying nothing, certain as stone.

He was thinner in that winter than he had been since the border. He was also, in a way the panel would count and he never would, more.

The snow would go. The passes would open. The pull would still be pointing.

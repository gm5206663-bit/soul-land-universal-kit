# Chapter 12: The Bull at the Water

The bull came down to the water at the edge of the dark, the way it had come down every evening for years, and he lay in the high fern above the shallows and read it down.

The summer stood at its height. Every fern run was loud with the young of the year, the heavy kinds were fat on the new feed, and the water in the low ground ran brown and quick. It was the best season he had ever known, and he was spending the first half of it doing nothing — because the doing of nothing was the work. The bull's run had to be read the deep forest's way: slowly, in signs, before a line was spent on it.

He learned the beast's whole day. Where it slept — a dry shelf above the ravines. Where it fed — along the low breaks where the feed was thickest. Where it drank — the wide shallows where the bank came down smooth and the ground under the water was firm — and how long it stood at the water with its head down and its ears working. He kept to the high ground a week, and then lower, and then close, and the beast never read him once, because he was careful, and because he was patient, and because patience was the thing he had paid for with the stag.

Then he went down to the old feeding lines and found them empty, and learned what his own word was costing him.

It came to him as the important things came now, in the feel of the country. The heavy kinds of the hot springs were drifting. The old feeding lines along the low breaks were being walked out and left. The heavy ones were up on the high ground where they had never fed before, and the run where he had killed the thick-paw stood with the sign of it still on it and no beast near it.

He put the two things side by side and the account was plain. His own sign was written in the ground in a dozen places now, and it said one thing: this ground is watched, and I am near it and eating. The beasts of the country could not read a name. They did not know what he was. But they could read what the ground read, and the ground read danger, and they had moved.

They had moved the bull with them. In the last days of the summer the beast's hours shifted: it came down to the water in the hot middle of the day now, when the flies were heavy and had made it stupid, and it drank fast and went back up to the shelf. The shallows stood empty at the edge of the dark, where it had drunk every evening for years.

It was his own word that had moved the old beast. He sat with that a while. In the deep forest a kill taken wrong is a lesson, and he took the lesson: the sign that guards the country guards the hunter too.

---

The smoke stood up over the forest while the heat and the flies were still on the water.

Two fires, down-country of the giants, where no fire had stood before. He read them from the high shoulder in the last of the light. They had walked in off the long road, gone up the giants' country, and split. One line went down toward the low breaks. The other stayed on the water line — and the staying line was the dangerous one, because a line that stays is a line that has read something and wants it.

The hound told him the rest. The wind on the third evening came up out of the low country, and he stood in the fern with it on his face and read them whole: men-smell, iron, the light that eats wood, and under it the hound-smell. The hound was working the ground out ahead of the line, reading the country as he read it, wanting what the ground had been near in the last day or two. They were not walking blind. They were working the water line.

He stood in the fern a long time with his jaw shut. They had marked the same country he had marked. They were working toward the same water. And on the old bull's run his own word stood in the moss in a dozen places, telling every beast with a nose that the ground was watched — and telling one hound the same thing.

He could not take a deep mark off the ground. He could only add to it. And the taking could not wait for a cleaner ground: their line was on the water, and the bull was at its flies-stupid hour, and the window was this or none. So he went up to the water that night and walked the shallows end to end, and where the old beast came down to drink he set a second word, low and careful, where the bank came down. It was the oldest trick he had, the same that had held the thick-paw's ground: a word that holds the ground for the hunter and keeps every other beast off it.

Then he went up to the high shelf above the ravines, and lay in the fern, and waited.

The bull came down at the hot middle of the next day, and he took it there, at the water.

---

The old beast came down the break at the same slow walk it used every day of the summer. It reached the shallows and put its head down. The moment its mouth touched the water he came off the high fern in a long silent rush and hit it in the ribs.

The bull did not go down. That was the first thing, and he felt it in every bone: he might as well have hit the bank. The beast came round on its back legs with the horns swinging, and the deep weight came with them, and he took the first horn along the shoulder plates and was thrown wide into the shallow water. The water went up. The world was a roar and a wall of brown.

He came up with the mud in his eyes, and the bull was on him. It had turned faster than anything that size had a right to turn, its head low and driving, and the horn coming was the whole of the world — and he met it. That was the whole of the fight, in the end: the meeting. He took the horn on his jaw and turned it, and the blow went along his skull and out through his neck, and it was like catching a falling tree.

Then the jaw had the bull's head. He set it deep, as he had set the thick-paw, but the bull's neck was the thickest neck he had ever closed on, and the old beast did not scream and did not run. It braced. It went down on its forelegs with its whole weight set forward and dragged him through the shallows, and it threw him off its head twice, and both times he came back onto it, because that was what had to be done and there was no art past it. His jaw locked higher, into the base of the skull. The bull rolled in the water, and rolled again, its legs in the air, the water coming up brown around them both — and it was still rolling when the rolling began to slow.

The light came up out of the old body while the body was still moving. He had read a hundred lights by then, and he had never read one like this. It rose low and slow, purple, deep enough that the eye wanted to go down into it and could not, and it stood in the air above the horns for a long breath. Then it went down into the ground like a stone down a well. The feel of it went down past everything he could reach and came back up as a weight.

He stood in the torn shallows over the old body and did not move until the water had stopped moving around him.

Then the bull was meat, and the meat was the deepest meal the summer had to give. He ate, and the two red things came, and this time neither of them frightened him.

He had thought the hunger would come for him at a kill like this one. The hunger had spent a year making him mark beasts and lose them while he sat watching; it had cost him the grey stag. It came now, the red pull with the meat in it, and he stood in it and let it burn and did not move, because there was nowhere to move to — the bull was lying in the shallows and the meat was his. And the boil came as it always came, the old rampage of his kind, and the rampage and the hunger met the choosing, and the choosing held them both without a fight.

He ate until the belly was tight, and then ate again, because the deep meat of a thing like that does not keep in the summer and the flies were already working the shallows. The dark came down and he was still at the water, heavy — too heavy to move far — and that was the danger. A beast that heavy can fight, but cannot run.

So he lay down in the fern at the top of the bank above the shallows, and let the meat go down in him, and while it went down the change came into his blood. It came as it had come after the beast from the far edge of the deep forest, two summers back, only deeper: the meat and the years of the bull going down into the places of him and coming back out as the old blood, and the old blood grading itself higher, scale by scale, in the dark. He felt the plates on his back lie tighter over him. He felt the crown of his head weigh more. And the pull of the old thing in his blood — the pull that had sung once in the deep summer — turned over once in its sleep. He slept, and did not sleep deep, and in the cold of the early morning he heard the men come.

---

They came at the water's hour, because that was their craft. He came up out of the sleep with the shapes standing in his nose — men-smell, iron, the smoke of their fires still on them, and the hound out ahead of the line, working the bank. He lay flat in the fern at the top of the bank with the dawn coming grey, the shallows below him, and about thirty paces of open ground between.

He could not run. That was settled, with the bull's meat in him, and he did not waste time arguing with it. So he did the other thing. He lay in the fern and stopped being a thing that was there.

The quiet went over him like water rising. It was the deep stilling, paid for in the deep forest and rebuilt all that summer, and he had never held it over ground this wrong: the shallows brown and torn, the bull's body in the water, his sign and the bull's blood on the bank, and thirty paces of open ground with a hound working it.

The hound came down the bank first. It was a low grey thing with a heavy chest, its nose close to the ground and its whole body in the feel, and he knew the moment it came on his line. The hound checked. It came up the bank a few paces and stopped with its head down in the torn moss where he had lain through the night and where the bull's blood had run in, and it stood there a long moment.

Then it sat down. It put its head up, and told the men.

The men came up the bank slow and spread. The one in front was the tallest; he carried the iron in both hands and there was no fire in his face at all. The young one came beside him with the list. The hound sat in the moss between them, head up, its whole body pointed at the fern.

He held the quiet. It was costing more than it had ever cost. His blood was loud with the bull's meat and the grading, and the meat was a heat the quiet could not put out. He lay in the fern with three men and their hound thirty paces away, the hound pointing at him, and the hound's certainty standing in the air like smoke.

The tallest man looked at the fern for a long time. Then he took the iron in both hands and walked three paces toward it, and he stopped, and he stood, and he looked. Behind him the hound sat and did not come. The young one said something quiet, and the tallest man answered without turning his head, and the iron came up — and then came down again, and the man turned around and walked back to the water.

He did not know what they said. He was not meant to, and it would have changed nothing if he had.

He took them out of the country as the ground carries a secret: by being nothing, for as long as it took. They stood at the water a while. They read the shallows, and the bull's body, and the bank, and the hound's sitting. Then they went back down the water line as they had come, and he lay in the fern and let the quiet down slowly, and the letting-down was its own cost — the ground had to learn him again after a quiet that deep, and the learning took the day. He slept in the fern that night, and woke with the cost paid, the meat settled, and the plates tighter on his back.

---

At the water's edge, when the men had come back down the bank and stood in the shallows, Gao Tie put his hand on the hound's head. He left it there for a while.

"It holds it like a name," he said. "It's been on this bank since before the light. It doesn't hold an hour for a piece of grass. There is something on this bank, and whatever it is, it was here last night with the bull."

"Then it killed the bull," said Xiao An. "Bull, and sign, and the body picked over, and it was here when we came. Not the day after. Not a day behind. Here. We walked on it."

Nobody spoke for a moment. The water went past their boots, brown and quick.

"You saw the fern," said Gao Tie. "Thirty paces of open fern, and nothing in it."

"I saw it," said Xiao An. "I walked it twice."

"And the hound?"

"The hound sat," said Xiao An. "The hound does not sit for nothing."

"Beasts leave sign," said Gao Tie. "A beast that size leaves sign going in and sign going out. There was no sign going out."

"There was no sign going in," said Xiao An. "Only the beast at the water and the bull's blood on the bank. Whatever ate the bull came out of the forest, took it, and went back into the forest the same way it came."

"The ground at the fern," said Old Qin. "Did you see it? Nothing. No track, no sign, no beast standing in thirty paces of open fern with a hound pointing at it." He looked at the fern a while longer. He was not a man who was ever in a hurry. "That is past reading," he said. "That is a thing that signs, and does not show, and now it shows that it can stand in front of us and not be seen."

He took out the list, and the brush, and the ink, and he wrote. The writing was careful and slow, as always.

"What do we put down for it?" said Gao Tie. "A name? We have no name. A kind? We have no kind. We have sign, and a bank, and a smell the hound will not leave."

"We put down what we have," said Old Qin.

"Two lines on it now?" said Gao Tie.

"One line, and the sign of the seen on it," said Old Qin. "When a line has been seen, the list goes to it. We do not go hunting the hot springs again for the deep lights. We go where the country says the beast has been, and we find what leaves these signs, and then the list will have its face and its name on it, and the season will have paid for itself twice."

He folded the list and put it away. "The stag's light and the bull's, if we can take it off the body in the state it is in — and we can — those go down the road with the rest. The tables buy depth for frames that have grown to hold it. That is the market, and the market is the work. But the beast —" and he looked one more time at the empty fern, "the beast is a line now, and a line does not sit on a list forever."

"And the other thing?" said Gao Tie.

"The other thing is a line with no face on it," said Old Qin. "We do not hunt what we cannot see. We hunt where it has been, and we read it, and one day we will read it wrong and it will be standing in front of us. When that day comes, we will find out what it is. Until then, the list."

They went back down the water line, and the hound went last. Twice it turned its head.

---

In the high fern, with the day's cost paid and the old bull's years working down in him, he read the water country in the last of the light. He read the track of their going down the water line. He read the fires come up again down-country of the giants.

He did not know what they had written. He never would.

But he had lain in the fern with three men and their hound thirty paces away and the hound pointing at him, and he had come out of it, and the plate on his heart knew the price of that. He had carried the quiet further than it had ever been carried, and it had held. And the second thing he knew was that it would not hold twice in the same way. A hound that has found you once can find you again.

He had spent a season learning that the sign that guards the country guards the hunter. Now he had spent a morning learning the other half of it: that a line with no face is a line that keeps its freedom, and the day it gets a face, the hunting changes.

Two lists over one country. Their list now carried the sign of the seen on the trail that was him, and the next hunt of theirs would not come with hands only.

It would come with eyes.

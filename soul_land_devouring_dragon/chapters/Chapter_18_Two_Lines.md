# Chapter 18: Two Lines

The water went down all summer and the low country came apart into pools.

By the middle of the season the two streams were chains of still water with long cracked flats between them, and everything that lived in the low country walked the same lines between the same pools. The slow heavy kind came down to drink at dusk. The birds worked the drying edges. And at the far end of each chain, where the water still ran over stone, the runs stayed quick and clear and cold.

He drank at the runs.

He had stopped drinking at still water in the first week. He came down to the main pool one night and smelled the hound before he reached the bank. She was staked in the reeds above the water on a short line, and there was a fire behind a screen of cut branches, and boots going round the edge in the morning's mud. He backed off and drank two miles up, at a run that came out of the rock, and lay there a while in the dark with his ears on the low country. He could not read whatever the men wrote, but he could read a watch, and the watch was on the water.

So the summer made its own rules. He hunted the upper water, where the meat came to drink at the runs. He took his kills standing in the stream over stone, ate in the water, and left the rest up on the bank for the crows. He crossed on the rock bars, gave the still pools and their long soft edges all the room they wanted, and drank where the water moved. It cost him miles and hunting hours every day, and the season was hot, and twice a month he came in with the flies at the cut places and lay up a day in the shade. He paid it. Nothing of him lay anywhere the ground could write.

The hunger came up after every meal the same as always. Over a full belly, in the heat, on bare stone, it asked its question, and the question did not change.

---

The watch at the water was small and patient, and by midsummer it had learned the country's hours.

Gao Tie put the wire across the low neck of the main pool on both sides and set the blind up off the ground, downwind. Xiao An walked the draw-down every morning and read the whole picture as it changed: the water shrinking a hand's width a day, the mud ring round the pool going hard, the trails of the heavy kind coming in to drink and going out fatter and slower. The younger man carried water up from the pool, mended the blind, and counted the days.

"There is another line on the sheet," said the younger man, one hot morning. He had the copy of the list in his coat, the way the lodge hands carried it. "I read it again last night. The pale beast. Heavy, pale-sided, wide-tracked. It stood the wire and never touched it. It killed at the water and went south."

"That is the line," said Xiao An. She was up to her shins in the pool's edge, reading the new water line where the mud had cracked.

"And this water is full of tracks," said the younger man. "Grazer tracks and pig tracks and more tracks, going in and going out. Not one of them is the pale beast's."

"Because the pale one is south in the dry," said Gao Tie. "It has its own water. It does not walk this far for a drink."

"So we sit here for a season and watch it drink somewhere else."

"We sit here because Old Qin said to sit here," said Gao Tie.

The hound laid her head on the ground.

She was slower this summer, and she slept deep in the heat of the day, and the three of them had watched her do it and none of them had said a word about it. But she worked every night she was put on the line, and the nights were when the water gave up its business.

The first of the hound's nights came in the middle of the season. She woke out of a sleep and stood and went to the end of her line and pointed across the water at nothing at all, her whole body a line drawn at the dark bank, and she stood there a quarter of an hour. Gao Tie got up and stood beside her with his hand on her collar and looked at the same nothing. The mud at the bank was clean of any print bigger than a fox's, and the water lay flat, and no bird spoke.

"Nothing," said the younger man, when he came out of the blind.

"Not nothing," said Gao Tie. "She has never pointed at nothing in her life."

They sat up the rest of the night. Nothing came, and in the grey she ate and slept. The second night she did it again, and the third; and after the third night Gao Tie moved his bedroll down to the pool's edge beside her and slept there, night after night, with his hand on her line. Whatever she had found, the other two left it to her and to him, the way you leave a thing to be paid for.

Then, ten days on, they found the kill.

---

It was Xiao An who walked onto it, on her morning round, a mile and a half up the water at the first of the runs. She came back at a fast walk and got the other two and the hound, and the four of them stood on the bank in the rising sun and looked at what the river had to say.

There was a grazer on the bank at the head of the run, a heavy beast of the slow kind, dead two days by the look of it. The crows had been at it. It lay with its forelegs in the shallows and its body up on the gravel, and everything that had been done to it had been done at the deep parts, and there was a wide drag-mark in the gravel that ran from the shallows up onto the bank.

"That is a kill," said the younger man. "Some beast killed it right here at the water and ate it. That is as clear as a line on paper."

"Yes," said Xiao An. "Now find me the beast."

She walked him through it as she had been taught. The soft mud went a full fifty paces along both banks, and it was soft enough to take the sparrows' feet, and it had taken them: bird trails and fox trails and the grazer's own deep prints coming in to drink. The gravel bank was loose and loud, and it held everything that touched it: the grazer's hooves in the drag, a fallen branch's mark, the crows' fat feet. And along the whole of it, up and down the bank for fifty paces, there were no tracks of a heavy hunter. None. No pad, no claw, no pressed place where something large had stood to eat.

"She is right," said Gao Tie. He was down on one knee by the drag-mark with the hound shuddering against his leg. She was working the bank in short, hard passes, and at the shallows at the head of the run she stopped and put her nose down into the water and barked, once, and then stood staring up the run where the water came down over the stone.

"She has the smell," said Gao Tie.

"Whose smell?" said Old Qin.

None of them had heard him come up the bank. He had walked in from the lodge road in the night and followed the tracks they had made that morning, and now he stood above them on the bank with the dust of the road on his boots, and he looked at the kill the way he looked at every page.

"The smell at the water," said Gao Tie. "She has it the way she had it at the big crossing two summers back. It is the same. It is not the pale one's."

"How do you know it is not the pale one's?"

"Because last spring she walked the pale one's tracks all along the south water and she was easy on them," said Gao Tie. "She knew them; she went down them like a road. This one she will not leave alone, and she will not cross running water, and the smell goes up the run and she cannot follow it, and she has tried four times since we came down."

Old Qin said nothing for a while. He stood over the kill and looked at the crows' work, the drag of the body, the clean fifty paces of bank. At last he took off his hat.

"A beast came to this water, killed a heavy beast at the run, ate the deep parts in the shallows, and went back up the water over the stone," he said. "It left the kill and the drag-mark; the body is still here. And it left nothing of itself to look at. Not one print. On gravel that takes the crows' feet."

"That is what the ground says," said Xiao An.

"Then the ground says the same thing my father's line said, and my father's father," said Old Qin. "The thing that signs and does not show." He put his hat back on. "So the paper takes a second line. Today. And this time we know the two of them apart; the pale one has a face and a track wider than this, and this one has neither and never has."

"Two lines for one country," said the younger man. "You told us at the black stone, two lines for one beast is one line too many."

"Two lines for two beasts is the count finally telling the truth," said Old Qin. "Write it."

---

They did not strike the watch. That was the first thing Old Qin said when they were back at the pool and the fire was going and Xiao An had the list out on her knee with the brush in her hand.

"We stay at the water through the dry," he said. "The wire stays. The hound stays."

"For how long?" said the younger man.

"Until the water moves again," said Old Qin. "Everything that drinks in this low country drinks at this pool when the dry is hard, and the dry is hard. Whatever signs and does not show — that thing must drink. Water has no choices in it. A beast can go round a blind, and it can cross stone, and it can hold its smell down out of the wind, but it cannot drink where the water is not."

"And what do we set the wire for?" said the younger man. "A beast with no face and no track. What shape is the snare, then?"

"The same shape as always," said Old Qin. "The rope, the meat, the blind, the hound. We are not going to build it a special door. We are going to make it come to the water and stand where we are looking." He looked at the pool, flat and brown in the firelight. "If a thing is there and never shows itself, then the whole season at the water is the snare."

Xiao An wrote the first line again under the old one, small and even in the shorthand the line keepers use, and under it she wrote the second. When she was done she sat with the brush in her hand and read the two lines over twice.

"What do you see?" said Old Qin.

"Something," said Xiao An. She did not sound settled.

"Say it or keep it."

"Keep it," said Xiao An, "until it is something." But her eyes went up the water, north along the run where the hound would not go, and Gao Tie saw her do it and said nothing.

---

He read the kill bank two days after they had left it.

The crows had finished with the body and gone; a scatter of ribs and the skull lay on the gravel where the drag-mark ran down into the shallows. The men had walked it in boots, four of them. One had the lodge road's dust on it, so the old man had come. They had stayed a long time at the head of the run, on their knees in the gravel, all of them in the same small patch where the hound had yelped at the water. Their knees had left marks. Their fire was lit at the pool down-country, and their wire stood in the low neck of it on both sides, and the smell of the camp came up the water day and night.

So that was written now, then: the kill, and the water, and the line. He did not know what words men put down for him, and he did not need the words. He could count their stops, and they had stopped at his kill and stayed, and the old man had come the road to stand over it, and the wire had not been lifted from the pool. They had put something down that day. Something about the water.

He stood on the bank a while with the sun on him and sorted it as he sorted everything, by what it cost him to eat.

They could not read him off the ground any more. That had held all season. He had printed nothing for a year and a half of seasons, and the ground he walked gave them nothing. But the ground was no longer the only reader. The hound read him out of the air, out of the water, out of the memory of a smell two summers old, and the hound had led them to his kill and stood over the place his feet had stood in the shallows. He could hear a man's voice from the pool below and he could not hear the words in it, and he did not need to: the men had turned and looked up the run, more than once, and a hunter who looks up the water is a hunter who has decided the water means something.

So the drink was the problem now, and the drink could not be dropped. He could go a week without meat and he could not go two days without water in the heat. The runs were thin by late summer, and the pools were watched, and every day the men sat at the main pool with the hound, and every night the hound walked her line above the water with her nose up.

He came down to the main pool once more that season, near the end, at the grey end of a night, to see it whole for himself. He lay on the rim of the low country with his head low, and read the camp by the fire and the wire in the neck of the pool and the smaller shape that was the hound, staked out past the fire at the water's edge.

She lifted her head while he watched. She stood up on the end of her line and turned to face up the water, into the dark north where the run came down. She stood like that, pointed like a thrown spear. The little wind that there was came off her and up to him and carried nothing but water and smoke and the smell of men.

She was reading the air for a smell that was not there, on a night when he was a long stone's throw away in the dark, and she did it because it had been there before and she believed it would come again.

He backed off the rim without standing up, and went north, and drank at the run in the black dark with his ears on the low country, and nothing came up the water behind him.

The men kept the water all the rest of the dry, and the wire stayed where it was, and their paper went down to the lodge at the season's end with two lines on it: the killer with a face, and the thing that drinks and does not show. And above them both, on the high ground, four stones stood in a row at the edge of a pan where a print no longer was.

When the rains came again the watchers would go up to the pan and the water would rise and the country would take back its writing, and the line would come down again the next dry, and the men and the hound would walk the same rounds, and he would hunt the same water the only way he had left.

It had stopped being a season's hunt somewhere in that summer. It was going to be a country now.

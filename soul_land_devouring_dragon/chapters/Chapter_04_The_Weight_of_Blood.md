# Chapter 4: The Weight of Blood

Summer came in, and the valley turned into a table.

That was how the little Devouring Dragon understood the season, because eating was the only language he had. The stream bend gave him bristle-backs. The fern country gave him deer-kind. The low woods gave him hares, ground-birds, and the slow, fat burrowers. The whole low country stood open under a long sun, and the sun seemed to want him fed.

He ate like the season ate. In the mornings he worked the stream. In the afternoons he lay in the shade of the big fern and did nothing at all while the food went down inside him. In the evenings he hunted again. Every third or fourth day, the deep sleep took him, and every time he woke from it, he was a little more than he had been: heavier across the shoulders, longer in the jaw, the scales on his back going from gray to a cold blue-gray that water ran off in beads.

And every few days, the air did what it had done at the bristle-back. This was the summer's real work, though he did not know it as work.

It happened at the start of a meal now, not the end. He would take a deer-kind or a boar-kind, stand over it, and eat — and the thin, warm draught would come off the kill and go into him. Behind it, the wide faintness of the whole summer country came in a little way too. The years of the small lives, the warm bodies in the grass, all of it pressing up against him like water against a held breath.

He learned to feel it coming, and he learned to let it come. That was the whole of the summer's teaching. The first time, at the bristle-back, it had torn out of him and knocked him down. Now it was his. It answered his attention as a leg answers a thought. And the more he fed it, the more it gave back: the deep sleeps were longer, the waking was stronger, and the world wore its lives a little more visibly every week.

He was still small. He was underneath everything in the valley that mattered. But he was growing, every single thing he ate went somewhere, and no beast of the low country had yet made him feel like the smallest thing in his own range.

Then the summer gave him two hard lessons in one week, and both of them were about the thing above him.

---

The first lesson was a husk.

He had found the wounded deer-kind two days before: a young buck with its flank opened by something that had not killed it, moving slow and separate from the herd, going downhill all evening. He had marked it, as he was learning to mark everything, and he had let it be. A wounded beast is a beast that will still be there tomorrow, and he had learned to prefer his meat fat and slow.

He came back for it in the gray end of the evening, three days later, and it was already dead.

Not killed. Used.

The buck lay in the open fern with its hide cut in a long line down the belly and its meat left lying in the grass, untouched except where a knife had parted it. Its eyes were open. And the pale, warm thing that should have been burning low in its chest was gone — gone as water goes out of a stream in the dry months, leaving a gray, flat place where a living thing had been.

Men had made that. They had come down the valley, taken the years out of a beast that was already dying, and left the meat for the ravens.

He stood over it a long time. He was hungry, and there was a lot of good meat there, and the summer had taught him to take what was on the ground. And he did not eat it. He could not have said why. The meat was fine. The husk was simply wrong — as wrong as a deer standing on its head — and he had no more reason than that. He turned away from it, went up the valley, and hunted properly, and he did not sleep well that night.

The men had hunted the same country as him. That was the thing. They had hunted it before him, and they had left a shape behind that he could not eat.

---

They came down into the low valley on the fourth day of the heavy heat, three of them, and the kill they made was not for meat.

They worked it in the open fern, with the sun going down behind the ridge. The oldest of the three knelt at the beast's head and did the work with a short knife: a long cut down the belly, the hide held back, the hands going in where the light sits in a beast. The light came out of the deer-kind thin and pale, the color of old snow. He laid it on the moss and set a cloth over it, and the other two stood with their backs to him and their eyes on the tree-line.

"That is thin light," said the one with the bow.

"It is a young beast," said the kneeling man. "Young beasts give thin light. It goes to the lodge in the list, and the list is the list. Hold the hide open."

"We are three days from the lodge for a light the color of dishwater."

"We are three days from the lodge for six lights," said the kneeling man. "Six lights are not dishwater. Hold the hide open."

The third man came in from the tree-line and stood with his hands on his belt.

"The hound will not settle," he said. "She has been reading the north ridge since we came down. Something up there moves on the top ground."

"Bear."

"A bear does not walk the top ground in the heat. Bears are down in their wallows."

"Then a cat."

"The cat we took in the spring came down to a kill. This one moves along and comes to nothing." He spat to one side. "There is something up on that ridge, and it has been up there two days, and the hound reads it from four hundred paces."

The kneeling man closed the hide. He wiped his knife on the grass and stood up.

"Write it in the book," he said. "Trouble on the north ridge, one line, no beast named. We give this valley one more season. If the hound is still reading it in the spring, we bring the line up the west side and leave this ground alone."

"They will ask at the winter meet what the trouble was."

"Then at the winter meet we will tell them," said the kneeling man. "And it will have a name by then, or it will not, and either way the lodge gets its six lights. Now say it plain. What did the hound read?"

"Something on the top ground," said the third man. "Not a bear, not a cat, and not the wind."

"Then we walk the west side home," said the kneeling man, "and let the top ground keep its business."

He kept all of it. The talk itself meant nothing to him — he heard the whole thing as flat, hard sound, and that was all he ever heard of men — but the shapes went in. It went into him next to the track of their feet on the pass trail and the pale light standing over the fern-country kill, and it made a shape he had not had before: the hunters. Not the death that has no length, and not the long, thin things. The hunters — the ones who walk through the whole low country and take the years out of beasts they did not even need to kill, and leave the meat in the sun.

His own list of the world was growing. The jaw beneath him, for the small lives. The hunters, outside all the laws he knew. And one more shape still missing, which the summer gave him at the end of the same week.

---

He heard the hound from the high ground above the valley, one evening near the end of the summer, and the sound made him stop walking.

It was a single voice, far off, somewhere down-country across the wide green, and it was not the hound-cry of a pack on a scent. It was long and thin, and it went up and stayed up a while, and it came from a place where he had never seen men and never smelled their smoke.

Then a man's voice came up the valley, small and flat. It was not a call, and it was not a cry. It was work — a hand put on a hound's line, and pulled.

"Down! Down, girl!"

The hound answered once, short, from far down the valley. It was still working, and still coming in.

A second voice answered from farther off, one flat word of the same work. "In!"

The first voice came again, going away down the valley with the hound, and what was in it meant nothing to him. "That's her. Come in."

Then the country was quiet, and the sun went down.

He stood on the rock with the evening wind in his face for a long time, and he thought about what he had heard, and the thinking had a plain answer. The men had gone deeper. That was all he could read, and it was enough. The men of the mountain did not stay on the roads and the pass trails any more. They were walking in the low country now — the country he had claimed for his own range that spring — and their dogs were standing on ridges where he stood.

He was going to have neighbors, and the neighbors would not be beasts.

---

The second lesson was the sky going dark over the ravine.

He had been circling his valley wider all summer, and by late summer he was ranging up to the black ridge north of the fern country, where the ground climbed into broken stone and the trees changed their kinds. He crossed that ridge twice. On the third evening, coming up over the top of it with the whole world opening below him, he went down onto the stone as if something had put a hand on his back.

Something was awake down there.

It was below him and ahead of him, in the black country of the ravine, and it was not moving, and not hunting, and not looking at him. It was simply there — heavy, slow, immense, steady as a mountain. Its weight came up out of the ravine and over him like the weight of deep water, and he lay flat on the bare stone with his legs splayed and his own small body pressed down into the rock by something that was not touching him at all.

He was afraid. The fear came up clean and whole: the fear of a small thing under a big thing, the same fear the stone-runners felt when he came. For the first time, he felt it from underneath — and now he knew what his coming had always felt like.

And under the fear, layered into it like a second color, was something else. His blood knew the thing in the black ravine.

Not knew it — he had never seen it, and he never would, not this summer. But his blood knew it as a body knows the smell of rain: from the inside, from before learning. There was something in the great, slow power below him that ran straight to something in himself, and his whole frame stood up along it and answered. The answer was not words, and it was not a shape he could have named. But if it had been words, it would have gone like this: I am of you. You are more than I am. And underneath both, quietly: one day.

He lay on the rock with his heart going hard, and the fear and the kinship ran in him together. The thing in the ravine went on being what it was. After a long while, the weight eased, as a hand comes off a table, and he got his legs under himself and went back down off the ridge, slower than he had ever gone anywhere in his life.

He went down facing the ravine. He did not turn his back on it at all.

He did not go over the black ridge again that summer. He thought about the thing in the ravine, in the new way he had begun to think about everything — keeping shapes whole, taking them out and turning them over in the long, still hours — and the shape it made was the biggest he had ever made.

Down there was a creature that had never feared anything. His own blood had recognized it, and it had recognized him. He did not know what it was. He knew what it was to him: the roof of his world.

---

The summer ended as the first one had ended, with the nights going cold and the leaves beginning to turn.

He was out on the high rocks above the ravine's mouth on one of the last warm evenings, standing where the trees broke and the whole valley opened below him, and he did a thing he had never done before. He did not look for a meal. He stood and held the country.

The forest went away from him wave upon wave into the evening haze: his valley, the fern country, the low woods, the far green beyond, and the ravine country lying under its own shadow to the north. Everywhere in it, he could feel the lives now — small and flat, or small and bright. One place held the memory of a hound's voice, standing and singing in the valley. One place, north, could not be read at all, and everything in him went quiet about it.

He held all three of the big shapes — the jaw beneath him, the hunters on their trails, the roof above him — and each one was solid now, a kept thing, as solid as the stones of his slope. He had learned them as he had learned the stream bend and the talus: by being there, and by paying, and by living.

And he stood there holding the whole country in the one place he had to hold it, and he was not afraid, and he was not full, and he was not content. He was waiting. He had not learned what to call that yet. In time, he would.

The light went off the far green. He climbed down to the fern country, made his kill of the day, and came back through the dark to his roots-house, with the meat going down in him and the deep sleep waiting at the end of it.

The first cold wind came two days later, off the pass, thin and sharp, and he stood with his nose in it. Winter again — his second, or near enough to count it one. He had a valley for it this time, and a den with two ways out, and a summer of meat laid down stone by stone inside him. And he had one thing more than he had owned the first winter: he knew what he was, and what the country was, and what the three big shapes were.

He went down into the roots-house with the cold wind at his tail, curled into the dug earth, and slept. And behind his closed eyes the country stayed lit, as the fern country had been lit at dusk — every life in it standing up in its own small light, and every one of them still waiting to be read.

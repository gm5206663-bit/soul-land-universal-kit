# Chapter 21: The Watch and the Pass

The pass kept its own hours, and the watch taught them to him a few days at a time.

The holder slept in the ground and was never seen. What he had instead was the ground's own behaving: the weight under the grass moved with the sun. In the mornings it lay in the middle distance, near the river. In the afternoons it drew slowly south and west, a tide of attention with no body showing, and by dark it was back at the river again. It never hurried and it never stopped. It kept its rounds as the water keeps its runs — not going anywhere, only being where it was, all the way to the edges of itself.

The edges' laws he learned first, because they were shown to him every dusk. The herd of the slow kind came down the south range's feet and fed to the same line, always the same line, and turned back up with nothing anywhere in sight. The birds crossed high or not at all, and the one place their lines bent around was the middle of the pass, a wide nothing in the air itself. At the lips, morning and evening, the small life of the forests stood at the edge of the grass and looked in and did not go in. A pass full of food, crossed by a river, standing empty between two loud forests. Nothing in his whole life had ever shown him a country so plainly owned.

The river's hours he learned by lying still and letting them happen. The pass's one voice was the river, and the river was busiest at night. In the black hours, quick small shapes ran the south lip — a fox-kind, a kind like a low cat, once a whole file of the quick beasts — each one hugging the bend where the river came closest to the range, crossing the open grass at a dead run and never once stopping to drink. They crossed in the hour after the moon set, when the grass was blackest. He counted their crossings as he counted everything else in his life: by watching. They came, they ran, they were gone into the north trees, and the ground under the pass never so much as turned over.

He came to know one file of them apart from all the others. They crossed on the same nights, coming down a dry run-off gully on the south range. There was one in the middle of the file that ran wrong — a young one, or a hurt one, whose stride broke twice on the open grass. The file slowed around it without a sound, closed it into the middle, and they all went into the north trees together. The ground under the pass turned over for none of them. He lay in the dark and watched that happen four times. On the fourth night he understood he was watching the whole shape of the pass at once: the lip-bend's grass was a road, walked nightly by the small, and the middle's grass was a mouth, and every beast on that ground knew which was which without ever being told.

A storm came in the second week, and the pass taught him its last hour. The rain came down hard and flat, and the river rose and browned and lost its voice in the rain's own noise — and the crossings stopped. The quick kinds came down the gully to the lip, stood at the edge of the grass in the wet, and went back up. Even the file with the hurt one. He lay through that whole night in the rain and saw nothing cross, and by morning he had it: the river's noise was not the river's. It was cover. A night when the water could not be heard apart from the rain was a night the road was closed, and everything that lived around the pass knew it without a sign.

So it could be crossed. He watched the quick kinds do it and held the watching close.

What the watch could not teach him, the pass taught instead, one fogged morning, in one breath.

An old grazer of the heavy kind came down the north range in the gray before sunrise and walked out onto the grass. It was big and slow and its years were heavy in it, and it walked as though it had crossed this ground all its life. It went out past the herd-line, past the birds' bent-around nothing, and on toward the river, and it was lowering its head to drink — and it was not there.

There was no rush of wind. There was no sound at all. The river's voice went on. The fog stood where it stood. The grass where the beast had been lay pressed flat in one long swept place, as if the ground itself had closed its hand, and before the fog moved the pressed place had begun to spring back up. The whole crossing had taken less time than one beat of a hunting heart.

No crows came. That was the part that stayed with him afterward, the part he turned over on the high seat for days. Every kill he had ever made or found had been answered — crows, foxes, the beasts that come to the kills of others, the whole gray procession of the ground's economy. The pass's taking had no procession. Nothing came down to the swept place, that morning or any morning. The pass kept what it took, all of it, down to the last feather of interest, and the country around it had long since stopped pretending otherwise.

He lay on the high seat with his own heart going hard, and he did not move for a long time, and he learned the last lesson of the pass from the far side of it: the holder did not chase, and did not need to. The middle of the pass was not ground you crossed. It was ground that decided about you.

The quick kinds crossed the lip-bend. The old grazer had crossed the middle. He was not a fox-kind; he was heavy, and long, and his shadow at night was nearer the grazer's than the fox's. But he had one thing the grazer had not had. He had watched.

He spent one more moon on the high seat, and then he went.

---

He went in the hour after the moon set, in the blackest grass, at the south lip's bend.

He came down off the range where the river ran close against the slope, and he went into the water itself. The river through the pass was shallow and fast over stones, no deeper than his legs, and its noise was a wall around him — his own sounds gone into it, his own smell pushed down and apart by the moving water. He had taken his meals standing in currents since his second summer. If any ground in the world was his ground, moving water was.

He kept the south lip at his flank and the river's bend tight against the slope, and he crossed the open grass inside the water's own noise, low, at the pace of the current, a shape of wet dark among wet dark. The stars over the pass were hard and small. Nothing moved on the grass. The herd-line lay far off to his left, an invisible seam he had promised himself not to touch. The middle of the pass, the swept place, the holder's morning bed — all of it far to the north of him across the empty grass, and he did not look at it. Looking is a kind of going. He kept his eyes on the water and the near bank.

The weight noticed him anyway.

It did not rise. It did not come. The ground under the river simply changed its mind about him — the current's voice ran on exactly as it had, but the water around his body went still, still as a held breath, a skin of quiet moving with him in the middle of the river's noise. He could feel the whole attention of the pass standing under him like a floor. A pressure moved over him, slow and total, the way a hand moves over stones in a field, reading each one — along his sides, over the ridge of his neck, down the new scars the road had given him, weighing what he was.

He did not stop. Stopping was a decision, and decisions belonged to things that had a say. He walked on in the current, in the little envelope of still water, with his heart going like a bird's, and his feet found the next stone and the next.

And under the pressure, the red thing rose. It was old in him and it knew every road out, and being touched without leave — weighed, read, gone over like ground — lit it like a struck match. The boil came up his neck and into his jaw and his feet wanted to stop and turn and answer the touch with his one answer, the take-and-keep, the oldest thing he had. He walked. He let the current carry the boil the way the current carried his sound — out, apart, downstream — and the red thing spent itself against the walking, and was gone, and the pressure above him did not so much as pause. It had not even noticed the war in him. That was the worst of it, and the truest: he had fought his own blood to a finish there in the cold river, and the pass had missed the whole battle.

The pressure finished with him. The still skin of water let go of him all at once, and the river took his flanks back, cold and loud and ordinary, and behind him the ground's attention slid away north across the grass like the shadow of a cloud, unhurried, already elsewhere.

He gained the north lip's trees with the gray light coming, and stood in them a long time, wet and whole, breathing.

It had read him and let him go. Not mercy — he knew the difference by now, better than anything alive. The pack's cry was noise; the hunter's chase was hunger; this had been neither. It had weighed him as a flood weighs a straw, found him nothing to its purpose, and gone back to its sleep. For the first time since the shell, being beneath notice had saved him, and he stood in the gray trees and let that fact walk all the way through him. The deep forest did not care about him. That was its danger, and its mercy, and both of them were the same thing.

The new country's first smell came to him with the gray light: wet stone and rot-sweet and a cold moving under the trees that was not wind, and under all of it the old deep smell of the great forest, thicker here than it had ever been on his border. He hunted at midmorning and took a small quick thing of the fern shadows, a stranger-kind like the one on the road's first night, and ate it listening, and the listening was the meal's other half: a country he did not know, reading him back. The first meal of a ground had been the road's taste once already. It tasted like that again.

The pull ran under the new ground, north, unchanged. Far off, deeper in, once, for a breath, he thought he saw a light standing on the land — and then not. He did not trust his eyes after a night like that, and he did not need to. The road knew where it was going.

He shook the water off, and ate, and went north. The pass was behind him, and he had crossed it as the country allowed: not by strength, and not by luck, but by knowing what the ground was.

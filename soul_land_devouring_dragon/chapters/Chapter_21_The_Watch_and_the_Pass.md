# Chapter 21: The Watch and the Pass

He made his camp on the shelf of rock on the south range and watched the pass, and the pass taught him its rules a few days at a time.

The thing that owned the pass slept in the ground and was never seen. What he had instead was the ground's own behavior. The weight under the grass moved with the sun. In the mornings it lay in the middle distance, near the river. In the afternoons it drifted slowly south and west, and by dark it was back at the river. It never hurried and it never stopped. He learned its rounds as he had once learned the water runs of his own country: not by understanding them, only by knowing them.

The animals at the edges showed him the borders, every day, and he marked what he saw, evening by evening, the same as he had once marked the watchers' wires and blinds. The first dusk he watched the herd come down, he picked a lone dead tree on the far side of their feeding ground and lay watching it against the light, and every animal that passed it, he counted. The next dusk he did the same, and the third, and the fourth, and by then he did not need the tree: the line they would not cross was as plain to him as a wall, though there was nothing there at all.

Each evening a herd of the slow, heavy grazing kind came down from the south range and fed out onto the grass. They always fed to the same line, and turned back at the same line, and there was never anything standing there to turn them back. The line was just where they stopped. The birds crossed the pass high up, or not at all, and the one place their lines bent around was the middle. At the lips of the pass, morning and evening, the small forest animals came to the edge of the grass, looked in, and did not go in. The pass was full of food, a river ran through it, and it stood empty between two loud forests all day. Nothing in his life had ever shown him a country so clearly owned.

The river's hours he learned by lying still at night.

The pass had one voice, and it was the river, and the river was busiest in the dark. After the moon set, small quick animals ran the south lip: fox-things, low cat-things, once a whole line of them in single file. They hugged the bend where the river came closest to the range, crossed the open grass at a dead run, and never once stopped to drink. They went into the north trees and were gone, and the ground under the pass never so much as stirred.

He came to know one of those lines apart from the others. They came down a dry gully on the south range on the same nights, and one of the animals in the middle ran wrong — a young one, or a hurt one. Its stride broke twice on the open grass. The others slowed around it without a sound, closed it into the middle of the line, and all of them went into the north trees together. He watched that happen four times, lying in the dark, and by the fourth time he understood what he was looking at: the ground at the south bend was a road, and the ground in the middle was not a road at all, and every animal around this pass knew which was which without being told.

A storm came in the second week, and the pass taught him its last rule.

The rain came down hard and flat all night. The river rose, turned brown, and its sound disappeared into the rain's sound. And the crossings stopped. The quick animals came down the gully to the lip, stood at the edge of the wet grass, and went back up. Even the line with the hurt one. All night, in the rain, nothing crossed.

By morning he had it. The river's noise was not the river's. It was cover. On a night when the water could not be heard apart from the rain, the road was closed, and everything that lived around the pass knew it without a sign.

So the pass could be crossed. He held that knowledge and kept watching.

What the watch could not teach him, the pass showed him instead, one foggy morning, in one breath.

An old grazer of the heavy kind came down the north range in the gray light before sunrise and walked out onto the grass. It was big and slow and heavy with years, and it walked like an animal that had crossed this ground all its life. It went out past the herd's line, past the birds' bent-around place, and on toward the river, and it lowered its head to drink.

And it was not there anymore.

There was no rush of wind. There was no sound at all. The river kept talking. The fog stood where it stood. The grass where the animal had been was pressed flat in one long swept patch, as if the ground had closed its hand, and even as he watched, the patch began to spring back up. The whole thing had taken less time than one beat of a hunting heart.

No crows came.

That was the part he kept thinking about, lying on the shelf for days afterward. Every kill he had ever made or found had been followed: crows, foxes, the animals that gather at other animals' kills, the whole gray procession of the ground's economy. The pass's taking had no procession. Nothing came to the swept patch, that morning or any morning after. The pass kept everything it took, down to the last feather of interest, and the country around it had stopped pretending otherwise long ago.

He lay still with his own heart going hard, and took the last lesson of the pass from the far side of it. The thing below did not chase. It did not need to. The middle of the pass was not ground you crossed. It was ground that decided about you.

The quick animals crossed at the south bend, in the river's hour, in the blackest grass. He was not a fox-thing. He was heavy and long, closer to the old grazer than to the runners. But he had something the grazer had not had. He had watched.

He spent one more moon on the shelf. Then he went.

He went in the hour after the moon set, at the south bend.

He came off the shelf in the last dark and moved down the range with the care of an animal that had spent a moon learning exactly how much noise the ground would forgive. He had picked his line days before: a dry route down the rocks, no loose stone, no scree, coming out above the bend where the slope met the water. Twice on the way down he stopped and stood for a long time in the black, listening, feeling the ground. Both times it said the same thing: morning, near the river, unhurried. The weight was exactly where its rounds said it would be. He went on.

He came down off the range where the river ran close against the slope and got into the water itself. Through the pass the river was shallow and fast over stones, no deeper than his legs. Its noise went around him like a wall. His sounds went into it. His smell was pushed down and apart by the current. He had taken his food standing in moving water since his second summer. If any ground in the world was his, moving water was.

He kept the south lip at his flank and the bend tight against the slope, and crossed the open grass inside the river's own noise, low, moving at the speed of the current. The stars were hard and small. Nothing moved on the grass. The herd's line was far off to his left, and he did not go near it. The middle of the pass, the swept patch, the morning bed — all of it was far to the north across the empty grass, and he did not look at it. Looking is a kind of going.

The weight noticed him anyway.

It did not rise. It did not come. The ground under the river simply changed its mind about him. The current's voice went on exactly as before, but the water around his body went still — still as held breath — a skin of quiet that moved with him through the middle of the river's noise. He could feel the whole attention of the pass standing under him like a floor. A pressure moved over him, slow and complete, like a hand moving over stones in a field, reading each one: along his sides, over the ridge of his neck, down the new scars the road had given him.

He did not stop. Stopping is a decision, and decisions belong to things that have a say. He kept walking, finding the next stone with his feet and then the next, with his heart going like a bird's.

Under the pressure, the red thing rose.

It was old in him and it knew every way out, and being touched without being asked — weighed, read, gone over like ground — lit it like a match. The heat climbed his neck and sat in his jaw, and every old line in him said stop, turn, answer the touch. He walked. He let the current carry the heat away the same as it carried his sound: out, downstream, apart. The heat spent itself against the walking and was gone, and the pressure above him never paused. It had not even noticed the fight. He had fought his own blood to a finish there in the cold river, and the pass had missed the whole battle.

Then the pressure finished with him. The skin of quiet let go all at once, the river took his flanks back, cold and loud and ordinary, and behind him the ground's attention slid away north across the grass, unhurried, already somewhere else.

He came up into the trees on the north lip with the gray light coming, and stood there a long time, wet and whole, breathing.

It had read him and let him go. Not mercy. He knew the difference by now better than anything alive. The pack's cry is noise. A hunter's chase is hunger. This had been neither. It had weighed him as a flood weighs a straw, found him nothing to its purpose, and gone back to its sleep. For the first time since the shell, being beneath notice had saved him, and he stood in the gray trees and let that fact sink all the way in. The deep forest did not care about him. That was its danger, and its mercy, and they were the same thing.

The new country's first smell reached him with the light: wet stone, sweet rot, cold moving air under the trees that was not wind. He hunted at midmorning and took a small quick animal in the fern shade, and ate it listening, because in a country he did not know, listening was half the meal. It tasted like every first meal of a new ground had ever tasted.

The pull ran under the new land, north, unchanged. Far off, deeper in, for one breath, he thought he saw a light standing on the land. Then he did not think so. After a night like that, his eyes were not to be trusted, and did not need to be. The road knew where it was going.

He shook the water off, and went north. The pass was behind him, and he had crossed it the only way it allowed: not by strength, and not by luck, but by knowing what the ground was.

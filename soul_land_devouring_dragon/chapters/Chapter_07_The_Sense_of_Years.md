# Chapter 7: The Sense of Years

He came up out of the deep sleep with the hunger already standing in him, went out into the cold morning, and read the snow.

The hare-kind under the snowbank read thin and bright and young, worth almost nothing. The foxes read bright and quick, worth a little. The wintering stone-runners read flat and small, worth less than the foxes. He passed them all without stopping, because somewhere out in the open snow, the heavy ones were standing up like banked fires. The old boar-kinds. The bears of the lower slopes. The heavy, slow things that had lived thirty winters and more. The moment one of them came into his sense, he went for it, and left everything else alone.

That was the whole of the new life, and the winter taught him what his own country was worth under it: not much.

He had a rule now, and the rule was simple. The thin lives were bread. The plain warmth of a hare feeds a body, and a body is not the thing that is in him. The heavy years were meat, and the thing that was in him ate only meat. He hunted only meat that winter — and the hunting was thinner than the bread had ever been. He went hungry in a country that could have fed him every day, because hunting the heavy ones meant working a range three times as wide for half the kills. That was the price of the crossing, and he paid it without complaint. There was no one to complain to, and nothing else to do with the season.

---

The heavier presence cost him more than miles, and he learned it in the worst way: standing over empty ground.

He came down a slope that winter with his hunt in front of him, doing everything as he had always done it — low, downwind, slow — and the game came up out of its browsing and went, without ever seeing him. He stood in the fern where the beast had been feeding and smelled the warm litter of it going cold. It had not seen him. It had not heard him. It had read him.

Twice that winter, the same thing. The trouble was in himself, and he knew it: the crossing had made him more, and more is louder. The weight in his blood went out ahead of him now like the smell of a fire. He could hold the quiet down over it, but the price of holding had gone up, and twice the price was more than the moment had in it.

He kept the account as he kept every account. Then, over the rest of the winter, he rebuilt.

Not by trying harder — by learning the new shape of himself as he had learned the old one: a little at a time, from failure, with the whole of his attention. And the quiet that came back was bigger than the one that had been lost, because the beast holding it was bigger. By the gray days at the end of the winter, he could come down through a stand of feeding hare-kinds and take one off the end of the line with the rest never lifting their heads. The quiet had been a way of hunting. Now it was a way of being.

---

In the still hours, the deep drawing learned to feed itself.

It had begun in the storm, when the fasting went past the point of arguing and the pull turned itself downward into the mountain's own ancient quiet. That had been necessity. What came after was habit. On the long windless afternoons, when there was nothing to do and the sun lay thin along the snow, he found that if he stood still enough and let the hunger stand in him without giving it anything, the drawing opened by itself, and the mountain's faintness came into him as water comes into a rooted thing.

It was thin. It was nothing like the flood of the storm, and it would never feed him like meat. But it was always there, and it cost him nothing, and every hour of it went into the same deep well as everything else he ate.

Sleep went deepest of all. In the old hollow of the fallen giant on the high ground above his valley — where a great tree had come down long ago and left a bowl in the earth under a shelf of black stone — the drawing went deeper than anywhere else he had ever lain. The stone there was old, older than the forest around it, old as the ravine country. He would go down into that hollow in the gray afternoon and come out of it in the dark, and stand up at the end of it with the hunger that had no meal a little less loud than before.

He did not understand what he was doing. He did not have to. His kind had never had words and had never needed them, and the body learned what it could use.

---

The thaw came, the country stood up again, and everything in it knew him.

That was the strangest spring of his life. He walked down out of the high ground into the summer, and the summer made room. The low lives came out of the winter thin and hungry and quick, and everywhere he went, they went flat and silent before him — the fox dens, the ground-birds, the burrowers, the whole small nation of the country. It was not as it had been in his second spring, when they had merely feared him. They feared him deeper now, and from farther away, and the older ones among them did a thing he had no name for.

They gave him ground. Not the ground he was standing on — ground on either side of him, a wide, respectful emptiness that walked down the valley with him like an escort.

He walked through the spring with the order standing around him, and the whole bright season was his — his to hunt, and his alone, and no beast in the low country argued about it. Sometimes, in the evenings, he remembered being a pale, soft thing that fell off a rock shelf. The memory was so far away it might have belonged to another country.

---

On the first clear spring night, the sense of years read the ravine.

He had kept away from the black ridge all winter, as he kept away from the roof of the world. But the sense was new in him, and the shape of the country was new in it, and one evening he let it run north, the way he had once let his eyes run.

The ravine answered.

It came back at him as a depth past all depths. The ancient ones of his own country — the old boars, the great gray stags of the high fern, the heavy things that had lived through a hundred winters — burned in his sense like the last coals of a fire. The thing in the ravine was the sun. Its years did not stand up out of it; they went down, and down, past where the sense of years could read. He sent the sense out over the edge of that depth and it came back with nothing but the fact of the depth itself.

And under the depth, thin and clear and singing, the pull ran.

His blood answered as it had answered on the ridge in the summer: fear first, flat and complete — and then the other thing, the thing that was not fear, standing up in him along the pull as long as the pull was there. And that was the evening's real news. Not the size of the thing in the ravine; he had always known that. The news was what it did inside him. Reading the ravine with the sense awake was like standing at the edge of a ledge with the wind at his back. There was a part of him that wanted to go down into the depth, as the pull had once wanted him to go down to the lodge lights.

He did not go. The rule of the ridge was in him, kept whole since the first summer, and he put the feeling down as a beast puts down a heavy thing it has decided not to carry. He lay in the fern until the pull went quiet.

He would hold that shape, he decided, as he held everything: until he grew to the size of it.

---

The men were deeper that spring, and he found their new work at the end of the summer, from the top of a slope he had never stood on before.

He had gone east over the pass trail, into the wide country beyond the ridge, hunting the heavy years that stood in his sense out that way. On the third day he came down a long slope into a bowl of country he had never walked — and stopped, because the ground below him had been changed by hands.

There was a line across a game trail at the bottom of the bowl. Not the bright line of the pass snare; this one was wire and wood, low and long, worked into the ground at both ends. Beneath it, in the litter of the trail, lay the old tracks of many beasts walking through a place where a beast would want to walk. The ground around the trail's mouth had been swept and re-swept. There were the prints of men, and the prints of spirit hounds, and the ash of a small campfire, and the cut branches of a blind built up off the trail, where a watcher could sit.

They had come deeper. The winter's snare at the pass had been the edge of their country. This new line stood in the country of the heavier lives, where the years were thick — and they had built it as a beast builds: patient, and read into the ground.

He stood on the slope above their work, and the understanding came whole. He hunted the heavy years. They hunted the heavy years. The trail to the heavy years was one trail, and there was no law in the whole low country that could say which of them it belonged to.

It was a race. He had known it since the fern country, and it had never been stated as plainly as their hands had stated it that afternoon, on his own range. He went back west at dusk without crossing their line, and the shape sat in him the whole walk home, heavy and clear.

---

The last thing the spring taught him was a truth about his own country, and he did not like it, and it was true.

He was hunting the low valley in the last month of the summer. He took a boar-kind, a good heavy one, and ate it down to the bones, and the fullness came up over him warm and complete — and the hunger that had no meal sat up in the middle of that fullness and said, quietly: more.

The country could not feed him. Not any more. A beast could live a hundred winters on the crops of that valley and never go hungry, and he had eaten down past what it had to give in three springs. The heavy years were few and getting fewer. Every season of hunting took the biggest beasts out of his range, the young of those beasts took ten winters to become heavy again, and he was finishing in one season what the country took ten to grow.

He lay on the warm rock above his valley in the last of the light and let himself read it plainly, as he read the land. This country was his home, and it had made him, and it could not take him any further. North and east of him, over the black ridge, past the ground of the thing in the ravine, lay the deep forest. Its faintness reached him at the far edge of his sense on clear nights: a place where the years lay on the ground as heavy as boulders. Every clear night, the deep forest stood out beyond the ravine like a banked fire bigger than all the fires of his range put together.

The pull that had brought him here from the slope had been a pull of belonging. The pull now was different. It was the pull of need, and it pointed into the deep forest the way the sense of years pointed at heavy years — and both of them pointed north.

He held the shape without deciding anything. That was the only honest thing to do with it, and the beast in him knew it. The country that made him was watching him. The roof of the world lay between him and what he needed. Nothing was ready yet — not the season, not his own weight, not the crossing of the ravine.

He sat on the warm rock until the last of the light was gone, with the whole low country laid out in his sense, heavy and thin, and the deep forest banked at the northern edge of it like a fire that would not go out. Then he went down to his roots-house and slept, and the shape went down with him, and waited.

---

He worked his way down the slope along the high side until the wind brought him voices. He lay above the camp with the whole picture of their work in front of him.

"A line here, and a line at the second spring," said the man with the axe. He was a broad man with his sleeves cut off, and he was notching a post as he spoke. "That closes the ground. Anything in that ground comes to one of the two, and the run between them is a day. We can work both lines out of one camp."

"The ground is too big for one camp," said the younger one. He was sitting on a stone with a skin of water, watching the hound. "We had three men at the water and two came home."

"Then we build the fence," said the first. "You build the fence at the second spring and we work the ground in two halves. That is the trade. Your line is short and the fence is long, and the fence will stand past the thaw."

"Do we have the wire for a wall that long?"

"We have the wire off the old water line," said the first man. "The line at the water was pulled when the permits came, and every length of it is coiled in the store at the lodge. Wire goes round a wall as well as it goes across a trail."

"What did the water give us last season?" said the younger one.

"Four lights and the pelts, and the lodge took half of both," said the axe man.

"Half."

"Half, and the ground, and the wire, and the man who argues with the list in the ninth month."

The younger one looked up at the bowl, at the ground the men were closing inside it, and did not answer. The hound got up from the ground, walked to him, and stood with its muzzle against his leg, and his hand went down onto its head without his looking at it.

"Say it," said the man with the axe.

"The ground here has been worked before," said the younger one. "The sign on the north side was left by a hunting line, and it is nine years old, and the man who made it is not on this list. Somebody's granduncle had this ground and lost it."

"Then somebody's granduncle was careless."

"Or something up there was patient."

The axe man put the post down. He wiped his hands on his coat and waited.

"Nine years," he said. "Nine years and no line in it, and the beasts have been breeding in there the whole time. That is not patience. That is good ground going to waste. We will take four lights out of it this season and six the season after, and the sign we leave will be the sign the next man reads."

"And the north side?"

"We put a line on the north side too," said the man with the axe. "That is the whole point of the north side."

"How long will the fence take?"

"One man, a hundred paces of wire, and twelve days."

"Then we sleep in the camp."

"We sleep in the camp, and we eat out of it, and in the spring it is ours."

The boy came up from the stream with the water and set the skin down by the stones. The hound took two steps toward the north ridge and stood, and the man with the axe called it back.

"Nothing up there but trees," he said.

The boy looked at the ridge without answering, and went to build the fire. The hound lay down by the fire with its head toward the north.

"The camp goes in the book with a name on it either way," the axe man said. "That is the part that matters. A camp with our line in it is ours to work. A camp with no line in it is a fight every time we come up."

He drove the post. The sound went up the slope to where the dragon lay in the high grass — one dull knock after another, steady and slow, all the afternoon.

# Chapter 8: The Deep Country

He woke on a morning at the end of the summer, went down to the stream bend, and drank. When he lifted his head, he turned north instead of going to his den, and began to walk, and did not turn around again.

That was the deciding. There was no moment for it, and there never had been. The shape had been sitting in him since the last spring — the need pointing north, the ravine between, the deep forest banked beyond it like a fire — and this morning his body simply did what the shape said. The walking was the deciding.

He stopped once, on the high fern slope, and looked back at his own country. There it lay, whole and small and his, in the morning sun: the slope where the shell had split, the rock seam, the talus with the stone-runners wintering under it, the broken pine, the seep, the stream bend, his roots-house with its two ways out. He looked at it the way a beast looks at a thing it is putting down. Then he lifted his head and went on north, toward the black ridge.

He did not know whether he would ever see it again. He did not think in those terms. But something in him made the look, and gave the country its due, and only then let him go.

---

He crossed the serpent's ground on the third day, and it took him the whole of that day.

He had taken the measure of the ground all spring, and he knew the shape of the ravine country as he knew his own valley: where the black ground began, where the trees stopped growing straight, where the weight lay thinnest. The old ones have their own kind of quiet. The great serpent's weight thinned in the deep hot hours of the day, when all the ancient lives of the country lay down in the heat and let their eyes close. So he went in the middle of the day, by the high rim of the ground, and never through its heart.

He held the quiet from the first step, and it had never been strained like this. He held it whole over the whole of himself, as a beast holds a breath down deep, and he walked the rim with his body low and slow, up where the old stone lay bare and the trees came apart, watching the ravine the whole time with every sense he had.

Halfway across, it moved.

There was no sound. The weight simply turned over, as a sleeper turns in the deep middle of the night, and the whole of the ravine country bowed under it, and the birds went quiet — and he became nothing. It was not a plan. His body did it before he could have chosen to. He went down flat behind a slab of old stone with the quiet shut to its last shadow, his breath gone to almost nothing, his blood gone silent, his own presence pulled in against his bones, until there was nothing on the rim but black rock and a hard, thin animal lying still on it.

And the thing in the ravine passed its attention over the rim of its ground.

He felt it come. It was a weight and a looking at once, and the looking did not hate him and did not spare him; it had no color to it at all. It touched the place where he lay as a hand touches a table. And the sense of years — which he could not shut off — read the looking completely, without flinching, as it read any living thing: depth beyond all depth, stacked years past all measuring, an appetite the size of the whole low country. And under all of it, the thin, clear pull that ran from that blood to his own.

His blood answered. There was nothing he could do about that either. It answered before his will had a word in it: the first crown along his skull and neck went hot, and the pull in him sang its one thin note up into the weight above.

The attention stayed on him for as long as the looking took. Then it passed. The sleeper turned over, the deep weight settled back into its own ground, the ravine country let its breath out — and he lay behind the stone for a long time after the weight was gone.

He got up at last, when the light had changed, crossed the last rim, and went down into the far country. The lesson of the rim went with him, and he never had to learn it again. The greatness above was not malice, and it was not mercy. It wanted nothing, and it gave nothing. Its ground was paid for in the whole of the care — not because it was cruel, but because being in its country at all was a debt, and the debt was everything you had.

---

Then the deep forest opened, and it was a different world.

He understood it in the first hour: the forest he had grown up in was the edge of this. The trees stood farther apart and taller than any trees he had ever walked under, with bark like old stone, and under them the ground was deep and quiet and held smells he had no names for. And the lives stood thick on the ground. His sense had read a country of thin, bright, small things and scattered heavy ones. The deep forest's sense came in like weather: great, slow, heavy lives everywhere — in the fern, in the rocks, in the streams, in the trees. He walked ten paces into that green and stopped, because the ground under his feet held more years than the whole of his own valley ever had.

Then he looked farther, and the sense reeled.

Beyond the thick lives, past the edge of what he could read properly, there were depths he could not read at all. They did not stand up like fires, as the heavy ones did. They stood as absences — wide, patient, unlit places in the feel, where his sense went out and came back with nothing, as an eye comes back from fog.

The sense bowed. He felt it happen inside him, in the middle of a green he had never seen, and it was not fear. It was the thing the sense did when it met its own limit: it stopped, came down, and left the whole truth of the matter as a shape with no measure in it. The ancient ones of the deep forest were beyond him. The map that had been the whole world in his head had, in the space of ten paces, grown more room than he had years to fill.

He was small again. That was the second thing the deep forest did to him, and he stood in the tall green and let it be so, because it was true.

---

The country read him back the same day.

He came down out of the old trees into a fern break toward evening, and the break was full of the lesser lives of the deep forest — hare-kinds grown big as the fox-kinds of his valley, ground-birds with long legs, small quick things in the thickets. They did what the lesser lives of his own country did now. They went flat.

But not all of them, and he learned the difference before dark. Some of the deep forest's lesser folk did not go flat at all. They moved apart from him, unhurried, giving him a ground he had not asked for — and the giving was not fear. It was how a small nation moves aside for a name it has heard in its old stories. Their blood remembered something.

And there were others that did neither. In the last light he came up a rise, and a beast stood on the path above him: not a giant, something of the heavy dozen-years sort, low and broad, with old fight in its face. It looked at him as an equal looks, and it did not move off the path, and for a moment neither of them did anything.

He gave the ground. Not out of fear — the feel did it. The beast was standing on ground that belonged to something else, and something in the country behind it was old and heavy in a way his own blood answered to without any argument. Some of the deep forest's words were older than his.

He walked around it. The beast watched him go, and the crown on his neck was hot, and the lesson was clear: he was a dragon in the deep forest, not the dragon of the deep forest. That night he dug a new hollow under a shelf of roots above a stream — one way in, another way out, as a slope had taught him long ago — and slept in it with the deep green all around him, and nothing happened to him at all.

---

He hunted on the fifth day, and it was the hardest fight of his life.

He had read the country hard before he chose. That was the whole of what had kept him alive: he did not hunt the greatest thing he could see, and he did not hunt the smallest. He took what was alone, heavy, old near the end, and unaware. And on the fifth evening the feel gave him one: a beast of the far edge of the deep forest, huge and slow and old, a jawed thing with a hide like bark, standing alone at a wallow in the last of the light, chewing roots that only its kind could dig out.

It was the largest thing he had ever hunted. He worked it for two days — its hours, its wallow, its trail, as he had once worked the stag. On the second evening he came out of the ferns at the wallow, and the old beast was in the water up to its shoulders.

He went in low and fast, and got his hold at the base of the neck.

His teeth found the hide — and stuck there. The hide was a thing his jaw could not pass. He tore with his whole body, and the hide did not split. The beast came up out of the wallow with him hanging from its throat and shook him, as a tree shakes in the wind. Its strength was stone. Its head came round, its arm-thick hind limb came down, the blow caught him across the shoulder, tore him off the hold, and opened his flank like a knife — and he went into the mud.

He got up. He circled. The beast turned in the water to keep its face on him, breathing like bellows, and he understood the fight plainly: he could not beat its hide, and it could not catch him, and the light was going. So he went in a third time, and this time he used the thing the deep forest itself had given him the shape of.

The voice.

It came up out of his chest low and heavy, and it was not the full roar of the winter. It was short — the weight of his blood spent on the air between them. The old beast had never heard anything like it.

It faltered. Half a step, no more — and the half step was the whole of the fight. The falter turned its head, the turn opened the line under its jaw, and he was in it. The hold found the thin place. He set it, locked his forelegs, and the beast took a long time to die. In that time it did more damage to him than any beast ever had: the shoulder torn down to the muscle, the flank opened, the ribs on his left side cracked somewhere under the armor. But it died. He held until it was done, because letting go is what kills a beast that is winning.

He ate before he slept, though he could barely stand. The years' blood smells go where smells go, and lives heavier than his own had followed his kills in the outer country. So he ate fast, ate the deep cuts, and limped away into the dark with the meat down in him and the smell of the kill standing in the open behind him.

---

The devouring was not like the outer meals. It was not like anything.

The years of the deep beast went down into him like roots going down into deep wells, and his body took them as dry ground takes the whole of a rainstorm. He lay in his hollow for two days, and the deep sleep held him under, and while it held him, the change worked through him in the dark.

What the beast had been — the strength, the thickness, the slow endurance of its long life — went into his body and stayed there. His frame thickened. The cracked ribs knit. The new scars laid down over the old ones.

And the deep part of it — the years, the true long fire, the part that had made it heavy in the feel and old at the wallow — went down past his flesh and into his blood. He felt it go as a beast feels a fever break: the blood drank, and the blood was more dragon for the drinking. The blue-gray of his scales went darker, deep as the underside of the old trees. The crown along his skull stood a shade higher. And far inside him, the pull that ran to the ravine — and past the ravine — sang a little clearer than it had ever sung.

He woke on the third morning stronger than he had ever been, and the hunger that had no meal was a bonfire. It stood up in him whole that day, the bloodthirst of his kind with the deep years of a great beast underneath it, saying: there. And again. And louder than it had ever said anything. He stood in his hollow with his legs braced and the red thing trying to walk him out into the summer.

He held it with the whole of the life he had behind him — every winter, every mistake, every scar, every meal lost, every wall broken. The holding was the hardest thing he had done in the deep forest so far.

He held it. Then he went out, drank at the stream, and did not hunt that day at all.

---

At the end of the summer he came on men's work again, and the race was where he had left it. In the low ground under the giants' trees, where the brooks came together, the men had a camp. They had been in the deep forest eleven days. There were three of them, and a hound, and a boy, and they built their fires small and their beds off the trail. They walked the country as men walk ground they mean to keep: slow, in daylight, reading every sign twice.

"Nine kills on this brook in eleven days," said the oldest of the three. He sat with a ledger open on his knee and a stick of charcoal in his hand, and he had been writing all morning. "Four boar-kind, two deer-echo, one cat, one bear, and one thing I have no name for."

"Read it to me."

"Cat at the third crossing, four days old. Bear on the high ground, two days old." He set the charcoal down and turned the page. "And this one. Eleven paces of it, up the brook and out. No print. The ground rolled flat where it walked, in a line as straight as a road."

"Big."

"Big and walking," said the oldest man. "Something at the far edge of the deep forest walks a straight line, and a straight line is a thing beasts do not walk."

The younger man came in from the brook with his sleeves up and his arms wet to the elbow. He stood dripping over the fire.

"The water is low," he said. "Three crossings dry above the second spread, and the fourth has a wall of stone across it that one man cannot move."

"Then the line goes at the fourth," said the oldest man, "and we take the water as it comes down to it. That closes the edge of the deep forest from the brook to the ridge, and anything that walks the edge walks the line."

"And what walks the line?"

"Whatever the edge holds, by my count," said the oldest man. "Four boar-kind, two deer-echo, one cat, one bear, and the thing that walks straight."

The boy looked up from the fire, where he was turning meat on a stick. He was the youngest of the four and the quietest.

"Is it the one that took the man at the water?" he said.

"Nobody was taken at the water."

"The story says a man."

"The north is full of stories," said the younger man, "and the men in them are always careless. Turn the meat."

The hound got up from the fire, walked thirty paces down the brook, and stood with its muzzle up, and did not come back. The oldest man watched it for a while, and then shut the ledger.

"Careless or not," he said, "that thing is reading us. She has been reading something for two days. We do not camp on the brook again."

"Reading it as what?" said the younger man.

"As a beast that walks in a straight line and goes where it likes," said the oldest man. "Which is a thing that will be in a book one day, or a thing that will eat one of us. Either way, we do not sleep on its road."

They turned the fire, and the boy turned the meat. The deep trees stood over them in the last of the light, while the country went on with its own long business around them.

---

The beast on the fern slope a day's walking east had read the camp through the ground and the air long before any of it was said. Then the wind carried pieces of it up the slope, and he took the pieces for what they were: sound, and work, and men.

"Line up through the break."

"Both banks. Every crossing."

"The hound's got it again."

"Let her work."

The noise moved off east through the low ground, and he lay in the ferns and let the deep forest carry it away. They were on the same trails, working the same heavy kinds, and they had brought their hounds and their long, quiet death into the country of the great years. The deep forest was wide — wider than any hunger could finish. There were years in it that no beast and no line of men could take in a hundred seasons.

The sharing was still the race. That was the shape, kept whole and laid with the others: his list and their list, written over the same country.

He went back to his hollow at dusk and slept in the deep dark, and the deep forest let him stay. Not forever — that was clear to him without anybody saying it. It was letting him stay the way a big animal lets a smaller one sleep at the edge of its ground: for now, and because he had paid, and for as long as he went on being worth the room.

The earning had only begun.

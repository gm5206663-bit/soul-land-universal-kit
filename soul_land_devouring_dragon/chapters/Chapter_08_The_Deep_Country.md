# Chapter 8: The Deep Country

The deciding was not a moment. It never had been, and this one was less of a moment than most.

The shape had been sitting in him since the last spring — the need pointing north, the wall of the ravine between, the deep country banked beyond it like a fire — and one morning at the end of the green he simply woke up doing it. He went down to the stream bend and drank, and then he turned north instead of going to his den, and the walking was the deciding, and he did not turn around again.

He stopped once, on the high fern slope, and looked back at his own country. There it lay: the slope where the shell had split, the rock seam and the talus with the stone-runners wintering under it, the broken pine, the seep, the stream bend, his roots-house with its two ways. His range, whole and small and his, laid out in the morning sun. He looked at it as a beast looks at a thing it is putting down, and then he lifted his head and went on north toward the black ridge.

He did not know whether he would ever see it again. He did not think in those terms. But something in him made the look, and gave the country its due, and only then let him go.

---

He crossed the serpent's ground on the third day, and it took him the whole of that day to do it, and half of what he knew he did not do at all — he simply stopped being a thing that could be seen. The reading of the ground had taken him all spring. He knew the shape of the ravine country as he knew his own valley: where the black ground began, where the trees stopped growing straight, where the weight lay thinnest. The old ones have their stillness. The weight of the great thing in the ravine thinned in the deep hot hours of the day, when all the ancient lives of the country lay down in the heat and let their eyes close, and he went in the stillness, in the middle of the day, by the high rim of the ground and never through its heart.

He held the veiling from the first step. It had never been strained like this. He held it whole, over the whole of himself, as a beast holds a breath down deep, and he walked the rim of the ravine country with his body low and slow, up where the old stone lay bare and the trees came apart, watching the ravine the whole time with every sense he had.

Halfway across, it moved. There was no sound. The weight simply turned over, as a sleeper turns in the deep middle of the night, and the whole of the ravine country bowed under it, and the birds went quiet, and he became nothing.

It was not a plan. His body did it before he could have chosen to. He went down flat behind a slab of old stone with his veiling shut down to its last shadow, his breath gone to almost nothing, his blood gone quiet. His own presence was pulled in against his bones, until there was nothing on the rim of the ravine country but black rock and a hard thin animal lying still on it.

And the thing in the ravine — the great thing, the roof of the world — passed its attention over the rim of its ground. He felt it come. It was a weight and a looking at once, and the looking did not hate him and did not spare him; it had no colour to it at all. It touched the place where he lay as a hand touches a table. And the sense of years, which he could not shut off, read it completely and without flinching, as it read any living thing: depth beyond all depth, stacked years past the counting, an appetite the size of the whole green country, and under all of it the thin clear thread that ran from that blood to his own.

His blood answered. There was nothing he could do about that either; it answered before his will had a word in it, and the first crown along his skull and neck went hot, and the thread in him sang its one thin note up into the weight above.

The attention stayed on him for as long as the looking took. Then it passed, and the sleeper turned over, and the deep weight settled back down into its own ground, and the ravine country let its breath out, and he lay behind the stone for a long time after the weight was gone.

He got up at last, when the light had changed, and crossed the last rim of the ground and went down into the far country. The lesson of the rim went with him, and it stayed, and he never had to learn it again.

The greatness above was not malice and it was not mercy. It wanted nothing and it gave nothing. Its ground was paid for in the whole of the care — not because it was cruel, but because being in its country at all was a debt you paid with everything you had.

---

And then the deep country opened, and it was a different world. He understood it in the first hour: the forest he had grown up in was the edge of this. The trees here stood farther apart and taller than any trees he had ever walked under, with bark like old stone, and under them the ground was deep and quiet and held smells he had no names for. And the lives — the lives stood thick on the ground.

His sense of years had read a country of thin bright small things and scattered heavy ones. The deep country's reading came in like weather: great slow heavy lives everywhere, standing in the fern, in the rocks, in the streams, in the trees. He walked ten lengths into that green and stopped, because the ground under his feet had more years in it than the whole of his own valley had ever held.

Then he looked farther, and his sense reeled. Beyond the thick lives, past the edge of what he could read properly, there were depths he could not read at all. They did not stand up like fires as the heavy ones did. They stood as absences — wide, patient, unlit places in the reading where his sense went out and came back with nothing, as an eye comes back from fog.

The sense bowed. He felt it happen in him, in the middle of a green he had never seen, and it was not fear. It was the thing the sense did when it met its own limit: it stopped, folded down, and left the whole truth of the matter as a shape with no measurement in it.

The ancient ones of the deep country were beyond his reading, and the map that had been the whole world in his head had, in the space of ten lengths, grown more room than he had years to fill. He was small again. That was the second thing the deep country did to him, and he stood in the tall green and let it be so, because it was true.

---

The country read him back the same day. He came down out of the old trees into a fern break toward evening, and the fern break was full of the lesser lives of the deep country — hare-kinds grown big as the fox-kinds of his valley, ground-birds with long legs, small quick things in the thickets. They did what the lesser lives of his own country did now: they went flat.

But not all of them. He learned the difference before dark.

Some of the deep country's lesser folk did not go flat. They moved apart from him, unhurried, giving him a ground he had not asked for — and the giving was not fear. It was how a small nation moves aside for a name it has heard in its old stories. Their blood remembered something.

And there were others that did neither. In the last light he came up a rise and a beast stood on the path above him: not a giant, something of the heavy dozen-years sort, low and broad with old fight in its face. It looked at him as an equal looks, and did not move off the path, and for a moment neither of them did anything.

He gave the ground. Not out of fear. It was the reading that did it: the beast was standing on ground that belonged to something else, and something in the country behind it was old and heavy in a way his own blood answered to without any argument. Some of the deep country's words were older than his.

He walked around it, and the beast watched him go, and the crown on his neck was hot and the lesson was clear: he was a dragon in the deep country, not the dragon of the deep country. That night he dug a new hollow under a shelf of roots above a stream — one way in, another way out, as he had been taught by a slope a long time ago — and slept in it with the deep green all around him, and nothing happened to him at all.

---

He hunted on the fifth day. He had read the country hard before he chose. That was the whole of the craft that had kept him alive: he did not hunt the greatest thing he could see, and he did not hunt the smallest; he took what was alone, heavy, old near the end, and unaware. And on the fifth evening the reading gave him one: a beast of the deep fringe, huge and slow and old, a jawed thing with a hide like bark, standing alone at a wallow in the last of the light, chewing on roots that only its kind could dig out.

It was the largest thing he had ever hunted. He worked it for two days. He learned its hours and its wallow and its trail as he had learned the stag, and on the second evening he came out of the ferns at the wallow when the old beast was in the water up to its shoulders.

And then he had the hardest fight of his life. The beast's hide was a thing his jaw could not pass. He came in low and fast and got his hold at the base of the neck, and his teeth found the hide and stuck there. The hide did not split; he had to work, tearing with his whole body. The beast came up out of the wallow with him hanging from its throat and shook him as a tree shakes in the wind. Its strength was a wall. Its head came round and its arm-thick hind limb came down and caught him across the shoulder, and the blow tore him off the hold and opened his flank like a knife, and he went into the mud.

He got up. He circled. He went in again, and this time he used the thing the deep country had just given him — the voice.

It came up out of his chest low and heavy, and it was not the full roar of the winter; it was short and low, the weight of his blood spent on the air between them. The old beast had never heard anything like it. It faltered — half a step, no more — and the half step was the whole of the fight, because the falter turned its head and opened the line under its jaw, and he was in it.

The hold found the thin place under the jaw. He set it, and got his forelegs locked, and the beast took a long time to die. In that time it did more damage to him than any beast had ever done: the shoulder torn down to the muscle, the flank opened, the ribs on his left side cracked somewhere under the armor. But it died, and he held until it was done, because letting go is what kills a beast that is winning.

He ate it before he slept, though he could barely stand. The lesson of the deep country had already been written that day: the years' blood smells go where smells go, and lives heavier than his own had followed the kill in the outer country. He ate fast, and he ate the deep cuts, and then he limped into the dark with the meat down in him and the smell of the kill standing behind him in the open.

---

The devouring was not like the outer meals. It was not like anything.

The years of the deep beast went down into him like roots going down into deep wells, and his body took them as dry ground takes the whole of a rainstorm. He lay in his hollow for two days and the deep sleep held him under, and while it held him the change worked through him in the dark:

What the beast had been — the strength, the thickness, the slow endurance of its long patient life — that part of it went into his body and stayed there. His frame thickened; the cracked ribs knit; the new scars laid over the old ones.

And the deep part of it — the years, the true long fire of the thing, the part that had made it heavy in the reading and old at the wallow — went down past his flesh and into his blood. He felt it go as a beast feels a fever break: the blood drank, and the blood was more dragon for the drinking. The blue-grey of his scales went darker, deep as the underside of the old trees. The crown along his skull stood a shade higher. And far inside him the thread that ran to the ravine, and beyond the ravine, sang a little clearer than it had ever sung.

He woke on the third morning stronger than he had ever been, and the hunger that had no meal was a bonfire. It stood up in him whole that day, the bloodthirst of his kind with the deep years of a great beast underneath it, saying there, and again, and louder than it had ever said anything. He stood in his hollow with his legs braced and the red thing trying to walk him out into the green. He held it with the whole of the life he had behind him — every winter, every mistake, every scar, every meal lost and every wall broken. The holding was the hardest thing he had done in the deep country so far.

He held it. Then he went out and drank at the stream and did not hunt that day at all.

---

At the end of the green he came on men's work again, and the race was where he had left it. In the low ground under the giants' trees, where the brooks came together, the men had a camp.

They had been in the deep country eleven days. There were three of them, and a hound, and a boy, and they built their fires small and their beds off the trail. They walked the country as men walk ground they mean to keep: slow, in daylight, reading every mark twice.

"Nine marks on this brook in eleven days," said the oldest of the three. He sat with a ledger open on his knee and a stick of charcoal in his hand, and he had been writing all morning. "Four boar-kind, two deer-echo, one cat, one bear, and one thing I have no name for."

"Read it to me."

"Cat at the third crossing, four days old. Bear on the high ground, two days old." He set the charcoal down and turned the page. "And this one. Eleven lengths of it, up the brook and out. No print. The ground rolled flat where it walked, in a line as straight as a road."

"Big."

"Big and walking," said the oldest man. "Something in the deep fringe walks a straight line, and a straight line is a thing beasts do not walk."

The younger man came in from the brook with his sleeves up and his arms wet to the elbow. He stood dripping over the fire.

"The water is low," he said. "Three crossings dry above the second spread, and the fourth has a wall of stone across it that one man cannot move."

"Then the line goes at the fourth," said the oldest man, "and we take the water as it comes down to it. That closes the fringe from the brook to the ridge, and anything that walks the fringe walks the line."

"And what walks the line?"

"Whatever the fringe holds, by my count," said the oldest man. "Four boar-kind, two deer-echo, one cat, one bear, and the thing that walks straight."

The boy looked up from the fire, where he was turning meat on a stick. He was the youngest of the four and the quietest.

"Is it the one that took the man at the water?" he said.

"Nobody was taken at the water."

"The story says a man."

"The north is full of stories," said the younger man, "and the men in them are always careless. Turn the meat."

The hound got up from the fire, walked thirty lengths down the brook, and stood with its muzzle up, and did not come back. The oldest man watched it for a while and then shut the ledger.

"Careless or not," he said, "that thing is reading us. She has been reading something for two days. We do not camp on the brook again."

"Reading it as what?" said the younger man.

"As a beast that walks in a straight line and goes where it likes," said the oldest man. "Which is a thing that will be in a book one day, or a thing that will eat one of us. Either way we do not sleep on its road."

They turned the fire, and the boy turned the meat. The deep trees stood over them in the last of the light while the country went on with its own long business around them.

---

The beast on the fern slope a day's walking east had read the camp through the ground and the air long before the words came. Then the wind carried pieces of it up the slope, and he took the pieces for what they were: sound, and work, and men.

"Line up through the break."

"Both banks. Every crossing."

"The hound's got it again."

"Let her work."

Then the noise moved off east through the low ground, and he lay in the ferns and let the deep country carry it away. They were on the same trails, working the same heavy kinds, and they had brought their reading and their hounds and their long quiet death into the country of the great years. The deep country was wide, wider than any hunger could finish. There were years in it that no beast and no line of men could take in a hundred seasons.

The sharing was still the race. That was the shape, kept whole and laid with the others: his list and their list, written over the same country.

He went back to his hollow at dusk and slept in the deep dark, and the deep country let him stay. Not forever. That was clear to him without anybody saying it. It was letting him stay as a big animal lets a smaller one sleep at the edge of its ground: for now, and because he had paid, and for as long as he went on being worth the room. The earning had only begun.

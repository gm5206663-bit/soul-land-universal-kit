# Chapter 9: The Keeping Earned

He came up out of the ferns with a full belly, and the wind brought him a smell that did not belong.

He read it standing, and it went into him cold. Something had been walking his trail — not following him, but working ahead of him, on the route he had walked two days before, and the smell on that ground was the smell of a beast that had been reading his blood.

The deep forest had been hunting him from his first week in it, and it took him a month to learn why: the kill at the wallow clung to him. The meat of a kill feeds the body and is gone, but the deep part goes into the blood and stays there, and the blood says where he has been. He had known it in his body without ever knowing it in his head. It was why the country never let him lie down fat and stupid, as other beasts did. Now he knew it in his head: he had been carrying the wallow kill on the whole of him, and every beast of the deep forest that could read had the right to come.

---

He learned the lesson in the worst way first.

He was eating at a fresh kill in the low fern when the country went wrong. He came up with his muzzle red and read the summer: two of the lean ones, hunters from the far edge, standing in the shadows downwind, waiting for the meal to end. They were not alone. He could smell the third.

He ate the deep parts fast and got moving — along the valley floor, which was the mistake. For the rest of that night and all the next day he was a beast being worked, the lean ones behind him and the smell of his own kill laid along his trail like a rope.

He lost them at the cold stream: in chest-deep, upstream, until the water took the smell of blood off his hide, and out on a shelf of bare rock half a day north. He sat dripping in the last of the light, chest heaving, and the shape of the whole thing stood in him at last.

The deep part of the kill belonged to the eater. The rest of it did not have to.

---

He spent the next month building the answer, and the month after that proving it, and the whole of it went like this.

He took his kills at a distance, with water or bare stone between the kill and his sleep. He ate the deep part at the kill itself — the heart, the hot meat behind the ribs, the parts that hold the fire — and carried none of it anywhere. He washed, always. And when he wanted the country to go somewhere, he left the rest of the kill behind on purpose.

The decoy came together by accident. He took a heavy beast in the far south of his range, ate it down, and went — and the beasts that came to his kills came in behind him, as always. Three days later, from the high ground, he read the answer still working the kill's ground while he lay full in his hollow a long walk away. The kill had spoken, the country had gone to it, and he had never been closer than the high ground.

So the rest of a kill was a tool — not waste, not bait; a thing left in a place, saying what he wanted said. He laid his first decoy at the edge of a country he did not want to hunt, and watched the beasts come to it from three directions while he hunted the other side of his range, unbothered.

The season had turned him from the hunted thing into the thing that writes. He had no name for it — only the shape, kept.

---

The voice learned its lesson in one day, the hard way.

He called on a hunt for the first time that summer — his own choosing, not terror. The beast was a broad, shy thing of the edge years, old and canny, and it had read the shout of him three mornings running and gone. On the fourth morning he stood up in the open, in front of it, and let the weight come out of him all at once.

The beast went flat. He took it in the next fifty paces, clean, and it never got up. The voice had bought the whole hunt.

Then the deep forest came for the roar. The low note had carried farther than his ears could follow, and inside half a day it drew a hunter of the middle deep up to the ground where he had called: a moving hill, a great low creature of the kind that had never once been made afraid. It came through the fern on his own trail. He went up the rocks, lay still, and watched it go by below — and it went by for a long time.

He rebuilt the quiet that evening, while the hill was still in his sense, and found the flaw in his own idea. Being still was the body. The quiet was the other thing — the thing around the body that tells the country there is nothing here. He had been treating them as one, and they were two.

By summer's end he could hold the quiet while he moved — not perfectly, but through the long, smooth stretches of a stalk — and the deep forest tested it twice more and lost the feel both times. The voice went onto the list of things he owned, and the quiet went with it — two different tools, never confused again.

---

The men came up the giants' country in the middle of the summer, and the first thing they left behind was a husk.

He found it on the fourth morning: a beast of the heavy years, twice his weight, dead in a break with its hide opened, its meat untouched, its years gone out of it. The flat gray place where its life had been stood in his sense like a hole cut in the summer.

And beside the body, on the moss, there was a light.

He had seen a light taken once before, from a root-wall above the fern country, three years ago. This was the same thing — and not the same thing. It stood banked and dark, turning slowly, a deep ring the color of old wine — so deep his sense did not so much see it as fall into it. The first light had been pale gold. This one was far darker. It was the color of a thousand years.

He did not touch it. It was not his, as the fires of the lodges were not his. But he stood over it a long time before he went. What he carried away was not fear; it was the plain arithmetic of the country, standing in the open at last. The men of the deep forest were not hunting meat, any more than he was. They were hunting exactly what he hunted — and a beast like the one in the break would have fed his own blood for a season.

The race had followed him into the deep forest. It was no longer a line of smoke on his map; it was a husk in the fern and a light on the moss, a day's walk from his hollow.

---

He was under his ledge when the hunt came through beneath him.

He had made his resting place on a shelf of stone above a green break, where he could lie in the sun and read the country both ways. In the middle of the afternoon, the hounds came into the break below. He had heard them in the distance for two days. He had never heard them this close.

They came up the break in a loose line with the men behind them, working — not running a scent hot, but reading the whole break slowly, as he worked his own trails. One of the men carried the curved bow of the long death, an arrow on the string. Another, older, walked behind the hounds with his eyes on the ground, reading it as a beast reads water.

"Fresh sign at the fork," said the one with the bow. "Two days old at most, and the pads are as wide as my hand."

"Wider," said the old reader. He knelt at the print without touching it and laid his own hand flat beside it, and the print was bigger than his hand by the width of two fingers. "And it is not a bear. A bear walks with the front of the pad down and the heel off the ground. This one puts the whole foot down."

"Cat?"

"A cat does not walk a break in daylight with its whole foot down." The old man got up and rubbed his hands. "Something heavy walked this break two days before us, and it walked it like a man walking his own road. Out one end and in the other. Not hunting, not feeding. Going."

"Then it lives near here."

"Everything lives near here," said the old man. "That is what the deep forest is. Work the line."

"Then we are hunting a thing that lives here."

"We are hunting its ground," said the old man. "We read where it walks, and we put a line where it walks, and one morning it walks into the line. That is the trade, and it is the only trade there is. Nobody in this country has ever hunted one of the big ones face to face. The men who tried it did not come up the break a second time."

"What if the hound reads it before the line does?"

"Then I take her head, and you hold the bow on the ground in front of her, and we walk out of the break backwards, slow."

"Slow."

"Slow, and never turn. Turn and you are prey, and the deep forest does not care which of us that is."

They worked the line up the break with the hounds spread and the boy at the back with the pack. The older man read the ground behind the hounds, and the one with the bow walked with his eyes on the walls.

"What did the box take last night?" said the one with the bow.

"A thin light off a cat," said the boy from behind. "Pale. The old man says the lodge will write it down and pay half."

"The lodge writes everything down and pays half."

"The lodge pays for what it can tally," said the old reader. "So we tally. And a light off a beast that dies thirty paces from the line is a light that stands where it fell and goes to nobody, because the man who takes a light has to be standing at it with the box open when it rises. That is two days of walking for a light we were not there for. So we do not wound. We kill where we mean to stand."

"There was one standing at the water, three weeks back."

"There was," said the old reader. "A bull went through a line at the water and walked thirty paces and lay down, and the light stood over it for two days before anyone got back to it, and by then it was pale as ditchwater. Write that in the book too. We lost four days and half a light to a beast that would not die where it was told."

The hounds moved up the break, noses down, working the ground a length at a time. And then the wind turned.

He felt it roll up the break and go through the fern, and he pulled the quiet down over himself, whole. The lead hound lifted its head.

It stood in the middle of the break, head up, ears forward, its whole body still, and it looked at the ledge — at the exact short length of stone that held him. He could see its eyes showing white from where he lay. It whined once, low.

"What's she got?" said the one with the bow.

"The wind," said the old man. He walked up beside the hound without hurrying and put a hand on the back of its head. "Wind off the ledge, and she'll take it for a beast every time. Down, girl. Down."

"Is there something up on the ledge?"

"Rock," said the old man, "and fern, and rock again. We walked the tracks at first light and the tracks ran south. Whatever we are after is on the south bank by now, and we are on the wrong side of the water."

He pulled the hound's muzzle down toward the ground and said the short, flat thing that meant follow. The hound went back to the ground. The whole line moved on down the break and out of it, and the afternoon went back to being the afternoon.

He lay on the ledge a long time with the quiet holding. They had read their way to the bottom of his ledge and not found him — closer than anything had ever come — and the reason was one old man's hand on a hound's head. He moved his resting place the next morning, and he never used the same ledge twice in a season again.

---

The thing on the high ground came for him at the end of the summer, and it was not a beast, and it was not the men.

He was working back to his hollow along the top of the long ridge, in the gray time before the last light, when the world stopped. No sound, no smell, nothing to see. The whole country simply stopped, as a heart stops — birds, insects, the small lives in the fern, all gone quiet at once — every hair on him stood, his blood went cold, and something put its attention on him.

It was not a weight that pressed. It was a looking. He had felt the ravine's weight; he had felt the crush of the unreadable depths; this was different from both. The attention had no malice in it, and no mercy, and it wanted nothing at all. It looked at him as the sky looks at a stone.

He went down into the fern and pulled the quiet to its last shadow, as he had under the ravine. He became nothing.

It made no difference. The quiet was not enough — had never been made for this.

And then the thing in his blood stood up on its own. The first crown went hot, the pull rose, and the deep voice of the dragon went out of him into the attention above — not his call, not his choosing. It was spoken by the blood itself, as a small king's name is spoken in the ear of a greater.

The attention took the voice in, and read it, and knew it. He was weighed. It was like being held on a scale; he could not move and did not try; and somewhere in the middle of it, the ancient thing found what it was looking for, and put the scale down.

The attention lifted. The country came back in a rush — birds, insects, wind, the whole green breathing again. He lay in the fern for a while. Then he got up, and his legs went, and he sat down heavily, and after a time he got up again and started for home.

He walked slower than he had ever walked, and did not stop until his hollow's dark was around him. The deep forest held one more thing, then. Not a beast, and not a hunger. A greatness that lived past all his sense, that had read the whole of him in the time it takes a bird to call, weighed him, and let him go.

He did not know why he had been let go. He would carry that question as he carried the barrier: without an answer, for the rest of his life.

---

The red thing nearly killed him in the last month of the summer, and the voice saved him.

He had been working a heavy beast all day in the broken country south of the giants, a long, slow hunt, and somewhere in the last length the patience broke open into more than patience. The red thing came up in him whole, took the spring before he had given it, and carried him at the beast with his jaws already open.

His jaws went to the standing leg. That is what the red thing does: it goes to whatever the body is nearest to. The leg was the wrong leg — the bone under the hide was thick as a young tree, and his teeth were not made for bone. They skated, they tore his own mouth, and the beast turned on him.

The horn of its shoulder opened his side, and he was down in the rocks with the whole of its weight coming, and nothing left in him but his own life and the noise in his own head.

He called.

The voice came up out of the red thing — out of the deepest part of him, where the rage and the blood and the weight all lived together — and it was the hardest calling of his life. It went out low and heavy and short, straight into the beast's face at four paces.

The beast faltered. It was the length of a breath, no more — the standing leg set wrong, the great head coming round — and in that breath, he had his hold. He found the neck. He held through everything the beast had left, and it was a long time, and it cost him blood he could not afford.

When it was done, he lay in the rocks beside the kill with his side opened and his mouth split, and the red thing still standing in him, walking him in circles inside his own skin. He held it. Then he ate the deep part of the kill, went to the water, and lay in the cold stream until the red thing lay down.

Afterward, turning it over in the long, still hours, he understood what the day had cost and what it had bought. The red thing had sprung him at the beast and nearly killed him — the flaw had carried him, as it had been carrying his whole line into the ground since the beginning. And the voice, the one thing he had made out of his own blood in three seasons of learning, was the thing that had pulled him back. He carried both facts the rest of the summer. He did not stop hunting, and he did not stop riding the red thing down.

---

At the very end of the summer, he stood on the high rocks above the giants' country and read himself. His scales were darker than the spring had made them — deep blue-black, the color of the underside of the old trees. The crown along his skull stood higher, and in low light it caught the light differently from the rest of him. And down in the blood, the pull sang a cleaner line than any he had carried, running north to the ravine.

He was more dragon than the beast that had crossed the serpent's rim in the spring. The deep forest kept him now. Not wholly — never wholly; the giants' trees still stood over him, and the unreadable depths still stood in his sense as absences — but truly. He slept in it, hunted it, wrote his marks in it, and the country answered him as a large house answers a tenant who pays.

He would need to pay again. He had not yet earned the middle of the deep forest, or the ground of the fallen giants, or anything in the far north where the absences stood.

But the first keeping was his.

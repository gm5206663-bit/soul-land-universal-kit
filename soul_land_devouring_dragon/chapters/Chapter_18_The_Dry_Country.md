# Chapter 18: The Dry Country

The water went down all summer and the low country came apart into pools.

By the middle of the season the two streams were chains of still water with long cracked flats between them, and everything that lived in the low country walked the same lines between the same pools. The slow heavy kind came down to drink at dusk. The birds worked the drying edges. And at the far end of each chain, where the water still ran over stone, the runs stayed quick and clear and cold.

He drank at the runs.

He had learned the still water in the first week of the dry, when he came down to the main pool one night and smelled the hound before he reached the bank. She was staked in the reeds above the water on a short line, and there was a fire behind a screen of cut branches, and boots went round the edge of the pool in the morning's mud. The men were back, and they were sitting on the water, and they did not mean to leave it while the dry lasted. He backed off and drank two miles up, at a run that came out of the rock, and lay there in the dark with his ears on the low country.

So the summer made its own rules. He hunted the upper water, where the meat came to drink at the runs. He took his kills standing in the stream over stone, ate in the water, and left the rest up on the bank for the crows. He crossed on the rock bars, gave the still pools and their long soft edges all the room they wanted, and drank where the water moved. It cost him miles and hunting hours every day, and the season was hot, and twice a month he came in with the flies at the cut places and lay up a day in the shade.

He paid it. Nothing of him lay anywhere the ground could write.

The hunger came up after every meal the same as always. Over a full belly, in the heat, on bare stone, it asked its question, and the question did not change.

---

The hound was the thing that would not settle.

He watched her from the rim of the low country, from the fern, from the far edge of the light, more than once as the season went on. She was slower than she had been; he could read the years in how she moved and how deep she slept in the day. But every night the man with her put her on her line above the water, and every night she worked the dark bank with her nose up, and some nights she did more than that.

Three nights he saw her stand and point at nothing.

She would be lying at the pool's edge, and then she would stand — no warning, no sound — and go to the end of her line and stand with her whole body pointed up the water, into the dark north where the run came down. She would hold it there, pointed like a thrown spear, while the fire burned behind her and the man came out of the blind and stood beside her. He would look at the same dark, and see nothing at all, and still stand. The first night he watched her hold it a quarter of an hour. The second night, longer. And after the third night the man moved his bed out of the blind and down to the pool's edge beside her, and slept there with his hand on her line, night after night.

She was reading the air for a smell that was not there — not on the nights he watched, because on those nights he was a long stone's throw away in the dark, lying still. She did it because the smell had been there before, in her lifetime, twice. She had held it in her nose at a water crossing two summers back, and she had never once lost it since, and she believed it would come again.

He read her patience the way he read a track. She was old for her kind, older than the men around her knew, and the work she was doing was the work she had done all her life, and it had never failed her before.

He lay in the fern above and watched her stand there, pointed at the empty dark, with the firelight on her ribs. The ground had stopped writing him a long time ago. The air had not.

---

The kill was up at the first of the runs, and he took it the way he took everything that summer: from the water, standing in the current.

It was a grazer of the slow kind, come down the stream to drink at sundown. He came up out of the shallows below the run and took it at the shoulder, and the two of them went down together in the deep part, and the water carried the red away downstream while he ate. He ate the deep parts there in the shallows, on the gravel, and left the body where the drag-mark showed, and went up onto the stone to sleep.

Two days later he read the men at his kill.

They had walked it in boots, four of them — one with the lodge road's dust on it, so the old man had come up from the south for this. They had stood on the bank a long time. And then they had gone down on their knees in the gravel, all of them in the same small patch, at the head of the run, in the shallows where his feet had stood while he ate.

He went over their marks slowly, and read the line of the morning for himself. The hound had come down the bank working hard, and at the water's edge she had put her nose down into the shallows where he had stood, and she had barked once. He could hear the shape of it in the ground she had left, all four feet planted and her weight forward. Then she had stood at the running water and would not step into it. She never had. He had watched her refuse a stream the summer before, and she had refused this one, with his smell in it, standing over the very stones his feet had crossed.

The men had looked at the water a long time after that. He read their boots going up the bank in little steps, and the prints of their faces where they had turned and looked north up the run, where the kill had come from and where he had gone. More than once, from the marks: a man standing and looking at fast water with a hound's word in his hand and nothing to put with it.

---

He understood the shape of the summer by then.

They had brought their wire and their hound and their paper to the water, and the ground he walked gave them nothing, because he had stopped writing. But the hound did not need his print. She read him out of the air, and out of the water, and out of a smell two summers old that she had never once let go. She had led them to his kill and stood over the shallows where his feet had stood, and the men had knelt in the gravel at the head of the run, at the one place in the whole low country where his own smell lay thick on the ground.

And they had no more use for that than they had for a fire in the rain, because it could not be carried: no track, no print, no body, and a hound standing where a beast should be and nothing there to see.

The drink was the problem now, and the drink could not be dropped. He could go a week without meat and he could not go two days without water in the heat. The men were on the main pool and the hound was on the air, and the country was going to get drier before it got wet.

So he answered it the only way the country let him. He drank at the runs, in the black of the night, standing in the current, with the taste of stone in the water and his ears on the low country behind him. He hunted the upper water, took his kills in the stream, dragged what he could not eat onto the stone and into the fern. He kept to rock, and to the dark, and to the fast water, and he made the men's water a place he did not go.

---

The dry ended, and then the thing he had been waiting for all summer happened without any warning at all.

He came down off the stone in the last week of the season to read the pool one more time, the way he read it every few days, and the work was gone.

The wire was up off both trails and gone into the packs; he found the two ends of its run and the holes where the stakes had stood. The blind was down, the poles lying in the reeds. The fire ring was scattered, and the ground around it put back to grass as well as hands can put it back. The hound was gone, and the line with her, and the men. Their trail walked south along the water and then turned down out of the low country, onto the long country, the road to their own places — and nothing about it was a patrol's walk. The packs had been heavy and the men had walked like men walking home.

He went over the whole camp twice. The ground had been used hard all summer and then left clean: no blood, no gear dropped, no dead man in the reeds. They had simply and finally finished, and gone, and taken their papers and their iron and their hound down the road with them, and nothing of the whole summer's watching stayed in the low country except the marks of it, which the grass would take in a season. And when he had read the last of it he stood on the pool's bank in the sun with the water going slowly down at his feet, and the low country empty around him, and the thing that had been in his way for three seasons was not in the country any more.

No fire came up the country that autumn. He read the low trail twice a month, and no boots walked it. The winter came down out of the north and he took his winter ground above the deep forest, and when the snow broke and the water ran and the spring came up the country, still no smoke stood anywhere in the low country. Their lodge was somewhere down-country, and the men who knew this ground were down there with it, and what they had learned of him they had carried away.

The country was his, all of it — the low trail and the springs, the road, the high ground with its pan and its four stones, the ridge above the deep forest where he sat in the evenings. And this time there was nothing in it that hunted him in return.

He crossed the high ground once that spring and stood with the pan below him and the four stones in their row at the edge of it. Grass had come up through the cracks in the pan and the old print was gone under it. A crow sat on the windward stone, and got up when it saw him, and went away down the hill.

---

He walked the whole of it in the first weeks of the spring, from the southern fern to the dry edge above the deep forest, and slept in a different place most nights, and drank where he liked.

It took him six days to walk it all, which was longer than the walk itself needed, because he went slowly at the edges. At the southern end he stood where the low trail went down out of the country, the road the men's boots had used for years, and read the last of their going in the wet mud: heavy packs, the hound's pads set among the boots, two of them walking together and two apart. The trail went down the long slope and out of sight, and the country behind it went on being the country.

The deep forest stood north where it had always stood, and the great beasts worked their country up and down inside it, and the pull under them was there the same as it had been since his first summer, far off and steady. The hunger stood up after every meal and asked its question, and there was no meal in the low country that could answer it, and he had long ago stopped expecting one to.

He was six winters in the world, or near enough, and the country that had seemed as wide as the world when he crawled out of the shell was one piece of the border of a forest that went on for a hunter's whole life in every direction. South, there were the men and their lodges and their lists. East, the mountain country. North, the deep forest, and beyond the deep forest, the country of the great beasts, and under all of it the slow pull that had been in him since the first hunger.

He stood on the dry edge at the end of that spring, with his own country behind him and nothing in it that could read him, and he looked north for a long time.

Then he went down into the low country to hunt, because that was the day's work, and the road to the north was a road for years.

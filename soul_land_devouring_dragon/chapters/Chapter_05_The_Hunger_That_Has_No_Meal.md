# Chapter 5: The Hunger That Has No Meal

The second autumn met him as an equal, and that was the first autumn he had ever had.

The first one had been a thing that happened to him. This one he walked into with his eyes open: the green going gold at the edges, the heavy kinds feeding up for the cold, the stream running low and clear, and the first thin frost on the high ground in the mornings. He knew the country now. He knew what it did in the second month and in the third. When the cold came, he would be ready for it, and the knowing made the whole season taste different, the way meat tastes different when the belly is already warm.

He ate well. He ate better than he had eaten in his life. And on the misty mornings, when he worked the fern country, he began to learn the last and hardest thing about hunting.

---

The trouble was the stillness.

He had learned stillness for prey that ran and prey that hid. A beast comes down to a stream and puts its head in the water; a beast grazes in a run with its head down and its ears working; a beast walks a path it has walked for years. For all of those, lying in the fern and not moving was enough, and it had been enough for two seasons.

The fern-deer were different. The fern-deer read the country as he read it.

He found that out on the first cold-mist morning of the autumn, stalking the fern country for the third day running. There was a herd of seven in the long grass at the top of the valley, and he had spent two days learning them, and on the third morning he lay in the mist-wet fern ten lengths downwind of the whole herd with his chin on his claws and worked out, comfortably, exactly which one he would take.

He did not move. He had not moved in a long time. And then the lead doe lifted her head and looked down the wind at the fern where he lay, and looked at it for two long breaths. The whole herd turned as one and went off up the valley at a walk that was not quite a run. They did not come back that day or the next.

He had not moved. He had not made a sound. Something in him had been standing up in the air like a shout, and the deer had read the shout.

He lost three hunts that month the same way, and the losing taught him more than the kills had. He learned it as he learned everything, on his belly in the wet fern, going over and over what had happened, and the shape that came out of it was this: he was louder than he looked.

Not in air and not in sound. In the other thing — the thing the foxes went flat for, and the low lives went down for, the weight in the blood that went out ahead of him and told the country what was coming. He had been walking through the world like a shout, and the only reason it had not mattered was that most of the country was too small to run.

The deer were old enough and quick enough to listen. And if the deer could hear him, then the predators that lived above him in the years could hear him too.

He started doing with the shout what he did with his body: holding it down.

It was not something he could master in a week. It was a thing he had to hold with his whole attention, and his attention could only be in one place at once. When he crouched over a trail and pulled the shout in against himself and held it there, the small lives of the fern went on about their business a length away from him, and the deer lifted their heads and looked and did not find the thing they were looking for.

It worked on the small and the quick. It broke the moment another hunger came up in him. And the first time he used it in a real stalk, the deer's head came up four times. Four times the deer looked and did not see. He took the doe in the low fern at the water's edge with one hold, and stood over her in the mist with the steam coming off the meat, and it took him a while to understand what he had just paid for it.

Attention. The patience of the small and the quick had made him patient, and now the shout was the price of the last length.

If he held it, he was slower than he should be, because a share of him was always busy holding. That was the toll. His hunts grew fewer and slower and surer, and by the end of the autumn he could walk his own trails hushed to a shadow, and the whole forest walked on around him without knowing he was there.

He would pay that toll for the rest of his life. It was the first thing he ever chose about himself, and he chose it for meat.

---

The other thing that autumn taught him was worse.

He was not growing any more.

Not stopping — he checked, as he checked everything, in the still mornings and the long sleeps. The meat still went down and the deep sleeps still came. But the change that had lived in him all summer, the answering, thickening, deepening change, had gone quiet as a fire goes quiet. He ate a deer and slept and woke, and nothing in him was different.

Then it got stranger. There came a week when he ate well every day and slept deep every night, and woke on the fourth morning with the hunger standing in him as tall as it had ever stood — as if he had not eaten in a month.

He ate again. The hunger did not go down.

It was not the belly. The belly was full. This was another hunger entirely, and it lived in the same place, and it had no name and no food. It said: more. It said: greater. It did not say where, because there was nowhere; there was only the country, and the country had been eaten down to the level of his own mouth.

He stood on the high ground over the fern country one grey evening and looked out at the whole green bowl of his range, and he understood, as he understood most things, without any words at all: he had grown past what this country could give.

The country was enough for a beast his size. It would feed him every day until he died. And it was not enough for the thing that his kind carried, which had fed once and now wanted to feed forever.

He had come to the first wall of his life. He could not see it, and could not measure it, and it stood across his whole future anyway, and the hunger — the one that had no meal — stood on his side of it, walking up and down.

He did not cross it that autumn. There was nothing to cross it with. But he marked it, as he marked everything, and went on hunting.

---

The last of the autumn gave him the stag.

He had been watching the great stag-kind for two weeks, up in the high fern country where the ground climbed into old broken rock. It was ancient — heavier than any beast he had taken, its coat gone ragged and its horns grown huge and crooked with every year of its long life — and it stood alone in that high green, as old things stand alone, slow and separate, keeping its own company.

It was sick. That was why it was still there and why no beast had chased it off its ground. He could see it in how it walked: one whole side of it stiff, the breath coming short after any long move, the great head hanging lower than a great head hangs.

In the old roots-house of his mind, the shapes lay where he kept them: the jaw beneath him, the takers on the trails, the roof of the world. The stag was not any of those three.

The stag was the wall.

He did not decide that. It was simply true, and he acted on it as he acted on appetite. He stalked the great stag for two more weeks, and on the first morning of the white — the real white, the cold come down to stay — he came up through the mist at the old beast's flank and took its shoulder in his jaws.

The stag did not run. It had not run from anything in years.

It came round on its hind legs and met him with the horns.

He had known the horns were on it. He had not known, until the first one hit him, that the weight of a life that long could be put into a blow. The horn took him under the ribs and threw him half a length up the slope. The second one opened his shoulder — not raked it, opened it, a long clean gash from the shoulder joint down to the rib, and the blood came out hot and fast over his own forelegs.

He got back in. He had to; the stag was above him on the high ground and there was nowhere to go but through it. It caught him again, and again, and the third time the horn came in under his jaw and his hold slipped and he was thrown down the rock, and he lay there with the whole world roaring and the blood running under him, taking stock of his own body as a fighter takes stock.

Then he got up.

The stag stood above him with its head down and its sides heaving and its breath going wrong, and the fight went on into its last quarter: he came in low where the horns did not reach, he paid for the last length with his flank, and his jaws closed on the stag's throat.

The hold was long. The old beast went down on its knees and stayed there with its eyes open, and its strength went out of it slowly, as a long life goes out. He held until the last of it was gone.

And then, while the great years of the stag were coming up out of the body, he did something he did not understand and would never be able to explain.

He let go, stepped back, and lay down in the snow beside it.

Not from mercy. There was no mercy in him and none in the country. It was the wall. The stag was the wall, and he had put his jaw into the wall and held until the wall broke, and the breaking had cost him more than any meal he had ever taken, and some part of him needed to lie in the snow a while and be the size of what he had done.

He ate when the cold began to bite. He ate the great beast slowly and completely, over days, keeping his own wounds off the ground; heavy slow full years going down into him, warmer and deeper than anything he had ever eaten, the pull working at them for days and nights, straining where it had never strained before and never once letting go.

He crawled into a rock-shelter on the third day and the deep sleep took him there under the first heavy snow, and this sleep was longer than any that had come before. Twice his own blood woke him. Twice he slept again.

When he came out, the winter had set in for good, the white lay deep over the high fern country, and his shoulder was closed to a long pale cord of scar.

He was changed. He could feel it in his feet and in his breath and in the strange new appetite that sat a little higher in him than before, less like a hunger and more like a direction. What the stag had given him was not the crossing of the wall. It was the shape of the crossing: he had fed the thing that had no meal on the oldest and heaviest wildness he had ever taken, and it had grown.

The wall was still there. But he knew now that it was not a wall to be got around. It was a wall to become bigger than.

---

He came down out of the high country two days into the white to find that the men had left him a present at the deep pass.

He read it from a long way off and did not go close until dark. A length of trail at the widest part of the pass, where the green country opened down to the low country, had been changed. There was a line across the trail at the height of his chest: a hard, thin, grey line strung between two cut posts, and below it, laid in the snow, the bait — good meat, untouched and fresh, laid out square in the open as if it were being offered.

He lay in the rocks above the trail for the whole night and watched the thing.

Nothing moved. No men came. No dog came. There was nothing there but meat and the thin line and the two posts, and every part of him knew that they were one thing and not three, and the whole of the thing had been set down by the takers, and the offer was not free.

He understood the meaning of it before he understood the shape. Men left death waiting. They could make their death that has no length sit in one place like a hunter with a line. If he wanted the meat on that trail, he could have the meat: he only had to touch the thing they had built around it, and then the line he could not see would hold him there until the men came.

The pass trail ran under that line. He would need it, in years to come. It lay between his valley and the country east, and the country east had been in his reading since the summer.

He went away from the snare with that fact kept whole. The takers could own a trail without standing on it. They could take the years from a beast that was not yet dead, in a place where they were not.

That night he went up onto the high rock above the ravine, the one place where the whole country lay open to him, and sat with the cold smoking off the ground.

The Black-Ravine Serpent's weight lay in its sleep to the north, huge and quiet, and he read it as a small thing reads a mountain: at a distance, without wanting to. The lights of the lodges had gone out for the season, the men were down-country behind their walls with their fires and their years, and one thin line of death stood waiting in the pass.

He did not cross the wall that winter. The wall would not be crossed that way, and he had known it since the stag. But he had learned what crossing it looked like: you grew until the wall was beneath your notice, as the pine had become beneath his notice, as the stone-runners' cracks had become beneath his notice, as the whole first slope had become too small to hold him.

The cold season was on the country, and the wall was on him, and he sat between them on the high rock with the direction standing in him clear as the stream in the thaw.

It pointed up.

---

The two men came up the pass in the afternoon and set the line while the light held, and the third one came up behind them with the wire over his shoulder.

"Higher," said the one who was kneeling at the post. "At the chest, not the leg. A calf catches and a bull carries the whole frame off with it. At the chest it goes down, and it goes down in the first ten lengths."

"At the chest," said the other. He eyed the trail, then took the wire and re-set the hitch. "Second post goes here."

"Not here. Six lengths down, where the trail narrows."

"Then the line is short."

"A short line that holds is worth a long line that misses," said the kneeling man. "Four lengths of wire in a narrow place will hold a bull. Ten lengths in the open will hold nothing but the wind."

The third man dropped the coil and stood with his hands on his hips, looking at the ground the line would close.

"How many days are we out?" he said.

"Two up, two down, and a night at the camp either way."

"For one line."

"For one line, and the line works for as long as the ground has beasts walking it," said the kneeling man. "Two lengths of this wire came off the old water line when the permits came, and the lodge charges us for wire whether we set it or not. So we set it."

"And the bait?"

"In the open." The kneeling man pointed. "Not under the brush. Under the brush it is a trap, and beasts do not take traps. In the open it is a bargain, and beasts take bargains. And no one comes up here after we leave, so nothing springs it early."

"What did it cost us?" asked the third man.

"Boar off the last kill," said the other. "Better meat than we eat. That is what it takes to close a pass."

The older man stood up and worked his shoulders, and looked down the trail as a man looks down a road he has to walk in the spring.

"The pass closes in another month," he said. "Snow will cover the line and the posts and we lose both. This is the last one we set before the spring."

"We could leave it year-round and check it at the thaw."

"We could," said the kneeling man. "And then a beast springs it in the winter and freezes into it and rots there, and we come back to a dead thing and a sprung line and nothing to sell. No. Winter for the lodges. The line waits for the thaw."

"And the hound stays at the camp with the boy?"

"The hound stays, and the boy stays with the hound, and both of them read the ground around the camp so the boy learns what a print looks like."

"He has been reading prints three years."

"Then he can read the ones you make," said the kneeling man, "and I will teach him the ones that matter. We are wasting the light."

They set the second post and strung the line and laid the bait in the open below it, and the third man coiled the last of the wire and slung it and stood looking north up the pass to where the trail went over into the lower country of the deep range.

"They say the big lights come out of there now," he said. "North and east, past the black ridge."

"Then let them say it," said the kneeling man. "The deep country eats men."

"It eats careless men."

"It eats men," the kneeling man said. "You can go north when you can read the ground well enough to know which men it is eating, and not before."

They gathered their gear and went down the pass, and behind them the line stood strung across the trail in the low sun, thin and grey, with the bait laid in the open beneath it, waiting for whatever the mountain would send down the pass in the spring.

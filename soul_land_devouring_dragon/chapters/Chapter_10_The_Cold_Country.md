# Chapter 10: The Cold Country

The cold came up out of the ground before anything in the air, and for three days he did not believe it.

That was the first thing the deep forest's winter did to him. In the outer country of his first years, the seasons had come from the sky: the sky went gray, the wind turned, then the snow. Here, in his first winter under the giants, the ground itself went cold first, days ahead of the air, and the heavy lives of the deep breaks read it and moved. He came back to his own hunting ground on the third morning of the new cold and found the wallows and the low grounds empty, the heavy beasts gone.

He set his ambush anyway. The outer-country lessons said: the wallow where the big ones drink; low; downwind; patient. He lay in it for three days. On the first day he told himself the beasts were shy. On the second day he was hungry. On the third day he was cold and light-headed, and the ambush was clearly empty, and he climbed out of it knowing one thing more than he had known going in: the old map was worth nothing here.

He went up after them, and the finding was the whole of the lesson. He followed the smell of green water up a long gorge, and the gorge let him out at the top into a shallow bowl of steaming ground. The hot springs: hollows of the country where the water runs hot under the rock and the snow does not lie. The heavy kind stood fat and calm in the steam, wintering among the warm rocks. He stood in the rim of that bowl with the steam going past his shoulders and paid the account of the three days honestly. The deep forest's winter was a new country: its own rivers, its own high country, its own rules. He wrote it in his blood, as he wrote everything, and went down into the hot springs to hunt.

---

The men wintered in the deep forest, and that was the second thing.

He smelled their fire before he saw their smoke. It came to him one gray afternoon on a wind off the far ridge — the sharp, thin smell of the light that eats wood — and his whole body went still, because that smell belonged to a place he had left behind. The lodges. The pass. The outer country. Men did not winter in the deep forest. That was a rule he had carried since his first winter, written on the same page as the cold coming down from the sky.

He went up on the ridge at second light and found them. There was a camp in the breaks across the valley, sheltered by a spur of the giants' rock, and the smoke stood up out of it in a thin line all day. Not a camp that came and went. A winter place. There was a wall of cut brush and piled stone, a frame of poles, the light that eats wood burning under it in the dark, and the ground around it swept and trodden until it had the flat, patient look of ground a man owns.

They were staying. He lay on the far ridge and read the camp as he would read a beast's den. Two men at least. Their spirit hounds. The fire, kept alive all day and all night, which was the thing in all the world that most made him want to go down and tear and eat. And the smoke standing over it day after day, so that even a day's walking away he could turn his head and read them.

The map changed that morning. His country had a line on it that had never been there before: a place where men lived in the winter, deep in the deep forest, on his own breaks, hunting his own heavy kinds.

The race had stopped being a season. It was a country now, and he was sharing it.

---

The cold taught his two tools what they were worth, and it did it in a week.

The voice went first, and the lesson came all in one hunt. A heavy beast had gone to ground in a snowbound break, and he called the low note to buy the falter, and it worked: the beast went still, and he took it, and the kill was good. And then, standing over his own kill in the falling snow, he could not get warm. It was not the ordinary cold. The voice had spent something more than air. It had spent heat, and the heat had gone out of him over the snow with the sound, and he stood shuddering over the meat for most of a day, and it took the whole of the hot meat to put the warmth back in him.

He put the voice away after that, like a tool that costs too much to use. Through the whole middle of the winter he called it twice — once at a hunt he could not take without it, once at a beast that stood between him and his hollow in the dark. He paid the price both times, willingly. And the discipline changed how he hunted: he got used to winning without it.

The quiet broke differently. Fear had never been able to break the quiet — he had carried it through the reaching of the hounds and the weight of the ravine, and it had held. Hunger broke it inside a week.

The lean stretch came in the third month, when the hot springs froze at their edges and the heavy kind moved into country he did not know. Six days of hunting that fed him once. And through those six days he could not hold the quiet at all. It was the hunger that spoke through him. Fear had been sharp and thin, and it had made the quiet easy — fear narrowed him, and there was less of him to hide. Hungry, his whole body was loud: the pull stood open in him, the sense reached out of him whether he asked it to or not, and every living thing in the country could read the shout coming off him.

The quiet is fed on attention. He learned that the hard way, on ground where nothing could be done about it. When his whole attention was bent on the next meal, there was nothing left over to hold the quiet with. He lost four kills to it. And by the cold month's end he had taught himself the trick, badly, in fits, as a beast holds anything new: hold the shout down, and let the belly be angry about it. The quiet is fed on attention, and attention is the only coin he has. He spent the rest of the winter paying it out, and it bought him back his hunting.

---

The hunger was the winter's last and hardest teacher, and it nearly beat him once.

He had carried the red thing in two shapes since the first summer. The boil was the old rage of his kind, the rampage that comes up out of fullness. The hunger was quieter than the boil, and worse: it waited. It stood behind his eyes in the long gray hours and pointed at the heavy lives of the country and whispered the hunt before the hunt had begun.

The grey stag was the nearest loss. It was a beast of the second water, up in the high hot springs, heavy with years — nearly the size of the old stag of the fern country, standing in his sense like a banked fire. He marked it as he marked everything: its hours, its water, its ground, its habits. He watched it for a week.

On the eighth morning, going up the long gorge to the place where he meant to take it, the hunger spent the hunt before it began. He could not say afterward where the control went. Somewhere in the climb he stopped hunting and started wanting. He hurried. He cut ground he should not have cut. He came up over the rim of the warm break with the quiet broken and the shout of himself going out ahead of him for half a morning — and the stag was gone. Not frightened. It had simply walked. He found its tracks going out the far side of the break, unhurried and steady, in the snow. It had read him coming from the other side of the morning.

Nine days later, in the lower breaks, he proved the other face of the law — and this time the loss went with him.

He was stalking a beast of the middle years, and he carried the hunger through the whole approach like a coal in his chest. At the rim of the break, the morning of the grey stag came back to him whole: the empty warm ground, the unhurried tracks going out through the snow, the day and a half of nothing. He stood one breath in that memory, on purpose. Then he came down the inside of it the other way, and the coal did what coals do — it lit the feel, it steadied the stalk, it put the last length on the end of the spring when the moment came. He took the beast clean.

He ate it in the warm break with the steam going up around him, and the fact sat in him as solid as any rock of his first slope. The hunger is not the enemy. The hunger is the fuel. What rides it is the choosing. It was the same law as the boil, in its colder and more patient form, and he carried both faces of it from that day.

---

In the deep middle of that winter, the country gave him the last layer of its map.

It came on the cold, clear nights, when the air stood still over the break and the whole sky was a lid of stars. His sense of years, sharper in the cold than it had ever been, reached farther than it had ever reached — out past the giants' country, past the far edge of the deep forest, up into the north he could not read — and on those nights, the absences gave up their shapes.

They were standing lights. That was the thing his sense finally told him. The depths he could not read were not places. They were lives — fixed in their own grounds as mountains are fixed, each one standing in the country around it like a pole in a tent, and everything in the country standing in relation to them. The ravine's weight away south was one. The unreadable north held others.

The cold country's walking had become a walking between them. He could feel the old standing lights when he moved through the high hot springs. He could feel where their grounds began, and where a careful beast went around. And deeper in his blood than all of it, thin and clear, carried farther on the cold air than it had ever carried before, the pull sang north toward the ravine.

He lay still a long time after the sense came back, with the whole cold country standing in him, and did not move until the stars had moved.

The last dividend was the quietest one, and he noticed it before any of the others: the cold had less hold on him than it should have had. His outer-country winters had gone into his bones; he had carried the cold out of the last two as a beast carries a debt. This winter the cold sat on him lightly. His blood kept its own fire now — deep meals and deep sleeps had paid for it, season after season — and the snow country that had nearly killed the hatchling was, to the beast he had become, merely the season. He noticed it, as he noticed everything, and kept it. He felt no pride. Nothing in his life had taught him the shape of pride.

---

And then the season turned, and the first line of the war was written in other hands.

He was up in the high hot springs on the day the men took the grey stag. He never saw it happen. He came down the long gorge two days after, and the stag's ground was a husk: the great body lying opened in the snow at the second water, the meat left for the ravens, and beside the beast's head, pressed deep into the moss, the two small prints where a man had knelt.

There was no light. He read the place as he read everything, and the place told him plainly what had happened in it. The light had risen when the beast went down — which takes no time at all — and a man had knelt in the moss and drawn it away. The smell of the drawing was still there, thin and sharp, gone into the moss and the snow. And on his sense lay the ghost of the fire it had been, the same deep colour he had stood over once in the summer — the dark ring turning slowly on the moss beside a dead beast, and him not touching it.

They had come up the gorge, taken the light he had marked, and gone down again. They had not even been on the ground when he walked it.

He had marked that stag. It had been on his list, with its hours and its water and its ground, and the men had walked into the deep forest in the dead of winter and taken it without ever knowing he had wanted it. He looked at the place a long time. Then he did what he did with everything. He wrote it down.

His list, from that day, was not a list of beasts any more. It was a list of what the country had promised him and what had been taken out from under the promise: the bristle-back, the deer, the stag of the fern country, the great stag of the high fern, the deep beasts. And now the grey stag of the second water — marked, watched, and taken by other hands before he ever walked its ground.

Their list was on paper, in a winter place in the deep forest, written in a hand he would never see. His was in his blood. Both of them were written over the same beasts, in the same breaks, in the same hours of the same cold seasons.

He turned north out of the stag's ground and went up over the rocks, and behind him in the moss the two prints of a kneeling man filled slowly with snow.

That was the war. Not a fight — he had never in his life seen a man close enough to fight. The war was two lists over one country, and the country would decide what each of them got. The next move would be made in the thaw.

---

The two men in the camp were splitting wood on the third morning, and their voices came up to the far ridge clean on the cold air. The boy worked the water and moved the hounds out of the wind.

"If we work the second water again we should work it north," said the kneeling man. He had a wedge and a mallet, and he was taking the split wood and stacking it tight against the brush wall. "That water has been hunted two winters. The heavy kinds are learning the south end."

"They learn," said the other from behind the poles. He was the older of the two, old enough that the winter in this camp had been his plan and not his luck, and he came out from under the frame with his hands wet from the water-bucket. "That is why we are still alive. The ones who do not learn the country starve in it. The beasts stay because the warm water is here. We stay because they stay."

"And the line?"

"The line goes in at the thaw. Second water, north end, both banks. And this year we do not pull it."

"Not pull it? The permit says the line comes out at the season's end."

"The permit says the lodge writes down what the line takes," said the old man. He threw the last of the water down the bank and set the bucket by the fire. "What the lodge knows is the lodge's business. Do you know what a light of the deep colour is worth in the low country? It buys a house. A house, a wife, and a winter with no line in it. A beast of that kind has been read at the second water twice in three winters, and I do not mean to stand in the thaw and watch some other man's line take it out from under me."

"You have been saying that for two winters."

"And for two winters the thing has not walked into a line," said the old man. "That water is deep and wide, and the beast that drinks at it does not come every night. Fine. This year we do not wait for the night it comes. We close the whole north end and we sit on it for the snow."

"With what? Two hounds and the two of us."

"We have the boy," said the old man. "The boy can hold a hound. And at the thaw the lodge sends two more men up if the camp is worth working, and the camp is worth working because I say it is."

"Then why not go where the big lights are?" said the kneeling man. "North, past the black ridge, where the deep forest starts."

"Because the men who went north did not come back," said the old man, "and their wire is still hanging up there in the trees."

"That is a story."

"That is a ledger," said the old man. "The lodge wrote them down as gone, and the lodge pays for wire whether we set it or not, and the wire up there was paid for and never used. I have read the page. Have you?"

The kneeling man said nothing and went back to the wood. The boy came up from the water with the bucket and stopped on the flat stone by the fire out of habit, and the old man looked at him as a man looks at a tool he has not decided about.

"What does the box hold?" said the boy.

"One light," said the old man. "A thin one. You carried it up yourself, so you know as well as I do."

"Then why is it up here and not at the lodge?"

"Because the box goes down when I go down," said the old man, "and I go down when the line is set and the list is made. That is the whole reason we live up here in the snow with the wind."

"There is nothing up here," said the boy.

"There is worse than nothing," said the old man. "Work."

He went back under the frame. The wood split, and the smoke stood straight up out of the camp all day, and the dragon lay on the far ridge with the cold air going past his crown, and read every word he could not understand, and went home in the dark.

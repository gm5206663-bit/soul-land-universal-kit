# Chapter 2: Super Nathan

**[canon: S1 slot 2 — The Evillustrator (production N° 102)]**

The apartment above the bakery street was, on the twelfth morning in the city, one and a half rooms. The bedroom was a room. The living room was a room. The kitchen was a decision his mother was still making, out of a box labeled KITCHEN in four colors of marker, because his mother labeled everything in four colors, and had decided, in the four colors, that the boxes were the kitchen for another three days.

Kamé was sitting on the rim of a cereal bowl.

"I have not eaten since the carousel," Kamé said. "I am now sitting on my dignity. I am telling you this so you understand the severity of the situation."

"If you tell my mother about the cuff, you eat plain," Keal said.

"About the cuff I already told her about the carousel. She cried. It was very effective."

Keal opened the box labeled with his name, in four colors of marker as well, because it was his mother's kitchen and her rules, and under a jumper and a pair of socks there was a bar of candy in a wrapper the color of the sea, and he put it in his pocket the way you do a thing that has to be done quietly, and Kamé, who had been keeping a count, kept a count of that too.

On his wrist the cuff was matte grey. Nothing. For the first time since the storm it was only a bracelet, and he wore it the way he had worn it for seven years, as a thing that did not exist. That was the trick of the band, he had come to understand: it had been hiding in plain sight for seven years, and plain sight, it turned out, was a very good place to hide.

He was cold. He was always cold. His sleeves were down to his knuckles, and he had the notebook folded in his jacket — the city, a little more finished than it had been the day before, the river a little straighter, the bridge still bent the wrong way.

The body was already ready when the morning found him, the mind three moves ahead of the day, the voice tuned to the frequency that made people believe him before they decided to. That was his nature, the part of him that worked on every single part of him, and it had not gone anywhere. But it was no longer the whole of him. On top of it there was now something else, something with a name and a pocket and a hunger for candy, and the two of them had not yet agreed on the terms of the house.

He showed eighty percent of the kid. He had been showing eighty percent his whole life.

Today was the first full day at school since the storm, and Paris had opinions about the storm.

The hallway at Collège Françoise Dupont at eight forty was a newsroom. Every phone was a television. The storm vlog was playing on six of them at once, at six different moments, and the park's morning had become the city's morning — the ice, the mask, the toddler, the roof, and at four minutes and twelve seconds the place where the eye was sure it had seen something, which was exactly the place the eye could not find.

Alya had her phone out like a shield, which was her natural posture, and she was running the clip for a cluster of four that had formed around it without deciding to.

"You're in the frame for forty seconds," she said, to the cluster, to the hallway, to anyone. "Forty seconds, and then the roof. Everyone watch the roof."

Keal stood with his bag in one hand and watched the hallway watch the phone, and his face did nothing, which was to say a great deal. He was the new kid. The new kid got forty seconds of screen time and one comment section, and the hallway had already started the sentence.

"Is that the new kid from Dupont? I've seen him at the school, he's been there twelve days and he's already on the school feed three times, that's not normal —"

"— and the thing on the roof, same build, I'm going crazy, 4:12 —"

"Shut up, the answer is the loud girl with the phone, loud people are heroes, that's how it works —"

"Twelve days," Keal said. "I've heard every version of the story. Not one of them are true."

The cluster turned, because the cluster turned when the new kid spoke, because the new kid's voice did a thing to sentences: it made them end where they should end.

"It's the new kid. Is he the green one?" said the tall girl with the blonde braid, who had not been shy about any part of the storm and intended to be no shyer about this one. "Honestly? He's nice. He's not a hero."

"He's not a hero," someone agreed.

"Nice," said someone else, with the specific disappointment of a person who has decided that nice is not enough.

Keal let the sentence land the way it landed, and turned, and walked to science.

The science classroom smelled of chalk and cut plastic, and had a seating plan that changed with the school's moods. Keal's seat was by the window, third row — it had become his in nine days, the way the bench had, and the way things in this city became his a little faster than other people's things became other people's. Juleka was two rows ahead, drawing her own quiet things in the margin of her notebook. Nathaniel Kurtzberg was two seats down, drawing in the margin of nothing — the comic, the superhero, the girl with the red hair and the bag full of half-finished designs, the two villains made of ice and pauses, and the new hero with the tablet, Super Nathan, who dropped into the scene and erased the umbrella and saved the day. In the last panel, the one that had not been drawn yet, the two of them were going to say the thing.

Olga Mendeleiev taught particle physics the way other people taught arithmetic: with a ruler, a list, and the calm of a person who had already decided what everyone in the room was.

She had decided what Nathaniel Kurtzberg was at the precise second his stylus touched the paper.

"Nathaniel."

The stylus stopped. The room's attention, which had been asleep, opened one eye.

"I will not ask you twice. The office."

Nathaniel got up, and his chair went with him, and he tripped over Mylène's backpack — the backpack was on the floor because backpacks are on the floor, and Mylène had not yet decided where it was going — and his bag opened its mouth in the middle of the aisle and gave back everything it had: the notebook, the stylus, the comic.

The comic hit the floor open.

The last page was on top. The page with the exclamation mark in the corner, his signature, the mark he put on everything, the same mark he had been putting on the margins of Dupont's walls for a year. And Chloé Bourgeois, who was standing at the head of the aisle with the gravity of a person who owned the room, picked it up.

She read it the way she read everything: out loud, for the room.

"And the new hero, Super Nathan, saved the girl with the red hair, and she said, I knew you were there all along."

The room was quiet the way a room is quiet when it is deciding whether to laugh.

"It's about Marinette," Chloé said, to Sabrina, who was standing behind her, and to the room, which was standing all around her. "About Marinette and her drawings. Some of us do real design. Some of us do —"

"— doodling in science," said the room, in four voices, because the room had decided.

"Office," said Olga Mendeleiev, picking Nathaniel up off the floor by the elbow the way you pick up a cat, gently and with no apology.

Nathaniel walked to Chloé. He held out his hand. The comic was in Chloé's hand, and he took it back the way you take back a thing that was never yours to lose, and then Chloé's thumb found the last page, and the last page came off the book, and the exclamation mark went onto the floor, and the room's four voices, which had been deciding, stopped deciding, because something in the room's temperature had gone down by one degree, and Keal, in the third row by the window, felt it the way he felt weather: a hairline, a tension, a wrong that was already walking toward the door.

He got up, and walked to the aisle, and stopped a step behind the boy who was holding a book with a torn spine.

"A last page isn't the whole book," Keal said.

Nathaniel stopped. The hallway was behind him, the office was in front of him, and the new kid was standing in the middle of it with the calm of a person who had just been in the ice and come out of it, and for one second — one second, the exact second the air in the classroom did something it had no right to do — Nathaniel looked at him.

Then he walked to the office, and the door closed, and the hairline in the air stayed.

Somewhere in Paris, the voice found the science classroom the way it had found the park: the way winter finds a window.

It had watched the storm footage twice. The face in the ice — clear, new, the kind of face the city was only just starting to place. The blur on the roof at four minutes and twelve seconds — a shape, a line that held, a color that moved like it was thinking. The city was collecting the pieces like a child collecting stamps, and the city did not know which pieces it was holding.

*This one is a boy who draws his own hero,* the voice said, to the black butterfly, which was leaving for the hallway. *The city has been drawing its own these days. A green blur on a rooftop. A new face in every crowd. I saw both in the footage.*

*The deal is the deal — the Miraculouses. And I will be watching the rest of you.*

The butterfly left for the floor of the aisle, where the torn page was lying, and where a stylus was lying next to it, and the grief in the page was still fresh.

At the far end of the hallway, in the light that came through the office window, Nathaniel Kurtzberg held the stylus up to look at it.

The black butterfly was on it.

*You drew a hero,* the voice said, gentle the way the cold is gentle, the way a door is gentle when it closes. *A hero who saves the girl, who erases the thing that hurts him, who has a name the city will learn. Be the best artist the world has ever known. All I ask in return is the Ladybug Miraculous and the Cat Noir Miraculous.*

Nathaniel looked at the stylus. The stylus looked back the way a thing looks back when it has just become something.

"Deal," he said.

The transformation that followed was the kind of thing a comic book would not believe: the drawing of a boy became the boy the drawing had always been — the one on the last page, the one in the panel that had not been drawn yet — in a coat that was a sketch come loose from the page, in a face that was an ink line with a pulse in it. The Evillustrator stood in the hallway with a tablet in one hand and a stylus in the other, and the hallway, which had been holding its breath, let it go, and the wall he was standing in front of was, for one second, a wall that had been drawn: the paint a little too flat, the molding a little too perfect, and then it was a wall again.

He went out the window.

Keal, in the science classroom, heard the window.

The part of him that had always known which chair in the room was going to creak did not creak. It listened: glass, wind, a sound like paper moving across the city. He looked at the window. The window was closed.

He looked at the torn page, which was still on the floor of the aisle, and at the exclamation mark in its corner, and he understood it before he understood it: someone in this school had just become a thing that drew itself real.

He said nothing. He sat down. He opened his notebook.

Keal's group was at a table on the library's second floor — the good table, the one by the window, the one the particle physics presentations were going to be built at, four chairs, and Nino Lahiffe, who was already in one of them, looked at the new kid the way he looked at everything: deciding, in real time, what you were.

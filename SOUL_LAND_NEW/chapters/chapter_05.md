## Part Three — The Week of Fires

The 考核 was not one day. It was a week — a grinding, glorious, terrifying week of brackets and seeds and rooms that fell around them like trees in a storm — and through all of it, the fire at the wall burned.

言天宇 did not miss a night.

It became the strangest rhythm of his life: by day, the arena, where he stood with the room and watched the 班长's team cut through the bracket like a blade through paper; by night, the quiet wall, where he fed whoever came — the room's champions on their rare free evenings, laughing and recounting the day's fights; the room's survivors, quiet and grateful; the room's frightened, who came because the fire was warm and the food was real and the golden grandson had never once made them feel like charity.

He learned, that week, that feeding a room was not the same as saving it.

The boy with the sprain — the one 周漪 had told to run on or leave, the one who had come to the fire those first nights with his jaw set and his pride in tatters — did not come on the fourth night.

言天宇 found him at the gate.

The boy was standing at the edge of the light with his bundle in his hands and his face a careful blank, and he did not need to say it. 言天宇 read it in the set of his shoulders, the way he read everything: 周漪's last cull had taken him. The gate had closed. The term had ended for him a week early, and the fire at the wall had not been enough.

"I'm sorry," the boy said, before 言天宇 could speak. His voice was rough, and he was not looking at him. "I know you— I know you spent your allowance on me. The fish, the— I know what it cost. I couldn't—" He stopped. Swallowed. "The doctor said the tendon needs another month. The academy doesn't wait a month."

言天宇 stood very still in the dusk, with the weight of the boy's words settling into him like a stone, and felt the shape of a lesson he had been circling all term finally close around him: he could feed the hungry. He could warm the cold. He could teach a boy to bank a fire and make one fish feed two mouths. And he could not, with all his golden strength and all his careful kindness, mend a torn tendon in a month, or move the gate that had been closing on this boy since the day he was born.

His light could not heal. He had known that, in the abstract, the way you know a thing you have never had to test. Standing at the gate in the dusk, watching a boy he had fed for weeks walk out of his life because of a leg, he learned it for real.

"Come here," he said.

The boy came, wary.

言天宇 reached into his sleeve and brought out the last of the week's allowance — not much, a few coins, the price of three crooked fish — and pressed it into the boy's hand. "It's not a cure," he said. "It's a fire for the road. The inn past the north gate is honest, and the kitchen there takes on boys with quick hands. Tell the cook 小宇 sent you." He smiled, and it was real, and it cost him everything he had. "You cook a better fish than I do. You'll be fine."

The boy looked at the coins. At the firelight behind them. At the golden grandson of 史莱克, standing at the edge of the dark with his hands empty and his chest full of the thing he could not give.

"...I'll pay you back," the boy said, very quietly. "Someday. I don't know how, but I'll pay you back."

"You already have," 言天宇 said. "Every night you came to the fire, you paid me. You just didn't know it."

He stood at the gate until the boy was a shape in the dark, and then he walked back to the wall — where the fire was burning low and 温宁 was waiting with her arms wrapped around her knees and her face a careful blank that he recognized because it was his own.

"Was it 陆二?" she asked.

"His name was 小陆," 言天宇 said. "He had a name. I should have known it sooner."

"I know."

"So why do you keep doing it?"

言天宇 looked at the fire — at the coals he had banked with his own hands, at the fish he had learned to cook from a girl who had nothing, at the empty place where a boy with a sprain had sat for three weeks and would never sit again.

"Because the fire's not for the ones who can be saved," he said. "It's for the ones who come back. 小陆 came back, every night, for three weeks. That meant something. It meant *he* meant something." 言天宇 looked at the girl who had taught him to cook on coals, who had asked him once, weeks ago, why he helped a boy who resented him.

"I'd like that," he said.

They sat by the fire in silence until the coals were low, and somewhere beyond the wall the 考核 went on without them — the 班长's team winning, the bracket narrowing, the room's three walking deeper into the week of fires — and 言天宇 thought about the boy at the gate, and the lesson he had paid for with three crooked fish and a whole week of wanting.

*The gate eats the ones who can't afford to lose,* he had told 霍雨浩, once, by a different fire.

He had not understood, then, that the gate also ate the ones who could not *win* in time.

*Tomorrow,* he thought, banking the coals with a careful hand, *I'll go back to the arena. I'll cheer for the room. And tomorrow night, I'll light the fire again.*

*Because that's what you do. You light the fire again.*

## Part Four — The Banner

"Can they win?" 温宁 whispered, when the silence had stretched so long that the room had stopped pretending to talk.

言天宇 looked at the great closed doors, behind which his room's banner was being decided by two teams of children with everything to prove.

"I don't know," he said. "But I know the 班长. And I know that if there's a version of that arena where the 班长 is standing at the end of it, he'll find it." He looked at her. "He's the one who couldn't afford to stop. That kind of boy doesn't stop at closed doors."

The doors opened.

It happened without ceremony — no fanfare, no herald, just the great doors swinging wide into the grey light — and the room surged forward and stopped all at once, because standing in the doorway, swaying on his feet, with a gash on his cheek and his threadbare robe half-torn and his eyes fixed and empty and full at once, was the 班长 of 新生一班.

Behind him stood 王冬 — golden hair wild, lip split, but *grinning*, blazing, utterly triumphant — and at 王冬's shoulder, 萧萧, leaning on her 鼎 with her twin braids coming loose and her small face lit up like a lantern.

The room did not cheer. For one long, strange moment, the room was silent — because the room knew what it was seeing, and the room did not have a sound big enough for it.

Then 霍雨浩 raised his hand — the hand that had carried them all — and made a small, tired gesture at the doors behind him, and said, in a voice that cracked on the last word:

"We won."

The room detonated.

言天宇 did not know he was crying until 温宁's hand found his and squeezed, and he realized his face was wet, and he did not care, did not care at all, because his room's three had walked through a sealed door against a hundred-year name and come out the other side with the banner in their hands.

The celebration that night was at the wall.

It happened without anyone deciding it — the way the best things in that term had happened, the way the fire itself had happened. Someone carried word. Someone brought 唐雅, who arrived with a sack of the 班长's own fish and a grin that could have lit the whole market, and 贝贝 behind her, quiet and proud. Someone brought the room — the survivors, the quiet ones, the ones who had eaten at the fire all week and the ones who had never needed to. And someone brought the three, washed and bandaged and glowing with the particular light of children who had done the impossible and were just beginning to understand it.

王冬 held court by the fire, recounting the final with increasingly heroic embellishments, until 霍雨浩 said, "You threw up before the second round," and 王冬 said, "That was a *tactical* vomit, it confused them," and the whole fire dissolved.

萧萧 sat at the edge of the light with her 鼎 quiet at her side and her braids being redone by a small girl from the fire, and she watched the two boys argue with a small, private smile that 言天宇 caught and returned — the smile of two people who watched rooms, who saw the same things, who had never quite said so.

And 霍雨浩 — the 班长, the captain, the boy who had carried them all — sat down on the ground beside 言天宇 with a fish in his hand and looked at the fire for a long moment before he spoke.

"You weren't there," he said. It was not an accusation. It was a question wearing the wrong clothes.

"The final was sealed," 言天宇 said. "No students."

"I know. That's not what I meant." 霍雨浩 turned the fish over the coals. "You weren't in the arena all week. Not as a team. Everyone else in the room — they were either on a team or they were watching from the benches. You were the only one who was just… there. With the room. Feeding them." He looked at him. "Why didn't you fight?"

言天宇 considered the question the way he considered most questions about himself — from the outside, as if the boy being asked were someone he was trying to understand.

"It isn't that I couldn't fight," 言天宇 said at last, "or that the room wouldn't have me. It's that the arena isn't my ladder this year. I'm wall-capped — I can't gain a level until my grandteacher opens my hunt. The 考核 is for the room's climb, and the climb isn't mine this year." He smiled, and it was almost true. "And even if it were… I'd rather stand with the room than carry it. You three carried it fine without me. Better than fine."

霍雨浩 was silent for a long moment.

"That's the most infuriating answer I've ever heard," he said finally, in exactly the tone he had used when 言天宇 had told him, by a different fire, that the dean's grandson had never once in his life been spent.

"I'm aware."

"No, listen." 霍雨浩 turned to face him, and his dark eyes were very serious. "The wall may be real. But a boy who'd truly made his peace with it doesn't watch a race the way you watched it all week. You were watching like you wanted to be in it — in *with* them, not above them. And since the climb wasn't yours this year, you found the one way into it that nothing could ever close." He looked at the fire. "You made a room, 言天宇. Out of nothing. Out of crooked fish and an old woman's charity and the children nobody else would feed. That's not a consolation prize. That's a thing I don't know if I could have done."

言天宇 was very still.

"You carried this room across a line," he said quietly. "The whole intake watched you do it. I just fed the ones who couldn't run."

"Yeah," 霍雨浩 said. "You did. And they'll remember that longer than anyone remembers the bracket." He smiled — the real smile, the one that cracked the careful mask. "You asked me, once, why I kept coming back to sit with the 班长 who had nothing. I didn't know how to answer then." He looked around the fire — at 王冬 holding court, at 萧萧's small smile, at 唐雅 laughing with 贝贝, at the room's survivors eating and talking and being, for one night, not afraid. "I know how to answer now. Because you're the only person I've ever met who makes rooms. Not wins them. *Makes* them. And rooms are the only thing that outlast the brackets."

The fire crackled between them.

言天宇 sat very still, with the warmth of the coals on his face and the words of a boy who had just beaten a hundred-year name settling into him like water into dry ground, and felt something enormous and quiet settle in his chest.

He had spent the term learning what a room was for. And it had taken a boy who had just won the impossible to show him what he had been doing all along without quite seeing it: the thing he could not climb this year was never going to be the thing he was. He had not been failing to compete. He had been building the thing that held the competitors when the arena was empty — and rooms were the only thing that outlasted brackets.

"王冬 told me you called him honest," 霍雨浩 said, after a while. "At the forms. He was insufferable about it for a whole day, which means it mattered." He looked at him. "I think you see people. That's your thing — not the rings, not the rank, not the golden grandson business. You see what people are actually made of, and you feed it." He stood, brushing ash from his robes. "Don't let the arena tell you that's worth less than winning. It's the only thing that's worth more."

He walked to the edge of the firelight, where 王冬 was calling for him, and paused.

"Same time tomorrow?" he said, without turning. "The stall's at the north gate. 唐雅's been saying I need a partner who actually knows how to talk to people." A pause. "I told her I knew one."

言天宇 watched the 班长 walk away into the light of the fire — the boy who had nothing, who had just given him, across a whole term of fires, the only thing he had ever truly wanted: a place where he was needed for himself — and felt the small weight that had sat in his chest since the roll call finally begin to lift.

*The room,* he thought, watching the fire that was not his and yet was, the room that was not his and yet was, the family that kept growing at the edge of the light. *So this is what it's for.*

*This is what the room is for.*

Behind him, 周漪's voice cut through the celebration like a blade through silk.

"言天宇."

He turned. The teacher stood at the edge of the firelight, her arms crossed, her hard face unreadable in the flickering dark. She had not been there a moment ago — or she had, and he had missed her, which meant she had wanted him to miss her until she was ready to be seen.

"Teacher."

周漪 looked at the fire. At the children. At the three champions at the heart of it, and the quiet ones at the edges, and the fish and the coals and the crooked, improvised, entirely unauthorized hearth that a rank-thirty eleven-year-old had built at the wall of her class.

"You've been running an unsanctioned kitchen on academy grounds for a month," she said.

言天宇's heart sank. "Teacher, I—"

"It's the best thing anyone in this class has done all term," 周漪 said, and her voice did not change, and the words landed like stones. "Better than the bracket. Better than the banner. Every one of those children will remember the year the golden grandson fed them at the wall and never once looked down at them — longer than anyone remembers the bracket." She looked at him, and in the firelight her hard face was, for just a moment, almost soft. "The ladder holds the climbers. The ground holds the ladder. You couldn't climb this year, so you became the ground — and you were too busy feeding the runners to notice that was the commanding, not the climbing."

She turned to go.

"Teacher," he said, before he could stop himself. "The year. I was never on the ladder for any of it. Was I—" He stopped, unable to put words to the question he had never once asked aloud: *was I part of the room, or only standing beside it?*

周漪 paused. She did not turn.

"You weren't on the ladder," she said, "so you became the thing it stood on. Champions are crowned on the sand, boy — but rooms are held together at the fire. You held this one." A pause. "You survived my term, and you survived it *useful*. That was never in question."

She walked into the dark, and was gone.

言天宇 stood at the edge of the fire with his room around him — his champions and his survivors and his quiet ones, his crooked fish and his banked coals and his whole unsanctioned, impossible, beloved hearth — and felt something settle in him that he did not have a name for yet, a warmth that had nothing to do with the fire and everything to do with the place he had made.

Above him, the stars turned over the sleeping academy. Somewhere beyond the wall, the great arena stood empty and already forgetting the week of fires it had held. And at the wall, in the light of a fire a boy had built himself, the room that could never have put him on its banner held him anyway — the golden grandson of 史莱克, who was waiting on his door, who had learned to feed, who had made a room out of nothing, and who had finally, at the end of the term that was supposed to end most of them, found the thing the bracket could never give him.

He was not the banner.

He was the fire the banner came home to.

And that, he thought, watching the room he had made laugh and eat and be alive in the firelight, was worth more than any crown a freshmen bracket could have set on his head.

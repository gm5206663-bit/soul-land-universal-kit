## Part One — The Teacher Who Refused to Be Impressed

The classroom of 新生一班 smelled of chalk dust and fear, and that was before the teacher walked in.

言天宇 arrived early — early enough to watch the room fill. He had chosen a seat in the middle, not the back and not the front, the way you choose the middle of a bridge if you want to see both banks without falling off either. The other children trickled in: the loud boy from registration who had claimed his uncle was a 魂王; a girl with quick, nervous eyes who kept smoothing her uniform; a cluster of boys who already knew each other and were loudly not impressed by anyone.

He had carried his few things across the yard before dawn, while the rest of the academy still slept — his grandfather's condition, stated flatly at the breakfast where all of this had begun: a student of 史莱克 slept where students slept, ate where they ate, and learned the world from inside it. So the corner room of the dean's house was empty now, and 言天宇 woke in the outer-court dormitory, in a narrow bed off a long corridor of boys who did not know his grandfather's name. The pillow was strange and the nights were loud, and his grandmother had made him promise, three times, to eat properly. He kept the promise. It was the closest thing to home he carried.

And then the doorway darkened, and 周漪 walked in, and every conversation in the room died at once.

She was not tall. She was not large. She was, by the look of her, a woman who had long ago stopped needing to be either. Her uniform was immaculate, her hair was pulled back so tightly it looked painful, and her eyes swept the room with the particular efficiency of a person counting livestock. When she reached the front and turned, the chalk in her hand had already been broken in two — 言天宇 noticed the small thing, filed it, and decided the teacher was either very angry or very controlled. They were, he would learn, the same thing in her.

"新生一班," she said. Her voice was flat, carrying, and utterly without warmth. "I am 周漪. For the next three months, I am the only thing standing between you and the gate that throws you back out into the world you crawled here from."

She set the broken chalk down.

"You are not special. You are not chosen. You are a roomful of children who paid or begged or connived your way through one gate, and the gate behind me — the one that makes you a real student of this academy — is not open yet. Most of you will not make it." Her eyes moved over the room like a blade over a whetstone. "I am not here to be liked. I am here to find out which of you can be forged, and which of you are only decoration. If you want to cry, cry now and save me the time."

The room was silent.

言天宇 sat very still in his middle seat, and felt, for the first time in his life, something he could not remember ever feeling in a classroom: *ordinary.* The dean's grandson meant nothing here. 穆恩's student meant nothing here. Rank thirty with two purple rings meant — well, it would mean something, he suspected, the moment anyone looked, but 周漪 had not looked. 周漪 was looking at them the way a blacksmith looks at a pile of ore: without respect, without interest, only for what could be made.

It was, he realized with a strange little jolt, a little like being looked at by his grandfather.

A late arrival slipped through the door.

The boy was beautiful in a way that made heads turn — golden-tinged hair, features so fine they could have been painted, and a proud, almost disdainful set to his shoulders that said *I am better than this room and I know it.* He walked past 周漪 without looking at her, scanning the seats with the particular arrogance of someone who had never once had to earn a thing.

He was 王冬. 言天宇 knew him by reputation — the outer-court whisper was that he had appeared from nowhere: no family on any file, no history anyone could trace, and a soul so bright it made the examiners blink. And right behind him, quieter than a shadow, came 霍雨浩.

The thin boy from the market. The boy with the fish and the cold that was not evil.

霍雨浩 met 言天宇's eyes for half a second across the room — and 言天宇 raised his hand an inch, a small wave, a *save me a seat* from the night before turned real. 霍雨浩's mouth did not move, but something in his shoulders eased, a fraction, and he sat down two rows ahead and one seat over, where he could see the door and the window and the teacher and, 言天宇 noticed, him.

The boy had been raised to watch every exit. 言天宇 filed that too, and felt the cold at his spine settle into something almost like recognition.

周漪's voice cut through the room like a blade.

"王冬. You're late."

The beautiful boy turned, and for a moment his chin came up — and then, surprisingly, it came down again. "I'm sorry, Teacher. I—"

"The dormitories are three hundred meters from this building." 周漪's voice was flat. "You had time. You chose not to use it. That is the first and last time you will be late in my class, because the next time, you will not be in my class." She turned to the board, dismissing him. "Sit."

王冬 sat. He did not argue. 言天宇 watched the small surrender with interest — the proud boy had folded in a second, which meant either he was not as proud as he looked, or 周漪 was exactly as dangerous as she sounded. Probably both.

The morning was drills. 武魂 control, soul-power circulation, the same foundations 言天宇 had been doing since he could stand — but 周漪 taught them like a war, with counting, with deadlines, with the flat threat of the gate hanging over every mistake. He did the exercises perfectly, automatically, the way you breathe, and tried not to notice the stares that started to gather on him like dust.

By midday, the whispers had a shape. *The dean's grandson. Rank thirty. Two purple rings. Do you think he's—*

He could feel the room dividing around him the way water divides around a stone. Some of the children looked at him with the particular hunger of people who wanted to be near power. A few looked at him with the particular hatred of people who resented it. Most just looked, and looked away, and made him feel, for the first time in his life, the weight of being seen.

He had spent eleven years inside the dean's house, where everyone knew his name and no one looked at him at all.

This was different. This was being looked at by people who did not know him and would never see the part of him that mattered.

At the end of the morning, 周漪 set down her chalk and looked at the room.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we run."

## Part Two — The Hundred Laps

### Panel: 周漪 — canon ep 4 (her own conduct: the hundred laps before the gate, the weakest nearly lost, the 特邀生 revealed). Butterfly T05/T02: for the first time her class holds a student her usual tests cannot reach — and she has decided what a teacher does with a furnace.

The track ringed the outer training yard like a brown scar, and the morning sun was already mean by the time 周漪 lined them up.

She had made the hundred-lap call the night before, alone in the empty classroom, with the roll spread under her hand and the chalk dust still settling. It was not cruelty. It was arithmetic. Every term the gate behind her culled the ones who could not be forged, and every term she did the culling herself, early, before the academy's softer hands could teach the weak to hope. The hundred laps were not a punishment. They were a price, and she had been pricing children for twenty years without once apologizing for it.

Three names had sat on the roll that night like stones in a riverbed.

The first was 霍雨浩 — rank eleven, the lowest she had ever enrolled, a boy with nothing but a 唐门 recommendation and a look in his eyes that she recognized because she had worn it herself at his age: the look of someone who had decided, long ago, that stopping was the one thing he could not afford. She had nearly refused him at the door. She had not, and she had spent the day waiting to see which of them was right.

The second was 王冬 — the beautiful one, the crowned one, the child every other teacher would have spoiled. Spoiling was not his sin. His flaw was a quiet curiosity: 王冬 wanted to see what a boy who had never once been asked to prove anything did on the day he was finally asked.

And the third name was 言天宇.

She had read his file twice, and the second time she had set it down and looked out the window for a while, because the file was not a file. Rank thirty at eleven years old. Two purple rings. The dean's grandson, and 穆恩's — she knew whose student the golden boy was; everyone in the academy knew. And she had looked at that roll and felt, for the first time in her career, the specific wonder of a teacher handed a student her usual tests could not reach. Not because he was barred from anything — he would run with the room like anyone, and finish like anyone, because the boy plainly intended to hold nothing back from the work. But because her tests were built to find one thing — who still stands when everything is spent — and a boy who could not be spent would answer that test with a lie.

The hundred laps would teach him nothing about himself, and worse — they would teach the room nothing, because the room would simply watch him win and learn that winning was for other people.

So she had decided, alone in the dark classroom, what a teacher does with a furnace on a day the whole forge runs.

You let it run with the others. And you watch the only thing about it that can still be tested: what it does with its fire when nobody is forcing it to prove anything. Does it blaze ahead and drink the applause? Or does it bank itself down to the pace of the children and learn their names while it runs? That was the question no file could answer.

She had not told him that. She would not have to. The morning would tell.

"One hundred laps," she said. "Anyone who does not finish by sundown is expelled. Not tomorrow. Not after a warning. Today. The gate you crawled through closes at sunset, and it does not care how close you were."

She paused, and her eyes moved over the rows of suddenly pale children.

"You may use your souls. You may use your skills. You may use whatever the heavens gave you. But if you stop — if you walk, if you rest, if you give up — you are out." A thin smile crossed her face, and it was the coldest thing 言天宇 had ever seen on a human mouth. "I suggest you find out, right now, whether you are forged or decoration."

The first children broke into a run before she finished speaking.

言天宇 did not run at first. He stood at the line and watched, and what he watched was a roomful of children learning, in the space of a few heartbeats, exactly how far the distance between what they were and what they thought they were really was.

The strong ones — the ones with 武魂 that fed speed or stamina — pulled ahead. The 魂王's nephew from registration was not, it turned out, fast; he was loud, and the two were not the same. The girl with the nervous eyes ran in steady, desperate silence, husbanding her strength with the precision of someone who had known hunger. And at the back, running with the particular economy of a boy who had learned to make every breath count, was 霍雨浩.

Rank eleven. The lowest in the class. Running on nothing but will.

言天宇 began to run.

He did not run fast. He could have — could have blazed past every one of them and finished the hundred laps by noon and stood at the gate watching the rest of them crawl — but that was not the lesson his grandfather had set him, and it was not the lesson 穆恩 had spent a decade teaching him. *Do not be the strongest in the room,* the old dragon's voice said in the rhythm of his own footfalls. *Be the one who knows what the room is for.*

So he ran at the pace of the middle pack, smooth and untaxing, his breathing even, his two furnaces burning warm and steady under his ribs. The cold at his spine stayed asleep. He did not need it. He did not need half of what he was, running against children who had never once in their lives met a soul like his.

It felt, strangely, like holding his breath. Not the dangerous holding — the other kind. The kind where you have a great fire in your chest and you choose, moment by moment, to let it bank instead of blaze.

By the thirtieth lap, the class had strung out into a long, ragged thread. The strong were strong. The stubborn were stubborn. And at the very back, lap after lap falling further behind, was 霍雨浩 — still running, still refusing to stop, but running on fumes now, his face grey, his breath ragged, the particular determination of a boy who had spent his whole life being told he was not enough and had decided, at some point, that the telling did not matter.

言天宇's feet slowed. Unbidden. He had spent the morning watching the room divide around the dean's grandson, and here, suddenly, was a boy dividing around nothing — a boy with nothing but his own two feet and a will that would not break. The cold behind 霍雨浩's eyes. The careful mask. The hands that gutted fish because dinner did not cook itself.

*He's going to fall,* 言天宇 thought. *And if he falls, he's out. And he'll blame himself for the rest of his life.*

He was three strides from turning back, from running beside the thin boy and carrying him through the laps on his own inexhaustible warmth, when he saw 王冬.

The golden-haired boy had fallen back from the front of the pack. He was not running beside 霍雨浩 — he was *pacing* him, a few strides ahead, glancing back with an expression that was trying very hard to be annoyed and failing completely. And 言天宇 understood, in the way he understood most things about people, that 王冬 had chosen.

*Not me,* he thought. *Not yet. He doesn't need the dean's grandson carrying him. He needs to do it himself, with the friend he's making.*

He turned back to his own pace, and ran on.

The last laps were a war. The sun climbed, the shadows shortened, and one by one the children who could not finish fell out — not walking, not resting, but *finished*, collapsed on the grass with the particular blankness of bodies that had given everything. 周漪 stood at the line, unmoving, and made no sound. The gate did not care how close you were.

言天宇 finished his hundred laps at a smooth, even pace, and stood at the finish line breathing easily, and felt the stares land on him like a weight. He had not meant to be first. He had meant to be middle. But the middle had fallen away hours ago, and the strong had faded in the last stretch, and so he had crossed the line alone, barely sweating, two purple rings' worth of furnace burning quiet in his chest.

He did not look at the stares. He looked at the back of the track, where a thin boy was still running — no, not running, *stumbling*, each step a small war — and where a golden-haired boy had stopped pretending to be annoyed and had taken hold of 霍雨浩's arm and was pulling him forward, lap after lap, refusing to let him fall.

*王冬,* 言天宇 thought, and something warm and complicated moved in his chest. *You're not as proud as you look either.*

He watched them cross the line — 王冬 half-carrying, 霍雨浩 half-dead, both of them finished — and he was still watching when 周漪's flat voice cut the air.

"霍雨浩. 王冬. You finished. Barely." A pause. "The rest of you who finished — go eat. You who did not — the gate is that way."

And then she looked at 言天宇, and for the first time all day, she actually *saw* him.

"言天宇." Her voice was flat. "A word."

## Part Three — The Word

She led him to the shade of the training hall, out of earshot of the straggling children, and turned to face him with her arms crossed.

"You ran like you were holding a horse," she said.

言天宇 blinked. "Teacher?"

"Don't 'teacher' me." 周漪's eyes were sharp and utterly unimpressed. "I've taught the sons of kings and the daughters of dukes. I've taught children who could have bought this academy with their allowance. And I have never once taught a child who runs a hundred laps at your age without breathing hard, unless they were holding back. You ran like a man carrying a full cup who didn't want to spill." She studied him. "Why?"

言天宇 considered the question. It deserved an honest answer, and he found, to his surprise, that he wanted to give one.

"My grandfather told me to learn what the world looks like from the inside of it," he said. "Not from above it."

周漪's eyebrows rose a fraction. "The dean said that."

"Yes, Teacher."

"Hmph." She looked at him for a long moment, and something in her face shifted — not warmth, exactly, but something like a blade being turned to catch the light. "Your grandfather is a fool," she said, "if he thinks sending you here with two purple rings and a rank that would make half my colleagues sweat will teach you what the world looks like from the inside."

言天宇's mouth opened.

"Don't argue." 周漪 held up a hand. "I'm not insulting him. I'm telling you the truth, because no one else in this academy will. You are the most gifted child I have ever seen walk into this classroom, and I have seen a great many. The world will not let you be ordinary, 言天宇. It will not let you stand in the middle of a room and learn what it feels like to be one of the crowd, because you are not one of the crowd, and everyone in that yard knows it." She paused. "But there is one thing this room can teach you that no palace ever could."

"What?"

"Who you are when no one is watching." 周漪's voice was flat again, but there was something underneath it — the same something that made her break chalk in her hand when she was angry. "A king's grandson can be gracious when the room is full of people who know his name. The question is whether he is gracious when the room is full of people who would cheer to see him fall. That is what I teach, 言天宇. Not 武魂. Not soul power. That."

She turned to go, then stopped.

"The thin one," she said, without looking back. "The one the golden boy carried. He's rank eleven. The lowest in the class. He has nothing — no family name, no backing, no advantage but stubbornness. He is the kind of student I would have expelled in the first week, because the gate does not care about stubbornness."

Her voice changed, just slightly.

"But I didn't expel him. Do you know why?"

言天宇 shook his head.

"Because I looked at him running those last laps," 周漪 said, "and I saw a boy who had decided, a long time ago, that he would rather die than stop. That is not stubbornness. That is a man being forged. And I want you to watch him, 言天宇, because I think you have never once in your life had to decide that. I think you have never had to choose between stopping and dying. And I think," she said, and finally looked at him, "that the day you do, the whole continent is going to feel it."

She walked away.

言天宇 stood in the shade of the training hall with his hands at his sides, and felt the cold at his spine stir — a ripple, no more — and breathed it down with the habit of a decade.

*The day you do,* she had said. *The whole continent is going to feel it.*

He did not know, yet, how right she was.

## Part Four — The Special Guest

That evening, the dining hall was loud with the particular exhaustion of children who had survived their first real day.

言天宇 ate. He always ate. But even he slowed when the whispers started.

周漪 had said it herself at the end of the day, flatly, to the whole class, daring anyone to argue: 霍雨浩 was here on an invitation he had not purchased and had not begged for, and anyone with a problem could take it up with her. She had not explained why. That, more than anything, had set the hall on fire.

"—heard he's a 特邀生—"
"—rank eleven, can you believe it—"
"—why would they invite *him*—"
"—my cousin said the Tang Sect—"
"—he can't even fight, my little sister could—"

The whispers orbited one table. 霍雨浩 sat at it alone, his food untouched, eating slowly and deliberately the way you eat when you are pretending the whole room is not talking about you. He had cleaned himself up since the run, but the grey was still in his face, and the careful mask was back in place, and he was very, very alone.

言天宇 looked at him across the hall. And he remembered, with a strange sharpness, what it had felt like this morning in the classroom — being seen by a hundred strangers who did not know him and would never see the part that mattered. 霍雨浩 was being seen the same way. Seen, and judged, and found wanting, by people who did not know the first thing about him.

*Cold,* he thought. *Cold that isn't evil. Cold that's—*

He stood up.

He did not think about it. That was the thing — for once, he did not hold the breath, did not weigh the room, did not measure the cost. He picked up his tray — and then, because he was who he was, he picked up a second tray and filled it with the best of what the hall had left, and walked across the room, and set it down across from 霍雨浩.

The whispers died. Every eye in the hall turned.

霍雨浩 looked up, and for a moment his careful mask cracked — just a fraction — and underneath it 言天宇 saw something that looked almost like fear, and almost like hope, and mostly like exhaustion.

"Mind if I sit?" 言天宇 asked. "The view's better over here. And you're not eating."

"...I'm not hungry."

"Liar." 言天宇 sat down and pushed the second tray across. "You ran a hundred laps on an empty stomach and you expect me to believe you're not hungry? Eat. I know food. I *am* food, most days. This is the good stuff — they hide it in the back and I've learned the schedule."

霍雨浩 stared at the tray. Then, slowly, he picked up his chopsticks.

"Why are you doing this?" he asked, without looking up. "Everyone in this hall is talking about how I don't belong here. And you—" He finally looked up, and his dark eyes were very tired. "You're the dean's grandson. You have two purple rings and a rank I'd kill for. You could sit anywhere. Why are you sitting with the 特邀生 nobody wants?"

言天宇 considered the question.

"Because I know what it feels like," he said finally, "to be looked at by people who don't see you."

霍雨浩's chopsticks stopped.

"I spent this morning being stared at," 言天宇 went on, "by a room full of children who only saw the dean's grandson. They didn't see me. They saw a title and a rank and two purple rings, and they decided who I was without asking." He met 霍雨浩's eyes. "You spent this evening being stared at by a room full of children who only see the 特邀生. They don't see you either. They see a boy with no rank and no name and no reason to be here, and they decided who you were without asking."

A long silence.

"You're not the same," 霍雨浩 said quietly. "You're—" He gestured, helplessly, at the warmth that seemed to gather around 言天宇 like light around a flame. "You're *chosen.* Everyone can see it. You walk into a room and people want to be near you."

"And you walk into a room," 言天宇 said, "and people want to be near you, once they stop being afraid of what they don't understand." He leaned forward, and his voice dropped, and for just a moment the golden warmth in his eyes went very serious. "I saw you running those laps, 霍雨浩. Rank eleven. Nothing but will. You crossed the line because you decided you would rather die than stop. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know how many children with two purple rings and a title would have quit on lap forty?"

霍雨浩 was very still.

"Nobody taught you that," 言天宇 said. "Nobody gave it to you. You built it yourself, out of whatever you've been through, and it's worth more than every purple ring in this academy." He picked up his own chopsticks. "The people in this hall will figure that out eventually. They always do. And when they do, they're going to want to be near you — and I want to be able to say I knew you before they did."

He took a bite, chewed, and smiled — the warm, open smile that made people trust him.

"Also, you cook better than this hall, and I'm going to need a reliable fish supplier. Think of it as an investment."

For a long moment, 霍雨浩 just looked at him. And then — slowly, like ice cracking in spring — the careful mask cracked, and a real smile broke across the thin boy's face. It was not the ghost of a smile from the market. It was real, and it was tired, and it was the most honest thing 言天宇 had seen all day.

"You're strange," 霍雨浩 said.

"I'm aware." 言天宇 grinned. "Eat your fish. You've earned it."

Across the hall, 王冬 watched the two of them with an expression that was trying very hard to be annoyed and failing completely. 唐雅, who had slipped in to check on her disciple, watched with a grin so wide it threatened her face. And 贝贝, who had come with her, watched with quiet approval, and said nothing, and was glad.

The whispers did not stop that night. But they changed — just a little, just at the edges — because the dean's grandson was sitting with the 特邀生 nobody wanted, and neither of them looked like they were going anywhere.

## Part Five — Two Fires, One Room

That night, 言天宇 lay in his narrow bed and stared at the ceiling, and thought about what 周漪 had said.

*A man being forged.* She had said it about 霍雨浩. But the words had lodged in him like a splinter, and he could not stop turning them over.

He had spent eleven years being forged — by his grandfather's standards, by 穆恩's discipline, by the careful breath that kept two furnaces balanced in one boy's chest. But he had never once been forged by *fire.* He had never once been told he was not enough. He had never once had to decide, between stopping and dying, that he would rather die.

The wall inside him stirred. Rank thirty. The third-ring gate. A door that would not open until he brought it something worthy — and that would, when it opened, be a fight rather than a meal.

*A beast worth ten thousand years,* 穆恩 had said, *will teach you which hunger is yours.*

He thought of 霍雨浩, crossing the line on will alone. Rank eleven, running on nothing, refusing to stop. And he thought of what 周漪 had said: *the day you have to choose between stopping and dying, the whole continent is going to feel it.*

He did not know when that day would come. He did not know what it would cost.

But lying in the dark, with the day-fire warm behind his breastbone and the night-cold asleep at his spine, 言天宇 made a decision he did not have words for yet — a quiet, absolute decision, the kind a boy makes in the dark when no one is watching.

When that day came — when he finally stood at the door of the third ring, or at the edge of some darker gate he could not yet see — he would not hold back. He would not run at the pace of the middle pack. He would run like 霍雨浩, like the thin boy with nothing but will: all the way to the line, and past it.

*And whatever the beast does to me when I try to feed,* he thought, *whatever it tries to make me into — I'll decide what I become. Not it. Not the blood. Me.*

The cold at his spine stirred once, like a great beast turning over in its sleep.

He breathed it down.

Tomorrow, there would be more laps. More drills. More of the room learning who the dean's grandson was, and more of him learning who the room was. And somewhere in the dormitories, a thin boy who carried his own cold was probably not sleeping either — but he was not alone anymore.

That, 言天宇 thought, was a start.

## Part One — The Old Dragon's Dawn

The light came up gold over 史莱克城 before the city did.

It was 言天宇's favourite hour — the only hour of the day when the whole world was still empty enough to breathe with. He sat cross-legged on the flat stone of the old pavilion above the outer wall, his hands loose on his knees, and let the first warmth of the sun find him through his closed eyes.

In. The morning air, thin and cold. Down through his chest to the place where the light sat — a hearth just behind his breastbone, warm as a banked fire.

Out. Slower. And as the breath left him, something else stirred at the far end of his spine, the way deep water stirs when something passes overhead. Cold. Patient. Asleep — or nearly.

*Neither one,* 穆恩's voice said in his memory. *Feed both, and neither rules.*

For a boy of eleven he was very good at this. He had been doing it since he was six, dawn and dusk, every day of his life that mattered. The breath that kept the day-fire bright and the night-cold sleeping was not a technique he had learned so much as a habit he had grown into, the way other children grew into the shape of their own hands.

Behind him, a sound like a mountain deciding to sit down.

"You hold it better than I did at thirty," said 穆恩.

言天宇 opened his eyes and turned. The old man stood at the edge of the pavilion, wrapped in a plain grey robe, and for a moment the morning light made him look almost young — the way light does when it lies to you about a face. The 龙神斗罗. Ninety-nine levels of the continent's old age, standing on a stone roof to watch a boy breathe.

He had come without 言天宇 hearing him. He always did.

"Grandteacher," 言天宇 said, and began to rise.

"Sit." 穆恩 lowered himself onto the stone beside the boy with the care of a man whose joints kept accounts. "You're at the wall."

It was not a question. 穆恩 did not ask questions whose answers he already held.

"Yes." 言天宇 looked down at his own hands — a boy's hands, strong from knife-work and drills and years of holding a breath that other people could not feel. "Thirty. Two rings. I can feel the third one waiting, Grandteacher. Like a door I can't stop looking at."

"Good," said the old dragon. "You should look at it. You should also know *why* you cannot open it yet."

言天宇 waited. He had learned early that the old man's silences were where the real lessons lived.

"Because the world is honest," 穆恩 said, "even when it is cruel. Rank thirty without a third ring is the wall every soul master meets. It does not care that you are the grandson of a dean, or that you woke with ten levels the day your soul did, or that you could have beaten half the teachers here at your age. It says: *bring me a ring worthy of the climb, or climb no higher.*" He turned his head, and his eyes — old, light, terribly clear — found the boy's. "Do you know what most geniuses do at that wall, 小宇?"

"They rush it," 言天宇 said quietly.

"They rush it." 穆恩 nodded. "They take the first beast that will let them win, and they feed it to themselves, and they tell the world they are strong. And for ten years they are — until the day they meet someone whose foundation was not built in a hurry." A pause. "Your foundation is not built in a hurry. It was built before you were born. Do you understand what that means?"

言天宇 did, in the way he understood most of 穆恩's lessons — with his body before his mind. The heat behind his breastbone. The cold asleep at his spine. Two furnaces, sharing one boy, kept balanced by a breath he had practised ten thousand times. His grandfather said he was a miracle. 穆恩 said he was *caused* — every perfect inch of him paid for in advance by something that had shaped him before his first cry.

"I think so," he said.

"Then answer me this." The old dragon's voice dropped, the way it did when the lesson was almost over and the real one was about to begin. "When the third ring comes — and it will come, 小宇, I have already spoken to your grandfather about the beast — what are you going to feed it?"

言天宇 blinked. "Feed it?"

"Mm." 穆恩 smiled, a thin winter sun of a smile. "You have two hungers, boy. Everyone thinks of the one at the table. But the other one — the one that goes cold and cavernous when you are angry, or afraid, or fighting too hard — that one is real too. And it is going to be *very* hungry the day a ten-thousand-year light sits down across from it and asks to be eaten."

言天宇's hands tightened on his knees. The cold at his spine stirred — a ripple, no more — and he breathed it down before it could become a wave. The habit was so old it was like blinking.

"Grandteacher," he said, very carefully, "you make it sound as though eating it is not the point."

"I make it sound that way," said 穆恩, "because it is not." He reached out and laid a hand — papery, impossibly strong — on the boy's head. "The point, 小宇, is that you learn which hunger is yours. A beast worth ten thousand years will teach you that, one way or another. That is why we will not rush it. That is why I am still here to watch you breathe."

The morning light climbed. Somewhere below, the city began to wake — the clatter of a cart, the first shout of a vendor, the thousand small sounds of a day beginning without asking anyone's permission.

言天宇 looked up at his grandteacher, at the old man who had claimed him on the day his soul woke, and wanted to ask the question he never quite dared: *Grandteacher, are you tired?*

He did not ask it. He had learned, too early, that some questions make the people you love tell you a gentle lie.

Instead he said, "Will you stay for breakfast? I'll cook."

And 穆恩, the 龙神斗罗, ninety-nine levels and a century of grief old, laughed — one syllable, winter thawing — and said, "You ask a starving man if he wants bread, 小宇. Of course I'll stay for breakfast."

## Part Two — The Grandfather's Rule

The breakfast room of the dean's private house smelled of honey and woodsmoke and something that had been roasting since before dawn.

蔡媚儿 set the last dish down with the precision of a woman who had once commanded a battlefield and now commanded only her family, and looked at her grandson with the only expression she ever truly wore for him: love pretending to be a scolding.

"You ate the kitchen's entire month of honey," she said. "Again."

"I shared it with Grandteacher," 言天宇 said, which was not entirely a lie and not entirely true, and they both knew which half was which.

"Mm." She sat. "And the roasted beast from yesterday?"

"That was yesterday."

蔡媚儿 looked at him for a long moment, and then she did the thing that always undid him — she laughed, warm and helpless, and reached across to ruffle his hair. "Eat," she said. "Before you faint and the whole academy blames me."

He ate. He was eleven, and he was always hungry, and the hunger was the one thing about him that he had never once had to hide. Two furnaces, his grandteacher said. The day-fire that burned for the light, and the night-cold that burned to stay asleep — and between them, a boy who could eat a grown man under the table and be hungry again by noon. The kitchen staff had long ago stopped being amazed. They had simply started cooking for an army and calling it Tuesday.

The door opened, and 言少哲 came in.

His grandfather did not walk so much as arrive. The dean of the 武魂系 carried himself like a man who had spent his whole life being measured and had decided, long ago, to exceed every measurement. In the morning light his face was stern and handsome and tired in a way only his family could see.

"言天宇," he said, and sat at the head of the table. No greeting beyond that. 言少哲's love was not in greetings; it was in the fact that he was here at all, at this hour, for breakfast.

"Grandfather," 言天宇 said, and set down his chopsticks. When his grandfather used his full name like that, something was coming.

"There is a thing we must discuss," 言少哲 said. "The academy's intake begins this afternoon. New students will register at the outer gate. I have decided that you will be among them."

The words landed like stones in still water.

蔡媚儿's hand, reaching for the teapot, stopped. "Shaozhe—"

"It is not a punishment," 言少哲 said, though his voice made it sound like one. "He is eleven. He is at the wall. And he has spent his whole life inside these walls as the dean's grandson and the old dragon's student — which means he has learned to be extraordinary, and has never once learned to be *ordinary*."

言天宇 sat very still. The stillness was a habit he had — the habit that made adults call him *mature* and made his grandteacher look at him with something like grief.

"I don't understand," he said carefully. "I've trained my whole life. I'm rank thirty. I—"

"You are rank thirty," his grandfather agreed, "and you cannot advance a single level until you take a third ring, and you cannot take a third ring until we have found the right beast and prepared you for what that absorption will cost." He paused. "The wall is not a punishment, 言天宇. It is a door that is going to stay closed for a while. And while it is closed, you are going to sit in a room full of children your own age who do not know your name, and you are going to learn what the world looks like from the inside of it."

*Do not be the strongest in the room,* 穆恩's voice said in 言天宇's memory. *Be the one who knows what the room is for.*

"You want me to—" 言天宇's voice caught. He did not know what he had been about to say. *Pretend to be less? Hide?* He had been hiding something his entire life; the idea of having to hide his *whole self* felt, for one dizzying moment, like the cruellest thing his grandfather had ever asked.

"No," 言少哲 said quietly, as though he had heard the unspoken word. "I want you to stop hiding behind this house and start standing in front of it. You will register like any other student. You will sit in the classes. You will eat in the dining hall. And you will be 言天宇 — not 'the dean's grandson,' not '穆恩's student,' not a title that opens doors before you knock." He looked at the boy with eyes that were stern and, underneath, something far more complicated. "The world is going to measure you one day, boy, against things that have nothing to do with your name. I want you to have practice at being measured."

蔡媚儿 had not moved. Her hand was still frozen above the teapot.

"You'll make him stand in line," she said at last, very softly, "with children half his strength. You'll make him—"

"He'll make friends," 言少哲 said. "He'll make mistakes. He'll learn that some boys are cruel and some are kind and most are simply trying to survive, exactly like him." He looked at his grandson, and for just a moment the dean was gone and only a man remained. "He has never had a friend his own age, Meier. That is the one thing this house could not give him. Perhaps the academy can."

The silence in the breakfast room was very full.

言天宇 looked down at his hands. At the boy's hands that could gut a fish and hold a breath that kept two worlds apart. At the hands of a boy who had never once, in eleven years, had a friend who did not already know his grandfather's name.

"...I'm meant to meet 贝贝 and 唐雅 at the north gate tonight," he said finally, his voice rough. "They said they're bringing someone new."

言少哲's eyebrows rose a fraction. "Are they."

"They said he's a boy they picked up on the road, and 唐雅's taken him on — her sect's first new disciple in years." 言天宇 lifted his eyes. His amber-gold eyes, warm as a banked sunrise, the ones that made people trust him before he spoke a word. "If I'm going to be a student, Grandfather, I should probably know what the students look like."

For a long moment, 言少哲 said nothing. Then, very slowly, something that was almost a smile crossed the stern face.

"Eat your breakfast," he said. "You'll need it."

## Part Three — The Line

The registration yard at the outer gate was a sea of children and their families, and 言天宇 stood in the middle of it feeling, for the first time in his life, like a fish that had forgotten it was supposed to swim.

The line was long. The children were loud. A girl behind him was crying because her father had told her she must pass or the family would have nothing. A boy ahead of him was loudly telling anyone who would listen that his uncle was a 魂王 and that he himself was certain to be the strongest in his year. The air smelled of dust and sweat and the particular desperation of families who had staked everything on a single gate.

言天宇 stood in the line with his hands at his sides and his shoulders straight, the way 蔡媚儿 had taught him, and tried very hard not to look like the dean's grandson.

It was not going well.

"Hey." A boy in front of him — the loud one — turned around and sized him up with the particular contempt of an eleven-year-old who had decided the world owed him something. "You're a bit tall for the intake. How old are you?"

"Eleven," 言天宇 said.

"Yeah? You look older." The boy's eyes narrowed. "What's your soul power?"

言天宇 considered the question. His grandfather had not told him to lie — had, in fact, pointedly not told him anything about what to say. The truth was that he could have flattened this boy with a single flare of his light, and the truth was also that doing so would be like using a forge-fire to light a candle.

"Enough to get in," he said, and smiled his warm, disarming smile.

The boy snorted and turned back around, apparently satisfied that the tall kid was a nobody with nothing to prove.

The line moved.

The examiner at the registration table was a thin man with the tired eyes of someone who had been watching children reveal their souls all day. He looked up as 言天宇 reached the table, blinked, and looked down at his papers, then back up.

"Name?"

"言天宇."

The examiner's pen stopped. "言—" He looked at the boy again, properly this time, and something like recognition — or alarm — crossed his face. "You're—"

"Yes," 言天宇 said pleasantly. "I'm registering. As a student." He held out his hand, palm up, the way the other children had done. "Should I show you my soul, Examiner?"

The man swallowed. "Ah. Yes. If—if you would, young master—"

言天宇 closed his eyes for half a breath. He let the day-fire rise — not all of it, never all of it, but enough. Warmth flooded his chest and out along his limbs, and when he opened his eyes and lifted his hand, the 光明凤凰 rose over his palm in a column of golden light.

It was not a full manifestation. It was a taste. Even so, the registration yard went quiet.

The examiner stared at the two rings that circled the phoenix's light — both purple, both thick and clean and ancient in a way that made his trained eyes go wide. A thousand years on a first ring was already absurd. Two thousand more on the second was the kind of thing that got written down in books. On an *eleven-year-old*, it was—

"Rank," the examiner whispered. "Your rank, young master—"

"Thirty," said 言天宇. And because the whole yard was now listening, and because he could feel the weight of a hundred stares pressing on him like a hand, he let the phoenix sink back into his chest and added, with the calm his grandfather had drilled into him since he could walk: "At the third-ring gate. I won't be advancing for a while. It's a good time to learn the basics properly."

The examiner opened his mouth. Closed it. Wrote something down with a hand that was not entirely steady.

"Pass," he said. "Report to 新生一班, teacher 周漪, at dawn tomorrow. Welcome to 史莱克学院, young—" He caught himself. "Student 言."

"Thank you, Examiner," said 言天宇, and stepped out of the line with the warmth still faintly glowing behind his breastbone, trying very hard to pretend his ears were not burning.

A hand landed on his shoulder like a small furnace.

"Well, well, well." 马小桃 stood there, arms crossed, a grin on her face that had made grown 魂王s back down in terror. "The little sun finally steps out of his greenhouse. How does the dirt feel, 小宇?"

"桃子姐," 言天宇 said, and meant it as a greeting and a warning in equal measure. "You're supposed to be in the inner court."

"I am *always* in the inner court." She slung an arm around his shoulders and marched him away from the crowd, heedless of the stares — hers were, if anything, more effective than his had been. "Heard you were registering like a common student." She snorted. "Let me guess — the old tyrant's idea." But there was no venom in it; 言少哲 had raised her from a child, and her insults were the shape her love took. "You know the second I'm gone, some little brat's going to try to test the dean's grandson to make a name for himself."

"I can handle a few brats," 言天宇 said.

"I know you can." 马小桃 stopped walking and turned to face him. For a moment, in the dusty yard, with the noise of the intake around them, she looked almost serious. "That's not why I came, 小宇. I came to tell you — if any of them make it hard for you, you come find me. Not because you need me." She poked his chest, where the hearth sat. "Because I want to watch their faces when their 'easy target' turns out to be the little brother of the 内院's worst nightmare."

言天宇 laughed — a real laugh, warm and loud, the kind that made people turn and look and then smile without knowing why. "You're a terrible influence, 桃子姐."

"I'm the *best* influence." She ruffled his hair so hard his neat black strands went everywhere. "Now go. I have a 内院 duel in an hour and I'm going to win it so hard they name a building after me."

She turned to go — and as she turned, 言天宇 felt it. A flicker. A wrongness in her fire, a half-second where the warmth at her core guttered and went dark, like a candle drowning in its own wax.

It was the 邪火. It always was. The fire that had been fighting her since she was a girl, the one she pretended she had tamed.

言天宇 did not think. Eleven years of training and two furnaces of his own made the response as natural as breathing: he let his warmth reach out, a light-wash, gentle and wordless, and laid it over the flicker like a hand over a flame.

马小桃's shoulders — which she never let anyone see tense — eased by a fraction.

She did not turn around. She never did, after. But her voice, when it came, was softer than he had heard it in weeks.

"Thanks, 小宇."

"Anytime, 桃子姐," said 言天宇, and watched her walk away into the crowd, holding his light steady until he could no longer feel the dark edge of her fire reaching back for him.

*One day,* he thought, *I'll learn to put that fire out for good.*

He did not know, yet, that some fires cannot be put out — only met by someone who understands them.

## Part Four — The Boy With the Fish

The clerk's pen had hovered over the name — 霍雨浩, no family listed, no backing, recommended by the 唐门 — and 贝贝 had stood there, patient and pleasant, until the receipt was in his hand and the matter was closed.

### Panel ends (贝贝).

The north-gate market at dusk smelled like the whole world cooking.

言天宇 found 贝贝 and 唐雅 where they were — near the edge of the north-gate market, watching over the newest addition to their small world — but his eyes went past them, past the crowd, to the small figure crouched over a brazier by the wall of the market.

The boy was thin. Not fragile-thin, the way a sheltered child is thin, but lean-thin, the way a blade is lean — as if he had been worn down to the essential parts and the essential parts were all that remained. His clothes were patched and clean. His hands, moving over the fish on the brazier, were quick and certain, the hands of someone who had learned early that dinner did not cook itself.

And he was cooking *well.* The smell coming off his brazier was better than anything in the market around him.

言天宇's stomach made a sound that was frankly embarrassing.

"小宇!" 唐雅 spotted him and bounded over, all bouncing energy and a grin that did not quite reach her eyes — though no one who did not know her would ever have caught that. "You came! Did you bring food? You always bring food. Oh, you're going to *love* this—" She grabbed his arm and dragged him toward the brazier-boy. "霍雨浩! 霍雨浩, this is 言天宇 — he's the— well, he's *from the academy.* He's one of us. And he's a *terrible* influence because he cooks better than half the chefs in the city."

The boy — 霍雨浩 — looked up.

His eyes were the first thing 言天宇 noticed. Deep, dark, and watchful in a way that had nothing to do with his age. The eyes of someone who had learned, very young, that the world was not safe and that kindness often had a price.

"Hello," the boy said. Polite. Careful. His voice gave away nothing.

言天宇 looked at him. And because 言天宇 had spent his whole life reading the space behind people's eyes — the warmth and the shadow that other children never noticed — he saw, beneath the careful surface of 霍雨浩, something that made his own two furnaces pause.

*Cold,* he thought. *There's cold in him. And it's not evil.*

He did not know what it was. He did not know the half of what the thin boy carried — where he had walked out of, or what it had cost him to walk out — and whatever it was, it was the boy's own, and 言天宇 would not have pried at it for the world. He only knew that this thin, watchful boy with the excellent fish was carrying something heavy, and that it did not make 言天宇 want to step back.

It made him want to sit down.

"That smells incredible," 言天宇 said honestly. "Would you— I mean, could I—" He stopped, embarrassed, and did the thing that always saved him: he laughed at himself. "I'm going to sound like a fool. I've eaten three breakfasts and a lunch and I'm already hungry again. That fish smells like the best thing in this city, and I was wondering if you'd sell me one."

霍雨浩 blinked. The careful mask flickered — just for a moment — and underneath it, 言天宇 caught a glimpse of something that looked almost like a boy his age.

"...It's not that good," 霍雨浩 said.

"It is," said 言天宇. "I know food. I *am* food, most days. Trust me — it's that good."

There was a pause. Then, slowly, 霍雨浩's mouth curved into the ghost of a smile — the first one 言天宇 had seen on him.

"Two copper," the boy said. "But you have to eat it here. Fresh is the whole point."

言天宇 sat down cross-legged on the dusty ground of the market, in his clean academy clothes, beside a boy he had known for thirty seconds, and ate the best fish he had had in weeks.

Behind him, 贝贝 watched with quiet approval and 唐雅 watched with a grin so wide it threatened her face. Neither of them said anything. They had both seen, in that small moment, the thing they had hoped for without quite daring to believe it: the golden grandson of the academy sitting in the dust of the market, and the careful, watchful boy they had brought in from the road — meeting like two ordinary boys, and finding out that the other one was real.

"Same intake," 言天宇 said, wiping his hands on a cloth he produced from nowhere — he was, as 唐雅 often said, a walking pantry. "You start tomorrow too?"

"...Yes," 霍雨浩 said.

"Then I'll see you tomorrow," said 言天宇, and stood, brushing dust from his robes, and held out his hand — not down, the way you offer help to someone smaller, but level, the way you greet an equal. "Somewhere inside those gates. Save me a seat if we land in the same room."

霍雨浩 looked at the outstretched hand for a long moment.

言天宇 did not know the boy's eleven years. He did not need to. Whatever they had held, no one had ever offered this thin, watchful boy a hand quite like this one — level, unafraid, as if he were worth meeting, not a charity case to be managed, not a road-boy to be pitied. He watched 霍雨浩 look at it, and saw the careful mask shift, and knew he had been right to hold his own hand out level.

霍雨浩 took it.

"Deal," he said. "But if you tell anyone I smiled, I'll deny it."

言天宇 laughed — that warm, open laugh that made people trust him — and walked away into the dusk of the market with his hands in his sleeves, trying very hard not to think about the cold he had felt behind 霍雨浩's eyes.

*Cold,* he thought again. *Cold that isn't evil. Cold that's—*

He stopped the thought. Some things, he had learned, were not for him to know yet. Some colds belonged to other people until they chose to share them.

He had a feeling, though, that he and 霍雨浩 were going to find out about each other's colds sooner or later.

The way you find out about a fire you're standing next to.

## Part Five — The Door That Waits

That night, 言天宇 could not sleep.

It was not the cold — he had learned, years ago, to shift his breathing until the night-cold at his spine slept warm enough to let him rest. It was the wall.

He lay in his narrow bed in the corner of the house that had been his since he could remember, and he felt it: the door inside him, just past the hearth of the phoenix, just past the sleeping dark of the dragon. Rank thirty. The third-ring gate. A door that would not open until he brought it something worthy of opening for — and that would, when it finally opened, be a fight rather than a meal. The eating-threshold his grandteacher had taught him was no immunity: a ten-thousand-year prey would not simply dissolve. It would contend. It would try to change him even as he tried to feed on it.

*A beast worth ten thousand years,* 穆恩 had said. *A beast that will teach you which hunger is yours.*

言天宇 turned onto his back and stared at the ceiling. Outside, 史莱克城 slept. Inside the walls, the academy that had raised him dozed like a great patient beast. And somewhere in the dormitories of the outer court, a thin boy who carried his own cold was probably not sleeping either.

Tomorrow, they would sit in the same classroom.

Tomorrow, 言天宇 would be a student — just a student, for the first time in his life — and the whole world would see what the golden grandson was made of when he was not standing on his grandfather's shadow.

The door inside him waited.

*Not yet,* he told it, the way 穆恩 had taught him. *Not yet. The beast isn't ready. I'm not ready. And when I am—*

He let the thought finish itself in the dark, where no one could see his eyes.

*When I am, I'm going to bring you something so worthy that the whole continent feels the table shake.*

The cold at his spine stirred, once, like a great beast turning over in its sleep.

And 言天宇, the golden grandson, the little sun, the boy who had never had a friend his own age until a thin boy with a fish had shaken his hand in a dusty market — 言天宇 closed his eyes, breathed both his fires down to embers, and let himself dream of doors.

Tomorrow, he would walk through one.

## Part One — The Rule of the Golden Grandson

A week passed, and 新生一班 learned its teacher was not done with them.

Every dawn the track. Every morning drills until the arms shook. Every afternoon 武魂 control at 周漪's counting, and behind the counting, always, the flat truth she had given them on day one: most of you will not make it. The gate behind them was still closing.

言天宇 ran with them on the dawns, at the pace of the middle pack, and learned the names the room was made of. The loud boy from registration — the 魂王's nephew — was called 徐磊, and he was not as loud as he pretended, only scared in a voice he hadn't grown into yet. The girl with the nervous eyes was 温宁, and she ran like she had been running from something her whole life and meant to keep ahead of it. The small girl with the twin braids, who never raised her hand and watched the room the way he watched it, was called 萧萧 — he had caught her watching him once, and they had both looked away without a word, and neither had mentioned it since.

一班 had begun the term with close to a hundred names on the roll. The hundred-lap day had taken the first of them, and 周漪's culls had taken more every week after — the gate behind them really was still closing, and no teacher in the freshman year shed students the way hers did. But the ones who stayed were the ones worth knowing, and by the end of the first week 言天宇 knew something true about nearly every one of them: which ones lied, which ones hid, which ones would carry the others, which ones would fold the moment the gate looked at them directly.

He alone was the one the room could not measure — the one name in it that had been written down before its owner ever stood in a line — and he knew it the way he knew the feel of his own furnaces.

It was the game his grandfather had taught him without ever naming it: *learn the room.* He was good at it. He had been good at it since before he could remember, because the dean's house was a room full of people who smiled at the grandson and meant forty different things by it.

What he was less good at was not caring that they were learning him back.

"言天宇." 温宁 had caught up to him on the way back from the track, her voice small and fierce at once. "Why do you help 徐磊? He called you a walking trophy behind your back. Twice."

"Twice?" 言天宇 said, pleased. "He's getting braver. Behind the back is where cowards practise, and 徐磊 isn't a coward — he's scared, and he hasn't learned the difference yet. The day he says it to my face will be the day I've made a friend."

温宁 stared at him. "That's not— that's not what I asked."

"It is, though." He smiled, and the dawn light seemed to gather at the edges of it. "You asked why I help a boy who resents me. Because resentment is just hunger pointed the wrong way. Feed the hunger, and it points itself straight eventually." He tilted his head. "Also, he's not wrong. I am a walking trophy — I've been one my whole life. If people have to carry that around with them, the least I can do is make the load worth carrying."

温宁 had no answer to that. She made a small, confused sound and fell back, and 言天宇 watched her go and thought: *that's the third one this week. Good.*

But the awe had teeth, and not all of them were kind.

The afternoon 周漪 stood at the front of the classroom with a piece of iron in her hand and a smile on her face that was worse than no smile at all, the room understood, in a single shared intake of breath, that the soft part of the term was over.

"Tomorrow," she said, "each of you will put on one of these." She dropped the iron garment on the desk; it landed with a sound like a grave being shut. "You will wear it for the whole of the morning. You will run the track until I dismiss you. This is not a drill and it is not a punishment. It is a selection." Her eyes moved over the room like a blade. "Before this term ends, this class will need a 班长 — a leader the rest of you will follow into the 新生考核. And this class will also shed its weak links, because the gate behind you is still closing and it does not feed on the strong. This run tells me which of you is which."

She let the silence sit.

"霍雨浩. 王冬." She did not look at them when she said it, and the two names landed like two stones in still water. "One of you I am watching because everyone else has already written him off. The other, because everyone else has already crowned him. This run tells me which of those judgments is worth a damn." Her voice went flat. "The rest of you — prove something, or be proven."

The room dissolved into whispers the moment she turned away. 言天宇 watched them swirl — fear, calculation, the particular frantic arithmetic of children who had just understood that tomorrow would cost something they were not sure they could pay — and he felt the familiar small weight settle on his chest.

*And I,* he thought, *won't be running. And for the first time, I think I know why — and it has nothing to do with being too strong to be allowed.*

He had known it since his grandfather had sat him down before the intake and told him the truth he carried like a stone in his chest: rank thirty was a wall, and a wall does not move until you bring it something worthy — and until 穆恩's hunt was ready, he could not climb a single rung of the ladder this room was climbing. He was not in this class to advance. He was in it to learn the world from inside — to make friends, to be measured as a person instead of a title, to find out what the room was for. That was his grandfather's whole purpose in signing him into 周漪's room, and it was 穆恩's whole teaching.

So the room's selections — the culls that decided who continued up the climb, the 班长 seat that would lead the room into the 新生考核, the team that would one day carry its banner — were simply not his. Not because a rule kept him out. Because they were the ladder's events, and this year the ladder was not his to climb. He would run every dawn with them, and every drill, and every hundred-lap morning, because a boy trained by 龙神斗罗 did not skip work, no matter how far above the room he stood. But a selection that would crown its leader in front of the whole 新生 year — that was for the ones whose feet were on the ladder. A boy who could not be spent had no honest place in a test meant to find the one who refuses to stop.

He told himself it did not sting.

He was getting very good at telling himself things that were almost true.

周漪 found him after class, in the empty corridor, and did not pretend it was an accident.

"I watched you during the announcement," she said. It was not a question. "Every child in that room started making private calculations the moment I said the word 'selection.' You went still. You already know you're not running tomorrow. I want to hear you say why — and then I'm going to have to decide whether it's wisdom or the most expensive form of pride I've ever seen."

言天宇 considered lying, and decided the woman who broke chalk in her hand when she was angry deserved better. "It isn't mine, Teacher," he said. "I can't advance until my grandteacher opens my door. I'm not on this year's ladder. And if I ran, I'd win — not because I'm braver than anyone in this room, but because I can't get tired the way they can. This class doesn't need a 班长 who won by being unable to lose. It needs the one who refused to lose anyway, on nothing but will." He met her eyes, steady. "That boy is in this room, Teacher. He just doesn't know it yet."

周漪 studied him for a long moment, and something in her hard face changed — not softened, exactly. Sharpened, the way a blade sharpens when it finds the whetstone it has been looking for.

"You know why your grandfather put you in my class," she said. "The real reason, under the 'learn the world' speech?"

It was. He had not let himself say it, even in his own head, but it was.

"Because you've never been spent," 周漪 said. "Look at you. Rank thirty, two purple rings, eleven years old — and you just told me, without a flicker, why the one office in this room you could have taken with your eyes closed isn't yours to take. Do you know how rare that is? Most children with half your power would be at the front of every line, demanding the crown they're owed. You walked to the back of the room and started learning names instead." She stepped closer. "Knowing what the room is for — you have that in spades, boy. That was never the lesson. The lesson is the other half. You have never once in your life run on empty. You have never had to choose between stopping and dying, because your fires never run empty. And the day you do — at your own wall, in a hunt no freshmen race could ever teach you — the whole continent is going to feel it."

She walked past him, and paused one step later.

"Stand with the room tomorrow," she said, without turning. "Not above it. With it. And watch the thin boy find the thing you have never had to find. Then come and tell me what it looked like."

She left.

言天宇 stood in the empty corridor with the afternoon light falling across him, and felt the small weight on his chest settle into something he could almost carry. Warmth was real. The smile he wore was real, the kindness was real, the hunger to be useful was real.

But underneath all of it, in the place he kept shuttered even from himself, a boy who had spent eleven years being set apart by the very thing that made him special felt the shadow stir.

*Never been spent,* she had said.

He had never once in his life stood at the edge of himself. He had never had to. Being the dean's grandson and 穆恩's student meant there was always a fire behind him, always a floor beneath him, always a door that would open when he was ready. 霍雨浩 stood at the edge of himself every single day, because there was nothing behind him at all.

Tomorrow he would stand with the room and watch it happen.

And for the first time in his life, he thought he understood what it would cost, one day, to find out what was standing at his own edge.

## Part Two — The Morning the Room Crowned Itself

The morning was grey and mean, and the track looked longer than it ever had.

The iron garments lay in a row at the starting line like a verdict waiting to be put on. They were not elegant things — thick woven vests shot through with metal plates, heavy enough that the first boy who lifted one staggered under its weight, and that was before he had to run in it. The class lined up slowly, and the particular dread in the air was thick enough to chew. A voice, low and venomous, from two places down the line.

"Look. The trophy doesn't wear iron." 徐磊's face wore its usual sneer, but there was something hunted in his eyes. "Too good to bleed with the rest of us."

言天宇 did not turn. "You know what I like about you, 徐磊?" he said, warm as a banked fire. "You say it to my face. Most people wait until I'm gone." He finally looked over his shoulder, and his smile was easy and open. "And you're wrong, by the way. I'm not too good for the iron — I ran every dawn this term in it. I'd run this with you, gladly. But I'd run it till noon and not feel it, and that's exactly why it's not mine to run. This run isn't for finding out who's strongest, 徐磊. It's for finding out who won't stop when they've got nothing left — and that's a question I can't answer honestly, because I've never once had nothing left. You can. So go answer it." He smiled, and nodded at the track. "I'll be where I can see all of you — loudest voice there."

徐磊's mouth opened and closed. Several people nearby made sounds that were trying very hard not to be laughter. And 周漪's voice cut across them all before anything else could be said.

"The rules are simple," she said. "You run until I dismiss you. You may stop to rest — I will not chain you. But the one who is running when the others have stopped, the one the room turns to when its legs give out, that one is your 班长. And the ones who stop and stay stopped," her voice went flat and final, "are the ones the gate will eat. Begin."

They began.

言天宇 took his place at the far end of the track, where he could see all of it — the spot he had chosen, because a boy who was not running owed the runners his whole attention. He leaned on the rail and watched the way 穆恩 had taught him to watch a beast: not for how it moved, but for when it would lie down. The first laps were a stampede of fear — everyone trying to look strong, no one willing to be first to slow. The iron clattered and clanked; the track turned into a river of noise. And then, one by one, the noise began to break apart into breaths.

徐磊 was fast for two laps and then died a public death, his face going from red to grey, his legs turning to rope. He staggered to the grass and sat, and the shame in his eyes was worse than the exhaustion. 温宁 ran with her old desperate economy, husbanding every breath, and she would last — 言天宇 saw it in the set of her shoulders — but she would last as a survivor, not a leader. A cluster of boys who had trained together ran as a pack, covering each other's pacing, and that was clever, and it was also not the thing the run was looking for.

And at the back of the pack, running with the particular economy of a boy who had spent his whole life learning to make every breath and every coin and every scrap of food count, was 霍雨浩.

Rank eleven. The lowest in the class. Wearing the same iron as everyone else, and carrying more of it than any of them, because everything he did was bought with will instead of birth.

The front runners began to fall. The track thinned. And 王冬 — 王冬 was a sight to see, the golden boy running like the iron was made of feathers, lap after lap, strong and tireless and *fast*, until the distance between him and the rest of the class grew so large that the run stopped looking like a race and started looking like an execution. He lapped them. Twice. And then, having proved what everyone already knew — that the golden-haired boy was a different order of creature — he slowed, and looked back, and something complicated passed over his face.

Winning had been boring for a while now.

王冬 had discovered that early — that the world handed him the front of every race and expected gratitude — and he had stopped feeling anything about it, which was its own kind of tired. The iron on his shoulders was nothing. The laps were nothing. He could have run until noon and stopped only because the run was supposed to decide something, and running until noon would decide it too fast and too obviously, and he had seen what happened to boys the room decided were already decided: the room stopped looking at them. He did not want the room to stop looking at him. He simply wanted it to look at him for the right reason, and he had not yet worked out what the right reason was.

So he lapped them. Twice. To be done with it, and because it felt like the honest version of himself — fast and bright and alone at the front where everyone put him.

And then he had slowed, because of the boy at the back.

He did not understand the thin boy. That was the irritating part. Rank eleven, the lowest in the class, running in iron that was heavier for him than for anyone — and he would not stop. 王冬 had watched him for four laps, waiting for the stumble that did not come, and something in his chest had gone strange and tight, the way it went when he saw something he could not immediately have or fix or win.

He glanced at the rail, and the other still figure was there: the dean's grandson, the golden one, leaning on the fence with his hands on the wood like he was holding himself back from something. 王冬 had opinions about the dean's grandson. He had decided, on day one, to dislike him — the way you dislike a mirror that shows you what being put on a pedestal looks like from the outside. He had expected the boy at the rail to watch like a prince watching servants: above it, bored, counting the minutes until his betters were done.

That was not what he saw.

The dean's grandson was not watching like a boy who thought himself too good for the race. He was watching like a boy who *wanted* to be in it — his hands were white on the wood — and had chosen not to be, and was cheering for them anyway, actually cheering, his voice carrying over the clank of the iron. And 王冬 did not know what to do with that, because his whole life had taught him that anyone who could take the front would take the front. He had. He had lapped them twice to prove it. And here was the one boy in the room who could have owned this race the way 王冬 owned it — rank thirty, two purple rings, the whole room knew it — and he had stepped off the track and given it to them, and 王冬 could not decide whether that was the wisest thing he had ever seen or the maddest, and he hated that he could not decide.

He turned his back on the rail and looked at the thin boy again — still running, still grey, still refusing — and made a decision that surprised him by feeling like the first real thing he had done all morning.

He dropped to a walk. Then he stopped, and turned, and stood on the track with his hands on his hips, breathing easily, watching the rest of them struggle. He would wait. He did not know yet what he was waiting for, or why it mattered, but he had learned to trust the tight thing in his chest better than he trusted the crown the room kept trying to put on him.

*He's waiting for someone,* 言天宇 thought. And then, with a strange small ache, *No. He's waiting for* him.

The class broke. One by one, in twos and threes, they fell out and sat or lay on the grass, the iron vests rising and falling like bellows. 徐磊 was already down. The trained pack was down. Even 温宁, her desperate economy finally spent, had sunk to one knee at the edge of the track, head bowed, iron shining with sweat.

And still running — alone now, the only figure left on the grey ribbon of track — was 霍雨浩.

He was not fast. He had never been fast. His legs were thin and his breath was ragged and his face had gone the colour of old paper. But he did not stop. He did not walk. He ran, and the strange thing was that the longer he ran, the steadier he seemed — as if somewhere inside the thin boy, past the exhaustion, something had quietly taken over the running so that the boy himself did not have to choose it anymore. His eyes were fixed and empty and full at once, and 言天宇, watching from the rail, felt the cold at his own spine stir in recognition.

*He's past his wall,* he thought. *He's running on the thing underneath.*

He had never seen it before. He had heard his grandteacher speak of it — the thing a fighter finds when the body is spent and the will has to decide whether to follow — but he had never watched a boy his own age walk into it and keep going. 霍雨浩 ran one more lap. Then another. His legs should have given out three laps ago. They did not give out, because he did not let them.

And then the class began to get up.

It started with 王冬. The golden boy, who had been standing on the track since the run ended, watching 霍雨浩 with an expression 言天宇 could not quite read, suddenly moved. He walked to the track. He began to run again — slowly, beside the thin boy, not ahead of him, matching his pace, refusing to lap him this time. And he said something 言天宇 could not hear, and whatever it was, it made 霍雨浩's head come up.

Then 温宁 got up off her knee. Then 萧萧, who had been standing at the edge of the grass with her arms crossed and her chin high since the thirtieth lap, uncrossed her arms and stepped back onto the track. Then the trained pack, shame-faced, pulled themselves off the grass. Then 徐磊 — and 言天宇 saw it happen, saw the loud boy who resented the walking trophy drag himself upright and back onto the track with his face twisted, not with pain but with something that looked almost like pride. One by one, the whole of 一班 rose and ran again, and this time they did not run as individuals. They ran strung out behind the two figures at the front — the golden boy and the thin one — a long, ragged, iron-clad tail following the boy who had refused to stop.

言天宇 stood at the rail with his hands gripping the wood, and felt something enormous move in his chest.

He had wanted to run. All morning he had wanted to run — not to win, not to show them, but just to be *in* it, to be one of them instead of the one who had stepped off the track. His furnaces had burned under his ribs, warm and ready, and it had taken every scrap of the discipline 穆恩 had beaten into him over a decade to hold to the choice he had made — because part of him, the loudest part, had wanted to vault the fence and run with them so badly it ached.

And now he understood why 周漪 had put him here.

*The one the room turns to,* she had said. He had thought it meant the strongest. He had been wrong. It meant the one who makes the others able — the one whose refusal to stop gives the room permission to refuse to stop. That was not 王冬, for all his golden strength. That was not the trained pack, for all their clever pacing.

It was the thin boy at the very back of the class, running on nothing but will, who had decided a long time ago that he would rather die than stop.

周漪 had seen it on the hundred-lap day. She had said it to 言天宇 in the shade of the training hall, and he had not understood, not really, not until now:

*A man being forged.*

The run ended when 周漪 raised her hand. The class staggered to a halt — all of them, iron-clad and heaving, with the golden boy and the thin one at their head — and for a long moment the only sound was the sound of a whole roomful of children breathing like they had forgotten how air worked.

Then 周漪 walked onto the track, and she did not look at the class. She looked at the thin boy, swaying on his feet at the front of them all, and she said, in a voice that was almost gentle:

"霍雨浩. You are the 班长 of 新生一班."

The thin boy blinked. His mouth opened. No sound came out. And behind him, the class — the whole of 一班, the loud and the quiet and the proud and the afraid — broke into a cheer so loud it startled birds off the roof of the training hall.

言天宇 did not cheer. He stood at the rail with his hands gripping the wood, and he watched 霍雨浩 sway, and he watched 王冬 step in and catch the thin boy before he fell, and he watched the class surge around them, and he felt the enormous thing in his chest crack open into something that was almost unbearable.

*No one was eliminated,* he thought. *He carried the whole room across.*

And then, because he was who he was, he let go of the rail, walked to the nearest water trough, filled a cup, and carried it to the edge of the crowd — where he stood, waiting, until the thin boy's dazed eyes found him over the heads of the cheering class.

言天宇 raised the cup. A small salute. A small thing, from a boy who had stepped aside from this race so it could belong to the room — to the boy who had just won it for them all.

霍雨浩 looked at him for a long moment. And then, over the noise, he smiled — the real smile, the one that cracked the careful mask — and he nodded.

It was not a victory 言天宇 had any share in.

It was, he thought, the finest thing he had ever chosen to watch instead of win.

## Part Three — The Report

The class dispersed in triumph, carrying their new 班长 on their shoulders halfway to the dining hall before 周漪's voice sent them scattering like pigeons. The morning's iron was piled in a clanking heap. The track lay empty and grey, already forgetting the shape of the class running.

周漪 stood at the rail where 言天宇 had stood, looking out at the empty track, and did not turn when he approached.

"Report," she said.

言天宇 considered for a long moment how to answer. Then he gave her the truth, because she had earned it and because it was the only answer that would satisfy her anyway.

"徐磊 folded in two laps and was ashamed of it," he said. "He'll fold again, but he'll hate himself more each time, and one day the hating will outweigh the folding and he'll stand. 温宁 husbanded every breath and lasted longer than anyone without a soul advantage. She'll survive this term. She won't lead it. The trained pack covered each other's pacing — clever, and useless, because the run wasn't asking for cleverness." He paused. "王冬 could have run until noon. He chose to stop and wait. He didn't know he was waiting — he thought he was being contemptuous. But he was waiting for 霍雨浩, and when 霍雨浩 wouldn't stop, the waiting turned into running beside him."

He stopped.

"And 霍雨浩," he said, "ran past the place where the body gives out and kept running on something else. I don't know what it is. I've never seen it before. But it's real, and it's the strongest thing I've ever watched a boy my age do."

周漪 was silent for a long moment.

"You watched him like he was a beast in a forest," she said finally. "Like 穆恩 taught you."

言天宇 did not ask how she knew his teacher's name. Everyone in the academy knew whose student the golden grandson was. It was one more thing that set him apart.

"Yes," he said.

"And what did you feel, watching him?" 周漪 turned to face him, and her eyes were sharp and unreadable. "Envy? He has nothing you don't. He has less than you — no name to open doors, no bloodline to carry him, no grandfather's house to catch him when he falls."

言天宇 was very still.

"—and yet he has the one thing you have never had to find," 周漪 finished, and her voice was quiet. "The thing that decides, when the body is spent and the will is the only fuel left, whether the will is enough. You have never been spent, 言天宇. You have never once in your life run on empty, because your fires never run empty. Do you understand what I am telling you?"

He understood. He had understood it since the shade of the training hall, since she had said *a man being forged*, and the words had lodged in him like a splinter.

"You're telling me," he said slowly, "that I don't know what I'm made of. Because I've never had to be spent to find out."

周漪 nodded once. "Your grandfather sent you here to learn the room. And you have — better than any child I've taught. But the room was never the lesson, boy. The lesson is the wall. Every child on this track is climbing, and they will spend themselves against their own ceilings a hundred times before they're grown — that's how they'll learn what they're made of. You walked in with your ceiling already higher than this room will ever reach, and a locked door at the top of it. When that door opens — and it will, when your grandteacher says the beast is ready — there will be nothing between you and the edge of yourself but the hunt. And you will not know what you are made of, because you will never have been spent to find out." She looked at him, and for a moment something almost like kindness crossed her hard face. "That is what this year is for. The thin boy found the thing this morning that only shows itself when there is nothing left. You are going to need that thing, one day, more than any of them — because when you stand at your own wall, there will be no room to catch you. So watch it now, while it's cheap. Learn its shape. So you know it when it's all you have left."

She walked past him, toward the training hall, and her voice drifted back.

"Go eat, 言天宇. A hungry boy is a fool. A hungry furnace is a weapon. Learn the difference."

He stood at the rail for a long time after she was gone, with the empty track in front of him and the echo of the class's cheers in his ears, and felt the shadow underneath the warmth stir again — and, beneath the shadow, something else. Something that had not been there before.

Hunger.

Not the furnace hunger, the deep animal appetite that had been his companion since before he could speak. Something quieter. Something that wanted, with a ferocity that surprised him, to one day stand at the edge of himself — in his own hunt, at his own wall, where no fire stood behind his — and find out what he was made of.

## Part Four — The Fish and the Fire

That evening, the dining hall was loud with the particular joy of a room that had survived something together.

言天宇 found 霍雨浩 not in the dining hall but on the stone steps behind it, sitting alone with his back against the wall and his 班长's pride nowhere in evidence. The thin boy looked smaller in the evening light, hollowed out by the morning's run, and his hands were busy with something 言天宇 recognized before he got close: a fish. A small, silver fish, cleaned and ready, lying on a scrap of cloth across the boy's knees.

"You skipped the feast," 言天宇 said, sitting down a respectful distance away. "The whole class is in there toasting your name. 王冬 is holding court about how he totally meant to slow down for you."

"王冬 talks a lot when he's embarrassed." 霍雨浩 did not look up. His fingers worked the fish with quick, precise motions. "And I'm not hungry for noise. I'm hungry for this." He paused, and a ghost of the real smile crossed his face. "Also I owed you fish. From the market. You said I cook better than the hall — I'm proving it."

言天宇's stomach made a sound that was entirely undignified. "That," he said, "is the kindest thing anyone has said to me all term."

"Don't get used to it."

They sat in comfortable silence while 霍雨浩 built a small fire in the sheltered corner behind the steps — quick, practiced, the fire catching on the first try — and set the fish over it. The smell was extraordinary. 言天宇 watched the thin boy's hands and thought about how many hungers a boy could carry, and how most of them showed in the hands.

"Why didn't you run this morning?" 霍雨浩 asked, without looking up from the fish.

言天宇 had known the question was coming. He had not decided how much of the truth to give. In the end, he gave most of it, because the thin boy had earned the same courtesy 周漪 had.

"The run was a selection," he said. "It was for finding out which of you would lead and which would break. I'm not… eligible. For the selections. My grandfather signed me into this class to learn the room, not to win it." He smiled, and it was almost true. "Also, Teacher 周漪 was right. I would have won it, and winning it would have taught no one anything — least of all me."

霍雨浩 turned the fish. "You make it sound like a punishment."

"It is," 言天宇 said quietly. "A gentle one. The kind my grandfather gives me instead of the ones he gives other people."

A long silence. The fish sizzled.

"Can I ask you something?" 霍雨浩 said. "Something honest?"

"You can ask me anything. I may not answer it honestly, but you can ask."

"Do you resent it?" The thin boy finally looked up, and his dark eyes were very tired and very clear. "Being the only one in this room who can't climb. Everyone here is fighting their way up a ladder — 王冬's lapping it, 萧萧's rising through it, I'm clawing my way up it one rung at a time. And you're eleven years old, rank thirty, and the whole world is in front of you — except it isn't, because every door is locked until your grandteacher decides you're ready, and nobody will tell you when that is." He said it flatly, without self-pity, a simple statement of fact. "I've spent my whole life being locked out of rooms for being too weak. You're locked out of the whole climb for being too strong for it. Doesn't that make you want to claw something?"

言天宇 looked at the small fire, at the fish turning gold over it, and considered the question the way he considered most questions about himself — from the outside, as if the boy being asked were someone he was trying to understand.

"Sometimes," he said. "And it's not quite the way you tell it, either. Nobody locked the door on me — it's locked from my side, and my grandteacher holds the key until the day I'm ready for what's behind it. The world didn't bench me from this morning's race; I stepped back from it myself, because the 班长's seat is the room's to give itself — and the room gave it to the right boy. The one thing the world ever truly did to me was make me different — and when I was younger, I used to dig my nails into my palms about that. My grandfather caught me once and asked what I was doing. I told him I was practicing for the day I could claw the world's eyes out for making me different." He smiled. "He said, 'Good. Aim well. And when you're done, come back and tell me what the world looked like through its own sockets — because you'll have missed the whole point if you only ever look from inside your own head.'"

霍雨浩 stared at him. "That's the most disturbing grandfatherly advice I've ever heard."

"It works, though." 言天宇 shrugged. "That's the thing about my grandfather — he never tells you the comfortable version. He tells you the version that survives contact with the world. And somewhere in it, I stopped wanting to claw anything and started wanting to understand the room instead. To be the one who knows what it's for." He looked at the thin boy across the fire. "I chose not to run this morning, 霍雨浩. But I got to watch a boy with nothing decide he would rather die than stop — and then lift a whole roomful of children onto his back and carry them across the line with him. Do you know how rare that is?"

霍雨浩's hands stilled on the fish.

"You keep saying that," he said quietly. "That what I did is rare. But I just… ran. I couldn't stop. If I stopped, I'd be out, and if I was out, I'd have nothing — no school, no future, no way to—" He stopped himself. "I just ran because I couldn't afford to stop."

言天宇 leaned forward, and for just a moment the golden warmth in his eyes went very serious. "That's what I mean. Everyone in that class could afford to stop. They had families, or money, or names, or the luxury of giving up and going home to something. You didn't. You ran because stopping wasn't a choice you were allowed to make — and that made you the only one in the room who couldn't be broken by the gate." He sat back. "The gate doesn't eat the strong, 班长. It eats the ones who can afford to lose. You're the only one in that room who can't. That's not weakness. That's the most dangerous thing a person can be."

霍雨浩 was very still. The fish hissed and spat over the fire, and neither of them moved to turn it.

"...You're strange," 霍雨浩 said finally, in exactly the same tone he had used in the dining hall weeks ago, when 言天宇 had first sat down across from him.

"I'm aware." The warmth came back into 言天宇's face, and he grinned. "Now turn the fish before you burn my dinner. I've been looking forward to this all day."

The fish was, as promised, better than anything the hall served. They ate in silence, then in companionable talk — 霍雨浩 telling a story about 唐雅 trying to teach him to sell and failing, 言天宇 telling a story about 玄老 stealing his breakfast that made 霍雨浩 laugh so hard he choked on a bone. And when the fire had burned low and the fish was gone, 霍雨浩 looked at him across the embers with an expression that was hard to read.

"Why do you do this?" he asked. "The market. The dining hall. The fire. You're the dean's grandson, you could sit anywhere, eat anything, be anyone. Why do you keep coming back to sit with the 班长 who has nothing?"

言天宇 thought about it. And then, because the fire was low and the night was honest, he said:

"Because you're the first person my age who's ever looked at me and not seen the trophy. You saw me at the market — the day I was just a boy buying fish. You didn't know who I was, and you treated me like a person anyway. Do you know how long it's been since someone did that?" He smiled, and there was something real and raw at the edge of it. "My whole life, people have looked at me and seen my grandfather's blood, or my rank, or my rings, or what I could do for them. You looked at me and saw a customer. A person. Someone worth sharing a fish with."

霍雨浩 was silent for a long moment.

"That's the saddest thing I've ever heard," he said quietly. "And I've heard some sad things."

"It's not sad," 言天宇 said. "It's just the price. And you," he pointed at the thin boy with a fish bone, "are the best payment I've received in eleven years. So yes, I keep coming back. You're the only person in this academy who treats me like I'm not made of gold. It's restful."

A clatter of footsteps on the steps above them. 王冬's voice, aggrieved and trying very hard not to be relieved:

"There you are! The whole hall is looking for the 班长, and he's—" The golden boy came around the corner, saw the fire, saw the fish bones, saw 言天宇, and stopped. His expression cycled through several emotions and settled on annoyed. "You're having a private fish party. Without me. After I *carried* him across the finish line."

"You stopped to rest," 霍雨浩 said mildly. "You were waiting for me to die."

"I was *pacing* you—" 王冬 threw himself down on the step beside them with a huff, and then, grudgingly, produced a wrapped bundle from inside his jacket. "Here. I saved you food from the feast. The golden grandson can eat his fancy fish; the rest of us need real food." He shoved the bundle at 霍雨浩, who caught it, and then looked at 言天宇 with an expression that was trying to be hostile and failing. "You. Golden Grandson. I've decided I don't hate you."

"High praise," 言天宇 said gravely. "I shall treasure it."

"Don't get used to it." 王冬 looked away, and his ears went slightly red. "But… what you said this morning. About the room. And what you did after — the water, the—" He waved a hand, frustrated. "Whatever. You're all right. For a walking trophy."

言天宇 blinked. And then he laughed — a real laugh, bright and warm in the gathering dark.

"You called me that too," he said. "Everyone calls me that. But you're the first one who's ever said it like it wasn't a fence."

王冬's ears went redder. "I'm not— that's not— shut up. Eat your fish."

The fire crackled. Three boys sat on the stone steps behind the dining hall — the golden grandson whose door had not opened yet, the 班长 who had nothing but will, and the golden-haired boy who had chosen his friend over his pride — and for a while, none of them said anything at all, and it was the most comfortable silence any of them had sat in for a long time.

A shadow fell across the steps.

"I *knew* it," said 唐雅, appearing around the corner with 贝贝 a step behind her, her bright face alight with unholy glee. "The golden grandson, sitting at my disciple's little fire, eating fish he didn't cook. You know what this means, don't you?"

"唐雅," 霍雨浩 said, with the particular weariness of a boy who had learned that sentence never meant anything good. "Please don't."

"It means," 唐雅 declared, sweeping forward and dropping into a crouch in front of 言天宇, "that it's time for the Tang Sect's recruitment pitch. Again. For the third time this month. 贝贝, back me up."

"门主," 贝贝 said apologetically, "he's said no twice already."

"He said *not yet*," 唐雅 corrected, without taking her eyes off 言天宇. "That's not no. That's a maybe wearing a disguise." She leaned in, and for a moment the bright-sad thing at the heart of her — the girl who was trying to rebuild a dead sect with her bare hands and her two disciples and nothing else — flickered through her face like a candle in wind. "You know what the Tang Sect was, don't you, little phoenix? The greatest in the world. Weapons that could pierce a 封号斗罗. Techniques that made kings kneel. And then it fell, and everyone forgot, and now it's just me and these two idiots, trying to build it back from nothing." She smiled, and it was warm and fierce and terribly young. "You're strong. Stronger than anyone in this academy your age. But strength isn't a purpose, golden boy. It's a tool looking for a hand. Come hold our tools. Help us build something that outlives the both of us."

言天宇 looked at her for a long moment. At the firelight in her eyes. At the two boys behind her — the thin 班长 who had chosen her dying sect over every easy road, and the golden-haired one who had chosen them. And he felt, for the first time in his life, the strange pull of belonging to something that was not his grandfather's shadow.

"I'll think about it," he said.

唐雅's grin could have lit the whole night market. "That's all I need. I'll wear you down, little phoenix. I wore down the 班长 in half a day, and he's made of rock."

"She did," 霍雨浩 confirmed, deadpan. "It was humiliating."

"I wasn't humiliated," 王冬 said hotly. "I don't even go here."

"Yet." 唐雅 fixed him with a look that could have appraised livestock. "You're next, golden-hair. First I get the phoenix, then I get the peacock, and the Tang Sect rises again on the backs of very pretty birds."

"That's not how sects work!" 王冬 said.

"It is now," 唐雅 said serenely. "There's a debt and a dream and two idiots—" she gestured at 贝贝 and 霍雨浩— "and soon, more. Come on, 贝贝. We have a convert to cultivate. Let the boys digest their feelings." She vanished around the corner, 贝贝 following with a small, warm nod to 言天宇 that said more than words.

The fire crackled. 言天宇 sat very still, watching the spot where she had been.

"She's going to ask you again tomorrow," 霍雨浩 said. "And the day after. And every day until you say yes or she dies of old age. Fair warning."

言天宇 smiled. "I know."

"So? Are you going to join?"

言天宇 looked at the thin boy across the embers — the boy who had nothing, and had chosen to give that nothing to a dying dream — and felt something settle in his chest that he did not have a name for yet.

"I don't know," he said honestly. "But for the first time in my life, I think I might want to belong to something that isn't my grandfather's."

The fire burned low. The night deepened. And somewhere above them, in the dark of the academy, an old man who had watched the whole scene from a rooftop no one had thought to check smiled to himself, and vanished into the shadows without a sound.

## Part Five — The Wall and the Old Man

言天宇 did not go to his dormitory that night. He went, instead, to the place he always went when the weight in his chest grew too heavy to carry through a corridor of sleeping boys: the quiet wall at the edge of the outer yard, where the stones met the dark and no one ever came.

The furnace hunger was awake tonight. It had been building all day — through the iron-run morning, through the report, through the fish and the fire and the warmth of the evening — a deep, animal pull in his chest that had nothing to do with food and everything to do with what he was. He leaned his forehead against the cool stone and let the hunger run through him, feeling it lap against the inside of his rank-thirty wall like a tide against a seawall.

*Thirty,* he thought. *You've been thirty since last winter. The wall doesn't move. The world's law: no third ring, no thirty-first level. And no third ring until you bring it something worthy of opening for.*

A sound behind him. Not a footstep — no one in the academy except two people could approach him like that, and only one of them would be out this late.

"You chose well today," said 穆恩's voice, from the darkness. "Not running that race — that took more spine than running it ever would. Any fool with your rings could have won it. It took a different kind of boy to see that winning it would have taught the room nothing, and stepped aside so it could find its own heart." A pause, and something almost like warmth in the old voice. "That is the doctrine I have been beating into you for a decade, boy. Today you lived it without being asked."

言天宇 did not turn. "Grandteacher."

The old man materialized out of the shadow beside him — not walking, just there, as if the dark had decided to give him up. He was small and wizened and looked like a beggar, and every 封号斗罗 on the continent would have dropped to one knee if they had known who was standing in the dark beside the boy. He leaned against the wall next to 言天宇, and for a long moment neither of them spoke.

"The thin boy," 穆恩 said finally. "The new 班长. He found it today, didn't he? The thing underneath."

言天宇 nodded. "I've never seen it before. I didn't know it existed until I watched him walk into it."

"Everyone has it," 穆恩 said. "The well that only opens when everything else is empty. Most people live and die without ever finding theirs, because they're never pushed deep enough to need it." He was quiet for a moment. "I found mine when I was younger than you, boy. I would not wish the finding on anyone. But I would not trade it for anything either, because it is the only thing that has ever been truly mine."

言天宇 turned to look at him. The old man's face was unreadable in the dark, but there was something in his voice tonight — a weight 言天宇 had heard growing, slowly, over the past year, like a clock running down. He did not know what it meant. He only knew it made his chest tight.

"Grandteacher," he said. "The third ring. I've been waiting—"

"I know." 穆恩 cut him off, gently. "You've been waiting since winter. You feel the wall in your chest every day, and you want to bring it something worthy, and you want to *prove* —" He stopped. "Prove what, 言天宇?"

言天宇 opened his mouth. Closed it. The answer was there, in the dark, and he did not want to say it.

"Prove that I'm more than a trophy," he said finally, very quietly. "That I'm not just my grandfather's blood and my rings and my rank. That I can be spent and still stand. That I have the thing the thin boy has." He laughed, and it came out raw. "周漪 said I've never been forged. She's right. I've been carved and polished and set on a shelf, and I've never once been through the fire that makes a person real."

穆恩 was silent for a long time.

"Then hear me," he said, and his voice was old and heavy and full of the weight of a century. "The beast that will give you your third ring is not prey, 言天宇. It is a contender. It is old enough to have its own will, its own hunger, its own claim on the world — and when you try to take it into yourself, it will not dissolve quietly and nourish you the way the small ones did. It will fight you. It will try to change you, to mark you, to make you over in its own image. And whether it succeeds or you do, nothing about you will ever be the same again."

He turned to look at the boy, and his eyes in the dark were old and kind and terribly sad.

"That is not a punishment, boy. That is the gift you have been waiting for. The small ones were food — they made you strong, but they did not make you *you*. The contender will make you choose, for the first time in your life, what you are. Not what your blood is. Not what your grandfather made. What *you* decide to be, when something that could unmake you is inside you, fighting for the wheel."

言天宇's heart was beating hard. The hunger in his chest roared and strained against the wall, and he breathed it down with the habit of a decade.

"When?" he asked. "Grandteacher, when?"

"Not yet." 穆恩's voice was gentle but final. "The beast is not ready, and neither are you. There is a thing you must learn first — the thing you watched the thin boy find this morning, the thing that only shows itself when there is nothing left. And there is a hunt to be arranged that must happen where no eyes can follow, because what you will do at that wall is not something the world is ready to see." He paused. "When the time comes, I will come for you. Until then — you are where you need to be. In that room. Learning the room — and learning, from the boy who found it today, the shape of the thing you will one day need to find in yourself."

The old man pushed off the wall, and for a moment he swayed — a tiny, unguarded sway, like a candle guttering — and then he steadied, and the moment was gone, and 言天宇 told himself he had imagined it.

"Grandteacher," he said, on impulse. "Will you be there? When I take the ring?"

穆恩 was quiet for a long moment. And then he smiled — a real smile, warm and old and full of a century of love.

"I will be wherever you need me to be, boy," he said. "That is what teachers are for."

He turned and walked into the darkness, and was gone.

言天宇 stood alone at the quiet wall, with the furnace hunger roaring in his chest and the shadow cold asleep at his spine, and felt something settle in him — a decision, older than words, taking root in the dark.

He would not rush the beast. He would not rush the wall. He would learn the thing the thin boy had found — the thing that only shows itself when there is nothing left — and he would learn to be the room instead of the trophy in it. And when the day came — when the contender was inside him, fighting for the wheel — he would decide what he became.

Not his blood. Not his grandfather. Not the beast.

Him.

Above him, the stars turned over the sleeping academy. Somewhere in the dormitories, the new 班长 of 新生一班 was probably not sleeping either — but he was not alone anymore, and neither, 言天宇 realized with a strange warmth, was he.

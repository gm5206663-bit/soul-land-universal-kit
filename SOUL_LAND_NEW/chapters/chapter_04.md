## Part One — The Fisherman's Hour

The week after the 铁衣 run, 新生一班 learned that being saved was not the same as being safe.

The cheer that had carried 霍雨浩 off the track had faded by the next dawn. 周漪 was back to her counting, her drills, her flat arithmetic of who could still move when the gate behind them kept closing, and the room settled into the particular rhythm of a class that knew most of its names would not be there by the 考核. The strong trained. The middle prayed. And the ones at the bottom — the ones who had eaten last their whole lives — made themselves small and fast and hoped not to be seen.

言天宇 saw them. He always saw them. It was the one thing 穆恩's training and his grandfather's lessons and eleven years of being the most watched boy in the academy had given him that no rank could: he knew, by the end of any week, which faces would not survive the next.

That Friday morning, 周漪 called the roll in her flat way, and her voice did not change when she read out the two names she had written a small mark beside. But 言天宇 heard the mark. He watched 温宁's shoulders draw up, paper-thin under her robes — she had been eating less all week, he had noticed, the way he noticed everything — and he watched 徐磊 not look at anyone, which for 徐磊 was the loudest possible confession that something was wrong.

*The gate eats the ones who can afford to lose,* he had told 霍雨浩 by the fire. He had meant it as a compliment. It occurred to him now, watching 温宁 hold her breath through her own name, that he had never once had to learn what it felt like to be one of the ones who couldn't afford to.

He had money. He had a name. He had a grandfather's house and a grandmother's table and a stipend that, by his grandfather's rule, was exactly the same modest allowance as any student of 史莱克 — no more, no less, because the golden grandson must learn what things cost — but it was still *his*, regular and safe, and it bought more than bread. He had never once in his life eaten last.

He thought about that all day. It sat under his ribs like a small, uncomfortable stone.

That evening, as the drills ended and the hall filled with the sound of a hundred hungry children eating, 唐雅 found him — materializing out of the crowd with the particular energy of a girl who had a plan and expected the world to applaud it.

"Little phoenix," she said, dropping onto the bench across from him, "I need you to do something for the Tang Sect."

"Absolutely not," 言天宇 said, without looking up from his supper.

"You don't even know what it is."

"I know it involves either my money, my name, or my becoming a disciple, and I've learned to say no to all three at speed."

唐雅's grin widened. "Wrong on all counts. I need you to *eat.*" She leaned forward, eyes alight. "Tomorrow night, the 班长 starts selling his 秘制烤鱼 at the north-gate market. A real stall. Wood, salt, a spot at the wall where the light's good — the whole thing. I've been trying to teach that boy to sell for weeks, and he's hopeless at the selling and a genius at the fish, so I've stopped trying to fix the wrong half. He cooks. I manage. You," she pointed at him with a chopstick, "come be a customer. Buy a fish. Look impressed. Tell everyone in earshot that the 班长 of 新生一班 roasts a better fish than any stall in the market."

"That's not a favour, that's advertising."

"It's *both*, and you owe me," 唐雅 said serenely. "I've only asked you to join my dying sect, like, four times. The least you can do is eat my disciple's cooking in public and make envious noises."

言天宇 looked at her — at the bright, fierce, terribly young face of a girl who was trying to rebuild a dead sect with a fish stall and a borrowed spot of wall — and felt the familiar pull, the one he had felt by 霍雨浩's fire the night the 铁衣 run ended. Belonging to something that was not his grandfather's shadow. He had told 霍雨浩 he might want that, someday.

"I'll come," he said. "But I'm not joining your sect."

"Yet," 唐雅 corrected, with the serene confidence of a girl who had worn down rock before. "See you tomorrow, little phoenix. Bring your appetite — I've heard it's famous."

She was gone into the crowd before he could answer, leaving him alone at his table with a half-finished supper and a thought that had been growing in him since the morning's roll call.

He had been in the room for weeks now — a name on its roll like any other, though he was not on its ladder this year. He had run its culls beside them and learned its names, and he had stood at the track's rail on the one morning that was not his and watched a thin boy carry the whole of it across a line. And he had begun to understand, with the particular clarity of a boy who was good at rooms, that a room was not something you watched over. It was something you fed.

*Tomorrow,* he thought, *I'll buy the 班长's fish.*

*And the day after — I'm going to find out what it costs to feed the ones who eat last.*

He finished his supper, bowed to the kitchen staff on the way out — habit, warmth, the small rituals that made him *him* — and walked through the dusk of the outer court with his hands in his sleeves, calculating. His allowance for the week. The price of fish at the north gate after the market's supper rush, when the day's catch went cheap because no one wanted it to spoil. The faces he would feed, and what it would take to make them come.

It was a small arithmetic, compared to the ones his grandfather's tutors had drilled into him. But it was the first arithmetic in his life that had ever been his own.

## Part Two — Twenty Fish

The north-gate market at dusk smelled like the whole world cooking, and 言天宇 moved through it like a boy who had been coming here his whole life, because he had — with 贝贝, first, small enough to be carried, then alone, learning which stalls were honest and which weighed light and which old women would sell a student the day's crooked fish for next to nothing because they liked the way he said thank you.

The first fish sold before 霍雨浩 had finished setting up the brazier.

That was the thing about his cooking — it did the selling for him. 唐雅 had laughed at him all week for being hopeless at prices and patter and the thousand small lies of the market, and she was right, he was; he could not look a customer in the eye and charge them double for a smaller fish, the way the old vendors did, because every time he tried he heard his mother's voice saying *be honest, be honest, it's all we have.* So he did the only thing he was good at. He cooked. And the smell that came off his brazier — clean salt and oil and the particular dark magic of fish roasted over a careful fire, learned in a lifetime of cooking because if he did not cook, nobody ate — did the selling for him.

The crowd arrived like the smell had a hook in it.

一班 classmates first — they knew the smell from the steps behind the dining hall, and they came running with coins and ribbing about the 班长 moonlighting as a street vendor. Then strangers. Then a knot of older students, drawn by the commotion and staying for the fish. 霍雨浩's hands moved without his permission, gutting, salting, turning, and the coins piled up in the little clay bowl 唐雅 had set out, and for the first time in his life he understood what it felt like to make something the world wanted.

He kept two fish back.

Not for a customer. For 唐雅 — because she had found the wood and the salt and the spot, and because she was trying to rebuild a sect that had died before she was born, and because the two of them had a deal about the last two fish of every night, and 霍雨浩 had learned young that the people who keep their small promises are the only people worth keeping.

The crowd had thinned to its last greedy stragglers when the girl arrived.

He noticed her the way you notice a fire in a dark room — not because he wanted to, but because the whole market seemed to lean toward her. She was older than him, tall and graceful, with a face that the murmurs around him identified before she said a word: the 校花. The school flower of 史莱克. Her name was something he had heard in the dormitory, always in the same breath as admiration and the same warning that she was not to be approached.

She approached him.

"Is that the last of it?" She nodded at the two fish on the board, and her voice was cool and pleasant, the voice of someone used to getting what she asked for.

霍雨浩 looked at the fish. Then at the clay bowl, where the coins had stopped because the night was done. Then at the girl, who was beautiful and would be beautiful tomorrow, and at the thought of 唐雅's face when she came back from the errand she had run and found her fish gone.

"Sorry," he said. "These two are taken."

The girl's eyebrows went up. "I'll pay double."

"They're not for sale at any price. They're for someone."

For a moment the school-flower just looked at him, unused to the word *no* in that particular register. Then her chin went up a fraction — not angry, he thought; something else, something he could not name and did not have time to — and she said, "Then I'll come back tomorrow," and turned away.

That should have been the end of it.

But the market is a place where other people's wishes are loud, and the girl's admirers had been watching, and one of them — a big, loud upper-year with the swagger of a boy who had never once been told no by anyone smaller than a teacher — stepped into the space she had left.

"Name your price, kid." His voice carried. "For both of them."

"Not for sale," 霍雨浩 said again, and kept his voice even, the way he had learned to keep it even when men twice his size stood over his mother's grave and told him to move along.

"Everything's for sale." The boy reached across the brazier — casual, like he was picking fruit from a stall he owned — and closed his hand around both fish.

They were gone before 霍雨浩 could move. His hands were still wet from the last fish he had cleaned, and his rank-eleven body was still a rank-eleven body, and the fury that went through him was white and useless, because he could not fight an upper-year and he could not outrun him and the fish were 唐雅's and they were *gone*—

"Put them down, 徐三石."

The voice came from behind him, quiet and utterly certain, and the crowd around the stall went very still. 霍雨浩 knew that voice. Everyone in the outer court knew that voice — the 唐门's 大师兄, gentle as morning light and, right now, with something underneath the gentleness that made the big upper-year's hand freeze halfway to his chest.

Across the market, 言天宇 had been coming through the crowd with his own evening's purchase wrapped in cloth under his arm — two of the old fish-seller's crooked ones, bought at the closing price, chosen deliberately because nobody else would buy them and a fish did not care if it was straight — when the noise changed.

He had heard the commotion at the new stall from three stalls away, and he had smiled, because of course it was 霍雨浩's stall, of course the boy who cooked like an angel had drawn a crowd to riot over. But the smile had died when he got close enough to see who was standing at the centre of it.

The tall upper-year held two fish in one hand like a trophy. 贝贝 stood across from him with his gentle face gone still in a way 言天宇 had learned to recognize — the stillness of a very kind person who had decided that being kind was no longer the answer. 唐雅 was at 贝贝's shoulder, blazing, and being held back by his arm with the particular ease of a man who had done it a thousand times. And at the edge of the crowd, moving away with her chin high and her back very straight, was the girl the whispers called the 校花 — the one who had started it all by being refused something, 言天宇 guessed from the fragments of talk around him, and who was now walking out of her own story while the boys fought over the shape of it.

He saw her face in the lamplight, just for a moment. And he saw, with the particular clarity that had been his gift and his burden since he could remember, the thing underneath the famous beauty — the thing nobody in that crowd was looking at because the crown was too bright to see past.

*Someone decided her whole life for her the day she got that face,* he thought. *And she hates it, and she can't say so, because everyone would think she was lying.*

The thought was quick, and true, and gone — because 贝贝 caught his eye across the crowd, and the look on his face was not the one he wore for 徐三石. It was the one he wore for family, and it said, very clearly: *not you. Not tonight. Go.*

言天宇's feet were already moving toward the stall before the look landed — toward 贝贝, toward 唐雅, toward the tall boy with the stolen fish — because that was what you did when your people were in a fight and you were the strongest thing in the market, and the fact that he was eleven and the tall boy was an upper-year was an arithmetic problem his body had never once stopped to solve.

A hand closed on his sleeve.

"小宇." 唐雅's voice, low and fierce, right beside him — she had left the circle and come to him in the crowd without anyone noticing, and her grip was iron. "No."

"They took 贝贝哥's fish—"

"They took *the 班长's* fish, and 贝贝 is handling it, and you," she said, and her eyes were bright and hard and terribly fond all at once, "are going to walk away from this market right now, with your ugly fish, and you are going to pretend you never saw any of this."

"唐雅—"

"I mean it, little sun." Her voice cracked once, on the name. "This is a street fight between upper-years and the dean's grandson is *not* going to be in it. Do you understand what happens to 贝贝 if the dean's grandson gets seen swinging in a market brawl? Do you understand what happens to your grandfather's experiment — the no-favours, earn-the-gate, one-student-one-stipend boy — if the whole city watches him fight an upper-year over two fish?" She shook his sleeve, once. "You are the one thing in this market that has to stay out of this. Because you're the one thing that would make it a *scandal* instead of a story."

言天宇 stood very still in the noise of the market, with his grandfather's name hanging in the air between them like a chain he had been carrying his whole life and had somehow forgotten he was wearing.

He looked past 唐雅's shoulder. 贝贝 was saying something low to 徐三石, and the crowd was drawing back from the two of them like water from a stone, and the tall upper-year's face was cycling through arrogance and uncertainty and something that looked almost like respect. The fish were still in his hand. The girl was gone.

*You're the one thing that has to stay out of it.*

Slowly, 言天宇 uncurled his hand from where it had tightened on the cloth-wrapped fish. He looked at 唐雅 — at the girl who was trying to rebuild a dead sect, who had pulled him out of a fight to protect *him*, who had called him *her* little sun while she shoved him out of her world's fire — and he smiled, and the smile was real, and it cost him something he did not have a name for yet.

"Fine," he said. "But tell 贝贝哥 he owes me dinner. And tell the 班长," he added, and his voice was steady, "that I came for the fish, and I'll come back tomorrow, and the day after, until he stops selling. His cooking's the only honest thing in this market."

唐雅's grip loosened. Her face did something complicated — relief and worry and a fierce bright love fighting for space — and then she shoved him, gently, toward the lane.

"Go home, little phoenix. I'll tell him you said so."

He went.

Behind him, as he walked down the darkening lane with his ugly fish wrapped in cloth, the market's talk followed him on the night air — the names carried on murmurs like sparks: 蓝电霸王龙. 玄冥龟. The 斗魂区. He did not turn around. He walked until the noise faded, and the lamps thinned, and the walls of the academy rose ahead of him in the dark, and he thought about how many ways there were to be told that the one thing you had to offer was the one thing you were not allowed to give.

#### Minor POV — the old fish-seller (Law 3: the legend in an outside voice — a stranger who owes 言天宇 nothing, and gains something from having watched)

The old woman who sold the day's crooked fish at the north gate had been watching the academy's golden boy for nearly a year now.

She did not know he was the golden boy, not by that name. She knew him as the polite one — the one who had first come to her stall on a senior's shoulders, years ago, small enough to be carried and already saying thank you like it meant something. She had watched him grow in the lamplight of her stall: watched him learn which fish to buy and which to let go, watched him hand over his coins without haggling even when she knew, because she was old and sharp and had sold fish for forty years, that he could not quite afford what he was paying.

She had wondered about him, in the idle way of the old. A student of 史莱克 — she could tell by the robes — who bought the fish nobody else wanted and paid full price for them and never once complained about the smell or the crooked backs or the way they flopped. A boy who ate last, she had decided early, or who fed someone who did.

Tonight he had come late, when her stall was nearly bare, and he had pointed at the two ugly ones at the bottom of the basket — the ones she had been about to take home for her own pot, because no one would buy them — and he had asked, in that careful polite way of his, what she wanted for them.

She had told him, half out of spite, twice what they were worth.

He had paid it without blinking. And then — because she was old, and she had earned the right to pry, and because something about the way he stood there in the lamplight with his too-thin robes and his too-steady eyes made her ask — she had said:

"What's a student doing buying fish like these? You could afford the good ones. I've seen you pass them by."

The boy had looked at the fish in his hands for a moment. Then he had smiled — a warm, tired, entirely unguarded smile, the kind a boy should not be able to produce at his age, the kind that made the old woman think of her own son before the war took him — and he had said:

"These are for the ones who eat last."

She had not known what that meant. She still did not, not exactly. But she had watched him walk away into the dark, two ugly fish wrapped in his arms like they were treasure, and she had felt something settle in her chest that she had not felt in years: the small, stubborn warmth of having seen something good in a world that mostly sold her crooked fish.

She would tell her granddaughter about him, she decided. The polite one from the academy, who bought the ugly fish for the ones who ate last.

It was a small story. But the old woman had lived long enough to know that small stories were the only ones that ever turned out to be true.

## Part Three — The Fire at the Wall

The 斗魂区's verdict reached the dormitories before midnight, carried on the triumphant tongues of boys who had watched it: 贝贝 had won — the gentle 唐门 大师兄 had faced the upper-year 徐三石 and his 玄冥龟 and walked out the winner, and the talk said he had walked out with more than pride, though what exactly had changed hands the gossips could not agree on. Some said a debt. Some said medicine. Some said the upper-year had lost something he would not be getting back.

言天宇 heard it through the wall of the dormitory, lying awake in his narrow bed, and felt the shape of the story settle around him like a garment that did not fit: his family had gone into a fire tonight, and he had been sent away from it, and they had come out whole, and the world had gone on exactly as it should have — without him.

He told himself it did not sting.

He was getting very good at telling himself things that were almost true.

It was two nights later that he found himself standing at the quiet wall at the edge of the outer yard — the wall where 穆恩 had come to him in the dark, the night of the 铁衣 run — with the week's allowance in his pocket and the memory of the morning's roll call in his chest.

He had not been able to stop thinking about it. The mark beside the two names. 温宁's held breath. 徐磊's averted eyes. The arithmetic he had never had to learn, of the ones who could not afford to lose.

He had spent this term learning the exact shape of the door in front of him. The wall at thirty did not move. The ladder this room was climbing was not his to climb until 穆恩 opened his hunt — and the selections, the races, the banner, the office, all of it belonged to the boys whose feet were on that ladder. Nobody had barred him from those races. He had stepped back from them himself, for the truest reason he had: he knew what the room was for, and the room was not his to carry over other boys' heads. Even the market — where family had pulled him from a fight that would have made a scandal of his grandfather's name — had not been a ban. It had been love wearing sense.

But standing in the dark with his allowance burning a hole in his pocket, it occurred to him that there was one thing on this side of his wall that was entirely, completely his.

He could not climb this year. Nobody had ever said he could not feed the ones who were climbing.

The first fire was a disaster.

言天宇 had cooked over his own flame since he was old enough to hold a pan — it was his one visible joy, the thing his hands did when his mind was elsewhere, the way he showed love to the people he could not protect with anything else. But cooking for himself, in the dean's kitchen, under 蔡媚儿's fond eye, was not the same as cooking for a hungry child in the dark, on a fire that had to be kept small enough not to draw the night patrols, with fish that had cost him two days of treats and would feed exactly three people if he got it right and exactly no one if he did not.

He got it wrong.

The first fish burned — not because the fire was too hot, but because his flame, which had never once in his life failed to answer him, flared with his mood, and his mood that night was a storm of small violences: the roll call, the market, 唐雅's hand on his sleeve, the story of his family going into a fire without him. He breathed it down — *照夜*, the old discipline, the dragon's cage made of breath — and the flame steadied, but the fish was already black on one side, and he stood there in the dark holding a ruined supper and feeling, for the first time in his life, the particular humiliation of having wanted to give something and failed at the giving.

"Your fire's angry," said a small voice from the shadows.

言天宇 went still. Not the stillness of fear — the stillness of a boy who had been trained since he could walk to know when he was being watched, and had somehow, for once, missed it.

温宁 stepped into the lamplight. She was wearing her dormitory robe, and she was carrying a bundle of something in her arms, and she looked, in the flickering dark, like a small animal that had decided to trust a fire because the alternative was the cold.

"How long were you there?" he asked.

"Long enough to watch you ruin a good fish." She set her bundle down — three thin, silvery fish, cleaned and ready, the kind that came from the stream behind the outer wall, the kind a girl who could not afford the market's prices learned to catch herself. "You keep your fire too tall. For fish like this you don't need a fire. You need a bed of coals and patience." She looked at him, and there was something in her eyes that was not quite fear and not quite defiance. "My mother taught me. Before."

言天宇 looked at the girl who had asked him, weeks ago, why he helped a boy who resented him. At the girl who ran like she had been running from something her whole life. At the girl who had just walked out of the dark to teach the dean's grandson how to cook a fish she had caught herself.

"Then I'm glad she taught you," he said, "because I clearly need the lesson." He knelt by the fire and banked it down with a careful hand — the flame shrinking, obedient, small. "Will you show me?"

She showed him.

It was a small thing, in the great accounting of the world — a girl and a boy and a fire at a wall, cooking three fish that would feed them and no one else. But 言天宇, watching 温宁's hands move over the coals with the economy of a lifetime of making little into enough, felt something shift inside him like a stone settling.

He had spent his whole life being given things — lessons, love, a name, a place at tables he had not earned. Nobody had ever once shown him how to make *enough* out of *little*. Nobody had ever needed to.

*This is the room,* he thought, watching the coals glow. *This is what it's actually for.*

The second night, there were four of them.

Word had spread the way word spreads among the hungry — quietly, in the dormitory after lights-out, in the line for supper where the portions ran out. 温宁 brought the small girl from the end of the roll who always ate last and never complained. A boy with a healing sprain that 周漪 had told him to run on or leave came because the alternative was the gate, and the fire was warm, and the fish was real.

And 徐磊 came.

He came late, when the fire was low and the others had gone, and he stood at the edge of the lamplight with his arms crossed and his face doing the complicated work of a boy who had spent a term being the loud one and had run out of loud.

"I'm not here for charity," he said.

"You're here for the fire," 言天宇 said, without looking up from the fish he was turning. "Sit. The fire doesn't care why you came."

徐磊 sat. He watched the fish. He did not eat for a long moment — pride wrestling with hunger, and hunger winning by a nose — and when he finally took the fish 言天宇 offered him, he ate it like a boy who had forgotten what food tasted like when it was given rather than earned.

"You're going to tell everyone I ate your charity fish," he said, around a mouthful, with his old sneer trying to climb back onto his face and failing.

"I'm going to tell everyone you showed up," 言天宇 said. "Which is more than you did on the 铁衣 morning, and more than you did yesterday, when 周漪 ran the drills and you folded in the first hour."

徐磊's face went red. The sneer died. Something raw surfaced in its place. "You were watching."

"I watch everyone. It's what I was raised to do — everyone in the dean's house smiles at the grandson and means forty different things by it, so you learn to watch, or you never learn anything." 言天宇 said it lightly, but there was a truth in it that made 徐磊 look away. "And I've been watching you, 徐磊 — it's not the only thing I do, but it's the thing I'm best at. You folded yesterday, in the run, in the drills, in your own head. I've watched you fold a dozen times this term. But I've also watched you get up. Every single time, you get up." He turned the fish, and the firelight ran over his face. "Folding isn't quitting. Folding is your body learning where the floor is. You've been standing on that floor for three weeks, hating yourself for it. Tonight you walked into the dark to a fire you thought was charity, because you didn't want to fold on your own anymore." He looked at him. "That's the first time this term you've stood on purpose. That's not nothing."

徐磊 was silent for a long time. The fire crackled. The other fish were gone; the bones lay in a neat pile by 温宁's seat, and the small girl had fallen asleep against the wall with her head on her knees.

"You said once," 徐磊 said finally, very quietly, "that the day I said it to your face would be the day you'd made a friend."

"I remember."

徐磊 looked at the fire. "You're not a walking trophy," he said. "You're a walking—" he struggled, gestured at the fire, at the fish, at the sleeping girl, "—you're a walking *hearth*. And I don't know what to do with that, because I've spent the whole term wanting to hate you, and you keep feeding me."

言天宇 smiled — the warm, open smile that made people trust him — and it was real, and it cost him nothing, because for once in his life it was not a performance.

"Good," he said. "Then we're even. I spent the whole term wanting to be useful, and you keep letting me feed you."

They sat in silence until the fire burned low, and then 徐磊 stood, and looked down at him, and did something that cost the loud boy visibly more than the fish had: he said, "Same time tomorrow?" — not a challenge, not a sneer. A question.

"Same time tomorrow," 言天宇 said. "Bring someone who eats last."

The third night, the old fish-seller's crooked fish were waiting for him under the stall, wrapped in cloth with a scrap of paper tucked into the twine. The paper said, in a wavering hand: *for the ones who eat last.*

言天宇 stood in the lamplight of the north-gate market with the bundle in his hands and felt something enormous move in his chest.

## Part Four — The Boy Who Cooks

The fire at the wall became a thing.

It was a small thing — a handful of children, a bed of coals, fish that would never have sold to anyone with a choice — but it became a thing, the way a hearth becomes a thing: not because of the flames, but because of the people who came back to it. 温宁 came every night now, and her hands were steadier, and she had stopped flinching when 周漪 called the roll. 徐磊 came early and helped with the gutting, and if his pride still wrestled with the charity of it, the fire was winning. The small girl came and slept. Others came and went, eating and saying little and returning, and 言天宇 learned their names the way he learned everything — quickly, carefully, as if they mattered — because they did.

His allowance did not stretch as far as he had hoped.

It was the first real arithmetic of his life, and it bit: two furnaces cost a boy a great deal of food, and a student's stipend covered a student's meals and nothing more, and the crooked fish at the north gate were cheap but they were not free, and by the fourth night 言天宇 had learned what it felt like to go to bed a little hungry and pretend he had eaten more than he had. He did not tell anyone. He told 蔡媚儿, on her weekly visit, that he was eating like a prince — which was almost true, if you counted the fish he gave away — and he told the furnace hunger, when it growled its cold cavernous complaint, to be patient, because the food was going somewhere that mattered.

*So this is the price,* he thought, lying awake in the dark with his stomach empty and his chest warm. *This is what it costs to feed the ones who eat last.*

*Worth it.*

The night the 班长 came, the fire had been burning for a week.

言天宇 was alone at the wall — 温宁 had gone to bed early, 徐磊 had been called to his uncle's, the small girl had eaten and run — turning the last of the night's fish over the coals and thinking about nothing in particular, when a shadow detached itself from the darker shadow of the wall and sat down on the other side of the fire without asking.

"Your fire's got a reputation," said 霍雨浩.

言天宇 looked up. The 班长 of 新生一班 was sitting cross-legged on the cold ground with a string of three silver fish across his knees — market fish, good ones, the kind that cost real money — and his thin face was lit from below by the coals, and there was something in it that 言天宇 had never seen there before. Not the careful mask. Not the wary watchfulness. Something looser, almost comfortable.

"Did 唐雅 send you to recruit me again?"

"No." 霍雨浩 held out the string of fish. "I came to pay a debt. You told me, at the market — the night of the stall — you told 唐雅 you'd come back for my fish until I stopped selling. You never came."

言天宇 blinked. He had forgotten, in the week of fires and empty stomachs, that he had said that. "I— the stall's doing well," he said. "You don't need a customer who buys one fish."

"I don't need the money." 霍雨浩's voice was quiet. "I need to know why the dean's grandson buys the crooked fish from the old woman at closing time, and cooks them at a wall in the dark, for children who can't afford the dining hall." He looked across the fire, and his dark eyes were very clear. "The whole market's talking about it. The old fish-seller told my stall- neighbour's daughter, and she told 唐雅, and 唐雅 told me — she said the polite one from the academy feeds the ones who eat last. I thought she was joking. She wasn't, was she?"

言天宇 was very still.

"No," he said.

"Then I came to help." 霍雨浩 set the fish down beside him and began, with quick, practiced hands, to gut the first one. "You cook for them. You're good — better than most — but you cook like a boy who's never had to make one fish feed two mouths. I've had to make one fish feed two mouths my whole life." He looked up, and there was no pride in it, only fact. "Let me show you."

And he did.

He showed 言天宇 things no tutor had ever taught him — how to judge a fire by the sound of it, how to turn a fish so the heat found the thin parts first, how to make the salt go further by putting it in the fire instead of on the fish, how to feed four people with two fish and a pot of water and patience. He did not explain where he had learned it, and 言天宇 did not ask, because he had learned young that some hungers belonged to other people until they chose to share them. But he watched the thin 班长's hands move — the same hands that had carried a whole class across the 铁衣 line — and he understood, with a strange ache, that he was being given something no one had ever given him: a skill bought with a lifetime of want, shared freely across a fire.

"Why?" he asked finally, when the last fish was spitted and the fire was settling into its long, patient burn. "You're the 班长. You've got your own fight — the stall, the 唐门, the 考核 coming. Why spend your night teaching me to cook cheap fish for children who aren't even in your—" he stopped.

"They're in my class," 霍雨浩 said simply. "That's enough."

The fire crackled. Two boys sat on either side of it — the golden grandson of 史莱克 and the road-boy who had become its 班长 — and for a while neither of them spoke.

"I heard about 王冬," 言天宇 said finally, to fill the silence with something true. "That 贝贝 and 唐雅 asked him to join 唐门. That they made it a condition — win the 新生考核."

霍雨浩 nodded. "He said yes before they finished the sentence." A small smile crossed his thin face. "He's been insufferable about it. He follows me around now talking about the glory of the Tang Sect and how the three of us are going to win the 考核 together, and he keeps trying to get 萧萧 to fill in her 修炼方向 as control type so we can all be in the same formation. I think he's lonely," he added, almost to himself. "I think he's been lonely his whole life and doesn't know how to say it, so he says 'join my sect' instead."

言天宇 looked at the fire. At the boy across it, who had nothing, and had given that nothing to a dying dream, and had just spent an hour teaching the dean's grandson to feed the poor.

"You got asked," he said quietly. "王冬 got asked. Everyone gets asked except me."

霍雨浩 was silent for a long moment.

"She's saving you," he said.

"Who?"

"唐雅. She told me — the night of the stall, after she sent you home. I asked her why she didn't ask you to join, when you're the strongest thing in the market and she's been after you for weeks." He looked across the fire, and his voice was very gentle. "She said: *'The little phoenix doesn't need another thing he can't fail at. He needs something that needs him. When he finds it, he'll come to us — or he won't, and we'll still love him. That's what family is.'*"

言天宇's hands stilled on the fish.

He thought about 唐雅, pulling him out of the market by the sleeve. About the fire at the wall, and the crooked fish, and the old woman's note. About the boy across from him, who had nothing, and had just given him the only thing that had ever been truly his — his want, his hunger, his hard-won craft — and asked for nothing in return.

"Everyone thinks the dean's grandson has everything," 霍雨浩 said quietly, into the silence. "When I first met you — at the market, the night I arrived — I decided you were the kind of boy who'd been given everything, and I hated you a little for it. Easy to hate, a boy like that." He looked at the fire. "Then you bought a fish from me and ate it in the dust like it was the best thing you'd ever had. And you sat with me in the dining hall when nobody else would. And you stood at the rail and watched me run, and you told me I was the most dangerous thing in the room." He paused. "And now you're here, in the dark, feeding the ones who eat last, going hungry yourself so children you barely know can eat."

He looked up, and his dark eyes were very tired and very clear.

"You're the first person my age who's ever made me think maybe the world isn't only made of people who take," he said. "You've never once been given what you actually want, have you? Not really. A name, a rank, a grandfather's house — they gave you all of it, and none of it was the thing." He smiled, a small, real smile. "So I came to give you the only thing I have. What I know about making little into enough."

The fire burned between them.

言天宇 sat very still, with the warmth of the coals on his face and the words of a boy who had nothing settling into him like water into dry ground, and felt something enormous crack open in his chest.

He had been told, all his life, that he was the one who had everything.

And it had taken a boy with nothing to see — to *see* — that the only thing he had ever wanted was the one thing no one had ever thought to give him: a place where he was needed for himself.

"Thank you," he said. It came out rough, and he did not try to smooth it. "No one's ever—" He stopped. Started again. "I didn't know anyone could see it. The thing underneath. I thought I was the only one who knew it was there."

"Everyone's got a thing underneath," 霍雨浩 said. "That's the whole secret. The ones who look like they have everything and the ones who look like they have nothing — it's the same underneath. We're all just hungry." He stood, brushing ash from his robes. "The stall's at the north gate every dusk, if you want to learn the proper way to roast a fish. 唐雅 says I'm hopeless at selling, but I'm not hopeless at teaching. And you," he added, with a ghost of the real smile, "you're not hopeless at feeding people. That's rarer than any rank."

He walked to the edge of the lamplight, and paused.

"One more thing. 唐雅 said to tell you — she's not done recruiting you. She says she'll wear you down eventually, and she's right, she wore me down in half a day." He looked back over his shoulder, and the firelight caught his face. "But she also said to tell you that if you never join, that's fine. Family doesn't stop being family because you won't sign up."

He vanished into the dark.

言天宇 sat alone by the fire for a long time, with the fish roasting and the coals glowing and the words of two people who had nothing to gain from him settling into his bones.

*Family doesn't stop being family because you won't sign up.*

He had spent eleven years being the golden grandson — the one everyone watched, the one everyone measured, the one who was set apart by the very thing that made him special. He had learned, from teachers and grandfathers and the shape of the world itself, that the races were not his this year — his door was shut until his grandteacher opened it, and until then the room's ladder belonged to the boys whose feet were on it. And he had built, without quite meaning to, the one thing on this side of his wall that was entirely his: a fire at a wall, where the ones who ate last came back every night, and where the golden grandson of 史莱克 was not a trophy to be admired or a threat to be measured — just a boy who fed people, and who was fed in return.

*So this is what it's for,* he thought, watching the coals glow. *The room. This is what the room is for.*

Above him, the stars turned over the sleeping academy. Somewhere in the dormitories, the 班长 of 新生一班 was probably not sleeping either — but he was not alone, and neither was 言天宇, and for the first time in his life, standing at the edge of a fire he had built himself, he understood what 周漪 had meant, all those weeks ago.

*The one the room turns to.*

Not the strongest. Not the fastest.

The one who makes the others able.

He banked the fire low, checked the coals, and walked back toward the dormitory with his hands in his sleeves and his stomach empty and his chest full — thinking about crooked fish and old women's notes and a thin boy who had given him the only thing he had, and about the wall inside him, rank thirty, waiting for a beast worthy of the climb.

*Not yet,* he told it, the way 穆恩 had taught him. *Not yet. There's a room to feed first.*

*And when the time comes—*

He let the thought finish itself in the dark, where no one could see his eyes.

*—I'll be ready to find out what I'm made of. Not because I want to prove anything to the world.*

*Because there's a fire at a wall that needs me to come back to it.*

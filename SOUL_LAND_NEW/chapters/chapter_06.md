## Part One — First Years

The week after the final, the academy did the thing it always did with a finished term: it folded the moment away and began the next one, as if children had not just stood in a sealed arena and beaten a hundred-year name.

言天宇 woke to a different room.

The survivors of 一班 had become 一年级 — not by ceremony, just by the calendar and the roll — and the difference was in the small things: the desks were the same but the fear was quieter; the drills were the same but the room ran them like it belonged there now; even 周漪's counting had changed register, from the blade that culls to the blade that sharpens. The gate had closed behind them for the last time. What was left of the room was hers to make into something, and she went about it with the same flat ferocity she had used to thin them, which 言天宇 had decided long ago was her way of loving.

He watched the room settle into its new shape and felt the strange lightness of having survived to belong somewhere.

The first new thing was 唐门.

It happened three evenings after the final, at the small house 贝贝 and 唐雅 kept at the edge of the outer court — the one 言天宇 had been "family" at for years without ever once being a member. 言天宇 arrived to find the yard lit and the company strange: 贝贝, smiling his quiet smile; 唐雅, blazing with a joy so fierce it almost hid the tired thing underneath; 霍雨浩, looking like a boy who had just been told he was part of something bigger than himself and was still deciding how to carry it; 王冬, chin high and ears pink, trying very hard to look like joining a dying sect was beneath him and failing completely; and 萧萧, small and still at the edge of the light, with something in her careful face that 言天宇 recognized because it was his own.

"Little phoenix!" 唐雅 spotted him at the gate and swept over, arms wide. "You came! I didn't think you'd dare — this is a *recruitment* night, and you're the one fish that keeps slipping the net."

"I came to witness," 言天宇 said gravely, "not to be netted. 贝贝哥 said there'd be food."

"There's always food with you," 唐雅 laughed, but her eyes were soft. "Come. Sit where you can see. I want witnesses for this — the Tang Sect is about to grow, and I want the golden grandson of 史莱克 to have *seen* it, so that when he finally joins, in about ten years, he can't pretend he doesn't know what he's signing."

"Ten years is optimistic," 霍雨浩 said from the fire. "Try thirty."

"Thirty years," 唐雅 corrected serenely, "during which I will wear him down."

言天宇 sat on the low wall at the edge of the yard, where he could see the whole of it and be part of none of it, and watched the 唐门's young 门主 do what she had been born to do. She was good at it — better than she knew. She did not lecture about the sect's glory or its fall; she told them what it would cost instead, and what it would give, and what she would ask of them, and she did it with the bright, fierce, terribly young gravity of a girl who had inherited a grave and decided to fill it with something alive.

She took 王冬 first — because 王冬 had earned it, because the whole market had watched him choose the 班长's side, because he had written 控制系 on a form and meant it. 王冬 knelt, and 唐雅's hands were steady on his shoulders, and when she spoke the old words over him 言天宇 saw something move in the golden-haired boy's face — the pride cracking, just for a second, into something younger and more real.

She took 萧萧 second — quietly, because 萧萧 was quiet, because the small girl with the twin braids had been watching rooms her whole life and had finally found one she wanted to belong to. 萧萧 knelt, and her voice when she repeated the words was steady, and 言天宇 saw 霍雨浩 and 王冬 exchange a look over her head that said the three of them were something now, something that would outlast the bracket.

And then 唐雅 straightened, and looked at the three of them — her two disciples and her two new ones — and for just a moment the tired thing under her brightness was gone, replaced by something else entirely.

"The Tang Sect was the greatest in the world," she said quietly. "And it fell, and everyone forgot. But I remember. And now there are five of us who remember." She looked at 言天宇, perched on the wall, and her smile was warm and uncomplicated. "Six, if you count the one who won't sign."

"Count me," 言天宇 said, and meant it. "Just don't ask me to kneel. I'm terrible at kneeling."

"Noted." 唐雅's grin returned, full force. "The Tang Sect accepts contributions from non-members. Food, fire, and moral support. All tax-deductible."

Later, when the fire had been lit and the food brought out and the little sect was deep in the particular loudness of new family, 言天宇 sat on the wall and watched, and felt the familiar ache — the one that came when he stood at the edge of a thing he could not enter. He had told 霍雨浩, once, that he wanted to belong to something that was not his grandfather's shadow. And here was belonging, right in front of him — a girl who was trying to resurrect a grave, and the family she was building around it — and he could not step into it, because the sect's arts were not his to take, and because his own secret meant he could not swear himself to anything that might one day ask him to choose between it and the thing he carried.

*So I'll be the one on the wall,* he thought. *The one who watches them belong. The one who feeds them when they're done.*

He had made peace with that, mostly.

What he had not made peace with was 桃姐.

The fire had been patient all day.

马小桃 knew its moods by now — she had lived with it for nineteen years, had been raised by it, had been told since she was small that it was her gift and her curse and her inheritance, the 邪火 that made her the era's first genius and the thing that would one day eat her alive if she could not find a way to feed it something it could keep down. She had learned to read it the way other girls learned to read the weather: the low grumble of a bad morning, the dangerous stillness before a storm, the particular heat behind her eyes that meant she needed to be alone.

Today it had been *patient*. That was the worst mood. Patient meant it was gathering.

She had done everything right. She had trained until her body shook, had sat through 言少哲's lecture without a flicker, had eaten, had breathed the way her teacher had taught her to breathe when the fire leaned. And still, by evening, she had felt it rising — the old familiar flood, climbing her ribs like a tide — and she had known that this time the walls were not going to hold.

She should have gone to her junior sun.

The thought surfaced even as she fled the 内院, even as her feet carried her toward the water — the one place the fire seemed to quiet, the great cold lake at the academy's heart where the 内院 students were allowed to walk at night. The boy. The dean's grandson, the one who had steadied her since he was small enough to be carried, whose light she did not understand and had stopped asking about because asking meant admitting how much she needed it. He was asleep now, probably — it was late, and he was a first-year now, and he had his own life, his own fire at a wall, his own small family of hungry children. She could not keep running to a child every time her blood boiled. She was nineteen. She was a 魂帝. She was supposed to be the strong one.

*The strong one,* she thought, as the fire crested and the world went red, *does not run to children.*

She did not remember the lake.

She remembered the flood — the roar of it, the crimson blindness, the terrible relief of finally *letting* it out after a day of holding — and she remembered two shapes at the water's edge, small and young, caught in the light of her fire like two leaves in a furnace, and she remembered knowing, with the part of her that was still her, that she was about to kill children and could not stop.

And then the cold came.

It was not the lake's cold — that was ordinary, friendly, the cold of water and night. This was something else. This was vast and old and *hungry in the other direction*, a cold that did not fight her fire but simply out-aged it, the way a mountain out-ages a spark. It rose out of the smaller of the two shapes — the thin one, the boy with the tired eyes — and it met her fire in the air between them, and her fire, which had never once in nineteen years been refused anything, *went quiet.*

The relief was so total it was almost pain.

马小桃 came back to herself on the stones by the lake, on her knees, with her hands pressed to the cold ground and her breath coming in great ragged sobs that were not grief and not fear but something she had no name for — the sound of a fire that had been burning for nineteen years suddenly, impossibly, finding out what silence felt like.

She looked up.

The two boys were still there — the thin one, grey-faced and swaying, and the golden-haired one, frozen between the thin boy and her, braced to die. They were looking at her like she was a storm that had stopped.

She opened her mouth to say she was sorry.

The world went away.

She woke in a clean white room that smelled of medicine, with the kind, severe face of 庄老 — the academy's great healer, old enough to remember when she was small — looking down at her, and the first thing she said, before she was even fully awake, was: "The boys. Are they—"

"Alive," 庄老 said. "Safe. Whole." His old eyes were unreadable. "You owe them more than you know, girl. And you owe them silence more than that. What happened at that lake tonight stays at that lake. Do you understand me?"

马小桃 understood. She understood that she had nearly killed two first-years, that something cold and vast had come out of one of them to stop her, and that the academy's healer was telling her, very gently, that the cold one's secret was now her secret too.

She understood something else, too — something she would carry for a long time, something she would not speak of to anyone.

For nineteen years, the only relief she had known had come from a child's light — the dean's grandson, her junior sun, who steadied her fire the way you steady a hand. She had thought that was the best the world could offer her.

It was not. It had never been. There was a cold in the world that could do what light could not — that could *quiet* the fire instead of merely holding it steady — and it lived in a thin, tired-eyed first-year who did not know what he carried.

*I will find out which one it was,* she thought, as the medicine pulled her under. *And I will never tell a soul. But I will find out.*

## Part Two — The Stall

Across the academy, on the night the fire broke by the lake, 言天宇 was at the north gate with 霍雨浩, learning to sell fish.

It had been 霍雨浩's idea — the partnership 唐雅 had teased about since the night of the market, when the 班长 had stood at the edge of 言天宇's fire and said, *the stall's at the north gate every dusk, if you want to learn the proper way to roast a fish.* 言天宇 said yes before he finished the sentence.

They made an odd pair of vendors.

霍雨浩 cooked — quick and precise and silent, the way he did everything that mattered, his hands finding the heat and the salt and the turning without ever seeming to think about it. 言天宇 sold — and discovered, to his surprise, that he was good at it, better than he had any right to be. He remembered names. He told the truth about which fish were freshest and which were yesterday's, and the customers came back because of it. He charmed the old women and disarmed the suspicious and made the market's children laugh, and by the second night the stall had a queue that the other vendors had begun to eye with professional respect.

"You're wasted on the academy," 霍雨浩 said, watching him talk a pair of hesitant sisters into a second fish each. "You could run this market in a year."

"I'm wasted on everything," 言天宇 said cheerfully. "It's my curse. I'm the most wasted boy in 史莱克. The 斗魂区 won't field me — apparently the dean's grandson scrapping for money in the arena would be a scandal and a half, and my grandfather has a reputation to protect. The 唐门 won't get me to kneel, and the market only wants me for my charm."

"Your charm and my fish."

"A devastating combination. We should franchise."

They worked the stall through the evening — the two boys who had met over a fish in this same market, months ago, when one of them had been the golden grandson nobody could measure and the other a road-boy selling his first fish to a stranger who ate it in the dust. The coins went into a clay bowl, and the bowl went, when the night was done, into 唐雅's keeping — because that was what the stall was for, really, had always been for: a dead sect's youngest 门主, kept alive by a boy who had nothing and the fish he could cook.

言天宇 watched 霍雨浩 count the coins and hand them over without being asked, and felt the particular ache of watching someone give everything they had to something that might never give it back.

"Why do you do it?" he asked, as they walked back through the darkening market with the empty bowls and the smell of smoke on their clothes. "The stall, the sect, the— you give her everything you earn. Every night. She's not—" he stopped, trying to find the kind way to say it. "She's not going to rebuild it overnight. It might take your whole life."

霍雨浩 walked in silence for a moment.

"She took me in," he said finally. "When I had nothing. Not because I was strong, or useful, or worth anything — just because I was a boy who needed somewhere to be. She gave me a name to carry and a thing to belong to." He looked at the dark street ahead of them. "The sect might take my whole life. But it's *mine* now. The way the stall is mine. The way—" he glanced at 言天宇, and something in his face softened. "The way the fire at your wall is yours."

言天宇 did not answer for a long moment.

They reached the corner where their ways parted — the 班长 toward the dormitory, the golden grandson toward the wall where his fire waited — and 霍雨浩 paused.

"Something happened today," he said, and his voice was odd — careful, the way it got when he was deciding how much to say. "At the lake. I can't— I'm not supposed to—" He stopped. Started again. "I have a secret now. A real one. The kind you can't tell anyone, not even the people you trust most." He looked at 言天宇, and his dark eyes were very tired. "I've never had a friend I wanted to keep a secret from before. I don't know how to do it."

言天宇 stood very still in the lamplight of the empty street.

He knew that feeling. He had known it since he was six years old, since the day he had learned what slept at his spine, since he had spent a decade learning to breathe around a thing no one could know. He knew the weight of it, and the loneliness of it, and the particular cruelty of having a friend you loved and a secret that stood between you like a wall you could not climb and could not explain.

"Then let me tell you how," he said quietly. "You don't tell me. You don't tell anyone, not until you're ready. And I don't ask. I won't ask, ever. But you should know—" he looked at the thin boy, at the friend who had taught him to feed the hungry and had seen the thing underneath his crown, "—that whatever it is, it doesn't change anything. You're still the boy who ran past his wall and carried the whole room across. You're still my friend. You don't have to earn that with explanations."

霍雨浩 was silent for a long moment. Something moved in his face — relief, and gratitude, and something else, something that looked almost like wonder.

"You really mean that," he said.

"I really do." 言天宇 smiled, and it was real. "I've had a secret my whole life, 班长. I know what it costs to carry one. And I know that the only thing that makes it bearable is having someone who doesn't need to know it to trust you." He clapped the thin boy on the shoulder. "Go. Sleep. Whatever happened at that lake, it's over, and you're alive, and tomorrow we sell fish."

霍雨浩 looked at him for a long moment. And then he nodded — a small, decisive nod, the kind he gave when he had decided something — and walked away into the dark.

言天宇 stood in the empty street for a while after he was gone, with his hands in his sleeves and the weight of his own secret settling back onto his shoulders like an old friend.

*One more person in this world who understands,* he thought. *Without either of us saying a word.*

*That's not nothing.*

## Part Three — The Dean's Table

The summons came two days later, in the careful handwriting of his grandfather's secretary: *dinner, at the house, this evening. Your grandmother is cooking.*

言天宇 went.

He went the way he always went when the summons came — through the dusk of the outer court, past the gates that separated the dean's world from the students', up the familiar path to the house where he had grown up, where his corner room stood empty now and 蔡媚儿 kept his favorite dishes warm on the assumption that he would someday eat enough to fill his bones. He was not the same boy who had left this house for the intake, and he felt it in the small differences: the way his hand found the gate latch without hesitation now, the way he walked into the warmth like a visitor who knew he was still welcome.

蔡媚儿 met him at the door and folded him into the particular embrace that had been the constant of his life — warm, uncomplicated, the one place in the world where he was just a boy. She held him a moment longer than usual, and he felt her check him the way she always did — the warmth of his hands, the light in his eyes, the thousand small signs she had learned to read in eleven years of loving a child who carried more than children should.

"You've lost weight," she said accusingly. "I told you to eat."

"I eat like a prince," 言天宇 said, which was almost true. "Your cooking, mostly, in my memory."

"Flattery will not save you. Sit. Your grandfather's been in a mood all day, and I want you fed before he gets to you."

言少哲 was in his study when 言天宇 came in — a tall, stern man with his grandson's eyes in an older, harder face, the 明凤斗罗 who ran the 武魂系 the way he ran everything: exactly, and with no margin for error. He looked up from his desk, and for a moment his face did the complicated thing it always did when he saw his grandson — pride and fear and love and the particular terror of a man who had lost one child and spent every day since trying not to lose the other.

"小宇." He set down his brush. "Sit."

They ate in the formal dining room — 蔡媚儿's cooking, which was the best food in the academy and which 言天宇 ate like a boy who had been starving for a week, which he had, more or less, in the particular way of a boy who gave his allowance to other people's dinners. His grandfather watched him eat with the same exacting attention he gave everything, and 言天宇 felt the familiar sensation of being measured, of being read, of being a problem his grandfather was trying to solve before it could become a tragedy.

He did not mind it. He had made peace with his grandfather's fear a long time ago — had understood, young, that the harshness was love wearing armor, and that the armor was the only way the man knew to love anyone at all.

"The new year suits you," 言少哲 said finally, when the plates were mostly empty. "You've found a rhythm."

"Yes, Grandfather." 言天宇 hesitated. "The room is good. The—" he almost said *fire*, and stopped. His grandfather did not know about the fire at the wall — the unsanctioned kitchen, the crooked fish, the small family of hungry children. It was not a secret, exactly. It was just his, the way the stall was 霍雨浩's, and he was not ready to share it.

"Good," 言少哲 said. And then, with the particular casualness that 言天宇 had learned to recognize as the most dangerous tone in his grandfather's repertoire: "I hear the 班长 of your old room is an interesting boy. 霍雨浩. The 特邀生 who won the 考核."

言天宇's fork paused halfway to his mouth. "He's a good boy, Grandfather. The best in the room. He carried us all through the term."

"So I've heard." 言少哲's voice was light, almost idle. "They say he has unusual abilities. A quick mind. A gift for—" he waved a hand, "—seeing things others miss."

The air in the room changed.

言天宇 felt it the way he felt most things — with his body before his mind. The warmth in his chest cooled a degree. The fork in his hand became suddenly, acutely real. And something old and careful in him, the part that had been trained since he was six to hear the question underneath the question, sat up very straight.

*He's fishing,* 言天宇 thought. *He's asking about 霍雨浩. Why would my grandfather, the dean of the 武魂系, be fishing about a first-year 班长?*

"His 灵眸 is very strong," he said carefully. "That's his 武魂 — everyone knows it. He sees well, and he's clever, and he refuses to lose." He set the fork down. "Why do you ask, Grandfather?"

言少哲's eyes were on him — sharp, unreadable, the eyes of a man who had spent forty years reading children's faces for the truth they were trying to hide.

"There was an incident," he said. "At the 海神湖, a few nights ago. A senior disciple lost control of her 武魂. Two first-years were caught in it." A pause. "They were not harmed. The matter has been handled. But I am the dean of the 武魂系, and I like to know what kind of boy walks my halls with abilities that don't quite add up."

*Her 武魂,* 言天宇 thought. And something cold and quiet settled in his chest, because he knew, suddenly, with the particular certainty of a boy who had spent his whole life learning to read the people who loved him, exactly which senior disciple his grandfather meant.

"Was it 桃姐?" he asked.

The silence that followed was very long.

"It was not your concern," 言少哲 said finally, and his voice had gone carefully flat — the voice he used when he was protecting something. "She is well. She is being— observed. The matter is closed."

"Was it 桃姐?" 言天宇 asked again, and his voice was very quiet, the way it got when he was holding something very still.

言少哲 looked at him for a long moment. And then, for just a second, the armor cracked — and 言天宇 saw the thing underneath, the thing his grandfather never let anyone see: fear. Not the stern fear of a dean for his academy. The other kind. The kind that lived in a man who had already buried one child and had spent eleven years praying he would not have to bury another.

"She is well," 言少哲 said again, more gently. "That is all you need to know. And you—" he reached across the table, and for a moment his hand rested on his grandson's, a rare and startling gesture, "—you will stay away from the 海神湖 at night. And you will not go looking for her. Not until I tell you it's safe. Do you understand me?"

言天宇 understood.

He understood that his grandfather was afraid — for him, and for something else, something his grandfather was not saying. He understood that the incident at the lake had shaken the academy to its bones, that a 内院 disciple had lost control near first-years, that his grandfather had spent two days investigating a thin boy with a gift that "didn't quite add up." And he understood, with the particular ache of a boy who had spent his whole life on the other side of secrets, that there were things his grandfather would never tell him — the same way there were things 言天宇 would never tell his grandfather.

Two people, carrying secrets, sitting across a table from each other, loving each other across the silence.

"Yes, Grandfather," he said.

The rest of the meal passed in careful, ordinary talk — 蔡媚儿's gentle chatter filling the spaces where the men could not speak. And when 言天宇 left, walking back through the dusk toward the outer court and his wall and his fire, he carried two things with him: the warmth of his grandmother's embrace, and the cold certainty that 桃姐's fire had broken, and he had not been there.

He did not know what that meant.

He only knew that the silence between him and the people he loved was full of things no one was saying, and that he was very, very tired of silence.

## Part Four — The Quiet After

马小桃 came back to the outer court six days after the lake.

言天宇 heard about it the way he heard about most things in the academy — through the particular telegraph of children who noticed everything and understood nothing. She was out of the infirmary. She was back in the 内院. She was different — quieter, they said, the 邪火凤凰 who had terrorized the outer court for years walking the halls like a girl who had seen something and could not speak of it.

He did not go looking for her.

His grandfather had told him to stay away, and 言天宇 had learned young that the men in his family did not give orders without reasons, even when they would not share them. But he did the one thing he could do — the thing he had always done, the thing that was his language when words failed: he cooked.

That evening, he left a bundle at the gate of the 内院 — not at the door, not where anyone official would see it, but at the small postern where the 内院's junior disciples came and went, where he had left things for her before, years ago, when she was a girl being trained too hard and he was a child who had discovered that food was the only medicine he knew how to give. The bundle held a fish — a good one, not crooked, bought with two days of treats — roasted over his own flame, wrapped in leaves, still warm.

He left no note.

He was halfway back to the outer court when the sound of running footsteps caught up with him, and a hand closed on his sleeve with the particular grip he had known his whole life — strong enough to hurt, gentle enough to never have.

"小宇."

马小桃 stood in the dusk of the path between the courts, holding the bundle like it was something precious. She looked different — thinner, he thought, and older, and there was something in her face that had not been there before the lake, something he could not name and did not dare to ask about. Her fire — he could feel it, the way he had always felt it, the particular warmth that was hers and no one else's — was *quieter.* Not gone. Not healed. But quieter, the way a fire is quieter after a long rain.

"You came," he said. "I wasn't sure you would. Grandfather said to stay away—"

"Your grandfather," 马小桃 said, "is a very large, very loud rooster who thinks he's protecting everyone by telling no one anything." She pulled him into a fierce hug, and the bundle pressed between them, and for a moment she was just 桃姐 again — the girl who had terrorized anyone who bullied him, who had taught him that crying in front of juniors was forbidden, who was secretly the person he measured his own fire against. "I'm sorry," she said into his hair. "I'm sorry I didn't come. I'm sorry I scared you. I'm—"

"You don't have to be sorry," 言天宇 said, into her shoulder. "You don't have to tell me anything. I just—" his voice cracked, just a little. "I was worried. You've never been quiet before."

马小桃 held him for a long moment.

"Things changed," she said finally, when she let him go. Her voice was careful, the way 霍雨浩's had been careful, the way his own voice was careful when he spoke around the thing at his spine. "At the lake. I can't— I'm not supposed to—" She stopped. Looked at him. "There's a thing I have to find out. A thing I can't tell you about. But I want you to know—" her hand came up and cupped his face, the way she had done when he was small and scared, "—that you've been good to me, 小宇. Better than you know. And whatever happens now, that doesn't change. You're my little sun. You'll always be my little sun."

言天宇 stood very still in the dusk, with his big sister's hand on his face and her words settling into him like stones.

"Always?" he asked.

"Always." She smiled — the old smile, fierce and warm and utterly her. "Now let me go eat this fish before it gets cold. It's the best thing anyone's given me in a week, and I've had a week that needed it." She turned, and walked back toward the 内院 with the bundle in her arms, and paused at the postern. "And 小宇?"

"Yes?"

"Stay away from the lake at night." Her voice was gentle, and something in it was very old. "Some fires need the dark to find their answer. That's not a thing you can fix by being near it." She looked at him, and for a moment her eyes were the eyes of the girl who had raised him, and there was a grief in them he did not understand. "But knowing you're out there, holding your light steady for the people who need it — that helps. That's helped me more than you'll ever know."

She vanished through the postern, and was gone.

言天宇 stood in the dusk for a long time after she was gone, with the smell of his own cooking fading on the air and the weight of her words settling into him like a thing he would carry for a long time.

*Some fires need the dark to find their answer.*

He did not know what she meant. He did not know that the answer she was looking for was a thin boy with tired eyes and a cold that could out-age a fire. He did not know that his light — the light that had steadied her for years — had met its match in something it could never be, and that the girl he loved like a sister was already walking toward the one thing in the world that could do what he could not.

He only knew that she was alive, and that she had called him her little sun, and that the silence between them was full of things she was not saying.

And he knew, with the particular clarity of a boy who had spent his whole life on the other side of secrets, that there was only one thing he could do with that silence.

He could respect it.

He walked back through the dark to his wall, and his fire, and his small family of hungry children — and he lit the fire, and fed them, and when the night was done and the coals were banked, he sat alone in the dark for a while and thought about the two people he loved most who were carrying secrets they could not share with him.

His grandfather, who feared for him.

His big sister, who was looking for an answer he could not give.

And his best friend, the 班长, who had looked at him with tired eyes and said, *I've never had a friend I wanted to keep a secret from before.*

*We're all carrying things,* 言天宇 thought, watching the last of the coals fade. *All of us. The golden grandson and the road-boy and the 邪火凤凰 and the dean. We're all carrying things we can't put down, and we're all pretending we're not.*

*But we're carrying them together now. That has to count for something.*

He banked the last coal, checked the embers the way 温宁 had taught him, and walked back toward the dormitory with his hands in his sleeves and his chest full of a strange, quiet warmth.

Tomorrow, there would be fish to sell and a room to feed and a secret to keep.

And the day after that, the wall inside him would still be waiting — rank thirty, patient, watching him the way he watched the room, waiting for the day he brought it something worthy of opening for.

*Not yet,* he told it, the way 穆恩 had taught him. *Not yet. There are people who need me on this side of the wall first.*

*But I'm learning. I'm learning what the room is for.*

*And when I finally stand at that door—*

He let the thought finish itself in the dark, where no one could see his eyes.

*—I'm going to be ready to find out what's on the other side of me.*

# Chapter 1: The Walk-In

Luo Chen was six years old when he walked into the Holy Spirit Cult.

He walked in on his own two feet, and that was the part nobody believed.

The gate stood at the end of a narrow street in Ming Du, at the bottom of the hill where the lamp-oil shops gave way to warehouses. It was an ordinary black gate in an ordinary wall, and the only mark on it was a coin nailed at eye level, stamped with two skulls facing each other. Children came to this gate sometimes, in carts or in chains, brought by grey-robed men who had bought them or caught them. Nobody knocked on it.

Luo Chen knocked on it.

The panel slid open, and a face looked out — a man in grey, middle-aged, with the flat eyes of someone who had opened this panel ten thousand times.

"You're lost," the man said.

"No," said Luo Chen. "I'm here to become a soul master."

The man looked at him once, and then again. What he saw was a small boy in an orphanage coat that had been washed until it had no color left, with black hair cut short and eyes that did not move around the way children's eyes move around.

"Go home," the man said. "The church doesn't take strays."

"Then it's a good thing I'm not a stray," said Luo Chen. "I came on purpose. You measure every child who comes to this gate — the measuring costs you nothing, and if I'm empty you throw me out and lose one minute. If I'm not empty, you've just thrown away something the other churches would have paid for. Open the gate and measure me."

The panel stayed still for a moment. The man behind it had heard begging, screaming, bargaining, and silence in his years at the gate, but he had not heard terms.

"What's your name," he said.

"Luo Chen."

"Wait there, Luo Chen."

The panel closed. Luo Chen stood in the street and waited, because waiting was the cheapest thing he owned, and he had spent six years learning what this city was and where this gate was and what walked in and out of it. He had chosen this church the way a man chooses a road — with both eyes open, knowing where it led, and knowing that it was the only road that went where he needed to go.

The gate opened.

"Inside," said the man. "Touch nothing. Speak when you're spoken to."

---

They took him across a courtyard, through a hall with a high roof, and into a room at the back that was built for one purpose. The room was round, and the walls were lined with lamps that burned without smoke, and in the center of the floor stood a platform of black stone with a pillar rising from it, and on top of the pillar sat a crystal the size of a man's head, dark and still.

Two people waited by the platform. One was a thin man in grey robes with a soft face and soft hands, who stood the way servants stand when they want to be trusted with things. The other was a woman, and she was not thin and not soft, and she looked at Luo Chen the way an appraiser looks at an object that has been carried in from the rain.

"A walk-in," said the thin man, as if the word tasted strange. "A child who walked in. Six years old, and he walked in. I've served this altar for nineteen years and no one has ever walked in."

"Measure him," said the woman.

The thin man smiled at Luo Chen. "I am Feng Si. Watch, and you will understand what happens here."

He lifted his right hand, and a flute of dark bone rose out of his palm and hung in the air, thin and pale as a finger-bone, and around the flute three rings lit — two yellow, and one purple, turning slowly.

"A martial soul wakes at six," said Feng Si. "Every person carries one — some carry a tool, a hammer, a blade, a flute like mine. Some carry a beast, and the beast stands behind them when they wake it. Mine is the Soul-Calling Flute. The rings are soul rings — every ring is a soul beast I helped kill, and every ring opened a gate in me that could not be opened any other way. Three rings make me a Soul Elder, at the thirty-eighth level. The rings are what the church gives to those who are useful. Do you understand?"

"Yes," said Luo Chen.

*Three rings at thirty-eight, and nineteen years of service,* he thought, with the eye he kept private. *Slow by the world's measure, fast by the pit's. The flute calls souls, and he spends his life calling small ones down stairs. Useful man. Remember him.*

"And if the crystal stays dark," said Feng Si, gently, "there is no soul power, and the church will give you a meal and put you back in the street, and you will live an ordinary life. That happens to nine children of ten. It is nothing to be afraid of."

"I'm not afraid," said Luo Chen.

The woman said nothing. Her eyes were grey, and they stayed on him.

The black stone of the platform was inlaid with awakening stones in a ring, old and worn smooth by ten thousand feet, and the crystal ball on the pillar above was dark, the way the soul-measuring crystal balls wait. Luo Chen stepped onto the platform.

He had done this before. That was the secret he carried under his skin, the one that had come with him across death itself — he had done this once already, in a room with no one in it, on the night of his sixth birthday, and the thing that had woken in him had filled the orphanage's water jars with frost and emptied them again before morning. He had measured himself against everything he remembered from the book of his old life, and he had known what he carried, and he had spent every day since preparing the ground for this moment.

But that waking had been a whisper. This room was built to make souls shout.

"Hands out," said Feng Si. "Wake it."

Luo Chen held out his hands, and let the thing inside him rise.

The lamps in the room went low, all of them, at once, as if something had drunk the light. The temperature dropped. The dark crystal on the pillar lit from inside — not the pale glow of ordinary soul power, but a deep moving red, like a coal seen through smoke, and the shadows in the room leaned toward the platform the way grass leans toward a fire.

Behind the boy, the air split open, and the martial soul stood up.

It was a dragon. It was black from horn to tail, long as a river, with scales that seemed to eat the lamplight, and its eyes were the color of the inside of the crystal, and when it opened its mouth nothing came out but a pull — a pull in the air, in the blood, in the light, a hunger so clean and so total that Feng Si took a step back without knowing he had done it, and his bone flute went dark in the air.

The woman did not step back. Her hand moved, and a whip of red bone cracked out of her sleeve and stood hissing at her side, and five rings rose around her — two yellow, two purple, and one black ring turning slow and heavy at the outside, the standard pattern of her kind carried to the top of it, five rings at the fifty-ninth level. The room steadied around her soul the way a room steadies around a pillar.

"A beast soul," she said, holding the dragon with her eyes. "Dragon type. Keep it out, boy. This hall has measured ten thousand souls and it is still standing."

The dragon looked down at the boy whose soul it was, and the boy did not flinch, because it was his, and it had never once eaten anything he did not give it.

"A dragon," said Feng Si, in a voice that had lost all its softness. "A dragon-class martial soul. Evil attribute, devouring type — Elder, that's a top-tier soul, that's the kind the registers get written about—"

"The crystal," said the woman.

Feng Si looked at the crystal, and stopped speaking.

The crystal was full. Soul power ran from one to ten, and the crystal read it the way a cup reads water — a dim glow for the first level, a bright glow for the fifth, and for the tenth the whole body of the crystal burning. The crystal on the pillar was burning from edge to edge, and it had been burning since the dragon first moved.

"Innate full soul power," said Feng Si. "Level ten at awakening — the top of Soul Scholar, one ring away from being a true Soul Master. Born full. Elder, I have to say it plainly, I have never measured a child born full. In nineteen years. Once a generation, the books say—"

"Quiet," said the woman.

She walked to the platform, and she looked at the dragon, and the dragon looked back at her, and something passed between them that had no words in it. Then she looked at the boy.

"What did you say your name was," she said.

"Luo Chen."

"You know what you are?"

"I know what my soul is," said Luo Chen. "A Devouring Dragon. I want to learn what to do with it."

"And you walked here," said the woman, "across the city, six years old, alone, to ask us."

"Yes."

"Why us. There are other churches in Ming Du. There is an academy with a gate twice the size of ours."

Luo Chen looked at her, and he answered the way he had decided to answer long before this day, because the truth in this room had to be shaped like something useful, and the most useful truth was the one that fed the people holding the leash.

"Because the academy teaches children who already have everything," he said. "And I don't have anything. The orphanage fed me because the law made them. Nobody has ever given me anything because they wanted me. Your gate is the only gate in this city that opens for children nobody wants."

The room was still for a moment.

"Devouring type," said the woman, half to herself. "Evil attribute, innate — not mutation, not corruption. Born. And the devouring is the soul's own — no ring, no teaching, the birthright kind. A soul that carries its feeding in from the womb."

Feng Si's face had gone quiet the way faces go quiet when a thing lands in them. "Elder, the registers will want the classification plain. By the continental standard—"

"I know the continental standard," said the woman. "A soul born evil is an evil soul master by the soul alone, whatever it eats or doesn't eat. I also know what the standard means for a child with no name and no coin — the Enforcement Squad of Shrek, or a pyre, because that is what the world gives such children. This church is the only door on the continent that opens for him, and he is clever enough to have found it at six." She looked at Luo Chen. "Your soul cannot be cultivated the honest way, boy. A devouring soul grows by devouring — the world calls that evil and hunts it. Here, it is called a method, and the church teaches the method to those who are useful. Remember that, because it is the whole of what you are being given."

"Yes," said Luo Chen.

He did not tell her what the book had taught him about evil soul masters, because the book had taught him everything she knew and one thing more — the ledger of the bargain. The speed at the start, faster than any honest road. The hunger that grows with the feeding. The mind that corrodes as the power grows. The backfire that comes to the man who stops feeding, and the early grave that comes to nearly all of them, spent by their own appetite or caught by the hunters. He knew the bargain whole, and he had come to this gate anyway, because a man who knows the price of a road walks it differently than a man who is told the road is free.

*Beasts, not men,* he said, in the private place, the way one sets a law down at the founding of a country. *My soul's own kind, taken by my own hands — the devouring of the hunter, not the butcher. And the corrosion meets the engine, and the engine does not corrode.*

"The boy speaks like a man," said Feng Si, softly.

"The boy speaks like a hungry man," said the woman. She turned from the platform. "Take him down. Feed him, put him in the pit with the rest of the intake. Send the report up tonight: martial soul Devouring Dragon, evil attribute, innate, top grade, innate full soul power. Write it exactly. If you soften one word of it, Feng Si, I will know."

"And the name, Elder? The registers—"

"No number for this one," said the woman, because the gate had reported the walk-in, and the walk-in was already being spoken about in rooms above this one. "He keeps his name. The altar will decide what else he keeps. Take him down."

---

They took him down.

The pit was a long hall cut out of the living rock under the altar city, with bunks along the walls and a fire in the middle and forty children between the ages of five and twelve, and it smelled the way all such places smell — of soup, and stone, and the small fear of the small. A boy at the gate above would have been searched and numbered and stripped of whatever he carried. Luo Chen carried nothing. He had made sure of it, because a boy who walks into a place like this with something to lose has given the place a handle.

Feng Si gave him a bunk and a bowl and a rule.

"We eat on obedience," he said, pleasantly, at the head of the hall. "Strength is what obedience buys. Remember it, because the church measures you every month, and what you are given depends on what you are, and what you are is decided by how well you keep the rules. The first rule is the bells. The second rule is the work. The third rule is that nothing in this hall belongs to you except your bowl. Do you understand?"

"Yes," said Luo Chen.

He did not get to eat right away, because the pit had its own customs, and the customs were older than the rules.

The largest boy in the hall was eleven, broad as a man, with a shaved head and a scar where his ear had been cut once and grown back wrong, and he had been watching the walk-in since the walk-in came down the stair. He was called Kui, and he was at the eighth level of soul power, and had been at the eighth level for two years, because his soul was a stone mill and no one gives rings to stone mills, and the pit keeps such children for the work until the monthly measure sorts them out. New children were the pit's only entertainment, and this one had come in without a number, which had never happened, and without fear, which happened even less.

"You," said the largest boy, standing up over him while Luo Chen held his bowl. "You walked in. Say it. Say you walked in yourself, because nobody walks in. You get brought, or you get sold, or you get caught. I got caught." He showed the scar. "You're a lie. Call yourself a lie and I'll leave you your bowl."

The hall had gone quiet around them, the way halls do, and forty children watched the new one the way the starving watch bread.

Luo Chen looked up at him, and set his bowl down on the bunk, carefully, because the bowl was the only thing in the hall that belonged to him and he intended to keep it that way.

"My name is Luo Chen," he said. "And you're in my light."

The swing came fast, for a boy that size — fast and mean and practiced, aimed to hurt.

It did not land. Luo Chen had been learning bodies for longer than the big boy had been alive, in a life that no one in this hall would ever hear about, and the orphan camps of the border wars had finished the lesson the book began. He stepped inside the swing the way a door swings past its frame, caught the wrist, used the weight that was coming at him, and the largest boy in the pit went down onto the stone floor with his own force and Luo Chen's knee in his throat, and the hall heard the air leave him all at once.

Luo Chen stood over him, breathing evenly, and let go of the wrist.

"I'm not a lie," he said, to the boy on the floor, and to the hall. "I'm the proof. If the gate is a trap, why does it open both ways?"

Nobody answered, because the pit had never heard anything like it, and because the largest boy in the hall was sitting up with his hand on his throat and the expression of someone meeting a fact.

"Kui," said Feng Si, from the head of the hall, without moving from his place. "Back to your bunk. The rest of you — the walk-in keeps his bowl, and his name, and his bunk. Anyone who touches him answers to the measure. Eat."

The hall ate. Luo Chen ate with them, and the soup was thin, and he did not mind, because he had eaten thinner, and because the bowl in his hands was the first thing in this second life that had cost him exactly what he meant to pay.

*One day,* he thought, with the private humor he kept for his own use, while the pit chewed around him. *I've been their property for one day, and already I've won a fight and passed an exam and made a master nervous. Keep this pace up and the church is going to be very expensive to keep.*

---

The lights went down at the night bell, and the pit slept the sleep of the watched, and Luo Chen lay on his bunk with his eyes open in the dark and went over the account, the way he went over it every night, because plans were the only property he had that this place could not number.

He knew what this church was. He had known since his old life, when he had read the book that told this world's future — the Holy Spirit Cult, the dark church of the continent, the thing that lived under the forest at the edge of Ming Du and raised its knives on softening drugs and broken wills, until the day a boy from another story walked into its own forest and burned it out root and branch. He knew its ending. He knew the names of its masters and the shape of its fall, and he knew that every plan built on that knowledge was a plan built on sand, because a story remembered is not the world itself, and the world had already begun to change around him the moment he knocked on the gate.

He did not care. A ladder is a ladder even if the last rung breaks.

What he needed from this place, he needed in order: a vessel, a ring, a method, and a road out. The vessel came first — his body was the boat everything else sailed in, and the orphan years and six years of deliberate work had built it better than any six-year-old's body in this city, and it would be built further, every night, in the dark, with the patience of a man who knows what a vessel is for. The ring came second — his soul power stood at the ceiling of the tenth level, born full, and the gate above the tenth level only opened one way: a soul beast's ring, taken from the beast his own two hands had killed. The cult would give him that hunt, in time, because the cult fed the hungers it could use, and no one in this hall had ever been as useful as he intended to become.

And underneath all of it, grain by grain, ran the other work — the draughts they would give him, the songs they would sing, the softening of the little knives. He knew it was coming. Let it come. He would learn every note of it, and the learning of the song would be his deafness to it, and the church itself would pay for the wall that kept the church out.

Above him, far up through the stone, the altar city slept over its own secrets, and under the city the forest breathed, and somewhere in the depths of the forest the things with dragon's blood in them hunted their night's hunger.

*Tomorrow,* thought Luo Chen, and closed his eyes. *Tomorrow the real measuring starts. Good. I've brought my own weights.*

# Chapter 2: The First Measure

One month passed in the pit the way months pass in such places — one day at a time, and every day measured.

The days had a shape, and the shape was not kind, but it was honest about what it was. The dawn bell woke the hall. The body work came first — the running and the carrying and the striking against the straw bags in the yard under the altar, forty children sweating through the cold, because the church had learned what every master of violence has learned, that a soul lives in a body and a weak body wastes a soul. Then the bowls. Then the lessons. Then the bowls again. Then the night bell, and the dark, and the hours that belonged to him.

In the dark hours he did his own work, because the church's work was the shape of the day, and his work was the shape of the years. He worked the body the way he had worked it since the orphan camps — the breath held and driven, the small repeated strains, the patient engineering of a six-year-old's bone and sinew toward what they would need to be. The book of his old life was plain on one point above all: the ring's burden is borne by the vessel, and a vessel is built or it is begged for, and nothing in this world is ever begged for cheap. And every night, before the work and after it, he listened to the pit the way a man listens to a country he intends to own — and the one thing that ruled it all was the monthly measure.

---

The method came on the ninth day.

Feng Si gathered the intake at the head of the hall — seven children who had come down the stairs in the same month as Luo Chen, and stood before them with his bone flute hanging at his belt, and spoke the way he spoke about everything, softly, as if kindness were a habit he had practiced until it was indistinguishable from the thing itself.

"You have souls," he said. "Now you will learn to feed them. Sit."

They sat.

"The world is full of soul power," said Feng Si. "It is in the air and in the earth and in every living thing, the way water is in the world, and the soul drinks it the way the body drinks water. The drinking is called cultivation. Every soul master on this continent, from the child with one ring to the Titled Douluo who carries nine, began exactly where you begin now — seated, breathing, drawing the world in. Watch."

He seated himself on the stone and closed his eyes, and the bone flute at his belt rose into the air of its own accord, and Luo Chen felt it the way one feels a change of weather — a draw, a current, the soul power of the hall moving toward the thin man like water toward a drain.

"When you draw the world in," said Feng Si, opening his eyes, "it settles in your center and becomes your soul power, and your soul power climbs level by level toward the gates — the gates at every tenth level, which open only to rings. You know the gates. You know what opens them. The church will give you both, the method and the hunt, if you are what the measure says you are. The method is called the Soul-Drawing Formula. It is the church's own, and it is given to no one outside these walls, and it is the first thing the church gives you, which means it is the first thing the church will ask back."

He taught them the Formula then — the posture, the breathing, the path the soul power was to walk through the body, down through the center and back, until the drawing became as natural as the beating of the heart.

Luo Chen learned it the way he learned everything, completely, and asked nothing, because the Formula was not a thing to ask about. It was a thing to take apart.

That night, at the end of the bench by the fire, with the pit snoring around him, he ran the Formula for the first time — and the soul power of the pit came to him the way the book said it came to souls like his, eagerly and in quantity, because a devouring soul draws the way a whirlpool draws, and the drawing was fast, and the fastness was real, and underneath the fastness something else stood up and looked at him, the way the draught had looked at him in the hall above.

The Formula pulled harder than any honest method had a right to pull, and it pulled in a direction. It did not only draw the world's soul power in — it reached, faintly, for the life around it, for the warmth of the sleeping bodies in the hall, for the blood in them, the way a root reaches for water it has not been given. That was the hook, and the hook was not an accident of the method. The hook was the method, and the method was the church's, and the church had put the hook in on purpose, because a soul that learns to reach for life at the very first lesson will never again be able to imagine feeding any other way.

Luo Chen sat very still in the dark, with the Formula running and the hook reaching, and the engine went over the hook the way the engine went over everything that touched him — completely, and without fear.

*So this is the shape of your leash,* he thought. *Honest work. The speed is real, and the reach is real, and the two come tied together, the way you tie everything. Very well. I will keep the speed. The reach, I will file down, grain by grain, in the dark, until the day you hand me a longer leash and believe it holds — and the filing is mine, and the keeping of it is mine, and that is the difference between us, that you do not know the file exists.*

He ran the Formula again, and this time the drawing came clean, and the reach came filed, and the soul power settled in his center like water settling in a cup, and he sat in the dark of the pit and drank the world in the way the world was never meant to be drunk — quietly, and without owing anyone for it.

---

The monthly measure came at the first bell of the new month, and the whole pit stood for it, because the measure was the pit's only truth.

It was done at the head of the hall, where the stone floor was worn smooth by ten thousand standings. Feng Si kept the ledger. The crystal ball stood on its black pillar — the soul-measuring crystal of the appraisal hall, carried down for the day, because the measure read what the children were, and what the children were was the church's only business. Behind Feng Si stood the assessor, grey-eyed, her bone whip sheathed, because the measure was not a matter for the whip, and her presence was the measure's real weight.

The children came forward one by one, and the crystal gave its light, and Feng Si wrote.

"Name?"

"Zhou Xiaoman. Level four." The crystal glowed low, a thin light at its belly. Feng Si wrote, and his face said nothing, and the boy went back to the line with his eyes on the floor.

"Name?"

"He Shun. Level six." A fuller glow. The same face. The same floor.

"Name?"

"Mo Ya."

The boy who stepped forward was twelve, tall for the pit, with a shaved head and narrow eyes and the stillness of a thing that had already learned what it was for, and he did not put his hand on the crystal the way the small ones did, diffidently. He put his hand on it the way an owner puts his hand on his own property.

The crystal burned nearly to its crown.

"Level nineteen," said Feng Si, and wrote it, and for the first time his face said something, which was satisfaction. "One ring. Soul Master, on the edge of the second gate. The pit's best this month, as last month. Step back, Mo Ya."

Mo Ya stepped back, and his narrow eyes went down the line of the intake the way a landlord's eyes go over a field, and stopped on the smallest child in the line, because that was what such eyes do — and then they moved on, because the smallest child was not worth the stopping, and the eyes went further, and found the one child in the line who had not looked down at the floor once.

"You," said Mo Ya. "The walk-in. A month in the pit, and I haven't seen your light. What level are you?"

The line went quiet the way lines go quiet, completely.

"The measure will read me when the measure reads me," said Luo Chen.

"The measure reads everyone," said Mo Ya. "I'm asking before the measure reads you."

"Then you're asking what the measure is for," said Luo Chen.

The hall made a sound then, small and swallowed, the sound forty children make when something dangerous becomes more dangerous, and Feng Si's soft voice came down on it the way a hand comes down on a flame.

"The walk-in's turn comes with the line," said Feng Si. "Stand where you stand, both of you."

The line moved. The crystal read. The small ones read small and the older ones read less small, and one boy of eleven read level twelve with a white ring, a ten-year ring, the kind no one with a choice takes, and the boy went back to the line wearing it the way a man wears a debt.

Then the measure read the walk-in.

Luo Chen put his hand on the crystal, and the crystal burned — edge to edge, crown to base, full, the way it had burned in the hall above, because he was what he was, and a month of pit did not change what a soul was born.

The hall went very still, because the hall had never seen a full reading, and did not know what it meant.

"Level ten," said Feng Si, quietly, and wrote it, and did not look up from the ledger. "As before. Brimfull, without a drop to spare — the boy stands at his gate, the way he stood at it the day he came in. The level does not climb past the tenth without a ring, and the church knows it. Write this in the ledger too, Elder," he said, to the assessor, still without looking up, "because the ledger should hold the truth of it: the walk-in has not climbed this month because there is nothing for him to climb. He is waiting on the gate, and the gate is waiting on the church."

The assessor looked at Luo Chen a long moment, and Luo Chen looked back at her, because looking down at the floor was what the floor was for, and he had never once in this life or the other one been good at floors.

"So," said Mo Ya, from the line. "A month in the pit, and still a Soul Scholar with no ring. The crystal burns for him like a lamp, and he hasn't even climbed." He was smiling the way boys smile when they have found the safe side of something. "Maybe the light is all he is."

"Maybe," said Luo Chen. "Ask the crystal what your light did at my level."

Mo Ya's smile stopped, because the crystal had read Mo Ya at seven the year before last, and the hall remembered it even if the ledger had been kept, and Feng Si's voice came down again, softer than before, which was the warning sign of it.

"The measure is done with readings," said Feng Si. "The measure has another part, and the intake will see it now, because the intake has earned the seeing. Mo Ya — the floor."

---

The floor was the hall's open center, ten paces of worn stone, and the pit gathered around it the way the pit gathered around everything that mattered, and the measure's second part was not written in any ledger, because it was not a reading but a showing.

"The church does not raise readers," said the assessor, and her voice carried the way the whip carried, without effort. "The church raises knives. A knife is proved by what it does against another knife. Mo Ya, you are the pit's best. The walk-in is the pit's question. Answer each other."

Mo Ya stepped onto the floor, and his soul rose with him.

It was a crow, black and lean, that stood on his shoulder with its feathers standing out like knives, and around the crow one ring turned — yellow, the honest hundred-year kind, the first true ring, the gate already passed. The crow's soul washed over the boy's body the way beast souls do, and he grew a finger's breadth in the light, and his fingers curled the way claws curl, and his narrow eyes went round and black at the edges.

"Ghost Crow," said Feng Si, to the intake, pleasantly, the way one names a thing for children who have not seen many souls. "A beast soul of the shadow kind. One yellow ring, one soul skill. Watch, and learn what a ring is worth."

Luo Chen stepped onto the floor, and let his own soul rise to meet it.

The lamps went low the way they had gone low in the hall above, and the shadows leaned, and the Devouring Dragon stood up behind him, long and black, and the pit's air went thin with the pull of it, and forty children took one step back together without knowing they had done it. Mo Ya's Ghost Crow ruffled and screamed, a thin sharp sound, and would not look at the dragon, because crows know what eats crows.

But Mo Ya did not step back, and Luo Chen filed it, because a boy who does not step back from a dragon at twelve is a boy worth knowing the shape of.

"Begin," said the assessor.

Mo Ya came fast, and the yellow ring flashed on his crow, and his soul skill woke — Crow-Shadow Step, the body gone thin as a shadow and quick as one, slipping sideways out of the light into the space beside Luo Chen, claws first for the throat, because the pit teaches nothing about mercy and a hundred-year skill does what its owner is.

It was fast, faster than anything the walk-in's body had met in this life, and the claws opened three lines on his shoulder before the engine finished naming them, and the blood came hot down his arm, and the pain came with it, sharp and honest.

And that was the end of Mo Ya's advantages, because the soul skill had been spent, and the engine had the measure of it now — the angle, the reach, the half-breath between the thinning and the striking — and Luo Chen had spent six years building a body and thirty years before them learning what a body was for.

He took the second strike on his forearm the way one takes a thing one has chosen to take, and his other hand caught Mo Ya's wrist, and his weight went forward into the boy's middle the way the orphan camps teach weight to go, and the pit's best came down onto the worn stone with his own speed and the walk-in's knee, and the air left him all at once the way it had left Kui, because that is the law of the floor, that speed borrows and weight collects.

Luo Chen stood over him with his shoulder bleeding down his arm, and let go of the wrist, and stepped back, because the floor had said its word.

The hall was silent the way only a hall of children can be silent, completely, and with all of it awake.

"A ring is worth a skill," said Luo Chen, to the boy on the floor, without triumph, because triumph is a thing that spends itself and he had never had coin to spare. "A skill is worth one surprise. After the surprise, it's a body against a body, and my body has been working longer than yours. Get up, Mo Ya. The floor isn't done with either of us."

Mo Ya got up. He looked at the walk-in a long moment with his narrow eyes, and what stood in the looking was not the hatred, because the pit's hatreds were for the weak, and what stood in it was the only thing the pit honestly gave anyone, which was the recognition of the strong for the strong.

"The gate is waiting on the church," said Mo Ya. "So is mine, for the second ring. We'll see what the church gives us, walk-in, and then we'll see the floor again."

"I'm counting on it," said Luo Chen.

The assessor looked at the blood on the walk-in's shoulder, and at the boy under it, and her grey eyes went to the ledger in Feng Si's hands, and she said the words that the hall had been built to hear.

"Write him in," she said. "The next hunt takes candidates at the month's turning, and the walk-in walks with them. His gate has waited a month for the church. Let the church stop making it wait."

---

The cup came that evening to the measured, and it came to the walk-in first.

Feng Si brought it himself, in the dark wood bowl, dusk-colored and warm, sweet to the nose the way the pit's food was never sweet, and the hall watched it go down the line to the bench by the fire the way the hall watched everything, because the cup was the church's kindness, and kindness in this place was a thing worth watching.

"The welcome of the altar," said Feng Si, pleasantly. "For the ones the measure keeps. Drink, and be warm."

Luo Chen looked at the bowl, and he knew what it was, because the book had told him the shape of it long ago — the softening of the little knives, the seasoning, the hand laid gently on the wall of the mind, sung daily until the song was the only music the knife knew. He had known it was coming since the day he knocked on the gate, and the knowing had never once changed what he meant to do about it.

He drank.

The draught went down warm and sweet, and for the space of a breath it was only a draught, and then it stood up in him the way the book said such things stood up — soft, and warm, and loosening, the whisper under the thoughts, the first note of the song. The boy's part of him felt the ease of it, the quieting of the pit's cold, and the man part of him went to work the way the man part of him always went to work, quietly and completely. The engine took the song's measure — its notes, its pull, the place in the blood where it went to root — and began to build the answer in the dark, slowly, the way the wall is built, grain by grain, until the day this song comes again and finds a wall where it expected a door.

*First grain,* said the man inside the child, while the child's face went soft with the draught's warmth and the child's eyes went a little heavy with the easing of it, and Feng Si watched him ease with the satisfied eye of the keeper of the ledger. *Sing it to me daily, then. Every note of your song is a brick of my wall, and the church is paying for the church's own wall, and that is the only kind of wall I ever trusted, the kind the enemy pays for.*

The night bell rang, and the pit slept the sleep of the watched, and Luo Chen lay on his bunk with three claw-lines burning on his shoulder and the draught's warmth in his blood, and went over the account the way he went over it every night.

The method was his now, filed and clean, the speed kept and the reach denied. The measure had read him, and the reading had gone up, and the reading had come back down as a hunt. The gate stood ahead of him on the road, the wall of the tenth rank, locked the way mouths are locked, and the price was in its mouth — a taken life, a soul beast's ring, the first true ring, of the dragon's own line if the forest and the moment allowed, because like fuel changes a soul toward what it is, and strange fuel only fills.

*The month's turning,* he thought, and closed his eyes, while the forest breathed far above the stone of the pit, and somewhere in the depths of it the things with dragon's blood in them hunted their night's hunger, and did not know that the hunger coming for them had a name, and a number of days, and plans. *Good. The vessel is ready enough, and the gate is tired of waiting, and so am I.*

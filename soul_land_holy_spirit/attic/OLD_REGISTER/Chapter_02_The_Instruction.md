# Chapter 2: The Instruction

The pit slept, and Luo Chen did not, because the pit's nights were the only hours that belonged to him, and he had work for them.

The work was the body. He did it in the dark of his sleeping-place, in the small room cut from the rock with eleven other boys, where the snoring stood thick as fog, and he did it the way he had done it in the camps and on the road — quietly, completely, with the patience of a man who knows what a vessel is for. The breath drawn down and held; the blood sent where the blood was sent; the small repeated strains that tell the bone and the sinew what they must become. He knew what grew in him and he knew what it grew inside, and the second knowing was older than the first, because the engine had been teaching him its one quiet lesson since the womb, which is that nothing is carried farther than the vessel allows.

And under the body's work, the sea. That was the work nobody could see and the work that mattered most, because the sea of him stood where no child's sea has any reason to stand — the mind and the heart of him were one, the way the old words say it, the Spirit Connection standing in him the way it stands in a grown soul master, the thoughts and the will a single thing — and the river of it ran on the law of the world, which is that the sea grows with the body until the body's fortieth year, and his sea was a man's sea riding a child's growth, and it went up in the dark of the pit like water rising in a well that has not met its bank.

The dragon slept in him, fed and quiet. Deeper than the dragon, the heart beat its one hidden beat. And Luo Chen worked his two works in the dark among the snoring, and filed the day away, and waited, because the report had gone up by the night brazier, and the answer would come down, and the answer was the door, and doors are what the patient walk through.

---

The answer came down on the morning of the eleventh day, and it came the way all answers of the Cult came — with grey cloth and soft feet and the hall going silent the way halls go silent.

The man who brought it was not Feng Si's kind of man. He was broad where Feng Si was thin, and still where Feng Si was pleasant, and he wore the grey of the altar with the black round mark upon the breast of it, the two skulls faint on the coin of it, and the children of the pit stood in their line and were very still, because even the pit knew the mark, and knew that the mark did not come down to the pit for small reasons.

Feng Si stood at the head of the hall, and for the first time in the memory of the pit, Feng Si stood the way the children stood.

"Gao Xu of the altar speaks," said Feng Si, and his pleasant voice was gone from him the way pleasantness goes from a knife when the work is real.

Gao Xu looked down the line of children with eyes that had no more warmth in them than the assessor's grey, and the looking of him went over the forty-one and rested where it rested, and the resting of it was on the boy who stood third from the left.

"The altar has received word from above," said Gao Xu, and his voice filled the hall the way water fills a vessel, without hurry and without gap. "The Holy Emperor's light shines upon the walking-in of the devoted. It has been read above that one of the little knives of this pit is sharp beyond the measure of the pit. The altar gives its child to the house under the forest. So the light is answered."

The hall was very still, because the hall had heard the words but not the meaning of them, and the meaning of them belonged to the men and not to the children. Luo Chen stood third from the left in his six-year-old body, and kept his face the face of a child who has been spoken over, and inside the face the man read the words the way the man reads everything, completely.

*The house under the forest,* he thought, and the thought was quiet and sharp, because the book had told him what lay under the forest, and the forest was the Evil Demon Forest, and the house was the edge of the thing the whole pit slept on and did not know. *They are not moving me out of the investment. They are moving me into it.*

"The named child will go at the evening bell," said Gao Xu. "The altar's gift goes with him. The devotion of the pit is remembered before the light."

He was gone the way he had come, with grey cloth and soft feet, and the hall breathed out the way halls breathe out, and Feng Si stood at the head of the hall a moment longer than the silence needed him, and then the day's ordinary began, and nothing in the ordinary was ordinary any more.

---

In the hall of the altar, in the hour between the reading and the bell, Gao Xu stood at the window where the altar city's roofs went down toward the forest's edge, and Feng Si stood behind him with his empty hands, because a report once sent up belongs to the hall above and not to the man who wrote it.

"You sent the report honestly," said Gao Xu, without turning from the window. "Fullness from one soul. The clean eating. The eyes. You wrote what you saw."

"I wrote what I saw," said Feng Si. "I did not write what I think, because what I think is not a thing with a shape yet."

"Give it a shape," said Gao Xu.

Feng Si was quiet a moment, the way he was quiet when the work was real. "The boy is the truest evil soul that has come through this altar in my years," he said. "And the boy is not evil. I have taught the pit long enough to know the difference between the hunger and the wanting-to-hurt, and the boy has the one and not the other, and the teaching does not take, on him, the way it takes on the rest. He was full of it before he came to us. I do not know what taught him."

"The Holy Emperor's light teaches whom it chooses," said Gao Xu, without turning from the window.

"The Holy Emperor's light is a doctrine," said Feng Si, softly, "and I am asking about a boy. Above read his name twice. I would know whose hand wrote the answer, so that I may know what the boy is being kept for."

Gao Xu turned from the window then, and his face had the stillness of it, the stillness that the pit had learned to fear without knowing why.

"You would not," said Gao Xu. "The hand above the altar is not a thing the altar asks about. The boy is being kept for what he is, novice-master — a hunger that does not spoil the meat is a hunger the church can feed, and a hunger the church can feed is a hunger that will eat where the church points. Tend your pit. The boy is the forest's now."

Feng Si bowed the way the altar bows, and Gao Xu went down from the hall with soft feet, and the answer to the question Feng Si had asked stood in the hall after him, and had no name, because the hand that had written the instruction had written it behind a curtain, and the curtain was a doctrine too.

---

The evening bell had not come yet, and the pit had an hour of its own to give, and the pit gave it the way the pit gives everything — with its whole and only voice.

"So," said Kui, standing over him with his bowl under his arm, because the midday meal was done and the hour was free, and Kui had been standing over things his whole life and had nothing else to do with the hour. "The house under the forest. The golden one goes up and the rest of us stay in the rock."

"The rest of you stay in the rock," said Luo Chen, "because I ate a boy and you didn't. That was the test, Kui. There will be others."

Kui looked at him the way simple things look at things that do not fit, and what stood in the looking was not the bullying, because the bullying had gone out of Kui on the floor of the midday hall some days before, and what stood in it now was the only thing the pit had ever honestly given anybody, which was the respect of the weak for the strong.

"I heard what they say in the wall-rooms," said Kui, quietly, in the voice he kept for the things that were real. "The ones that go under the forest don't come back up. The ones that go under the forest belong to the forest after."

"I know," said Luo Chen. "I'm counting on it."

There was a thing in Kui's face then, a thing that moved and was gone, the grief of the pit for the pit's own, and Kui stood there with his bowl under his arm and could not say what the thing was, because the pit had not given him words for it, and in the end he did the only thing the pit had given him, and he held out the bowl.

"Take it," said Kui. "The evening's. I'm not hungry."

Luo Chen looked at the bowl, and at Kui, and took the bowl, because refusing would have cost Kui the only thing Kui had to spend, and he ate of it standing in the hall with forty pairs of eyes around him, and the meal was the meal of the pit and it tasted like the meal of the pit, and he wrote Kui down in the place he kept the things that were his and not the Cult's — the second name, and the reason for the keeping of it.

---

The assessor came at the last of the hour, and she came alone, and her coming was the last of the things that belonged to the pit.

"The gift of the altar," she said, and set down on the stone of the hall-floor the things of it — a scroll bound in grey cord, and a bowl of dark wood, and in the bowl a draught the color of the dusk, warm, and sweet to the nose the way the pit's food was never sweet. "The method of the first step, for the soul that hungers. And the welcome of the altar, for the body that carries it. Drink, little knife, and be warm on the road."

The hall watched from the wall-rooms, because the hall knew a gift when it saw one, and had never once been given one. Luo Chen stood before the gift in his six-year-old body, and the man inside the body looked at the gift the way the man looks at a thing he has been waiting for, because the book had told him this would come — the drugs of them, the teas of them, the softening of them, the seasoning of the little knives — and the book had told him the shape of it without ever telling him the taste, and the taste was the thing he could not know until the knowing of it.

He drank.

The draught went down warm and sweet, and for the space of a breath it was only a draught, and then it was not only a draught, because the thing in it stood up in him the way the book had said such things stood up — soft, and warm, and loosening, a hand laid gently on the wall of the mind, a whisper under the thoughts, the first note of the song that the Cult sang to its little knives until the song was the only music they knew. It was not poison. It was worse than poison, because poison is honest about what it wants, and the thing in the draught wanted his wanting, and asked for it sweetly.

The boy's part of him felt the pull of it the way the book said children felt it — the warmth of it, the ease of it, the quieting of the pit's cold.

The man part of him was already at work.

The engine knew the draught the way the engine knows all things, by the body's own report, and the report said what it was and what it did and where it went, and the man gave the engine its instruction the way he had given it instructions all his life, quietly and completely: *learn this. Meet it. Take its measure. Build the answer in the dark of the blood, slowly, the way the wall is built, grain by grain, until the day this song comes again and finds a wall where it expected a door.* And over the work, for the grey eyes watching him from the far side of the hall, the six-year-old's face went soft with the draught's warmth, and the eyes of it went a little heavy with the easing of it, and he stood in the hall swaying with the welcome of the altar the way a child sways, and the assessor watched him sway, and wrote in her own place what she was writing, and the writing of it was satisfied.

*First grain of the wall,* said the man inside the child, to the dark of his own blood. *They will sing this song to me every day of my devotion. Good. Let them teach me, on their own draughts, how to be deaf to them.*

---

He walked out of the pit at the evening bell with the scroll under his arm and the draught's warmth in his blood and the two names in his private place, and the grey man led him up through the altar city as the lamps were coming on in the dusk, and the road went down at the city's far edge, down toward the trees, and the trees stood where the trees of all forests stand, which is at the edge of the light, and the dark of them stood beyond the light the way it has always stood.

Luo Chen walked into the dark of the Evil Demon Forest with the step of a six-year-old and the balance of the thing that had built him, and the forest received him the way the deep places receive their own — without sound, and without welcome, and without end — and the man inside the child went down the road with his plans standing around him in the dark the way his plans stood, not as a schedule, not as a promise, but as the direction of the heart: the vessel before the gate; the first ring from the dragon's own line, when the vessel proved itself and the hunt found its moment; the glue of the whales in the city behind him, mispriced and waiting, when coin and leave could come; the secrets of the body-sect, far away in their fortress country, for the day the road went there; and over all of it, the list of the lamps he must never be seen by, standing in the dark of his own mind like stars he had chosen to steer around.

The gate of his cultivation stood ahead of him on the road, the wall of the tenth rank, the first wall of the world's ladder, and the wall said what walls say to the full, which is: *come up, and bring the price.*

The price was a taken life, because the world's ladder is built of taken lives, and he knew it, and he walked toward it down the road under the trees, and the forest breathed around him, and somewhere in the depths of it the things that had blood of the dragon in them went about their night's hunger, and did not know that the hunger that was coming for them walked on two legs, and was patient, and had plans.

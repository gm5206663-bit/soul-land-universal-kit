# Chapter 1: The First Supper

They gave him a number before they gave him a bed, and the number was the first collar of the Holy Spirit Cult, and Luo Chen wore it the way he wore his six-year-old skin — patiently, and as a disguise.

Nine days. He had been the Cult's property for nine days, in the novice pit under the altar city, and in nine days he had learned the pit the way he had learned everything in this second life, which is completely, and with his eyes open. The pit was a long hall cut out of the living rock under the altar, and it held forty-one children, and the forty-one children were the Cult's smallest investment, and the investment was managed by a man named Feng Si.

Feng Si was thin the way a knife is thin, and cheerful the way the knife's owner is cheerful when the work is going well. He wore the Cult's grey, and he spoke to the children softly, because the children who were spoken to softly did not scream as much, and the screaming wasted the goods.

"Morning, little knives," Feng Si said at the bell, standing at the head of the hall with his hands folded. "Who can tell me what we eat on?"

"Strength," said the children, in the ragged voice of children reciting.

"Wrong," said Feng Si pleasantly. "We eat on obedience. Strength is what obedience buys. Say it."

"We eat on obedience," said the children.

Luo Chen said it with them, in a six-year-old's voice, and thought about the altar above his head with the adult mind behind the voice, because he knew what the altar was and what stood under the altar and what was coming to all of it. The headquarters of the Holy Spirit Cult lay under the Evil Demon Forest near Ming Du, and he was in it, and in his memory of the book that had been his first life there was an ending for this place, and the ending had a date the way a debt has a date, and nobody in the pit knew it except the small boy standing third from the left in the morning line.

*Enjoy your altar,* he thought at the stone above him, with the humor he kept for his own private use, the humor that had kept him sane through six years of being an infant. *I'm going to eat very well on it before the bill comes.*

---

There was a boy in the pit named Kui, and Kui was large for his years and had been in the pit the longest, and the Cult had taught Kui the only lesson the pit taught, which was that everything smaller than you is food.

"Walk-in," Kui said at the midday bowl, looming, because the pit had a story about the new little one, and the story offended Kui the way stories offend the simple. "You walked in. Say it. Say you walked in yourself."

"I did," said Luo Chen, eating.

"Nobody walks in," said Kui. "You get brought. You get sold. You get caught. I got caught." He showed the scar where the catching had happened. "You're a lie. Say you're a lie and I'll leave you your bowl."

The hall had gone quiet the way halls go quiet when there is going to be something to watch, and forty pairs of eyes came around, and Luo Chen finished his mouthful, because the body he ran was small and its fuel was scarce and he had plans for every grain of it.

"I'm not a lie," he said. "I'm the proof. If the door is a trap, why does it open both ways?"

"Smart mouth," said Kui, and swung.

It was not much of a swing, by the standards of the world Luo Chen remembered; it was a big child's swing, and the body of a six-year-old was under it, and the body of the six-year-old had been built from the egg of his first day in this world by something that did not waste anything, and Luo Chen had spent six years finding out what that something had built. He was not inside the swing. He had simply been somewhere else a breath earlier, and the somewhere else was behind Kui, and Kui's own weight did the rest of the work against the floor.

The hall made a sound. Feng Si appeared the way Feng Si always appeared, which is suddenly and without hurry, and looked at Kui on the floor, and at the small boy standing over him with the bowl still in his hand, not spilled.

"What is this," said Feng Si.

"He asked me a question," said Luo Chen. "I answered it."

Something moved in Feng Si's face, quick as a fish under water, and was gone. "Finish your bowl," he said. "Both of you. The assessor is here."

---

The pit told the story of the walk-in the way the pit told everything, in pieces, and the pieces were true.

The border war had made him alone before he had words for alone, and the camps had made him number-shaped, and the camp had been burned by men with cold shining things, and the burning had not been in any book he remembered, and that was the first thing the new life taught him — that the book did not hold everything, and the edges of the book were sharp. He had been fed by the road and by the winter's pity, which is a thin feeder, and the feeding had been only half of it, because the man inside the child had begun his work on the first day the child could stand, training the body in the camps and on the road the way a man trains a vessel he means to keep, with the winter for a teacher and the hunger for a whetstone, because he knew what the body was for, and what grows in a body, and that neither can be carried farther than the vessel allows.

At six had come the awakening, in the camp's last ruined shrine, and the thing that stood up out of him was a dragon — dark-blue, three-fold scaled, thick of limb, short of arm, head like a third of the whole of him, tail dragging, and hungry, hungry the way a word is hungry for its meaning. And behind the dragon, deeper than seeing, in the place where his blood ran, a second thing had stood up without standing, a furnace the shape of his own heart, and it had beaten once, and hidden itself, and he had understood in that beat that he would never show it to any eye in this world. The hunger had come that night and had not left.

The road out of the camp country had run past a town with a black round mark on its doors, a mark like a coin with two skulls carved faint on it, and he had seen the mark before, in the book, and he had known what lived under the mark, and what the living under the mark did to children with souls like his, and he had walked up to the door anyway, because the outside world would hunt what he was, and the inside world would feed it, and he had plans for the inside world, and the plans were large, and the hand that knocked had been steady.

---

The assessor came down into the pit in the afternoon, and the assessor was not Feng Si, and the difference between them was the difference between the knife and the hand that decides where the knife goes.

She was old without being bent, in the grey of the Cult, and her eyes went over the forty-one children the way a merchant's eyes go over a shipment, and stopped here and there the way merchant's eyes stop, on the goods worth handling. The children stood in the line and showed their souls the way they had been taught, because in the pit you were taught everything once, and the second teaching was done with pain.

Luo Chen stood third from the left and showed his soul, and the showing of it was the one thing he had practiced hardest in nine days, because the showing was the lock and the lock had to hold. The Devouring Dragon stood up out of him in the grey light of the pit, and it was what it was — dark-blue, three-fold scaled, thick of limb, head like a third of the whole of him — and its hunger came off it the way heat comes off a kiln, and it was the honest soul of an evil line, and he let it be completely what it was, because the best disguise is the thing itself, shown all the way to the edge of what the viewer expects to see. The heart he sank. The heart was not a thing that was shown; the heart was the furnace of his blood, body-bound, beating under everything, and he sank it the way you sink a stone in deep water, and held it there with the part of him that had been practicing since the shrine.

The assessor stood before him a long moment.

"Full," she said, and her voice had changed, and she said it again, to herself, the way people say things twice when the things are worth more than they had bid. "Innate fullness. From one soul." She looked at him the way you look at a coin that has come up out of the dirt gold. "What is your number."

"The number is his number," said Feng Si, pleasantly, from somewhere. "He hasn't asked to trade it."

"He will," said the assessor. She leaned down, close, and her eyes were grey and without any warmth at all, and Luo Chen looked into them with the face of a six-year-old and the patience of a man who had read the ending of her church. "The soul is the hunger," she said, softly, to him alone. "Everything else is the leash. Remember it, little knife."

"I'll remember," said Luo Chen.

He would remember the way the book remembers — completely, and with a use for it, because the woman was real and she was not in the book, and that made her dangerous the way all unwritten things were dangerous, and he had decided on the first day of the pit that he would never be dangerous to nothing, and never be nothing to the dangerous.

---

The first supper came at the evening bell, and the first supper was the lesson the Cult taught all its little knives, and the lesson had a shape, and the shape was a cage.

The cage was brought down by two of the grey men, and in the cage was a boy of perhaps twelve years, thin as the winter, chained at the wrist, and the boy had been in the pit once, because the pit knew him the way the pit knows its own — he had been the one who could not take the teaching, whose soul had come up weak and stayed weak, and the Cult does not keep what cannot be sharpened.

"A failure spends our rice," said Feng Si, to the hall, in his pleasant voice. "Tonight, the rice is repaid. The first of you who pays it eats at my table, and sits warm, and is written in the book that goes up." He looked along the line of children, and his look rested, and the resting of it was not chance, because Feng Si did nothing by chance. "You," he said. "The walk-in. Come."

The hall breathed out. Kui breathed out loudest.

Luo Chen walked out of the line with legs that belonged to a six-year-old and a balance that did not, and the cage was opened, and the failed boy was brought out on his chain, and a knife was put into Luo Chen's hand, and the knife was heavy the way real things are heavy.

The failed boy looked down at him. There was no pleading in the look, because the pit had taken the pleading out of him long before; there was only a kind of tired curiosity, the curiosity of the condemned for the shape of their ending.

"What's your name," said Luo Chen, quietly, in the space under the hall's silence.

The boy blinked. "Nobody calls me anything," he said. "Not here. Before, it was Shi Tou."

"Shi Tou," said Luo Chen, and filed the name in the place he kept the things that were his and not the Cult's. "Shi Tou, I can't not do this. You know that and I know that. But I can do it the one clean way, and the one clean way is all I'll take from you. You won't feel the second thing."

"I don't want to feel the second thing," said Shi Tou. "Do the first thing quick."

And Luo Chen did, because the body was small and the arm was short, but the something that had built the body from the first day did not miss, and the dragon in him rose with the stroke the way it had been waiting all his life to rise, and the hunger of the soul met the going-out of the boy's warmth and DRANK, and the drinking was — 

There are no words for it and he had two lives' worth of words and none of them fit. It was the first meal after six years of hungering, and it was horrible, and it was the truest thing that had ever happened to either of his lives, and the adult mind stood in the middle of it the way a man stands in a flood, and did not look away, because looking away was the one luxury his road would never afford him, and the dragon drank its fill of Shi Tou's going-out, and settled, and was quiet for the first time since the shrine.

The hall was very still.

Feng Si came and stood over him, and looked down at the small boy with the knife, and what Feng Si was looking for was the joy, because the joy was the mark of the true evil soul, the joy in the hurting, the sweetening that the Cult put in its tea. There was no joy in the boy's face. There was no cruelty. There was appetite, deep as a well, and under the appetite there was something Feng Si had never once seen in a novice of the pit, and did not have a name for.

"Clean," said Feng Si, at last. "You ate him clean."

"The soul wants the kill," said Luo Chen, in his six-year-old voice, because it was true, and because the truth was the safest mask, and because he had decided in the pit that he would never lie where the truth was stranger. "It doesn't want the hurting. The hurting spoils the meat."

Feng Si looked at him a long moment. "Who taught you that," he said.

"Hunger," said Luo Chen.

---

High above the hall of the pit, in his small room, Feng Si sat before the night brazier with the day's report in his hand, and the part of the report that mattered he read over once more before he sent it up, because the part of the report that mattered was the part he did not understand.

Of the forty-one, the shipment was ordinary. One was marked for the pain school, two for the herb gardens, the rest for the outer service in the fullness of time. One was different, and he had written him separately. The boy was the walk-in, the one who had come to the door himself, of whom he had reported at his taking. He was six years old, and his soul was the Devouring Dragon, full of the hunger, and his innate power was full — Feng Si had written the word twice for the copying clerk, that it might be copied correctly. Full, from one soul. At the first supper the boy had paid the rice, and he had paid it clean — no joy, no sweetening, no waste of the goods in cruelty; he had killed the failure-boy the way a butcher kills, and he had spoken to the boy before the stroke, and asked him his name. I do not know what to call this, Feng Si had written. He is the truest evil soul that has come through this altar in my years, and he is not cruel, and I have asked the brazier to ask the hall above whether these two things can stand in one boy, and what the hall above wants done with him if they can. His eyes are the last thing I will write. He looks at us, all of us, the way a man looks at a road he has already walked. Send down the instruction. The boy is waiting, and I do not think he is waiting for us.

He set the report into the brazier's mouth, and the fire took it the way the fire took everything, and the light of it stood a moment on the wall and was gone.

---

Luo Chen lay in the dark of the pit with the number of his collar and the warmth of his first meal and the name of Shi Tou in the place he kept his own things, and he did his ledger the way he did it every night, because the ledger was the only book in this place that was being written by him.

Nine days in, and the mask was holding, and the assessor's word had gone up, and the word was the first rung, and the rungs of this place were made of usefulness, and he would be useful the way the flood is useful — completely, and in his own direction. The dragon slept in him, fed and quiet, and deeper than the dragon the heart beat its one hidden beat, warm as a coal in the furnace of his blood, and the warmth was his own and no one's else, and it would stay no one's else until the day he chose.

Above him, under the altar, in a hall that stood in his first life's book with its every pillar drawn, a candle burned over a report, and somebody with the power to move the whole of the pit with a word read the name of the walk-in twice, and the reading of it was the first stitch of the road, and Luo Chen slept the sleep of the fed, and did not dream, because men with plans do not need to dream.

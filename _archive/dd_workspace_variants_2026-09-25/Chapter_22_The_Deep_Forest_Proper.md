# Chapter 22: The Deep Forest Proper

The forest north of the pass was not the forest he had been born in. It was what that forest had been told about.

He felt it before he could name it, in the first miles of walking. The light came down green and thin, strained through leaves so high and so layered that noon became a rumor and the dark never fully came. The trunks were towers. Some were wider than the ravine den was long, and their roots stood out of the ground like the ribs of country, and between them the soil lay soft and black and deep, made of rotted ages, giving under his feet and holding his heat. The air itself was thick. The old deep smell that had ridden the winds of his border his whole life was here the water he swam in.

He walked slowly, and he read as he went, and the reading took the whole first season.

Everything in it was old. The fern halls stood in acres. The moss took the fallen giants whole, and the giants beneath the moss had fallen onto giants before them. The small life was old — the quick things of the floor were heavier and slower and surer than their border cousins, colored like the dark, unafraid of the ordinary and gone at the first strange thing. The silence had weight in it. Not the pass's single held weight, one owner sleeping under one ground — this was a ground with many sleepers, far apart, their attention laid over itself like weather, so that the whole forest carried a faint pressure everywhere, as the low country had carried wind.

He was nothing in it. That was the change, and it took him a season to stop bracing against it. In the low country his blood had been a law. On the road it had been a scent on the wind. Here it was not even that. He was a young thing in an old house, walking between the floorboards of giants, and the forest did not know him at all.

---

It was the small life that taught him how to live there, and it taught him by walking.

The deep forest kept roads. He found them the second month: lines of polished ground running under the great trees, narrower than the river-road but the same idea — ground beaten by use, one road for many feet, going where the food and the water were. The deer-kinds made some, big-shouldered and silent, their dung old in it. The root-boar-kinds made others, torn and muddy. The small quick kinds ran all of them at dusk and dawn, and the birds crossed them low, and every road was walked by everyone, all day, one country's ordinary business.

He had spent his whole life leaving no print and no pattern. His first season in the deep forest, he did the same — off the roads, in the unmarked dark between, a shape avoiding shapes. And all that season the forest had a feel he could not place, like a room going quiet when he entered it, and he could not find the cause, because he was doing everything right.

The cause found him in a fern hall in the second month, over a kill.

He had gone off the road for it — a root-doe of the fat old kind, feeding alone in the ferns at midday, downwind, with a full hour of the slowest approach behind him, every trick of the watchers' years paid out clean. The take was perfect. He was standing over the body with the first hot mouthful in him when the hall went quiet.

It did not happen around the edges, as quiet happens in other forests. It happened from the ground up. The birds stopped. Then the insect-noise, in a widening circle, as if the floor itself had lifted its head. And under his feet, in the rotted black soil, he felt something come toward him through the ground — not sound, not smell, nothing his senses had a name for. A attention, moving. The roots of the giant above him had said him to the forest, every step of his slow hour, and the forest had walked its quiet ahead of him the whole way, and now something was coming to see what the quiet was about.

He never saw it. The pressure arrived the way the pass's had arrived, in the ground, total and unhurried — but where the pass's holder had read him in passing, this stopped, and stayed. It stood in the fern hall's quiet like a wall of held water, and it was interested, and he understood with his whole body that he had been made visible by the one thing he trusted most: that no one had seen him come.

He did the hardest thing he had ever done on a kill. He backed off it. Body low, warm meat abandoned behind him in the ferns. He walked — did not run; running was a confession — out of the unmarked dark, across the open ground, to the nearest walked road. He stepped onto it, turned north, and went at the ordinary pace, in the open, like one of the forest's own.

The pressure followed him to the road's edge. He felt it stand there, at the line of the polished ground, the way a man stands at a door. It did not cross. And then, slowly, as it had come, it drained back into the ground, and the birds came back on behind him, first one, then the floor's whole voice.

He walked for miles before his heart came down. Then he thought it through while he walked.

The road was the camouflage. That was the deep forest's law, the opposite of every law he had owned. On his border, the strange thing survived by being nothing — no print, no pattern, no road twice. Here, in a country where the ground itself repeated every step to whatever listened, there was no being nothing. There was only being ordinary or being a question. The unmarked dark was where questions lived, and the forest sent its quiet into that dark as his old country sent rain. The walked road was where everyone was, and everyone was beneath notice together.

He had crossed the pass by knowing what the ground was. He would live in the deep forest the same way. He walked the roads. He hunted what the roads allowed — the sick, the slow, the unlucky, taken at the edges at the ordinary hours, as every other hunter on the road hunted, nothing owned, nothing pressed. And when he wanted more than the road allowed, he went to the water.

The water said nothing to roots. That was the second law, and it was given to him rather than learned, his oldest possession come back to feed him: moving water was the one ground in the deep forest the forest could not read.

He proved it the first evening he went looking. The stream ran cold and dark under the leaves, quick over a bed of old stone, and in the tail of a fallen giant, where the current slowed and turned, a fish-kind held — a deep-bodied old thing, dark-backed, thick with years, holding its place in the moved water with no effort at all. He came down the bank at the ordinary hour, plain as a drinking beast, and stood in the shallows and let the water carry his shape apart as it had always carried his sound. The fish-kind did not read him. Nothing read him. The current said nothing to roots and the roots said nothing to the forest, and for the length of that whole evening he was, for the first time since the fern hall, a beast that nothing anywhere was thinking about.

The take was short and hard. The old ones are all jaw and opinion, and this one turned in the current and used the moved water against him, as he would have. Then it was his, and the years in it were his, and he stood in the noise eating, with the water pushing at his sides like a wall that had always held. He had been a beast of moving water since his second summer. In the deep forest proper, it was the only country that was still his.

The streams gave him more than the road's meat: the old fat kinds, the drinking beasts at dusk with their heads low, the seasons' slow plenty of a forest that had never once been hunted by anything that thought like him.

---

The seasons turned, and he turned with them, and the deep forest paid him.

The meals there were old. The fish-kinds carried years in them like the rocks carry heat, and the road's edge-killings were of beasts grown fat and heavy in a country where dying of age was a longer business than dying of teeth. He ate the years of the deep forest a meal at a time, and after the full meals came the deep sleep, and in the sleeps the eaten years became him, and he grew. His line lengthened. His scales came in darker, with a depth in them like the soil. By the second wet season he walked the roads of the deep forest like a local trouble — known, allowed for, a young heavy thing with a jaw, nothing the country needed to think about twice.

And one thing grew in him that was not body. After the fern hall, he could feel the quiet coming. It came on as weather comes, in a change too wide to see at once. The floor's voice thinned a half-mile ahead of him; the birds' pattern went wrong in a way he could not have named but never once mistook again. The feel of it arrived before the reason for it, so that many times in those seasons he knew the forest was going quiet for some other stranger before the stranger himself could know. It was a sense his border country had never needed and could not have taught: the reading of a whole ground's attention, learned from the pass the day the pass had read him, when he was too young to know he was learning it. He had paid a kill for that sense. On the roads of the deep forest, it was worth a kill a month.

Twice in those seasons the roads themselves taught him the last of the forest's manners. Once, the road he followed bent — all of it, the whole polished line of it, swinging wide around a country of darker trees for a day's walk, for no reason his eyes could find, and he took the bend, because when a road that everyone walks goes around something, the something is not his business. Far off through those darker trees, in the black hour, he saw a light standing on the land, and it was not fire, and he looked at it exactly as long as he had looked at the middle of the pass, and walked on. The other time, in the dry, a wind came down out of the north that smelled of stone and snow and enormous distance. Every old thing on the roads stopped walking and stood facing it. He stood with them. He did not know what they were hearing, and knew that not knowing was the ordinary condition of the young.

The pull ran under all of it, north, as it had run under the pass. But in the deep forest proper he came to know it better, because there was so much else to know it against. It was older than the forest's attention. The roots repeated steps; the quiet came for strangeness; the roads bent around the sleepers; and under all of that machinery, unmarked by any of it, the pull ran like a river under ice, and nothing in the forest seemed to read it at all. It did not press. It did not hurry. It was the oldest patient thing in a country of old patient things, and it was his.

And in the second dry season, on a spine of rock the road climbed to cross, it changed.

He had come up out of the trees with the road, onto the bare stone, and the country opened — the deep forest going on and on north, wave after wave of dark trees to the far haze. He stopped, because the pull had stopped being a direction. All his life it had been north, plain as hunger. Now, standing on the stone with the whole country open, it leaned — a few points east of north, toward the far country where the haze sat lowest and the sky had a weight in it that his eyes kept calling mountains. Not a swerve. A settle, like a current finding its channel. The road ahead still ran north. The pull no longer followed the road.

He stood on the stone a long time, reading the difference, the wind out of the north carrying the stone-and-snow smell that had stopped the old things on the roads. Then he came down off the spine and took the road north, because roads were how the country allowed walking, and because he had learned in the fern hall what going his own way cost.

But he carried the lean with him, and weighed it at every bend. Somewhere ahead, the road and the pull would come apart for good — the road going where feet had worn it, the pull going where nothing he knew of had ever walked. When that day came, he would have to leave the ordinary and go into the unmarked dark again, carrying all his hard learning about what that cost.

That was the road's next question, and the deep forest proper, old and green and patient under its many sleepers, held the answer somewhere under its leaves.

He walked on north, on the walked road, at the ordinary pace, one of the forest's many ordinary troubles, and grew.

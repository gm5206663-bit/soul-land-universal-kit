# Chapter 23: The Parting

The road went north, and the pull leaned, and for a while the difference was small enough to ignore. That was the trouble with it. A lean of a few points, held over a season of walking, became two countries.

He had known it on the rock spine and carried it north without letting it mean anything. The road knew where it was going. It kept to the broad valleys, the low passes, the water and the food, the ground feet had chosen for good reasons — and every day the pull cared less about those reasons. It rose in him toward the higher, stonier east, patient as always, asking nothing, and the road and it came apart the way two rivers part at a stone, slowly, and then all at once.

He stayed on the road longer than the pull asked of him. He told himself the road was also going north, which was true, and that the lean was small, which was daily less true. But the truth under both was simpler and less comfortable. The road was warm. He knew its kinds and its hours, its dangers and its manners; he was a local trouble on it, allowed for, fed by its edges; and a belonging is a hard thing to walk out of even when you can name it. He had spent a whole border childhood being nothing anywhere. The road had made him somebody — some small, ordinary body — and the body remembered what that was worth.

So he tarried, and the forest paid him no attention, and the lean grew.

His last full day on the road, he did not yet know it was his last, and the ordinary gave him its best, as if to be weighed honestly against what was leaving it. The evening was long and gold. The deer-kind crossed the road ahead of him in a loose string, does and that season's young, fat from the summer, and not one of them broke stride at his shape lying off the verge. He was furniture, a standing fact of the road. The young ones had grown up around his smell as young things grow up around the smell of a stream. He took his meal that dusk at the ordinary hour and in the ordinary manner: a slow unlucky old thing, feeding apart from its kind at the road's edge, taken in three strides and a turn, quickly, before its trouble could start. He ate what he needed and left the rest for the small life, and lay up full and easy in the warm dark between two giant roots.

And the pull ran through all of it, thin and straight and patient, under the gold and the fat and the belonging like a wire under a field, and somewhere in the second black of that night his feet decided, and in the gray morning he let them be right.

The parting came at a ford.

The road dropped into a stream valley and crossed the water at a wide shallow run, and on the far bank it went on north, climbing gently toward the next low pass. The stream itself came down out of the east, out of stone country, cold and quick and dark over its bed. He stood in the ford with the water pushing at his flanks and felt the two of them plainly for the first time: the road ahead, asking nothing, going where feet had always gone — and the pull running up the stream, against the current, east and north, toward ground where nothing walked.

A root-boar of the road kinds was drinking downstream of him. It lifted its head and looked at him across the water, the flat measuring look of a beast that knew him, and went back to drinking. That was the whole of the ordinary's farewell, and it was enough. He stood in the ford until his legs went cold, and then he turned upstream, and the road went on being the road without him.

He did not look back. Looking is a kind of going, and this time there was nothing to learn from behind him.

---

The plan was already made, because it was the oldest thing he owned.

The road's lesson had been belonging: walk where everyone walks, be ordinary together. But the road was gone now, and the unmarked dark between waters was where questions lived. So he would go the other way. The water said nothing to roots, and the roots said nothing to the forest — that was the second law of the deep forest, and it was the same as the first law of his whole life, worn down and remade. Where he could not be ordinary, he would be absent.

He went up the stream in the stream. The water was quick and cold and mostly stony; where it ran soft he kept to the rock ribs along the banks, and where the banks were root-ground he stayed in the current, walking, letting the noise hold him. He ate in the water — the old fat kinds again, and once a drinking beast that came down at dusk to the very reach he lay in, head low, reading nothing. He slept on the gravel spits and the mid-stream stone, where no roots could carry him, with the cold water talking all night on both sides. Once, on the second night, a long low swimmer-kind came up the dark pool below his spit and fished it for an hour, quick and busy and utterly unbothered. It worked the water ten lengths from a sleeping thing that could have ended it in two. That was the water's ordinary, and he lay in it with his flank to the current, and slept as a nobody again for the first time since the ford. It was his border life remade, at the world's bigger size: no print, no pattern, no road twice. The only thing missing was the being nothing, and the water gave him that.

The forest noticed none of it. For days at a stretch the quiet never came near him, and he began to know the parting had been real. The ground here was changing under the water. The beds ran stonier. The banks rose into rock the great trees could not hold to, and the dark over his head broke open more and more often into sky. The pull ran straight up the valley ahead of him like a rope pulled tight.

Then the second stream betrayed him.

It came in from the north, and he took it, and for a day it was good water, quick and rocky and loud. On the second day the walls closed in. The stream narrowed to a gorge and the gorge dropped, and the water gathered itself and went into the dark under a shelf of stone and did not come out — a sound like the swallowing of a den, and then only the drip of the wet walls, and no stream at all.

He stood at the lip of the sink with the noise of the gone water behind him and read the country ahead, and it was bad. To the north and east, before the next water could be reached — he could smell it, a colder, thinner water running somewhere beyond a stretch of the old dark — lay a reach of root-ground: giant trees on deep soil, moss to the knees of it, dim and still and thick with roots. Ground that would say every step of his to whatever listened. A day of it, perhaps more, at the gray light or not at all.

He went at the gray light, and the ground took his message, and the forest took it down.

He felt the quiet coming before it arrived — the floor's voice thinning somewhere ahead and to the east, the pattern of the small life going wrong in that too-wide way his body had learned to read at the fern hall. It might be for him. It might be for some other stranger walking the same dark. He never learned which, and it did not matter. The moss ahead of him stopped talking all at once, and he ran out of time for choices.

He went to ground in the oldest way he had: a hollow between two root-buttresses, deep in moss, with his body pressed into the cold damp of it and his breathing dropped to the smallest fire that would keep the legs alive. No print, no pattern, no breath wasted. The border's tricks — learned in the watchers' years on a mountain far south of here — held him while the forest came to look.

The pressure passed over him twice.

It came through the ground, as the pass's had, but it was not the pass's. The pass had been one great sleeping weight, reading in passing, gone about its own business. This was a walker. It moved — he could feel the steps of it in his spine, unhurried, heavy, spaced like something that owned its country down to the roots — and where it passed, the whole floor of the forest held its breath. It came near enough that the moss between the buttresses trembled, and a smell came with it, like deep stone after rain, cold and mineral, the smell of a thing that lived where the sun did not reach. It stood. It stood for a long time, somewhere off to his left, doing what he himself had done over a hundred kills: listening to the quiet, asking it where the strange thing was.

The red thing rose in him while it stood.

It rose the way it had risen in the river of the pass — lit by being held still under an attention — and there was no river here to carry it out. The boil climbed his neck and sat in his jaw, and every old road in him said take and turn and answer the touch, and he gave it nothing. He could not walk it out and he could not let the water spend it, so he spent it himself: breath by breath, into the moss, holding himself still by his own held will, until the boil had burned down to nothing and all that was left of him was the quiet he was making. What the river had once done for him, he did alone in the moss, and did not move, and the walker's attention hung over the dark like weather.

Then the steps went on. North and east, deeper into the root-country, the heavy spaced steps of it fading down into the ground until the moss began to talk again, first in one place, then everywhere, and the birds came back wrong-side first, and the forest was only forest.

He lay until full dark, and then longer. Then he rose out of the moss, and crossed the last of the bad ground under the stars, slow and light, and came down before dawn to the colder thinner water he had smelled, and got into it, and let it hold him while his heart remembered how to be ordinary.

---

After that the country rose to meet the pull, and the parting was finished in the land itself.

In the high country of the climb the stream went up in a chain of falls and pools, white thread after white thread down the gray steps of the stone. He went up with it through the splash-zones, wet to the bone and easy in it, taking the black pools' holding-kinds one after another — small, quick, cold-muscled things that fought like the water itself. The falls were ladders and the pools were pantries, and the loudness of the water was his cover the whole way up.

The waters ran straighter and colder and louder. Stone showed in the beds, then stone took the beds, then whole banks of it, gray and lichened, warm in the afternoon sun. The great trees fell back from the streams — first to stands, then to singles, bent versions of themselves growing out of cracks — and the sky came in over the water in long bright wedges. The nights got cold with a new cold, dry and thin, stone-seasoned. He was climbing, and his body knew it before his eyes confirmed it: the air's weight was changing, as it had changed once before, on the walk down from the mountain of his hatching, in the other direction, a lifetime of growing ago.

And then, one gray morning, the haze that his eyes had called mountains for two years stopped being haze.

They stood on the world's edge, and they were not trees. Stone — ranges of it, running north and east into more stone, blue-gray, snow on the high shoulders even in the warm season, and the wind that came down from them was the wind that had stopped every old thing on the walked roads: stone and snow and enormous distance. The pull did not lean anymore. It pointed, straight at the highest whiteness, like a smell held on a straight wind.

He came up out of the stream country onto a bare shoulder of rock to see it whole, and the deep forest proper lay behind him like a dark sea. Somewhere in it, roads he would never walk again were being walked by everyone, and everything ordinary was going on without him, and the thought of it was warm and far and nothing at all like regret. He was unknown again — a stranger in a country of strangers, one young heavy thing with a jaw and a pull — and that was the old way of being his, and it still fit.

On the shoulder of stone he took his first meal of the mountain country: a sure-footed kind of the high rocks, gray as its ground, feeding on the sparse green shelves of the stone. He ran it on open stone in the plain light — no quiet to read, no roots to tell, no tricks wanted, just the long rise and the take — and ate it looking at the wall of the world, and the years in it were thin and cold and clean, like the water they drank.

The road was behind him. The pull was ahead, and it had never once asked him anything. Between the two of them, for the first time since the shell, nothing at all stood in his way but the world.

He drank from the thin water, and went up.

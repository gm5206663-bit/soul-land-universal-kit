# Chapter 6: The Road East

> **Canon reference:** Soul Land 3, novel ch 32–33 ("Heavy Silver Storage Rings" tail — the last visit, the advice that soul power is the foundation and that a man must not fuse with just any spirit soul, the soul-guide thread and Mang Tian's "you'll understand why in the future"; the parents' private resolve, held vague exactly as canon holds it) and ch 33 ("First Arrival in Eastsea City" — the soul train, the rucksack, the father's written list whose first rule is about strangers, the station, the cold young master and the black soul car, the enforcement tower, the academy bus)
> **Timeline:** the week of departure — the last forge visit, the station, the coastal line · Tang Wulin, 9, rank 12

---

The forge was already cool when Wulin came, which told him what kind of evening this was.

Mang Tian had lit the small lamp and nothing else. The anvils stood under their cloths like sleeping animals, and the master sat on his bench with the ledger closed at his elbow, which the boy had seen perhaps three times in his life. A closed ledger in an open shop meant the working was finished and the talking was about to be.

"You leave in five days," Mang Tian said.

"The station coach goes at dawn on the sixth. Father bought the seat already."

"Then this is the last talking, and I have been thinking about what to put in it, and I have decided to put in less than I could, because the rest you'll learn with your hands the way you learned everything else." He tapped the bench beside him, and the boy sat. "Three things, and then we are two smiths again and not a teacher and a student, and you can argue with me about wrist angles by letter.

"One. Soul power is the foundation of everything. Not strength — strength is yours and it is real and it will carry you far, but it has limits, boy, everyone's does, and on the Douluo Continent no trade and no road holds at the upper reaches without soul power under it. Forge with your arms. Live on your cultivation. Never the other way around.

"Two. When the day comes that the Pagoda offers you a second spirit soul — and it will come, they will offer and you will be tempted, because every spirit soul in the trade is a door someone else has already priced — do not fuse with just anything. A spirit soul and a martial soul have to fit the way a hammer fits a hand. Ask what it is before you let it in. You are going to hear a lot of people tell you what you are worth in a hurry. The metal never hurries. Be the metal."

He paused, and drank from the mug, and looked at the boy over the rim the way he did when he was deciding whether the next sentence was a lesson or a fact.

"Three. The workshops all send their work to Eastsea, which means I will come across water two or three times a year, and there is a bench in my coast shop with a vise that has been waiting eight years for an apprentice worth the extra coal. It is yours. The address is on this." He slid a scrap of paper across the bench, folded twice, addressed in the shop's service-ink. "Report to the guild there within your first month — a note came back from the coast about you already, and it was short and dry, which in a guild note counts as excitement. Take your bench hours seriously. School is your base and forging is your craft, and the day one of them slips, write and tell me, because I would rather hear it from you than from a customer."

"Yes, Master."

Mang Tian looked at him another moment, and then asked, in a different voice, the kind that comes when a man suddenly decides to spend one curiosity: "Boy. What do you make of me?"

Wulin frowned. "You're the best smith in this city."

"That is a fact about the city. I asked what you make of me." The master turned the mug slowly on the bench. "You have seen the rings. Four of them, and a martial soul the guild measures higher than my hands deserve, and here I sit mending kettles at the far edge of the map."

"You said it yourself," Wulin said. "The rings buy nothing at the anvil."

"They buy nothing at the anvil," Mang Tian agreed. "There is a reason, and it is not modesty and it is not a story for tonight. You'll understand why in the future, and when you do, you will be far enough along that the reason will matter less than it does to me now." He stood, picked up the lamp, and walked the boy to the door. "There's one more thing, and it is the only one of the three I will check on. The hammers stay quiet. Not hidden like a crime — quiet like a good tool that has nothing to prove to a street. Nobody in a strange city needs to know what your coat is carrying until you are the one deciding who finds out."

"The rings are quiet," Wulin said. He turned his wrists, showing the dull grey bands, and the two of them stood a moment in the doorway with the lamp between them.

"Go on," Mang Tian said. "The fire's out. I'll see you on the coast."

The boy walked home down the long lane, and he did not look back, because looking back was for luggage and he was not luggage yet. At the corner, the master stood in the doorway until the small shape had gone from sight among the evening's coal-smoke. Then he shut the door, went back to his closed ledger, and sat with it a while before he opened it.

---

The house spent the last five days the way houses do. The mother began packing on the first day, finished on the first evening, and then repacked everything twice more for reasons that had nothing to do with packing.

Laid out on the bed by the end: two spare shirts, the good coat, wool socks she had made in one fierce week the previous month, and a sewing kit with more needles than a boy could lose in a year. There was also a bag of food. It was checked, sealed, and then entirely replaced on the morning it mattered, because the first bag had been packed by a woman whose hands needed something to do. Into the rucksack went the food, the shirts, the kit, and a small leather money-purse with its inner gusset sewn shut by his mother's own hand. Into the side pocket, with ceremony, went the letter of transfer and the recommendation's sealed copy.

"Money rules," she said, at the table, where money rules got said. "The wage comes to you from the coast shop; half of everything comes home, in the envelope, the first of the month. Write the amount in the letter, not because I don't trust you but because a number in a letter is a thing a family can hold. Food first, then rent, then tools — no. Food first, then tools if the tools are needed for work, then rent, and no boy ever died of a small room. Never lend. Never borrow. If a man offers you a bargain in the street, the bargain is him."

"Yes, Mother."

"Say it back wrong so I know you were listening."

"Food, tools, rent. The bargain is him."

She looked at him for a while, and then got up and did the washing up, which was how the evening ended whenever it had gone as far as evenings went in that house.

Na'er came up while he was folding the fourth shirt, the way she came into things; she did not knock and she did not show up at the door. She sat on the truckle bed's edge and was quiet a while. Then the two of them worked out the packing between them without a word of discussion: she handed him the shirt, he folded it, she handed him the next thing. Somewhere in the third exchange it occurred to Wulin that this was the first household task the two of them had ever done as equals, rather than as a boy and a found girl. Somewhere under the window, in the place where the walls of the house stopped being a room and became a thing outside, the grass turned its slow attention over and filed that too.

"Does the city have a sea?" Na'er asked.

"It has the sea," Wulin said. "The whole reason the work goes there. The port is the biggest thing on the coast."

"Then you will bring back a thing from the sea," she said, in the tone of a girl who had just remembered she had been raised by a trader's habits. "A white stone. Not bought. Picked up from the actual shore, and not from a shop, and not from somebody's stall."

"I'll be working," Wulin said.

"You will have some afternoon," said Na'er, with total confidence in the shape of the future.

He looked at her then: the small, pale, careful girl who had walked into this house out of a rain a few weeks ago, and had lived in it since like a coin that had been part of the family's pocket all along. The question that had sat unanswered at the dinner table the night of the graduation sat between them now, small and patient. What happened to the house's money arithmetic when its road went east and its room list stayed at four was not a question a nine-year-old could answer. It was not, in the end, a question a nine-year-old was being asked.

"When the first letter comes," Wulin said, "the stone comes with it or the letter explains why not."

"Then write early," said Na'er, and took the rucksack and did the strap up herself, in a knot that would not open, which settled several things.

Downstairs, Tang Ziran had spent the evening at the table with a sheet of good paper, a brush, and the small cramped handwriting of a man who wrote plans for a living. When Wulin came down in the morning, the paper was folded into the side of the rucksack where it would be found on the train. The first line was written in the exact ink of the workshop's job orders: *Trust no stranger quickly.* Under it, five more lines in the same cramped hand — the station, the enforcement tower, the academy buses, what to do if a person would not leave him alone, and the guild branch's address. And under all of it, in a line of its own, in a gentler hand than the rest, this: *If you are ever truly lost, come home. The ticket works both ways.*

They walked him to the station themselves, and stood on the platform, and did not let the morning be sad, which took all three of them working on it.

The Glorybound stop was small: a single roof, a board with the day's times chalked on it, and a platform just long enough for the coastal line's two short cars. Lang Yue, having run out of packing, ran through the instructions one final time. She got through half of them before her voice decided it had done enough and her hands took over, straightening the collar of the coat that did not need straightening. Tang Ziran put a hand flat on his son's head the way smiths and carpenters do, a weighing with nothing on the scale, and said, "You are nine, and you are going to a city of three million people. I could go with you. Your mother and I talked about it. But you have been looking after yourself among grown men since you were six years old, and it would be a lie to pretend you don't know how, and a boy who is walked to every door never learns which ones he can open." He took his hand back. "So: go and see it for yourself. That's the whole of my advice. The rest is on the paper."

The train came in with a long breath and a hum in the rails. Wulin climbed up with his rucksack and stood at the window. The last thing he did before the cars pulled away was raise one hand — a wave he had practiced not turning into a speech. The two on the platform raised theirs. Then the morning took the platform away.

---

A new place, for the first time in a hundred years.

That was the grass's whole ledger for the first hour of the journey, and it was not a small entry. Its hundred years had been one hillside, one water cycle, one sky with the seasons hung in it like cloths. Then a crate, a shelf, a boy, a room, a lane — all of it the same square of the world wearing different weather. Now the world itself was moving past the window of a soul train, and the grass watched it move with the deep, still attention it gave to any genuinely new fact.

It could not see, exactly. Through the bond it had the boy's senses, thin as soup, and its own root-feeling for ground. And the ground of a train was a floor, moving. That was philosophy of a kind the grass had never had to do before. So it did what it did with everything it did not understand. It took the measure of the change, set it beside the entries it already carried, and grew a root-hair deeper into the boy's soul-space, which for a grass is the whole of what holding on means.

Fields went by. A river, wide and slow, doing the work rivers do of being older than the cities. Roofs in a different color than Glorybound's roofs, which was either a fact about paint or a fact about the world, and the grass filed it as both. And then, for a long stretch, smells began coming through the cracks of the car: salt, cold fish, a bigness of air the grass recognized without ever having met it. Ground knows water the way a sleeping man knows a door opening.

The boy spent the journey as his father's paper had trained him to, which is to say he stayed in his seat and watched everything twice and told no stranger anything. Once, a woman with a baby and an armful of parcels came down the aisle. Wulin stood up and gave her the window seat. She thanked him, and then, being tired and kind, asked where a boy was traveling to alone. Wulin said he was meeting family at the coast, and she nodded, and that was the whole conversation, and the paper in his pocket stayed folded. But when she left at the town before Eastsea, she pressed a paper twist of dried fruit into his hand anyway. He ate it slowly, because it would have been rude not to. From its still, patient place, the grass noted that the world's rules had exceptions, and that they traveled by hand.

---

Eastsea arrived as size.

The station alone was bigger than the largest building in Glorybound City: a long vaulted roof of cast metal tubes, bolted and pressed. Wulin read it the way he had been taught to read work. The joins were true. The finish was plain. Whoever built it had expected it to still be there in a hundred years. He stepped off the train into the crowd holding his rucksack straps in both hands.

The crowd carried him a little way and then split, all at once, the way water splits around a stone. In the gap stood a black soul car, sleek as a fish and clearly richer than every other vehicle on the wide street outside. Two men in black stood by its doors, watching the passengers. Wulin slowed to look, the way anyone would. One of the men came toward him — no; past him. "Young Master," the man said, and a boy about Wulin's own age stepped out of the stream of passengers behind him: blue from collar to boots, brown hair, unhurried. He had a cold, clean look to him, the look of a person used to the crowd getting out of the way.

Someone in the crowd jostled Wulin as it closed back in, and his shoulder bumped the blue boy's shoulder.

"Sorry," Wulin said, at once, because he had bumped a stranger, and that was the end of his part of it. The blue boy turned his head a quarter, looked at him down the length of a straight nose — a flat, unhurried look, the kind that files a person under *nothing* — and kept walking. The man in black was not so clean about it. He put a hand on Wulin's chest and shoved him back into the crowd.

"Watch it, bumpkin," the man said, in the tone of a man reading a sign to a dog, and the two of them saw their young master into the black car, and the car left with a low hum, and the crowd went back to being a crowd.

Wulin stood in the middle of it with his shirt front flattened and his ears going hot. *Big city*, he thought, in the voice his mother would have recognized from the day he was told to sit on his hands. *Big city, big manners.* He had met rich boys before. Glorybound's richer families sent their sons to the same school as everybody else, and Wan Yunchao had once eaten four pork buns at a time in his family's own carriage. This one had not even bothered to be rude — that was the part that sat in the boy's chest like a swallowed stone. He had been looked at, and filed, and forgotten, all in the time of one bounce.

He took one breath to give the temper somewhere to go. There was nothing to hit and nobody worth hitting it at. So he reached into his rucksack, pulled out his father's paper, and looked down its first line the way his mother had made him look at the envelope arithmetic: *Trust no stranger quickly.*

The next stranger found him in under a minute. A slim middle-aged man with a face like a borrowed umbrella came past with a smile already open, glancing down at the rucksack, up at the boy, and back again. "Little friend, first time in Eastsea? Where's your family? A boy alone — the city's a big place, I know the safe places, I can—"

Wulin folded the paper along its first line and put it in his pocket. "No, thank you," he said, and walked away at the speed of a person going somewhere specific, which was the second rule on the paper and a fact he had not needed his father to teach him.

He found the tower by walking toward the highest thing on the square and reading: the blocky structure said *Administrative Law Enforcement* in letters taller than he was. Two officers in uniform stood on its steps. Wulin went up and asked them where the bus to the Eastsea Academy left from. His politeness came from a house that had taught him to bow to the Guardian and to the grain stand alike.

The nearer officer pointed along the wide street with his thumb, and then looked down and saw what he was pointing a nine-year-old at, and his voice went soft around the edges. "Right down there, the blue sign by the pole. Where's your family, son?"

"Uncle," Wulin said, straightening, "I'm old enough to ask for myself. Thank you."

The officer laughed — a short, surprised laugh the wind carried off behind them. Wulin walked down to the blue sign and stood among the other students gathering with their trunks and their parents. The city of three million pressed on around him: soul cars, shops, buildings older than the language he thought in. At the end of every street stood the smell of the sea, like an open door.

---

So this was the city the road had been for.

The grass took it in the only way it could: not as a view, but as a change in the ground it was standing on. In Glorybound the ground had been small and known — a lane, a shop floor, a yard, all of it held together by one boy walking the same square every day. Here the ground stretched past counting in every direction, and the boy's own senses, which the grass wore the way animals wear their noses, kept opening onto more: more smells, more voices, more shape. It watched its boy stand under the blue sign with both hands on the rucksack straps, smaller than anything. It watched him not look afraid — which is different from not being afraid, and a braver thing. The grass filed that too.

It had three entries now where there had been one. The sleeping entry: a hillside, a hundred years, one sky. The crossing entry: a room with a box of dead balls and a boy's stubborn hands. And under those, still wet, tonight's entry. It was longer than the other two only because the grass did not have words and had to write it in weather. A long cold breath of a moving floor. A river older than cities. Salt arriving before the sea. A city that was one door after another, all of them open, none of them yet entered.

Ahead, past the blue sign and the street and the second street, stood what the bus driver had pointed along: roofs among roofs, towers among towers, and somewhere in there the place where the road stopped being a road. The boy's grip on the rucksack tightened once, the small movement that in this house was what a promise looked like. The grass set its roots a little deeper. It was ready to be walked into the next room of its life, the way it had been walked into every room so far.

Ahead of them, the sea. Behind them, a paper that said the way home worked both directions. It would do.

The grass set a root-hair toward the morning, and said nothing, in the only way it had ever said anything: it grew.

The boy got on the bus.

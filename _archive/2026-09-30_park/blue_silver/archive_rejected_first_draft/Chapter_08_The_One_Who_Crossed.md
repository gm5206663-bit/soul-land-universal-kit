# Chapter Eight — The One Who Crossed

*Still the deep era. Still the glade at the edge of the deep band. The keeper holds the
line the old one kept, and the old one — done keeping — is going quiet at his edge, tap by
tap, season by season, until nothing is left of the two-way speech they built but his own
pulses sent out into the silence. And into that quiet the wet years come, bringing a
sickness that eats the green, and the deep band watches to see whether the keeper falls.
And one small thing of the deep, the weakest of its kind, crosses the boundary to be kept.*

The old one's taps grew fewer in the years after the eater left, the way light leaves a
long day — by such small degrees that he could not say when the change began, only that it
was there.

It had never tapped much. That had always been the wonder of it, the great careful restraint
of a thing that could shake the ground with one blow and chose instead to make the ground
speak softly. But in the years of its rest it tapped less than it had, and then less than
that, until a season would pass with no tap at all and he would feel the silence where the
lesson should have been and know, in the way he knew the turning of the year, that the old
one was running down the way all things ran down, toward the deep quiet that waited under
everything.

He did not grieve. Not yet. He kept watch, the way he had kept watch through the hard
winter, and he sent his pulses through the ground against its side — *I am here, I am here*
— and the old one lay against his roots and breathed, and sometimes its great head would
shift a little at the feel of him, and he would know it had heard. That was what was left of
the language they had built: not taps now, but the old one's breath against his roots, and
his pulses rising to meet it.

He held the boundary through those years as the old one had taught him, and it was strange
how much of the teaching had become simply himself. He knew the deep band's hours now the
way he had once known the glade's footfalls. He felt the patient weights that came to the
edge of his sensing and did not cross, and he knew which were testing and which were only
passing, and he let the testers find the line and the passers find the way around. The deep
fringe had grown quiet around his ground. The place by the water, the word had gone among
the half-light hunters, was kept — below as well as above, in sickness and in health — and
whatever the deep band sent to learn whether that was still true learned it was.

And the glade itself went on being a glade. The rodent families turned over their
generations at his edge, and the ground birds raised their broods in his lee, and the small
bright lives that sheltered in his green grew old and died and were replaced by their own,
and he kept them all the only way he had ever known — by being there, season after season,
the green that did not fall. He had learned, across the long years, not to count them. Not
to count the generations, or the deaths, or the small bright patterns that came and went
against his roots. He kept the pattern of each one the way he kept everything, and he let
them go the way he had let the fledgling go, into the ground that kept all things.

The old one had not tapped for a full season when the wet years began.

It came as weather, at first — the kind of weather that was simply a long, soft, patient
rain, a season of it, then another. The stream ran high and spoke constantly. The ground
stayed dark and damp long after the rains let up, and the meadow that had always yellowed in
the dry came up lush and stayed lush, and everything green grew soft and rank and glad. The
small lives bred more, because that was what soft wet years did to small lives. And then,
in the third soft summer, the sickness came.

He felt it first in the far meadow, beyond his ground — in the grass that was not his. A
patch of it yellowed and slumped in a way that was not the yellowing of the dry season, not
the slump of thirst. It was a *wrongness*, a grey-green rot that spread from the soil up,
and it moved. It moved the way water moved, along the damp, from the meadow to the slope,
sickening the grass where it passed, and the herbs the small lives ate, and the blades of
his own kind that grew at the far edge of the stream bend.

He felt it come for him the way he had felt the serpent come, and the eater — as a slow
certain pressure, patient as they had been patient. But this was not a thing with teeth or
an armored body or a hunger he could meet. It was a sickness, and a sickness had no intent
and no fear and no place in the ground where he could cage it, and for the first time in his
life he faced a harm he could not reach.

It touched his outermost blades in the autumn, and he felt it as a stain — a grey bloom on
the silver-green, a wilting that did not come from thirst, a rot that began at the leaf-tip
and worked down toward the crown. He tried what he knew. He hardened the blades, and the rot
ate through the hard. He drew the water back from them, and the rot fed on the dry. He
turned his roots against it, and found nothing to grip — there was no digger in the soil, no
enemy beneath, only the slow grey wrongness that had come on the wet and lived in the damp.

It was the small lives that taught him, in the end. Not on purpose. They never did anything
on purpose. But he watched the meadow's grass die, and he watched the ground birds and the
rodents of the far slope grow thin and slow and fewer, and he watched the plants that
survived — the ones that did not try to hold the rot, that let the touched parts die and
fall away and grew new from below the stain — and he understood, in the way he understood
everything, that the rot could not be fought. It could only be outgrown.

So he shed.

It was the hardest thing he had ever done, harder than lying down for the frost, because the
frost took the whole of him at once and he lay down and waited and it passed. This was
piecemeal, blade by blade, season by season — he let the grey blades die, drew the life out
of them and back into the crown, and let them fall, and grew the new green from below the
stain, smaller and harder and carrying the memory of the rot the way his blades had carried
the memory of the fire. The crown held. The taproot held. And the rot, finding nothing soft
to eat at the heart of him, worked its slow way along the outer edges of his ground and did
not reach the deep silver-green where the small lives sheltered.

It cost him. Everything cost him, and he paid without counting, the way he had always paid.
The kept depth of him, the well that had opened in the serpent night and deepened through
the years of keeping, ran low as he drew on it to push the clean green up through the rot —
a season of it, then another, and the hollow after each drawing was real, a thinness in his
blades that the small lives could feel as a lessening of the heavy safe warmth of his
ground. Some of the small lives he could not save. A rodent family whose runs crossed the
sickened edge of the meadow sickened and dwindled, and he felt them go one by one, and the
holes they left in the pattern of his ground did not fill. The newest brood of the ground
birds hatched into a sickened summer and did not fledge. He kept what they had been, and he
let them go into the ground that kept all things, and he went on shedding and growing and
holding, because that was what a keeper was for.

And the deep band watched.

He felt it watching, in the years of the sickness — the patient weights gathering a little
closer at the edge of his sensing than they had come in a long time, testing the air the way
the deep tested everything, reading the thinness in his green the way he read the ground. He
was weaker than he had been since the serpent night. The kept depth that had made his ground
sweet was running low, and the deep could smell the low water, and he knew — the way he knew
the frost — that they were waiting to see if the keeper fell.

He did not fall.

It cost him almost everything he had kept, in the end — the deep-fringe hunter that came at
last, in the second year of the sickness, came at night and came low and came for the
sanctuary's edge, and he met it with the last of his strength and the whole of his ground. It
was not the serpent. It was not even the shadow cat. It was a lean patient hunter of the deep
fringe, grown old in the grey light of the boundary's far side, and against the green that
had held the line through the serpent's night it should have been nothing — but the rot had
taken his edges and the well had run low, and the reach he had spent a lifetime weaving was
thin as the last of the water in a dry well, and it was all he could do to hold it at the
boundary, blades seizing, roots tripping, presence pressed thin. It fought him
for a long time in the dark, and he held, and held, and held — the way he had held against
the shadow cat, the way he had held through the serpent night, the way the old one had
taught him a keeper holds: not by being the strongest thing in the forest, but by being the
thing that does not stop being there.

And the deep-fringe hunter, bleeding from a dozen cuts, found that even the low-water keeper
was more than it could finish, and it broke off and went back into the dark, and the deep
band — watching from the edge of his sensing — understood that the keeper was thin but not
fallen, and drew back, and waited, the way it always waited.

He kept watch through what was left of the night, too spent to grow, too spent to do
anything but hold the line with what he had. And at dawn, when the light came over the
silver-green and the small lives came out of the deep green where he had sheltered them
through the dark, he felt the old one's breathing at his edge — slow, shallow, but there —
and he turned his whole tired self toward the sun, and began, again, the slow work of being
more than the world had tried to make him.

---

The found one came in the worst of the sickness, and it came out of the deep.

He felt it first as a wrongness at the boundary — a small weight, smaller than any weight
the deep had ever sent, crossing the line not low and hunting but *stumbling*, uneven,
wrong. He felt it the way he felt everything now, as a pattern in the ground, and the
pattern was not the patient circling of a hunter or the slow dig of a root-eater. It was a
fear-pattern, the particular hurried unsteady rhythm of a thing that was running from
something, and running wrong.

It crossed his outermost roots and fell.

He felt it go down at the edge of the sickened ground — a small body, light, gray as the
deep's twilight, its breath coming in the shallow panicked gasps of a thing that has run too
far and hurt itself running. It did not get up. It lay where it had fallen, at the very edge
of his ground, in the grey rot-tinged grass beyond the silver-green, and it trembled, and
it was small, and it was of the deep band — he could feel the deep in it, the particular
oldness of the soil it had grown up in, the dark-country smell of it that his roots read the
way his blades read light.

He did not understand what it wanted. Nothing of the deep had ever come to his ground
wanting anything but his root or his small lives. He held still, and he watched it with the
whole of his attention, the way he had watched the shadow cat and the serpent — waiting to
understand whether this was hunger or testing or intent.

And the small gray thing, trembling in the rot-grass, dragged itself a little closer to the
silver-green and stopped, and made a sound — a small, thin, hopeless sound, the sound a
young thing makes when it has run as far as it can run and there is nowhere left — and he
felt, through the ground, that it was not hunting. It was not testing. It was not even
lost.

It was *asking*.

He had no word for what it was asking. He did not need one. He had felt that asking before,
in the fledgling that tumbled into his blades on the day the hawk came, in the rodents that
pressed themselves into his deep green and believed it safe — the wordless asking of the
small and the lost, who had learned, by the long rumor of the forest, that the green by the
water was the place the world did not end. He had never thought to ask where that rumor
reached. He had never imagined it reached into the deep.

He let it come.

He did not know if he could have stopped it — it was so small, so spent, so clearly dying —
but he did not try. He drew his blades back from the sickened edge, the way he had drawn
them for the mantis, and the small gray thing dragged itself across the last stretch of the
rot-grass and into the deep silver-green, and it lay there against his root, and its
trembling did not stop, and its shallow breath did not steady, and he felt it the way he had
felt the fledgling's heart hammering against his crown on the day he first saved something:
a small warm weight, asking to be kept.

He kept it.

He could not heal it — he was a grass; he had no hands, no warmth to give but the warmth he
held from the sun, no medicine but his own presence — so he gave it what he had. He folded
his blades over it, the deepest green, the place where the rot had never reached, where the
ground was warm and the water ran clean, and he held it through the day and through the
night and through the days after, the way he had held the fledgling, the way he had held the
mantis through the fire. It was of the deep. He did not know what it ate, or what it feared,
or what had driven it across the boundary with its life in its mouth. He only knew it was
small, and it was lost, and it had crossed the one line in the forest that was kept, and it
lay against his root and breathed, and would not be turned away.

It healed slowly, the way small things heal — by sleeping, and eating what it could find in
the safe green, and sleeping again. He felt it grow stronger by small degrees, the way he
felt the spring come back into the ground: by the deepening of its breath, by the day it
first stood on its small legs and did not fall, by the days it began to move a little in his
green, learning the bounds of the sanctuary the way every small life that came to him
learned them. The rodents of his ground kept their distance from it at first, the way prey
kept distance from anything that smelled of the deep. But it did not hunt them. It was not a
hunter. It was the smallest, weakest kind of the deep's people — a grazer of the fringe, a
thing that ate the soft green and the fallen leaves and asked nothing of anyone — and in a
season the small lives of the sanctuary had learned, the way the living learn, that it was
no more danger than they were.

He did not know its name. He did not know if it had one. He knew its weight now, the way he
had known the fledgling's weight — a small warm presence at his edge, near the place where
the old one lay, closer than any creature of the deep had ever come to him without taking
anything. It slept against his root in the cold months, and it woke with him in the spring,
and when the sickness finally broke — when the rains thinned and the ground dried and the
rot, finding no more damp to travel, sank back into the soil it had come from — it was still
there, small and gray and alive, having crossed the boundary to be kept, and having been
kept.

He did not know, yet, what it meant — that a thing of the deep band had crossed his line not
to take but to be taken in. He only knew that the deep was not one thing, the way the glade
was not one thing: it had its great hungers, its serpents and its eaters, and it had its
small and its lost as well, its weak and its wandering, its fledglings of the dark. He had
held the boundary against the first. He had not known, until the found one, that the
boundary was also a door — that the keeping was not a wall against the deep, but a keeping
for the small and the lost of every dark that came to it.

The found one stayed.

---

*It had been small all its life, and the deep was not kind to the small.*

*It did not think in words. It thought in cold and hunger and the endless dark, in the
shapes of the great ones that moved through the deep like weather and never saw it, in the
long grey country where it had been born one of many and survived by being quick and quiet
and afraid. It had never known warmth the way it learned warmth in the green by the water.
It had never known a place where the ground did not shake with the passing of something
greater than it. It had never known a place where it was not the smallest thing, the
lowest thing, the thing that would be eaten if it was seen.*

*It had been driven out of the deep by a sickness of its own — a grey rot that had come on
the wet and taken the soft green it ate, taken its kindred one by one, until it was alone
and starving and the great ones of the deep, who did not sicken, had begun to notice the
small things that were dying and to hunt them while they were weak. It had run. That was
all it had ever known to do, run and hide and be quick and be quiet. It had run until the
deep grew lighter and the ground changed and it found itself at a line it had never crossed
— the line the great ones spoke of, the boundary where the kept green began.*

*It had heard of the green. Even in the deep, in the whispered way the small things know
things, it had heard: there was a green at the edge of the deep where nothing was taken.
The great ones circled it and did not cross. The ones that had crossed had not come back,
and the small things said it was because the green was kept by something that fought — and
the small things said, in the same breath, that the green kept things, that the small and
the lost of the middle band went there and were not eaten, that it was the one place in all
the forest where being small was not a death sentence.*

*It had not believed it. Small things learned early not to believe in places where being
small was safe. But it had run until it could run no more, and the green had been there,
and it had crossed the line because there was nowhere else left in all the world to cross
to.*

*And the green had taken it in.*

*It did not understand why. It had never understood why anything did anything. It only knew
that the ground by the water was warm, and the blades were deep, and the thing that kept
the green — it could not have said what it was, only that it was vast and still and
everywhere under the ground, like the deep itself but green, green the way the deep was
dark — had not eaten it. Had not turned it away. Had folded the green over it and held it,
the way it had once, in a dream it barely remembered, been held.*

*It stayed. It did not know how to leave. It did not want to leave. For the first time in
its small grey life it was not the smallest thing, the lowest thing, the thing that would
be eaten if it was seen. It was the thing that had been kept. And the small and the lost
remember what it is to be kept, the way the cold remember what it is to be warm.*

---

The old one's last tap came in the spring after the found one healed, on a morning when the
glade was new and the rot was gone and the silver-green stood dense and bright over the
whole stretch of the stream bend.

He felt it as a change in the breathing at his edge — the old one's breath, which had been
slow and shallow for so long, drawing in deep, once, as if gathering itself. And then the
great foreleg — the good one, the one that had tapped the ground for longer than the glade
had been a glade — lifted, and came down.

*Thump. Thump.*

Two blows. Slow. The oldest blows, the first blows, the ones that had meant, on a night a
hundred years gone, *here, resting* — and had meant, ever since the serpent night, *you are
the keeper now*.

He felt the silence after them, and he understood, in the way grass understands, that they
were the last. Not a message he had to read. A farewell he had to know. The old one had
held its speech back through the years of its going quiet, spending nothing, keeping the
last of its strength against the deep root of its life — and it had spent it now, on the
morning the sickness broke and the found one stood in the green and the keeping was whole,
to say the only thing it had ever needed to say.

*You are the keeper now.*

He sent his pulse back through the ground against its side, the strongest he could make it,
the whole kept life of more than three hundred winters pouring into one message:

*I am here. I will always be here.*

The old one's great head lay against his roots, and its breath went on — slow, shallow,
even — and it did not tap again. It did not need to. The keeping was whole, and the keeper
knew it, and the last of the old one's speech had been spent on the one message that
mattered, and there was nothing left in all the world that the old one needed to say.

---

The sickness broke, and the glade came back, and he came back with it — changed, the way he
had come back changed from every sickness of his life, only more so. The blades that rose
from the rot-years were not the blades that had sickened. They were denser, and the silver
ran deeper, and there was something in their green that had not been there before — a
resistance, a memory of the rot that had sunk so deep into his substance that it had become
part of his very making, the way the fire's memory had become part of him. The rot could
not be answered with strength. He had learned that. But it could be *shed*, and outgrown,
and remembered, and grown into the next green — and the next green was always more than the
green before it had been.

The well had run low in the sickness years, and he had drawn it down to the mud to hold the
line and keep the small lives, and now it was filling again — slowly, the way wells fill,
from the deep water of the kept years — and he felt it deepen as it filled, the way a thing
feels a wound heal deeper than the skin. The kept count of his life had not stopped
climbing through the sickness. Nothing had ever stopped it. It had climbed through the
frost and the fire and the shadow cat and the serpent and the eater, and it had climbed
through the rot, and the well that held it all was lower and deeper both, like a river that
has run through a dry year and carved itself a deeper bed.

The found one stayed. It grew a little, in the green years after the sickness, and it
learned the sanctuary the way every kept thing learned it — by being safe in it — and it
slept against his root where the old one's breath was slow and warm, and it went out into
the edge of the glade in the evenings and came back, the way kept things came back. The
small lives of the sanctuary had long since accepted it, and the deep fringe, which had
watched it cross and had not understood, watched it stay and did not understand that either
— but the word of it went into the deep ahead of the found one's small grey footprints, the
way word travels in the deep: a new thing was true about the green by the water. It was
kept, below and above. And now it was known that the green kept even the deep's own small
and lost, if they crossed to it and asked.

He did not know what the found one would become in his life — how long a thing of the deep
lived, or whether it would stay or go back across the boundary it had crossed, or what the
deep would make of the fact that one of its own now slept at the keeper's root. He only knew
that it was there, small and gray and alive, and that the ground he held had a new weight
on it that was not a burden, and that the glade at the edge of the deep was, for the first
time in a very long time, not quite as quiet as it had been.

And he knew that the old one was going quiet in earnest now — that the tap that had come in
the spring was the last of its speech, and that what remained was only the slow breath at
his edge, the great body resting against his roots, done with everything but being there.
He did not know how long that would last. He did not know whether it would be a season or a
year or a long quiet age. He only knew that he would be there for it, the way it had been
there for him, holding the line and the green and the small and the lost and the found, and
that when the end came — if it came while he was still what he was — he would keep that too,
the way he kept everything, and go on.

The road to the deep country lay in the direction the old one had turned its head at dawn
all its life. He did not know its name yet, or its queen, or the shape of the great one he
was becoming. He did not know that the kept count of his seasons was climbing toward a mark
no grass of his line had ever touched, or that the well in him was the consolidation of a
life outrunning its years, or that somewhere in the deep forest, in the direction of the
core, an ancient Empress read the grass through her domain and had not yet found a reason
to look twice at any blade of it.

He only knew that the sickness had broken, and the green was whole, and the found one slept
against his root, and the old one's breath was slow and warm at his edge, and that he had
held the line through the worst of it and come out the other side more than he had been —
the way he had always come out of everything, the way the talent had always brought him
out, kept and spent and grown, season after season, never once stopping, never once
satisfied.

And in the spring, when the light came back over the silver-green and the small lives came
out of their burrows and the stream spoke again along its bed, he turned his whole kept
self toward the sun, the way he had turned it every spring of his life, and he grew — a
little deeper into the ground he held, a little further along the boundary he kept, a
little closer to the deep country he did not yet know was waiting for him.

The old one breathed at his edge. The found one slept against his root. The boundary held.
And the one who keeps, kept.

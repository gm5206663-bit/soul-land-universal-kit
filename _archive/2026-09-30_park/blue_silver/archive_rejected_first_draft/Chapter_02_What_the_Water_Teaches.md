# Chapter Two — What the Water Teaches

*Still the deep era. Still the glade. Years pass the way they pass for grass — not counted,
but kept: every drought and flood and frost written into the root, every lesson spent on
the next blade. He does not know he is being taught. The thing in him does.*

The seasons taught him everything he was, the way they had always taught grass — except
that grass forgot, and he did not. That was the whole difference, and it grew with him.

The first winter came down on the glade while he was still small, and it came as a
surprise. The light had been leaning away for a long time, day after day, the warmth
thinning like water running out of a pool, and he had felt the change and had not known what
it meant — only that the world was taking something back from him, a little every day, and
that he had no way to hold it. Then the cold came, and it was not like the cold of the
ground he had been born in, that patient heaviness of winter-dark. This was a cold that
reached up out of the earth and into him, that crept up the green of his blades and into
the water in his cells, and it hurt. It hurt the way the bite had hurt, a slow bright
freezing ache that would not stop, and around him, all through the glade, he felt the
carpet do something he had never seen it do: it died back. Blade by blade, the green of the
meadow yellowed and stiffened and fell, and the whole wide world of his kind lay down upon
the ground and went still, waiting for the thaw the way they had always waited, patient,
mindless, sure.

He did not want to die back. Every cell of him that was still warm wanted to stay green,
to stand up to the sun, to go on being. So he stayed. He stood stiff and green through the
first deep cold, and the frost took him standing, and the ache in him turned to a numbness,
and he learned — in the only way the thing in him ever let him learn, by paying for it — the
first hard truth of winter: a blade that would not lie down was a blade that froze and
died.

The root crown held. It always held. But when the thaw came, he rose from that first winter
smaller than the blades around him, paler, thinner — the price of his stubbornness written
in his leaf. And the thing in him, which had felt the frost take him standing and had felt
him nearly die of it, kept the lesson the way it kept everything. The next autumn, when the
light began to lean away and the cold began to creep, he did not fight it. He felt himself
let go — felt the green drain out of his blades by his own slow choice, felt himself lie
down with the rest of the carpet, sinking what was left of his summer into the root crown
where the dark could not reach it. And it was strange, that first willing dying, stranger
than anything the bite had taught him, because it was the first time he had ever chosen to
give something up in order to keep the deeper thing.

He survived that winter the way the carpet survived it — by becoming, for a season,
nothing but root and waiting. But he survived it differently too, and he knew it, in his
rootbound way. All around him the carpet had survived by being grass, by the old blind
rhythm of their kind. He had survived by *deciding*, by remembering the frost that had
nearly killed him and choosing, a season early, to do what would keep the root alive. The
thing in him had turned his first winter into a teacher, and he had passed the lesson, and
the lesson was now part of him — a thing he carried the way the root carries water, deeper
than any blade, to be drawn on the next time the light began to lean away.

That was the first lesson. The drought year was the second.

It came in a summer when the rains forgot the glade. The stream's tremor in the ground,
which had been a constant voice under him since his first day, began to fade — day by day
the wet threads in the soil grew thinner, slower, more reluctant, until the earth around
his roots was drinking nothing and giving nothing, and the sun stood high and pale and the
meadow that had been green began to yellow.

He felt the other blades failing first. It came up through the ground, through the
tangle of roots he shared the soil with: the carpet was going dormant in waves, blade after
blade ceasing to strive, folding in on itself to wait out the dry the way it waited out the
cold. It was the same patience, the same blind trust that the world would turn again. Most
of his kindred made that choice, and it saved them, and he felt them go still and knew they
would be waiting when the rains came back.

He could not make himself do it. The thing in him would not let him. Where the other blades
folded inward and waited, he felt a pull — a lean — the same lean that had turned his first
root toward the old rotted channel of rich soil in the spring of his birth. There was water
somewhere below. He could feel it the way he had felt the stream's tremor in his first
season: a memory, deep in the ground, of the wet that had flowed here when the world was
full. The other roots around him were too shallow to reach it, too young, too grass. But
he had been growing downward every season, storing the shape of the soil the way he stored
everything, and he knew — not knew, *felt*, the way the thing in him felt everything — that
if he grew his root a little deeper, a little harder, past the dry crust where the world had
stopped giving, there was a place where the old water still ran.

So he grew, through a dying summer, into the dark.

It cost him. Every inch of new root through the hard dry earth was taken from his blades —
he felt the green above him thin and pale as the root below him lengthened, felt himself
giving up the sun's gift to buy the dark's, felt the grazers' shadows pass over his
yellowing leaf and know him for the sickly thing he looked like. No blade around him did
what he was doing. They had chosen to wait; he had chosen to reach. And for a long time,
deep in that dry ground, there was nothing — only harder earth, and the memory of water,
and the thing in him refusing to turn back.

Then his root broke through into the old channel, and the water was there.

It came up into him like the thaw had come up into the seed, that first time — a flooding,
a filling, a warmth that ran from the tip of his deepest root all the way up into the pale
starved blades above, and he drank, and he drank, and he was saved. Around him the carpet
waited, dry and still and patient, and he stood green in the middle of the yellowing
meadow, drinking from a place no grass his age had any right to reach, holding the summer
while the world around him gave it up.

And he paid for that too, in his way. The grazers found him. He had grown, that dry
summer, the only green thing in the meadow's yellow — and the world's hunger finds the
green. They came for him the way they had come for the tall grass of his first spring, and
they cropped him down to the crown again, and the herd passed, and he regrew from the root
that had drunk the deep water, smaller, harder, changed.

But the thing in him kept the lesson. It always kept the lessons. The next time the rains
thinned, his root did not wait to be driven — it reached for the deep channel on its own,
following the memory of the water it had found, the way a creature follows the memory of
a path to food. And the blades he grew after the drought were not quite the blades he had
grown before it: a little narrower, a little tougher, holding water longer in the heat,
shaped by the dry year that had nearly killed him the way the frost had shaped him, the
way the bite had shaped him. Every harm the world did him became, in the turning of the
thing in him, a small instruction in how to survive the next harm — not immunity, never
that, he would learn that lesson too, in time — but a lean, a readiness, a blade that
remembered.

The flood year taught him the third lesson, and it was the strangest one, because the
flood did not hurt him the way the frost and the drought had hurt him. It simply took the
world away.

It came in the spring, after a winter of heavy snows, when the stream that had been a
voice in the ground all his life swelled and rose and overleapt its bank. He felt it
coming as a change in the tremor — the deep steady voice of the water turning loud and
wrong, a roaring where there had been a murmur — and then the water was on the glade, not
as rain, not as the slow soak of a wet season, but as a moving wall of brown that picked up
the meadow and carried it.

He felt the carpet go. Blades he had grown beside for years, whole clumps of his kindred,
were torn loose from the soil by the water and tumbled past him, roots in the air, gone
downstream where he could not feel them anymore. The soil itself was moving — the good
dark earth he had drunk from all his life lifted off the ground in sheets and slid away,
and his roots, his careful deep roots that remembered the channel and the dry year and the
shape of every stone, were suddenly holding nothing, gripping water, gripping the empty
place where the world had been.

The flood taught him that he could be moved.

Not the way the grazers moved him — they took the top of him and left the root, and the
root was him, the oldest him, safe in the dark. The flood took the dark itself. It took the
soil his root was woven through, and for a long terrible stretch of the brown water he felt
himself going with it, felt the grip of his roots find nothing, felt the whole small
weight of him beginning to lift out of the ground —

and then his root, his deep root, the one that had reached the old channel in the drought,
found something that held. A stone, a buried root of the fallen giant, something in the
torn earth that did not move. He gripped it with everything he had, the way he had gripped
the dry ground in the dying summer, and the water tore at him, and he held, and the water
passed.

When it was over, the glade was not the glade. The stream had found a new bed, farther
from him; the good soil of the meadow was gone, and what was left was mud and stones and
the drowned wreckage of the carpet. Most of his kindred were gone with the soil that had
held them. The ones that remained had been carried here from upstream, strangers, their
roots in the mud where they had been dropped, already putting out new blades in a place
they had never chosen.

He stood where he had always stood. He had held. But he had held by luck — by the buried
root of the fallen giant, by a grip he had not chosen — and the thing in him, which kept
every lesson, kept this one too, and it was the hardest lesson yet, because there was no
answer to it. The frost could be answered by lying down. The drought could be answered by
reaching. The bite could be answered by growing tougher. But the flood had shown him that
the world could take the ground itself out from under him, and no blade, however tough,
however deep, could answer that.

He could only hold. And go on holding. And grow his roots into the new ground the flood
had left, and drink the new water, and wait for the glade to become a glade again.

The years after the flood were quiet ones, and they were the years he learned the listening.

It had been with him since before he broke the soil, though he had not known it for what
it was: the ground itself was never still. Water moved through it, thread by thread, and
the stream drummed against its bed, and roots drank and the soil settled and shifted by
grains, and every living thing that walked the glade sent its weight down into the earth
before it. Any root feels some of this, dimly, the way any skin feels the wind. But the
essence that had been gathering in him for forty years had made his roots into something
no grass's roots had ever been — a listening thing, deep and fine — and what had been a
dim blur of pressure had become, slowly, a language.

Not the grass. He had stopped reaching toward the grass long ago. His kindred were silent
in a way that had no words for it — no presence, no pressure, nothing to feel at all — and
that silence was the truest thing about them, and about him, and about the distance
between. What he learned to read was the living ground itself, and the world that walked
on it, and that was more than enough to fill a century.

He learned the glade's footfalls the way a child learns names.

There was the steady heavy drag of the old badger, whose territory line passed within a
root's length of him and who had been crossing it, twice a day, for longer than he had
been alive. There was the quick scatter of the digging-rodent family, their small clawed
feet telling him the soil above was being turned, that new tunnels were being dug, that a
nest had moved. There was the light single-footed touch of the hunting birds, landing and
gone, landing and gone, and the heavier, slower weight of the thing that walked the glade
at night — he never knew its shape, only its rhythm, the patient unhurried pace of a large
animal that feared nothing in this part of the forest. He knew the deer trail's season by
the rhythm of hooves that came and went with the green; he knew the rain was coming by the
way the ground went tight and still a day before the first drop fell; he knew, once, in a
year of his youth, that something vast was crossing the valley — felt it for a week as a
deep slow pulse in the earth, a weight so great it seemed to press on the water in his
roots, and felt the whole glade go silent and still while it passed, the way the birds
went silent when the grazers came, only more so, only longer.

He learned all of it through the listening. And the thing in him, which had made him able to
hear it, was not idle even in the quiet years. It was always working. He felt it in the
slow deepening of his root, in the way his blades had begun to hold their green a little
longer into the autumn than the blades around him, in the way the frost's lesson and the
drought's lesson and the bite's lesson and the flood's fear had all settled into him like
rings in a tree — layer on layer, season on season, each one making the next blade
slightly more than the blade before it had been.

He did not know he was changing. To himself he was simply himself, the only self he had
ever been, growing the only way he had ever grown. He had never seen another blade like
him, so he had nothing to measure against. Through the second decade and the third, the
grazing herd still came back, year after year, and cropped the meadow down to stubble, and
he was cropped with it — he felt the teeth take him, felt the bright tearing that he had
learned to fear and now only braced for, felt the world end at his crown the way it always
ended — and when he regrew, tougher at the base, quicker to rise, harder along the bitten
edges, he did not think *I have changed*. He only grew. The herd's shadow, which he had
learned to feel a breath before the pain, made him draw himself in, made his blades lie a
little flatter against the ground in the season the grazers came, and he did not think *I
have learned*. He only leaned.

That was the talent's way with him, and it would be its way for all his long life: not a
voice telling him what to do, not a power he could call on, but a shaping, a leaning, a
constant slow becoming — the world doing things to him and the thing in him answering every
time, turning every harm and every hunger and every quiet year into the next blade of
himself. He was being grown, the way a tree is grown by its weather, ring by ring. Except
that trees do not remember their weather, and he did. He remembered all of it — the frost
and the drought and the flood and the bites, the shadow that fell before the pain, the
deep channel of water in the dry earth, the buried root he had gripped in the brown water,
the weight of the vast thing crossing the valley. It was all in him, layer under layer,
root under root.

And in the late years of his fourth decade, the remembering crossed a line — and became
change.

It came on as a tightening, a deepening, the way the earth tightens before a storm. For
weeks the light seemed to pour into him and stay, pooling where it had never pooled; the
water ran up his root and did not leave; and the gathered life of forty springs, forty
summers, forty autumns and forty winters — every harm and every hunger and every quiet
year he had kept — thickened inside him until something in the crown of him, some small
deep knot he had never known he owned, gave way, and the whole gathered weight of him
settled into a new shape, the way a seed settles into a root.

He could not see it. But he felt it happening, in the only way he could feel anything — as
a slow brightening of himself, an unfolding, a becoming. And when the new green came up in
the year that followed, it was not the green that had come before it.

The blades that rose from his crown were different. Deeper. A blue-green so dark it was
nearly blue, holding the light the way deep water holds it, and along the length of every
blade a pale silver line ran up the middle, vein-fine, catching the sun like a thread of
the stream's own silver. The leaves were thicker than any grass's leaves, stiffer along
the edges, curling slightly at the tip like a claw sheathed. The crown itself had
hardened into a dense, tight knot of growth — not the loose tuft of a common weed but
something closer in its stubbornness to a young tree's trunk. A keen eye — and there was
no such eye in the glade, for no eye in the glade ever looked twice at grass — would have
stopped on him and known at once that this was not common Blue Silver Grass. This was a
grass that had been *changed*: a different thing of the same line, the way some grasses
in the wide world are born dark and hungry, the way the great line of the grass can run
gold in its rarest blood. He was becoming his own kind. The talent in him — the thing
that had woken him in the dark and kept every lesson since — had reached its first full
turning, and it had rewritten the green of him to prove it.

He was a soul beast. A true one now, unmistakably: a hundred-year-class soul beast in the
only measure the deep forest keeps, born of the commonest plant on the continent, at an
age when a grass should hold nothing but water and green. If a soul master had come to the
glade and cut him down, he would have found a ring where no ring had ever been — pale
yellow, the color the world gives a hundred-year beast, near the bottom of all colors but
real — and the wonder would have been right, because in all the ages of the world no grass
had ever been what he was becoming.

And something else had begun in him, small and strange, in the turning of that year. He
could feel it when the wind pressed his blades or a beetle crawled his stem: a new
instinct, buried in the changed green, like the ghost of a hand. When he *wanted* it —
and he barely knew how to want it, it was so new — a single blade could bend, slowly, a
thumb's width, curling toward the thing that touched him. It was nothing. It could not
hold a falling leaf, let alone stop a mouth. But it was his, the first fruit of the
changed green, the first sign of the power that lived in the grass line when it was
allowed to live at all — and it would grow, as he grew, through every gate he crossed.

The grazing herd still cropped him when it passed, and he still lay flat and let it,
because there was nothing in him yet that could stop a mouth, and he knew it the way he
knew the frost — a truth of the world, to be answered by endurance, not defiance. To every
creature of the glade he was still grass, and would be grass for a long time yet.

But he was no longer what he had been. The impossible had stopped merely being invisible
and had begun, blade by silver-veined blade, to be *real* — a changed grass, awake and
alone and growing stranger, in a glade that had not yet learned to look at him twice.

And then, in the green of a morning, in the year the glade had finally grown back into
itself after the flood, a new rhythm entered his turf.

He felt it at first as nothing — a lightness on the carpet, a touch so brief and quick it
was almost not there, the way a falling leaf touches. But it came again, and again, and
each touch was different, deliberate, alive: a small thing moving through the grass near
his base, pausing, testing, moving on. It was not a footfall like the badger's drag or the
rodents' scatter. It was something else entirely — a many-pointed touch, delicate and
certain, the weight of a creature that did not walk the way the ground-walkers walked but
stepped and rested and stepped, the way the hunting birds stepped, only smaller, nearer,
closer to him than any bird had ever come.

He felt it stop. It was very near his crown — closer than any creature had ever come to
him without taking him — and it was still, and he felt it there, a small bright presence
in the green of the morning, the way he had once felt the first warmth of the light, the
way he had once felt the first thread of the water in the dark.

He did not know what it was. He had no name for it, and no way to look at it, and nothing
in all his stored memories of the glade to compare it to. He only knew that something new
had come into the small world of him — something that had stopped, near his crown, in the
grass that was him and not him, and was resting there the way nothing had ever rested near
him before.

It was not the herd. It was not the badger, or the rodents, or the birds, or the great
slow thing that walked the night. It was something that had never been in his world, and
it had chosen to stop — of all the ten thousand blades in the glade — within a breath of
him.

And the thing in him, which had been watching the world since before he broke the soil,
watching and learning and remembering, did the only thing it knew how to do with something
new:

it paid attention.

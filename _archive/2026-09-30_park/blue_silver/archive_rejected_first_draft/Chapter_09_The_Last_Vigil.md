# Chapter Nine — The Last Vigil

*Still the deep era. Still the glade at the edge of the deep band. The sickness is broken
and the green is whole, and the keeper keeps on. The old one — its speech long spent —
goes quieter yet at his edge, breath by breath, until the keeping it passed to him is all
that is left of it above the ground. And the found one of the deep, grown steady in his
green, begins at last to cross back to the boundary and return — and the deep, which
watched it cross in, must learn what it means that one of its own small now sleeps at the
keeper's root. The last vigil is kept. And the keeper keeps on, alone, the way he was
always going to have to keep on.*

The years after the sickness were the quietest of his life, and he did not know, while
they were passing, that he would one day look back on them as a kept thing.

The glade had come back changed, as it always came back changed. The blades that rose
where the rot had been were denser and carried the deep silver of the rot-memory in their
green, and the small lives had rebuilt their generations on the new ground, and the stream
ran its old course, and the deep band — which had watched the low-water keeper hold —
withdrew to its patient distance and waited, the way it always waited. Nothing pressed the
line. The herd came down along the water in the dry season and cropped the far meadow and
did not cross into the silver patch, because the silver patch was not a meadow anymore and
had not been for a very long time. The ground birds raised their broods in his lee. The
rodent families turned their generations over at his edge, and he kept the pattern of each
one and let them go, and the green went on being a green, and he went on being the one who
kept it.

At his edge, the old one breathed.

It had been breathing for so long that its breathing had become part of the ground he
stood in, the way the stream's voice was part of it — a slow, shallow, even tide at the
edge of his sensing, fainter than it had been in the sickness years, fainter than it had
been the spring it tapped its last two blows. He did not measure the fading. He had long
ago stopped measuring the things he could not stop. He only kept his pulses going out to it
against the rhythm of its breath — *I am here, I am here* — and felt the great head shift
a little at the feel of him, sometimes, and knew it had heard. What was left of the
language they had built was this: its breath, and his pulses rising to meet it, and the
long silences between, which were not empty. They were the old one's silence, the silence
of a thing that had said everything it had to say and was resting in the saying.

And the found one stayed.

It had grown in the years after the sickness, the way things grew when nothing was eating
them — slowly, and without fear. It was not the trembling small gray thing that had dragged
itself across the rot-grass anymore. It was a steady small gray thing of the deep band's
fringe, quick and quiet as its kind were quick and quiet, but without the constant
starting-away of a thing that expected to be hunted. It slept against his root where the
old one's breath warmed the ground. It grazed the soft edges of his green in the mornings
and the evenings. It knew the sanctuary the way every kept thing knew it — by being safe in
it — and the small lives of the glade had long since stopped treating it as a stranger,
because it was not one. It was the keeper's, the way the fledgling had been the keeper's,
the way everything that came into the deep green and stayed became the keeper's. He had no
word for what it was to him. He only knew that after the long years of the sickness, when
every keeping had cost him something, here was a keeping that had cost nothing and given
back the one thing the glade could not give him: a small warm presence that was not afraid
of him, and did not leave.

It was the found one that first showed him the boundary had two directions.

He felt it go one morning in the third quiet year — a small gray shape moving out of the
deep green, not running, not stumbling, but walking, the way a thing walks when it knows
where it is going. It crossed the line of his outermost blades and stopped, and stood at
the very edge of the silver-green, looking out across the rot-scarred fringe toward the
deep. He felt its weight there, at the line, and understood — slowly, in the way he
understood everything, by pattern and pressure — that it was not trying to leave. It was
*looking*. It had come out of that dark, and it had never once, since it crossed, gone back
to the edge of it to see it from the safe side.

He did not hold it. That was the whole lesson of the found one, the lesson it had taught
him without words: the keeping was a door, and a door that only opened one way was a wall.
He let his blades be still, and he let it stand at the line as long as it wanted, and when
it had stood long enough it turned and came back into the deep green and lay down against
his root, and that was all.

But it went again. Not every day, not even every season at first — but the going became a
habit of its small life, the way the evening grazing was a habit. It would walk out to the
line and stand, sometimes for the space of a whole morning, and then it would come back.
And one day it did not stop at the line. It crossed it — crossed back, into the gray
rot-scarred fringe of the deep band that had once been its whole world — and he felt its
weight go out past his roots and into the ground that was not his, and he did not hold it,
because holding it would have been the one thing he had never done to any kept thing.

It was gone for the space of a day and a night.

He kept watch through that day and that night the way he had kept watch through the worst
of everything — rooted, still, sending his pulses out along the boundary after the small
weight, the way he had once sent them after the fledgling when it flew. He did not know
what the deep would do to one of its own that came back smelling of the green. He only
knew that it had crossed to him once because there was nowhere else left in the world to
cross to, and that if the deep took it now, he would have kept it for nothing.

It came back at dawn, unhurt, moving a little faster than it had gone out, and it came
straight into the deep green and lay against his root, and its breathing was quick for a
while and then steadied, and he felt — in the pattern of its small weight, in the particular
way it pressed itself into the ground against him — that it had been afraid, and had come
home anyway, the way kept things came home. It had gone out into the deep and the deep had
not eaten it. It had gone out carrying the green on it, in the deep-country smell of the
soil that had grown its coat, and the deep had smelled the green on it and let it pass, and
let it return.

He did not know what to make of that. He only knew that the found one went out a little
farther each time after that, and always came back, and that word of it went into the deep
ahead of its small gray footprints, the way word traveled in the deep — by scars and
silence and the absence of eaten things. A thing of the deep that walked into the deep and
walked out again, carrying the green. A thing the green had kept, that the deep had let go.

The word would take a long time to mean anything. He did not know that yet. He only knew
the found one was happy — if a small gray grazer of the deep band could be said to be happy,
and he believed it could, because he had felt it afraid and he had felt it not-afraid, and
the not-afraid had grown like a green thing in his ground — and that he had a kept thing at
his root that went out and came back, and that the door he kept swung both ways.

---

The old one's breathing slowed through the seasons after that, the way it had slowed
through every season for longer than he could remember, and he kept his vigil the way he
had kept the vigil of the hard winter — by being there, season after season, sending his
pulses out against the fading tide of its breath. The found one learned the rhythm of it,
too, the way kept things learned the rhythms of their ground: it would lift its head when
the breath between the old one's slow risings stretched too long, and it would settle again
when the next breath came, and it slept against his root where the warmth was, patient as
small things were patient.

There was no warning. There was never a warning, in the end, for the things that went
quietly.

It was a spring night, in the third year after the found one first crossed back to the
boundary. The stream was talking along its bed with the melt-voice it only had in the
thaw. The small lives were asleep in their runs. The found one lay against his root,
breathing its small even breaths. And the old one — whose great head had lain against his
roots for the whole of its rest, whose breath had been the ground's slow tide for longer
than the glade had been a glade — drew one breath, and then did not draw another.

He felt it as a stillness where a stillness had never been. Not the silence of the going-
quiet years, which had been the old one holding its speech back, saving its strength. This
was the ground itself going still where the great body lay — the particular stillness of a
thing that has finished. He sent his pulse through the soil to the place where its side had
been, the way he had sent it ten thousand mornings, and the ground answered with nothing.
He sent it again, and again, through the long dark of that spring night, until the light
came over the silver-green and the small lives came out of their burrows and the found one
stirred against his root — and the old one's head did not shift at the feel of him, and
did not shift, and did not shift.

It was not a thing he had to understand. It was a thing he had to know, in the way grass
knew the frost: the last of the old one's kind had finished its going, and what remained at
his edge was the great body, still warm in the dawn, and the whole of what it had taught
him, rooted in him where its head had lain.

He did not grieve all at once. Grief did not come to him all at once — it came the way the
old one's going had come, by small degrees, so that he could not say when it began. His
green went thin that first season. Not from any harm, not from the rot, not from the dry:
he simply did not reach for the light the way he reached for it, and the blades that came
up came up paler, and the small lives felt it as a lessening of the heavy safe warmth of
his ground, and the found one pressed itself closer to his root and stayed, the way kept
things stayed.

He learned, in that thin season, the thing the old one had been trying to teach him all
along without ever having the words: that the keeping did not stop at the boundary, and
that the hardest ground a keeper ever held was the ground of his own loss. The talent could
not carry this. He felt it, dimly, as he felt everything — the endless engine of his life
turning beneath him, looking for the harm to harden against, the lesson to outgrow, the
hunger to feed — and finding nothing, because there was no harm here and no lesson and no
hunger. There was only a place at his edge where the ground had gone still, and some losses
were not changes to grow into. Some losses were just losses.

He did not adapt to it. That was the whole point of it, and he knew it the way he knew the
turning of the year: he did not adapt, he did not outgrow, he did not become more than the
loss by passing through it. He simply kept it. He kept the place at his edge where the
great body lay, the way he had kept her husk, the way he had kept the place where the
fledgling slept — kept it through the thin season and the seasons after, because that was
what a keeper was for. The talent made him more than grass in a thousand ways. It could not
make him less than a keeper, and he would not have let it if it could.

---

The ground did what the ground did, in the seasons after. It was the oldest rhythm of the
place, older than the glade, older than the keeping: everything that lived went into the
ground, and the ground gave it back as green.

The great body softened and sank by slow degrees, becoming soil, becoming the small bright
things that fed on soil, becoming — by the time the winters had turned over their count of
years — the deepest, richest stretch of ground in all the length of the stream bend. Grass
grew over it, denser and darker than the grass around it, carrying the silver deeper, and
the small lives of the sanctuary raised their young on the ground where the last of the old
one's kind lay, never knowing, the way small lives never knew the reasons of the ground
they stood on.

He did not reach for it.

That was the hardest keeping of his life, harder than the vigil, harder than the thin
season — because he could feel it, the whole slow giving of the old one's years going into
the earth like water finding a root, and some part of him, the deep endless part that had
never once stopped climbing, the part that was the talent and the hunger and the well all
at once, leaned toward that richness the way the eater had once leaned toward his sweetest
root. It would have been so easy. It was lying in his own ground, and the ground was his,
and nothing in all the forest would have thought twice of a keeper drawing the years of a
fallen great one up into his own keeping. That was what the deep did with its dead. That
was what everything did with everything.

He drew that part of himself back, and let the ground have the old one whole.

He had learned, across the long years, that he did not become more by taking. He became
more by keeping — by being there, by holding the line, by letting each kept thing go into
the ground that kept all things and carrying what it had taught him in his roots instead.
The old one had given him its whole keeper's knowledge while it lived, tap by tap, and
that was the part of it that was his. The rest — the body, the years, the long slow tide
that had gone out — belonged to the ground, the way everything that lived belonged to the
ground in the end. He kept the teaching. He kept the place. He let the ground have the
rest, and the ground, in its way, gave it back as green, and the green was dense and silver
over the old one's place for as long as the glade was a glade.

The found one slept against that place now. It had slept against his root where the old
one's warmth had been, and when the warmth faded and the ground went still, it had not
moved away — it had moved closer, the way kept things moved closer when the ground changed,
and it lay against the densest patch of the silver-green, where the grass remembered the
old one's shape in its growth, and it was warm there, and it stayed.

He kept the place. He kept the found one. He kept the thin season and the seasons after,
and slowly — the way everything healed, by small degrees and not by becoming something new —
his green came back to its color, and the blades that rose over the old one's place carried
the silver deepest of all, and the glade went on being a glade, and the keeper went on
being the keeper.

And the deep, which had watched the boundary for longer than the glade had been a glade,
learned that the old weight was gone.

---

The word went out by absence, the way the deep's word always went out. The great walking
thing that had held the seam for longer than the seam's own beasts could remember was not
at the boundary anymore. It was not lying at the edge of the green, breathing. It was gone,
and the ground where it had lain was rich, and the keeper of the green was alone on the
line for the first time since the line had been his.

They came in the second year after, in the long dark of the cold season.

Not the serpent — the serpent carried its wound and its lesson, and would not come again
while the ground was held. Not the eater — the eater had learned the ground was not food.
Not any one great thing that could be met and turned, the way a keeper turned a testing
presence. It was the fringe itself that came: the lean patient hunters of the seam's far
side, drawn by the richness the old one's passing had left in the soil and by the absence
of the weight that had once made even the deep pause. Not together — the deep did not move
together, it had no leaders and no armies, only hungers and rumors and the long patience of
things that had lived longer than glades. But they came close enough together in the same
dark season that it felt like one push, one slow pressing of the deep against the line to
learn whether the line was still a line without the old one behind it.

He felt them come the way he felt everything — as patterns at the edge of his sensing,
patient weights gathering at the boundary's far side, testing the air, reading the changed
ground. And he understood, in the way he understood the frost, what was being asked of him.
Not whether he could fight. The deep knew he could fight; it had known since the serpent
night. What it was asking was whether the keeping would hold without the old one's shadow
falling across it — whether the line was the old one's line, held by its presence, or
whether it was a line that would hold because the ground itself held it.

He answered by holding.

It was not the fight of the sickness years, when he was thin and spent and could only just
keep the one place where the small lives sheltered. It was a different thing, a wider
thing, and it cost him — everything cost him, and he paid without counting — but it was the
whole of what he had been becoming since the serpent night, called up at last to its full
length. The hunters came at the fringe's edge and his presence pressed them back before
they reached the silver. They circled, found the soft stretches, and his roots were waiting
in the soft stretches, woven through the ground along the whole seam where the old one had
once walked its round — because he had been extending them there for years, root by root,
season by season, without ever deciding to, the way a keeper extended into the ground he
kept. The one that pressed closest, boldest, drawn farthest by the richness, met his blades
at the line and was cut, and carried the cut back into the dark with it, and did not come
again.

They broke off before the spring. Not because he drove them off — he had not moved, had not
chased, had not killed, had never killed anything in all his kept life. They broke off
because the ground itself held the line, and the ground did not tire, and the keeper did
not tire, and the deep band — reading the outcome the way it read everything, by what came
back and what did not — understood at last what the found one's staying had already begun
to tell it: the old keeper was gone, but the green by the water was kept by the green
itself now, and the green did not stop being there, and there was nothing left at the
boundary for the deep to take.

He held his ground through the rest of the cold and into the thaw with his roots stretched
farther along the seam than they had ever been, and when the light came back and the small
lives came out and the found one stirred against the silver-green over the old one's place,
he was still there. He had always been going to be still there. That was the whole of the
keeper's truth, and the old one had taught it to him, and now it was his alone to keep, and
the line was his alone to hold, and he held it.

---

The years after that were the years a keeper keeps when the teaching is done and the
teacher is gone.

The glade went on being a glade. The stream turned its seasons. The sanctuary's small
lives turned their generations over in his green, and he kept each pattern and let each one
go, the way he had always done. The deep band kept its patient distance, and the deep's
word, traveling by scars and silence, changed by slow degrees: the old keeper was gone, and
the line was held by the green, and the green kept even the deep's own small and lost — and
now, strangest of all, the deep's own small went out from the green into the deep and came
back again, unhurt, and the deep let them pass. The found one went out a little farther
each season as the years turned, ranging the rot-scarred fringe it had been born in, where
the rot had long since sunk back into the soil and the soft green had regrown — and the
fringe's other small and lost, the ones that had survived the way it had survived, by
being quick and quiet and afraid, learned to know it by the smell of the green on it, and
some of them, in time, followed it back to the line and stood there, looking in.

It never brought one across. He did not know if it understood that it could. He only knew
that the door stood open, and that the word was traveling from the inside now, and that
whatever the deep made of the green by the water, it made of it slowly, the way the deep
made everything.

And at his edge, where the great body had lain, the densest grass in the glade grew over
the old one's place, and the found one slept against it, and he kept them both.

He was alone now among the powers of the line — the only keeper of the boundary, the last
of the old one's teaching standing rooted in the ground where the last of the old one's
kind lay. He had known, since the mantis, that this was how it would go: everyone he loved
would end, and he would not, and the keeping would go on. He had felt it coming for a
hundred years of the old one's rest, and he had kept the feeling the way he kept
everything, and now the feeling was a kept thing too, and the ground at his edge was still
where the great head had lain, and he was still the keeper.

He was not alone. That was the other thing the found one had taught him, without words,
across the years of its staying: alone was not the same as it had been when he was young.
When he was young, alone had been the whole of the world — a blade among mindless blades,
no one to answer his pulses, no one to know he was there. This was different. This was a
green full of small lives that pressed into him for safety, and a kept thing of the deep
sleeping against the silver-green where the old one lay, and the whole
kept record of his bonds — her husk and her molts and her egg-case shell at his base, the
fledgling's place and pattern, the old one's teaching and the old one's place, all of it
kept, all of it still with him. Alone among the powers of the line, and not alone at all.

The road to the deep country still lay in the direction the old one had turned its head at
dawn all its life. He did not know its name yet, or its queen, or the shape of the great
one he was becoming. He did not know that the kept count of his seasons was still climbing
toward a mark no grass of his line had ever touched, or that the depth in him — the well
that had run low and filled again lower and deeper through the sickness and the going and
the keeping of these years — was the slow consolidation of a life outrunning its years, or
that somewhere in the deep forest, in the direction of the core, an ancient Empress read
the grass through her domain and had not yet found a reason to look twice at any blade of
it.

He only knew that the sickness had broken, and the keeping was his alone, and the found one
slept against the ground where the old one lay, and the line held, and he had kept the last
of the old one's kind the only way a rooted thing could keep anything — by being there
when it went into the ground, and by staying after, and by letting the ground have it
whole, and by keeping what it had taught him. The grief was still there. It would always be
there, the way her grief was always there, the way the fledgling's was — a kept thing in
the ground of him that the talent had never once tried to take, because some things were
not for adapting.

And in the spring, when the light came back over the silver-green and the small lives came
out of their burrows and the found one went out to the boundary and came back, the way it
did now, unhurried and unhurt and carrying the green out and the deep back, he turned his
whole kept self toward the sun, the way he had turned it every spring of his life, and he
grew — a little deeper into the ground he held, a little farther along the boundary he
kept alone, a little closer to the deep country he did not yet know was waiting for him.

The found one slept at his root. The dense grass grew over the old one's place. The
boundary held. And the one who keeps, kept — alone, and not alone, the way he had always
kept, and the way he would always keep, for as long as the ground held him.

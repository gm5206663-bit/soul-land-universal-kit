# Chapter Ten — The One Who Answers

```
◆ STATUS — Chapter 10: years 500–580
KIND      : Blue Silver Grass, common line. Keeper of the seam, alone, with eight of the
            deep's small living in his crown.
AGE       : ~580 at the chapter's close
CLASS     : upper thousand-year class, ≈9,000 carried. The reach opens in this chapter.
THREAT    : a Hall assessor spends a season in grid nineteen and files the first report
            that names the grass rather than the walker.
SPEECH    : the found one answers. First two-way speech of his alone-keeping. The drum
            begins, and the door becomes a language.
```

---

He had been alone for forty years when the found one answered him.

That is not quite right, and the correction matters. He had not been alone — there were
eight small things in his crown and the whole middle depth went quiet around him the way it
had gone quiet around the old one, and the deep band leaned on his seam every season with
the regularity of weather. But nothing had ever answered him. The vocabulary of taps had
died with the thing that invented it, and the only sound in the whole of his life that had
ever been *addressed to him* was two blows on the earth at dusk, and there was no more of
that.

So he went on pulsing. Not to anyone. The root-pulse was the only thing he had that was
not growing, and he used it the way the old one had used the round — as a habit that had
outlived its purpose, the whole hundred feet of the seam going taut and letting go once at
dusk, because that was what had been done at dusk for an age, and because a keeper keeps.

The found one heard it.

It had been hearing it for a hundred years. That was the thing he had not understood: the
small grey grazer that had stood at his edge in the winter of his four hundred and second
year had been living inside the pulse its entire adult life. It was the rhythm of its home.
It went out through the loosened layer at dusk and came back at dawn, and it came back to
the taut-and-let-go the way a bird comes back to a nest, and in the forty-first year of the
alone-keeping it did something it had never done in two centuries.

It stamped back.

---

Two blows. With its feet. On the packed ground over the old one's place.

He felt them come up through the root-mass and he did not understand them, because he had
no framework in which a small grazer's stamping was a reply rather than a small grazer's
stamping. He had watched the eight of them move across his crown for two hundred years and
every step of it had been noise.

So he pulsed. Once. The way he always did.

And it stamped twice again.

Same two. Same spacing. The same spacing the old one had used — because the old one had
lain at the edge of this bank for a hundred and sixty years with its head on the ground,
tapping two
blows at dusk, and a small grazer had been living forty feet away for sixty-six years
listening to it, and small grey things remember a rhythm.

He had no words. He had four, inherited, and none of them were his. But he had two hundred
and thirty years of ground-listening and a root-mass that could read the difference between
a hoof and a hand, and what came up through him in that moment was the same thing that had
come up through him when the tapping first stopped: the difference between a thing that
happened and a thing that was put there.

He pulsed twice.

It stamped three.

They went on like that until dawn.

---

The vocabulary took seventy years to build and it never got past eleven words.

That is worth saying plainly, because it would be easy to make this larger than it was. It
was not a language in any sense a person would recognise. It was eleven shaped patterns of
blows and pulses, passed back and forth between a bank of grass and a small grazer, over
ground, at dusk, in a glade in grid nineteen, and it was the entire content of the first
conversation he had ever had with anything that was not the old one.

*Here. Home. Safe. Danger. Gone. Cold. Many. One. Come. Stay.*

And the eleventh, which the found one invented, which took eleven years to agree on, and
which he would go on using for the rest of his life — a single blow, hard, once, that meant
none of the other ten:

*I am here, I am answering, I am one of the ones who stays.*

It was a small thing. It was the second true speech of his life and it had eleven words in
it and a grazer on one end of it.

And it taught him something the old one's sixty years of taps had not, which was that
speech is not what a keeper does with a keeper. The old one had passed the office to him —
the round, the three places, the difference between a wall and a door. It had given him a
job. The found one gave him something else, and it gave it by accident, by stamping back at
dusk because the rhythm was the rhythm of its home, and what it gave him was the discovery
that the keeping had a *who* in it.

He was not keeping a line. He was keeping eight small things, and one of them knew his name.

Not his name. He had no name; he would not have one for another hundred and sixty years, and
it would come from the far end of the wire and it would be the oldest word in a grazer's
drum. But it knew *him* — the taut-and-let-go at dusk, the loosened layer, the warm dark in
the crown — and it answered, and answering is what turns a post into a home.

---

It taught the fringe young.

That happened without him deciding it, the way everything in his life happened. The found
one ranged back across the boundary and returned, unhurt, and some of the fringe's small
and lost followed it to the line — and the ones that followed it learned the stamping,
because the stamping was how you stood at the edge of the woven mass and said *I am small
and I am cold and I am not trying to come through you*, and the ones that learned it got
let in, and the ones that did not learned it from the ones that did.

By his five hundred and sixtieth year the drum was not one grazer. It was a hundred small voices at the
edge of his ground at dusk, stamping the eleven words in the packed soil, and the ground
carried all of it up through his root-mass at once, and he answered the whole of them with
one pulse, and the low ground by the stream was the only place in the Star Dou middle band
where the deep band's refugees had a language for asking.

He had made a people. He had not meant to. He had no concept of a people. But the door had
become a route, and the route had become a habit, and the habit had become a vocabulary,
and a vocabulary shared by a hundred small things is a people whether or not anybody in it
has the word.

---

*The Hall assessor came into grid nineteen in the summer of his five hundred and
fifty-seventh year and stayed a whole season, and he was the first person in four centuries to look at
the grass and mean it.*

*He was not a hunter and not a surveyor. He was a records man — thin, patient, fifty, named
Orrin Vase, with no rings at all and a Hall issue coat that had never been in a fight — and
he had been sent because the sealed-pending designation on this grid was four hundred years
old and had never been justified by anything the registry could point at, and somebody two
levels up had decided to know why.*

*"Nine weeks," his superior had said, handing him the file. "Nine weeks, one mule, and if
the walker is alive you come straight back."*

*"And if it's dead."*

*"Then the designation comes off and the grid opens for hunting, and you'll have saved the
Hall a survey team." The superior tapped the file. "It's a dead walker, Vase. They die.
That's what they do."*

*He did not go looking for the walker. He went looking for the paperwork.*

*He spent nine weeks in the glade. He walked the boundary — the actual route, the old one's
round, which he found in the ground the way you find a path in a field, and which he walked
every day for nine weeks with a measuring cord and a notebook. He dug four soil pits. He
counted the small things at dusk from the meadow ridge, and he counted them again the next
dusk, and the third, and he wrote the numbers down and compared them.*

*On the twenty-second day he wrote to his superior, and the letter took six weeks to go and
six weeks to come back, and the reply was two lines:*

*"You are asking me to send a specialist team to look at grass. Explain yourself in one
sentence or come home."*

*He sat with that for three days. Then he wrote back.*

*"The boundary of grid nineteen is maintained daily, against pressure, by something that is
not the walker, and I have walked it forty-one times and it has not moved."*

*The reply came back in the autumn, and it was one line:*

*"Granted. One team. Vase, if this is grass I am putting you on latrine rotation at the
western post until you retire."*

*And on the forty-first day, while he waited, he sat down in the middle of the low ground
with his notebook open and stayed perfectly still for four hours, and at dusk he felt the
seam go taut and let go under him, and he wrote one line, and then he sat for another hour
and felt it again, and he wrote the time.*

*The team came in the spring. There were four of them and a Soul Ancestor, and they spent
eleven days in the glade, and on the twelfth day the Soul Ancestor came and stood over
Vase's notebook and read it, and he was quiet for a while.*

*"You've been out here nine weeks," he said.*

*"Nine weeks and four days."*

*"Alone. Four miles from the ridge. In a grid the Hall has had sealed for four centuries."
The Soul Ancestor looked out at the low ground. "And you found a plant."*

*"I found a boundary, sir. The plant is what's keeping it."*

*"Plants don't cultivate."*

*"No, sir."*

*"Then what is it."*

*Vase had been asking himself that for nine weeks and he had written the answer down
eleven times and crossed it out eleven times, and he said it anyway, because he was fifty
years old and he had no rings and he had walked a dead animal's round forty-one times.*

*"I don't know what it is," he said. "I know what it does. It keeps that line, every day,
and it has been keeping it since before the walker died, and if we take the seal off this
grid the low ground opens and everything in the middle band west of the ridge comes through
it within a season." He closed the notebook. "I'm not asking you to believe me about the
plant. I'm asking you to believe me about the line."*

*The Soul Ancestor stood there a long time, and then he walked the round himself, once, with
the measuring cord, and he came back at dusk, and he stood in the low ground and felt the
seam go taut and let go under his feet.*

*He did not say anything. But the seal stayed on.*

*His report went to the Hall that autumn. It was eleven pages and it was careful and it was
the first document in four hundred years that did not put the grass in the vegetation
column.*

*It said: the sealed-pending designation on grid nineteen is justified, but not for the
reason recorded. The walker in this grid is dead and has been dead for approximately one
hundred and forty years; the round it walked is still being kept, by something else. The
boundary of this grid is maintained. It is maintained continuously, it is maintained
against pressure from the deep side, and it is maintained by the vegetation.*

*It said: there is a plant-type growth at the stream bend covering approximately one hundred
feet of low ground which will not come out of the soil, which is layered rather than
rooted, and which responds to contact by tension. There is a population of low-class
deep-band fauna resident within it, estimated between eighty and one hundred and twenty
individuals, which enters and leaves by a route I have not been able to locate, and which
appears to be regulated.*

*It said: I do not believe this is a soul beast. Plants do not cultivate, and there is no
soul power detectable at this site by any instrument I carry, and I am aware of what I am
suggesting. I am suggesting it anyway, because the boundary is kept, and something is
keeping it, and I have eliminated everything else.*

*It said: recommend reclassification of the grid and specialist assessment. Recommend the
specialist be somebody who will take the vegetation seriously.*

*The report went into the registry in the autumn of his five hundred and fifty-seventh
year, and it sat there, and the specialist assessment did not come for another hundred and fifty
years, because the Hall was busy and the middle band was quiet and one records man's
eleven pages about a patch of grass did not move anybody who mattered.*

*But it was in the file. And it was the first time in four centuries that anybody had
written down, in an official document, in the registry of the largest institution on the
continent, that the grass in grid nineteen was keeping a boundary.*

*In the glade, at dusk, a hundred small things stamped eleven words into the packed soil
over an ancient keeper's grave, and the seam went taut and let go, and the assessor sat in
the middle of it with his notebook open and did not move, because he had spent nine weeks
learning what the sound meant and he did not want to interrupt.*

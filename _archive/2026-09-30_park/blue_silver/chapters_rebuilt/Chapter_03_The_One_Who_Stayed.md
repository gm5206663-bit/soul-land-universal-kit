# Chapter Three — The One Who Stayed

```
◆ STATUS — Chapter 3: years 40–105
KIND      : Blue Silver Grass, common line — silver-veined deep-blue variant, spreading.
AGE       : ~100 at the chapter's close
CLASS     : crosses the thousand-year mark in carried cultivation near his hundredth year.
            Self-protection is only just beginning to open. He is still, in practice, salad.
THREAT    : a father brings his son into the glade to take the boy's first ring. They kill
            forty feet from him. He watches the whole thing and cannot do anything about it.
BOND      : the mantis. The first being in his life who comes to him and stays.
```

---

She arrived in the sixty-first summer, and she arrived the way everything in his life had
arrived — by walking into his ground and doing what she wanted there.

He felt her before he knew her: a lightness, then a weight, then a lightness again, moving
across the crown of him in short precise stops. Six points of contact. A body built for
stillness. Something that hunted by not moving, in a place where nothing else was worth the
trouble of not moving.

She was jade-green, longer than his tallest blade, with the flattened prayerful forelegs of
her kind folded under her chest and a head that turned all the way around to look at things.

She looked at him.

That was the first strange thing. Everything that had ever come into his ground had looked
*through* him — the herd, the heron, the hunters, the boy who had cut him for rope. She
looked at him the way a person looks at a chair they are considering sitting in: with
assessment, and then with satisfaction, and then she settled down on the broadest blade of
his crown and folded her legs and stayed.

She stayed for forty-three years.

---

Her mind was not like his, and it took him a decade to learn how to be around it.

She thought in movement. Not in words — she had no words, and neither did he, and for the
first ten years of their acquaintance neither of them had anything that could have been
called a thought in the way a person would mean it. She had patterns: *still, strike, still,
clean, still.* He had patterns: *water, light, hold, grow.* They sat in the same patch of
ground for a decade and did not once communicate anything at all, and it was the best thing
that had ever happened to him.

What he learned from her was that a predator could choose.

The herd had not chosen him. The flood had not chosen him. The man with the bad blade had
not chosen him; he had chosen cordage and there had been grass there. Every single thing
that had ever touched him had touched him by accident, and here at last was a creature
sitting on top of him because she had looked around the whole glade and picked this spot.

She had reasons and he could feel some of them. His crown was the densest cover in the
glade. It was warm — the dark blue blades held the evening heat longer than the meadow
grass, and she liked to be on him at dusk when the moths came. It did not move. And when
the wind came up, the whole patch leaned together and held, and she did not have to hold
herself.

She used him. That was the honest word for it. He was furniture, and cover, and warmth, and
she would have left in a day if any of those had stopped being true.

And she stayed.

In his ninety-second summer a weasel came into the glade and took the digging family's
young out of the far bank, and on the way back it passed within a foot of his crown, and
she came down off him so fast that he felt the blade she had been sitting on go springing
afterwards for an hour.

It was not a fight. It was a thing that took perhaps two seconds. But she came *off* him to
do it, which meant she had been on him, which meant she had been on him when a weasel came,
and it was the first time in his life that anything had spent itself in his vicinity on
purpose.

He kept it. He did not know what he was keeping it for. He kept it the way he kept the
flood and the cutting and the frost, in the deep place where a plant puts what it cannot
use, and it sat there, and years later it would turn out that he had been keeping the first
thing he ever loved.

---

The killing happened in his ninety-eighth year.

They came in the early autumn, two of them, and they were not lost and they were not
hunting for money, and that made them worse.

The father was thin and grey at the temples and had the careful unhurried way of a man who
had done this enough times to be bored by it. The boy was eleven or twelve and had been
dressed for the forest by somebody who had never been in it — new boots, a clean coat, a
knife at his belt with the wrapping still bright on the grip.

The boy talked the whole way in.

"Does it hurt them."

"Yes."

"When they see you."

"They don't see me, they see you." The father said it without unkindness. "You're the one
it'll come at. That's how it works. It comes at you, you hold, I do the rest. Don't drop
the knife."

"What if it comes at you."

"Then we'll both be embarrassed."

They came down to the stream and the father read the bank for a while — the same reading
the broad hunter had done forty years before, the same soft ground, the same conclusion —
and then he pointed at a hollow under the roots of a fallen tree on the far side and said
that a hundred-year badger-line beast had its bed in there, and that it would come out at
dusk, and that the boy was to stand where he was told and not move.

The grass by the water understood none of the words.

It understood the waiting.

---

The beast came out at dusk, and it was smaller than the boy.

That was the thing that stayed with him afterwards, in the deep place, longer than the ring
or the light or the sound: it was *small*. A grey blunt-nosed thing the size of a fat cat,
squinting in the last light, and it came out because it was thirsty and it had come out of
that same hollow every dusk of its hundred years and nothing had ever been standing there
before.

The boy held. That much he did right. He was shaking so hard the knife rattled against his
belt, and he held.

The father did the rest.

It was over in the time it takes to draw a breath twice, and it was not cruel, and it was
not kind, and the grass forty feet away took the whole of it into its roots because the
ground carried it and the ground was him.

Then the father knelt and put his hand on the body, and he told his son to watch, and the
ring came up.

It was small. It was white — a thin pale hoop, barely the width of two hands, and it came up
slowly and wobbled in the air the way a soap ring wobbles, and the boy made a sound that
was not a word.

"Ten years," the father said. "Maybe twelve. It's a good first. Don't be disappointed, a
white first is a proper start, half the men in this province started on white and lived to
be Soul Elders." He nodded at it. "Go on. Hand on it. Breathe."

The boy put out his hand.

It went into him the same way the yellow had gone into the broad hunter — not scattering,
not fading, going *in*, the way water goes into dry ground — and the boy sat down hard in
the grass with his mouth open and his eyes shut and stayed there for the best part of an
hour while his father sat beside him and said nothing and watched the trees.

The grass by the water watched the trees too. Not because it was being careful. Because
there was nothing else to look at, and because something had just happened in its ground
that it had no way whatsoever to respond to.

Twelve years. The thing in the hollow had been alive for a hundred and had come out for a
drink and had been worth twelve to a boy who was frightened of it.

And the men did not leave.

They camped on his bank — the same flat ground, the same fire ring, the same trampled forty
feet — and the boy was different in the morning, quieter, older, sitting up straighter, and
the father made him carry his own pack, and they stayed two nights and went.

They did not cut him for rope this time. Nobody needed rope.

---

He could not have named what he learned that autumn. He had no names for anything. But the
record in him took the evening apart and kept the pieces, and the pieces were these:

*The small one came out for water.*

*The boy was frightened and did it anyway.*

*The years came out of the body and went into the man.*

*They slept on me.*

And under all of it, in the place where the cutting and the flood already were, something
new went down and settled for good, and it did not come back up again in seven hundred
years:

*everything that comes here ends, and I do not.*

---

She was on his crown when they left.

She had been on his crown for most of the two nights, motionless through the whole of it,
and afterwards she came down and cleaned her forelegs the way she always did and settled
back onto the broadest blade and folded herself up.

She was not comforting him. She had no idea anything had happened. She was a predator on a
warm platform in a glade full of moths, and the platform was hers.

But she stayed.

That was the whole of it, and it was enough. She had six more years, and all through those
years she was there — the lightness, the weight, the lightness again — and every dusk when
the moths came she unfolded herself and did the thing she was built to do, and every night
she came back down and folded up on the same blade.

He did not know he loved her. He had no word for it and no self clear enough to have one.

But when she did not wake, one autumn not long afterwards, he waited through the whole of
the winter for her, the way roots wait, and in the thaw he felt for the first time the full
size of the silence where her weight used to be — and the ground where her warmth had
rested stayed strange to his roots for a whole season, because the pattern of one small
life was gone from the place.

That is a long way off. In the ninety-eighth year, on the bank by the stream, a hundred-year
grass with silver veins and a jade-green hunter on its crown stood in the last of the
autumn light while two men packed up their camp, and nothing in the world looked down.

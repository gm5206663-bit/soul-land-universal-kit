# Chapter Fifteen — The One Who Is Named

```
◆ STATUS — Chapter 15: years 753–775
KIND      : Blue Silver Grass, common line. No royal blood, no gold, no domain. A King, and
            nothing more than that.
AGE       : ~775 at the chapter's close
CLASS     : ten-thousand-year class. Stage Three. Rooted in the valley of his line's dead.
MEETING   : A Yin. Of the line's great ones, the one who has been counting.
NAMING    : she names him Home. The first name the Blue Silver line ever gave a wild blade.
KILL COUNT: zero. Permanent. Unbroken across fifteen chapters and seven hundred and
```

---

It was not wind.

He had been reading the valley's grass for eighteen years and he knew what wind did in it
— the whole nine miles went over in long slow waves from the southwestern mouth, and the
waves came from one direction and went to the other and there was no such thing as a patch
of the valley floor moving against the wind.

This moved against the wind. At the eastern edge, in grass that grew up to his own crown's
height, something came toward him across four hundred feet of the line's own ground, and it
did not part the grass, and it did not flatten it, and it did not touch it.

It came through it.

That was the first thing he understood, and it was the first true asymmetry of his life,
and it took him a season to find the shape of it: the valley's grass was the same kind of
thing he was, and he had spent eighteen years rooted in it and holding it and reading its
surface, and he could no more go through it than he could go through the air. Whatever was
coming at him was not moving through the grass. It was moving *with* it — the whole nine
miles of the valley floor answering to it the way the low ground at the stream bend had
answered to him, only completely, only everywhere, only without the four hundred years of
slow root-laying it had taken him to make a hundred feet of ground do anything at all.

She came out of the eastern grass and stood in the open ground at the edge of his crown,
and she was the first person he had ever seen.

---

She was not a person. That is the thing, and it is the whole of the canon of her and it
would be a lie to soften it: the Blue Silver line did not take human form in this era. The
rooted age was not over. The great ones stood in a ring and did not move, and the valley
was nine miles of grass and nothing else, and the shape that came to the edge of his crown
and stood there in the open ground was made of the valley's own growth — vine and blade and
the long silver-veined structure he had spent two centuries developing, woven into the
suggestion of a face, with gold running through it in threads the way water runs through
sand.

Gold.

He had no word for it and no framework for what it meant, and what it meant was that she was
not of his line, or rather that she was of his line and he was not of hers — that the
common blade in the middle band of the Star Dou, the deep-blue variant, the one with no
blood and no history and no name, was standing in the presence of the thing his kind had
been the pale copy of since before the flood.

She looked at him for a long time.

Not with eyes. She had no eyes; she had a face woven in the vines the way a reflection has
a face, and the looking was done through the grass, and the looking went all the way
through him, and the going-through was the second asymmetry and it was worse than the
first.

She could read him. He could not read her.

Every root in his crown was a sense organ and he had spent eight centuries learning to use
them, and he could tell a vole from a weasel by the weight of its step at twenty feet, and
he could read three hundred and ninety-one lines in nine miles of valley soil — and he
could not tell her anything at all. She was in the grass and the grass was hers and the
grass was his and there was no direction in which that was symmetrical.

Then she unfolded her domain, and the valley went still, and she spoke to him in his own
language.

---

Two blows. Deliberate. Spaced.

Through the ground, at the edge of his crown, put there on purpose by something that meant
them — and the spacing was the old one's spacing, the spacing that had been tapped into him
four words at a time over sixty years, four hundred miles away, at the end of a round that
had been walked for an age.

He did not answer. Not because he could not. Because he was, for the second time in his
life, in the presence of something that knew exactly what it was looking at, and the first
time had been a woman in a Spirit Hall issue coat standing in his ground with nine blades on
a cloth, and she had not found him.

She tapped again. Three. The pattern for *come*.

And then — and this was the thing that undid him, the thing that eight centuries of
ground-listening had not prepared him for, the thing that would be the only word he ever
had that was not a tap or a pulse or a stamp —

she stamped.

One blow. Hard. Once.

*I am here. I am answering. I am one of the ones who stays.*

The eleventh word. Invented by a small grey grazer out of its own fear, in the winter of his
four hundred and second year, taught to the fringe young, carried by eighty small voices at
the edge of a woven root-mass four hundred miles away — and she knew it. She knew it the way
she knew the old one's spacing, the way she knew the drum, the way she knew the whole of
the vocabulary that had taken him two hundred and fifty years to build out of four words
and a root-pulse.

She had been listening. Not for a season. For an age.

He pulsed. Every root in a hundred feet of low ground, all at once, the whole crown going
taut and letting go — the answer he had given every dusk for four hundred years, to a
hundred small things, to a dying grazer, to an ancient walker, to nobody at all in the last
eighty years of the empty door.

She took it, and she did not tap again, and the valley went on being still under the
weight of her domain, and after a while she told him what she had come to tell him.

---

She had been counting.

That was the whole of it, and it was the answer to the question the ground had been asking
him for eighteen years, and it had never occurred to him that the question had an answer
that was not in the soil.

The nine great ones of the valley had been standing in a ring since before the deep band
had a seam, and they were rooted, and rooted means immobile, and immobile means that when
the ring began to thin there was nothing any of them could do about it. They could feel it.
They could feel the gap at the western point stay a gap for four hundred years, and the
three at the southern arc go in a single season between them, and the whole age-long
thinning of four hundred down to nine, and every one of them had read it the way he had
read it — as age, as time, as the ordinary arithmetic of enormous lives ending.

She had not.

She was the youngest of the great ones by a margin of forty thousand years, and she was the
only one of them whose domain reached past the valley's boundary, and she was the only one
who had ever felt what the world outside the nine miles was actually like — and she had
felt, in the soil at the valley's edge, in the seasons when the lines went down through the
ground, the signature of what had done it.

She had known for an age.

And she had told no one, because there was no one to tell: eight great ones rooted in a
ring, unable to move, unable to leave, unable to do anything at all except stand in the
middle of a valley that kept itself and watch the ring get smaller, and the telling would
have changed nothing and the knowing had been hers alone for as long as the cull had been
going on.

She had been carrying it.

That was what he understood, standing in the open ground at the edge of his crown with a
hundred feet of low ground around him and a King's cultivation in the well and four words
and a drum of eleven, and the understanding was not a word and not a thought — it was the
same thing that had come up through him when the tapping stopped, when the small grey body
went still in the packed soil, when the great one at the northern point let go of the
valley floor and put itself down into the ground at the end of a natural life.

The recognition of another thing that had been keeping something alone.

---

Her domain drew.

That was its nature and it is written down in the law of her blood rather than in anything
she chose: the Blue Silver Domain takes vitality from the grass it unfolds in, and it had
been unfolded over nine miles of the line's own growth, and the drawing was not cruel and
not deliberate and not stoppable, and everything in the valley that could give was giving.

He refused.

Not by force. He had no force that meant anything against a domain of her blood; the well
was deep and it was a thousand-year King's well and she was an Emperor's daughter standing
in the grass of her own dead. What he did was the thing he had done at the meadow four
hundred miles away, and the thing he had done under the ring three seasons ago, and the
thing he had done in the eleventh year after the liar, in the dark, without malice, without
knowing he was doing it:

he held.

The whole hundred feet of low ground, layered against itself, woven, kept — the same craft
that had stopped the deep band's diggers for two centuries and held a ten-thousand-year
serpent for four hours and closed over a learned liar without once tightening. The domain
drew and the ground did not give, and the not-giving was not resistance, it was simply what
the ground was, and after a while the drawing went around it the way the deep band's
pressure had gone around it, the way the mid-forest hunters had gone around the glade, the
way twelve men with nets had worked within four hundred feet of the best-kept boundary in
the middle band without once looking at it.

She stopped.

Not because he had beaten her. Because she had felt what the not-giving was made of, and
because she had been listening for an age, and because the whole of the last three hundred
and ninety-one lines in the valley's soil had gone down through the ground in single
seasons, in the middle of enormous lives, taken by something that came in and took and left
— and here, at the edge of her own valley, in grass that was common and blue and had no
gold in it anywhere, was a thing that had been offered the whole of it and had not taken a
single year.

She had been counting the dead for an age.

She had never once counted a keeper.

---

She named him at the end of the seven hundred and fifty-third year, and the naming took
the whole winter, and it was not a word.

That is the honest account and it is the only one the serial has ever had for him: she did
not give him a sound. She had no mouth and he had no ears and the whole of the vocabulary
between them was taps and pulses and eleven stamped patterns in packed soil, and what she
gave him was a shape in the ground — a pattern of pressure laid down through the valley
floor at the edge of his crown, in the same register as the three hundred and ninety-one
lines, only soft, only deliberate, only put there on purpose by something that meant it.

And the shape was the shape of a kept place.

Not a grave. Not a line. Not a single season's worth of a great cultivation going back into
the earth because something had come into a valley that nothing could enter and taken it
and left. A *kept* place — the forty feet of densest silver-veined growth between a crown
and an ancient keeper's body, the packed soil over a small grey grazer, the hundred feet of
low ground at a stream bend where the door had been and the seam had gone taut and let go
at dusk for four hundred years.

She laid that shape down at the edge of his crown, and she held it there, and the holding
was the naming, and the name was the oldest word in the drum and the only one of the eleven
that had never been about asking:

**Home.**

The first name the Blue Silver line ever gave a wild blade. Not a title, not a rank, not a
designation in a registry — a name, given by the youngest of an age-culled ring to a common
grass from the middle band of a forest four hundred miles away, for the crime of having
kept a boundary that did not need keeping, for a hundred small things that had been taken
out of it in cages, and for having stood in the soil of three hundred and ninety-one of her
own dead and not taken a single year of any of them.

He had no way to answer. He had four words and a drum and no self clear enough to hold a
name, and the name went down into him anyway, into the place where the mantis was and the
arithmetic and the sixty years of taps and the law that had been written in the dark by a
thing that could not have explained it, and it stayed there for the rest of his long life.

The valley went on. The eight stood in the ring with a gap at the northern point. The deep
band stopped nine miles out and had always stopped.

And four hundred miles away, in a records room with no windows, somebody finally connected
them.

Her name was Berrit Ohn and she was thirty-one and she had been given the southwestern
consolidation because nobody else wanted it — eleven grids, four centuries of
designations, and a directive to reduce the Hall's sealed-territory overhead before the
end of the fiscal year.

"It's just arithmetic," her superior had said. "Sealed grids cost us patrols. Find the ones
that are sealed for no reason and open them."

She had found nine in eleven grids. All of them sealed for no reason. All of them sealed by
a dead walker, or a report nobody had read, or a records man who had walked a boundary
forty-one times with a measuring cord.

Then she got to grid nineteen, and then she got to the southwestern valley, and she put the
two files side by side on a desk in a room with no windows and she sat there for a long
time.

"Ma'am." Her assistant was in the doorway with a lamp. "It's the fourth night."

"I know what night it is."

"You haven't eaten since—"

"The vegetation note in grid nineteen is four hundred and fifty years old." Berrit did not
look up. "It says the grass is blue-silver, it says the grass is tough, and it says *worth a
look*. Nobody writes worth a look about grass." She turned the second file around. "The
valley report says the floor is continuous vegetation, nine miles, will not come out of the
soil. And it says nine plant-type signatures at the centre in excess of sixty thousand
years each."

"Those are four hundred miles apart."

"They are." She put her finger on the third file — the eleven pages, the personal one, the
one the nephew had sold. "And this man spent nine weeks in grid nineteen and walked a dead
animal's round forty-one times and wrote that the boundary is maintained daily by the
vegetation, and he asked for a specialist team, and the specialist team came, and the seal
stayed on, and then the grid was opened for hunting anyway because of a budget shortfall,
and then it was marked exhausted." She looked up. "Do you know what's in a grid that gets
marked exhausted?"

"Hunted-out low-class stock."

"Right. And do you know what the valley floor is." Her assistant did not answer. "Neither
does anybody. But it's the same kind of thing, four hundred miles away, and it's nine miles
of it, and it has nine sixty-thousand-year signatures standing in a ring in the middle of
it." She closed the file. "That's not two grids. That's a line. And something has been
killing it."

"Ma'am — that's—"

"That's what the files say." Berrit stood up. "Four hundred and fifty years of a note that
says worth a look, a hundred and fifty years of a seal on a dead walker, a records man who
asked for a team and got a joke, a Soul Ancestor who threw a commission file into a fire
two hundred miles out, and a valley nobody can enter with nine things in it that my
instrument case cannot read." She picked up all three files. "I'm filing a consolidated
designation. Both grids. Permanent seal, highest class, and a standing request for an
Emperor-level assessment."

"They'll laugh at you."

"They laughed at Vase." She walked to the door. "And they opened his grid for hunting
anyway, and it came back exhausted in eleven weeks, and nobody ever asked why a grid with a
hundred-foot boundary that wouldn't come out of the soil went empty that fast." She stopped
in the doorway. "Something took forty-one small things out of grid nineteen in eleven weeks
and then stopped, and I want to know what it was doing before that, and I want to know what
it went on to do after."

She filed it. The consolidated designation went into the registry in the spring, and it sat
there, and the Emperor-level assessment was not funded that year, or the next, or the next
fourteen.

But the seal was permanent, and the seal was highest class, and the seal was in Berrit
Ohn's name, and in forty-seven years, when a young man from a fallen clan came
out of the Star Dou Forest with a spider on his wrist and the whole continent started
looking for the Blue Silver line, the first file anybody pulled was hers.

And at the edge of the crown, in the blue-silver grass, a common blade of the line that had
no gold in it and no blood and no history stood rooted in the ground of its own dead with a
hundred feet of low ground around it, and it was named, and it kept.

It would keep for another three hundred and forty years.

It would never once kill.

# Chapter 2: The Path Forward

## Canon Reference: Novel Chapters 2-8 — Early Training, Na'er Introduction, Forging Apprenticeship
## Timeline: Days to months after Awakening Day, Age 6

---

The days following the awakening ceremony were difficult for Tang Wulin.

At Red Mountain Academy, the children had been divided into different classes based on their martial souls and innate soul power. Those with powerful martial souls and high soul power were placed in the elite classes, while those with weak martial souls or no soul power at all were relegated to the ordinary classes.

Wulin, with his Blue Silver Grass and level 3 innate soul power, had been placed in the lowest class.

The teacher, a middle-aged man with a perpetually bored expression, had barely glanced at Wulin during the meditation instruction. While other students received detailed guidance on how to circulate their soul power and nourish their martial souls, Wulin was given only the most basic instructions.

"Blue Silver Grass," the teacher had muttered, barely concealing his disdain. "Just try to meditate and see if anything happens."

The other children in the class weren't much better. Several had no soul power at all and would never become Soul Masters. Others had weak tool-type martial souls — hoes, shovels, basic knives — that would only help them in mundane labor.

Wulin sat alone during lunch, eating far more than any other child. His appetite had increased dramatically since the awakening, as if his body was desperately trying to compensate for his weak martial soul. He ate enough to fill half a dozen other students, earning him the nickname "Rice Bucket" from his classmates.

But Wulin didn't care about the nickname. He cared about the whispers.

"Did you hear? That kid with the pretty eyes? Blue Silver Grass. Level 3 soul power. What a waste."

"I heard his friend got a sword martial soul with level 5 soul power. Must be nice."

"Blue Silver Grass is trash. He'll never be a real Soul Master."

Wulin clenched his fists under the table, his knuckles turning white. The words stung, cutting deeper than any physical wound.

*Is my martial soul really that bad?* he wondered, his depressed expression gradually changing into one of stubbornness. *Even if my martial soul is no good, I'll definitely become an important Soul Master. Dad told me before, success is 99% effort and 1% talent. Since my martial soul isn't any good, then I'll just have to put in even more effort!*

With his optimistic nature, he had already overcome the depression of the day while inadvertently resolving himself.

After school, Wulin met up with Lin Hao outside the academy gates. His friend was waiting for him, leaning against a soul-powered street lamp with his arms crossed.

"How was your first day?" Lin Hao asked, pushing off the lamp and falling into step beside Wulin.

Wulin shrugged. "Fine. The teacher didn't pay much attention to me, but I learned the basics of meditation."

Lin Hao's amber eyes narrowed. "Did they treat you badly because of your martial soul?"

Wulin hesitated, then nodded. "Some of the kids made fun of me. Called Blue Silver Grass trash."

Lin Hao's expression darkened, but he kept his voice calm. "What did you do?"

"I ignored them," Wulin said, lifting his chin. "I'm going to work harder than anyone else. I'll show them."

A proud smile spread across Lin Hao's handsome face. "That's the spirit. And I'll help you. We'll train together every day after school. I'll push you, and you'll push me."

Wulin's eyes lit up. "Really? You'd do that?"

"Of course," Lin Hao said, punching him lightly on the shoulder. "What are friends for? Besides, I need someone to train with too. My Adaptation Talent works better when I'm challenged."

---

That evening, the two boys met in a small clearing behind the Lin family's house. The sun was setting, casting long shadows across the grass.

"Okay," Lin Hao said, sitting cross-legged. "Let's start with meditation. The teacher showed us how to sense our soul power and circulate it through our bodies, right?"

Wulin nodded and sat down across from him. "Yeah. You're supposed to focus on your dantian — that's where your soul power gathers — and then guide it through your meridians."

"Right. Let's try it."

Both boys closed their eyes and began to meditate.

For Wulin, it was like trying to grasp smoke. His soul power was weak and elusive, slipping away every time he tried to focus on it. He could sense it there, a faint warmth in his abdomen, but it refused to move when he commanded it.

*Come on,* he thought, frustration building. *Move. Just move a little.*

After ten minutes of concentrated effort, he managed to coax a tiny trickle of soul power to flow through one meridian before it dissipated.

He opened his eyes with a sigh. "This is hard."

Beside him, Lin Hao was still meditating, his expression serene. But inside, his Adaptation Talent was working furiously.

He could feel the inefficiency immediately. The standard meditation technique was wasting energy — nearly half of what he gathered was being lost as it flowed through his meridians. His body was different from the textbook template, his meridians slightly wider in some places, narrower in others.

Automatically, his Adaptation Talent began rerouting the flow, finding secondary pathways that reduced the energy loss. The circulation pattern shifted, becoming smoother, more natural to his unique physiology. Within minutes, he'd optimized it by nearly a quarter.

Lin Hao could feel the difference immediately. His soul power flowed more smoothly now, gathering in his dantian with less resistance. What would take other children an hour of meditation, he could accomplish in forty minutes.

He opened his eyes and glanced at Wulin, who was struggling with his own meditation.

"Having trouble?" Lin Hao asked gently.

Wulin nodded, frustration evident on his pretty face. "I can barely get my soul power to move. It's like trying to push water through a straw."

Lin Hao thought for a moment. "Let me try something. When you meditate, don't force your soul power. Just... observe it. Feel where it wants to go naturally."

Wulin frowned. "But the teacher said we have to guide it through specific meridians."

"I know," Lin Hao said. "But everyone's body is different. Maybe the standard path isn't the best path for you. Try this: just sit quietly and feel your soul power. Don't try to move it. Just... listen to it."

Wulin looked skeptical, but he closed his eyes and tried again.

This time, instead of forcing his soul power to move, he simply observed it. He felt the faint warmth in his dantian, the way it pulsed gently with each heartbeat. And then, slowly, he noticed something.

The soul power wasn't stagnant. It was moving, just barely, in a pattern he hadn't noticed before. It flowed in a slow spiral, circling his dantian like water around a drain.

*That's it,* he realized with excitement. *That's the natural flow.*

Instead of forcing it into the standard meridian path, he gently encouraged it to continue its spiral, widening it slightly with each rotation.

The effect was immediate. His soul power began to circulate more freely, flowing through his meridians with less resistance than before.

"It's working!" Wulin exclaimed, opening his eyes with a grin. "Hao, it's actually working!"

Lin Hao smiled. "Told you. Sometimes you just have to find your own path."

---

---

The next few weeks fell into a routine. Every day after school, Wulin and Lin Hao would meet to train. They meditated together, practiced martial soul manifestation, and ran physical exercises to build their bodies.

But Lin Hao's training didn't stop there.

One evening, after their joint training session, Lin Wei called his son into the family's small forge behind their house. The forge was modest compared to Mang Tian's workshop, but it was well-equipped and filled with the tools of the blacksmith trade.

"Hao," Lin Wei said, handing his son a small hammer. "You're six now. It's time you learned the family trade."

Lin Hao's eyes lit up. He'd watched his father work the forge countless times, mesmerized by the way metal yielded to skill and heat. "Really? You'll teach me?"

Lin Wei smiled. "Of course. Every blacksmith's son should know how to shape metal. Besides," he added with a knowing look, "your martial soul is a sword. Understanding how blades are forged will help you wield one."

For the next hour, Lin Wei taught his son the basics — how to read the color of heated metal, how to strike with controlled force, how to shape iron without weakening it. Lin Hao's Adaptation Talent went to work immediately, analyzing his father's techniques and optimizing his own movements.

*The angle of the hammer strike matters more than the force,* he realized as he worked. *Father adjusts his grip slightly with each blow, compensating for the metal's resistance. I can do that too.*

By the end of the session, Lin Hao had forged a simple iron nail — crude compared to his father's work, but solid and well-shaped for a first attempt.

Lin Wei examined it with a critical eye, then nodded approvingly. "Not bad for a beginner. You have good instincts, son. With practice, you'll be a fine blacksmith."

Lin Hao beamed with pride. This was something he could share with his father, a skill that connected him to his family's legacy.

A few days later, Lin Wei took his son to meet an old friend — a retired sword master named Chen Long who lived on the outskirts of Glorybound City. Chen Long was in his sixties, with graying hair and sharp eyes that missed nothing. He'd been a Spirit Ancestor in his prime, with a sword martial soul similar to Lin Hao's.

"This is the boy you told me about?" Chen Long asked, studying Lin Hao with interest.

Lin Wei nodded. "He awakened a sword martial soul — Stormbringer Sword. Wind and lightning attributes. I've never seen anything like it."

Chen Long's eyebrows rose. "Stormbringer Sword? That's... unusual. Let me see it, boy."

Lin Hao summoned his martial soul. The silver-blue sword materialized in his grip, wind swirling around the blade, tiny sparks of lightning dancing along its edge.

Chen Long's eyes widened. He circled Lin Hao slowly, examining the sword from every angle. "Remarkable," he murmured. "This is no ordinary sword martial soul. The fusion of wind and lightning, the quality of the manifestation... this is a high-level martial soul, far beyond what one would expect from a commoner family."

He looked at Lin Wei. "You were right to bring him to me. A martial soul like this deserves proper training, not just basic forms."

Lin Wei bowed deeply. "I'm just a blacksmith, Master Chen. I can teach him the craft, but I don't know the first thing about swordsmanship. Will you take him as your student?"

Chen Long studied Lin Hao for a long moment, then smiled. "I'll teach him. But I warn you, boy — I'm a strict teacher. I'll push you hard. Are you willing to work?"

Lin Hao met his gaze without flinching. "Yes, Master Chen. I'm willing."

"Good." Chen Long nodded. "Come back tomorrow at dawn. We'll begin with the basics — stance, grip, footwork. Once you master those, we'll move on to actual sword forms."

Over the next few months, Lin Hao's schedule became even more demanding than Wulin's. Every morning before school, he trained swordsmanship with Chen Long. Every evening after school, he trained with Wulin, then practiced blacksmithing with his father.

His Adaptation Talent thrived under the pressure. His swordsmanship improved rapidly — Chen Long was amazed at how quickly he mastered complex forms, how naturally his body adapted to the movements. His blacksmithing skills grew as well, his strikes becoming more precise, his understanding of metal deepening.

"You're a fast learner," Chen Long told him one day, after Lin Hao executed a particularly difficult sword form flawlessly. "Faster than any student I've had in forty years of teaching. Your Adaptation Talent is remarkable."

Lin Hao smiled modestly. "I just practice a lot, Master Chen."

Chen Long chuckled. "Modest too. That's rare in someone with your talent. Keep working hard, boy. You have the potential to become a true sword master someday."

---

One afternoon, as they walked home from school, Wulin's stomach growled loudly.

"I'm starving," he complained, rubbing his belly. "I ate three bowls of rice at lunch, but I'm already hungry again."

Lin Hao laughed. "You've been eating a lot more since the awakening. Maybe your body is trying to compensate for all the energy you're using during cultivation."

"Maybe," Wulin said. "But it's embarrassing. The other kids call me Rice Bucket."

"Let them talk," Lin Hao said. "You're working harder than any of them. That's what matters."

As they walked down the main street, something caught Wulin's attention — a flash of silver in the afternoon sunlight.

He stopped walking and looked toward the roadside. A little girl was squatting there, slightly shorter than him, with a head of short silver hair that gleamed in the light.

She wore shabby clothes and had a dirt-stained face, clearly resembling a little beggar. But what caught Wulin's attention were her eyes — big, crystal clear amethyst pupils that seemed to glow with an inner light.

It was as if there was some sort of profound attraction between them. The young girl raised her head and returned his gaze, her pretty eyes somewhat bewildered.

Wulin had unconsciously stopped walking. Two pairs of eyes peered into each other across the distance.

"Little girl, where are your parents?" a voice called out.

Wulin's attention snapped to the source of the voice. Several young delinquents — teenagers, maybe fourteen or fifteen — had surrounded the girl, attracted by her silver hair.

The little girl avoided their eyes, once again lowering her head.

The delinquents shot looks to one another before one of them called out, "Silver hair is really rare! Could you be from one of the other two continents? I reckon the black market dealers will really like her silver hair and purple eyes."

Greed colored their eyes as they nodded towards each other.

Lin Hao's expression darkened. "Wulin," he said quietly. "Something's wrong."

"I know," Wulin said, his voice tight with anger. "They're going to hurt her."

The youngster who had spoken squatted down. "Hey, little girl. Where's your family?"

Without speaking, she shook her lowered head.

Beaming with smiles, the youngster said, "Are you hungry? Big brother will bring you to eat some good things, how about it?"

The little girl shook her head once again, but this time, she shook it much more forcefully.

The youngster shot a meaningful look to his companions before reaching out his hand to pull the little girl's arm, lifting her up against her will. His companions encircled them, obstructing others from seeing this.

Even as the little girl let out a frightened scream, the youngster already had her over his shoulders.

"What are you doing?" Wulin called out, his voice full of fury.

The delinquents turned to look, revealing resentful faces. The person who had come to aid the girl was unexpectedly a tiny pretty boy who didn't even reach their waists.

Lin Hao stepped forward beside Wulin, his amber eyes cold. "Put her down," he said, his voice calm but carrying an edge.

The youngster in the back revealed a trace of cruelty. He raised one of his legs and kicked towards Wulin. "Brat, you dare to meddle with us."

But before the kick could connect, Lin Hao was there. His Adaptation Talent had analyzed the attack instantly — trajectory, speed, power — and he'd moved to intercept it. He caught the teenager's leg mid-kick and twisted, sending the boy stumbling backward with a yelp of pain.

"You guys are villains!" Wulin shouted, charging forward to bar their way.

The youngster who was carrying the little girl exposed a vicious face. A cold light flickered from his wrist and a dagger appeared in his hand. He gestured towards the boys, "F*ck off if you don't want to die!"

Wulin stubbornly glared and furiously replied, "Villains won't have a good end. I'm a Soul Master; I'm not afraid of you. Release her!"

As he spoke, Wulin lifted his right hand, and a light blue ring of light twinkled. Blue Silver Grass appeared in his palm, waving gently. A faint energy undulated outwards from it.

What could rank 3 soul power do? It only made him a bit more powerful than his peers. He didn't have a soul ring to support his soul. His soul was far from being battle capable.

But it was enough to make the delinquents pause.

Lin Hao stepped forward, his Stormbringer Sword materializing in his grip. Wind swirled around the blade, and tiny sparks of lightning danced along its edge. "I'm also a Soul Master," he said coldly. "Rank 5 Soul Scholar. Do you really want to fight us?"

The delinquents hesitated. If it was only regular boys, they wouldn't have to worry. But children who possessed soul power were different — the government maintained special records on them, and the Spirit Pagoda kept tabs as well. If anything happened to these special children, the federal government would definitely expend a lot of resources to search for the cause.

"Such bad luck!" the leader muttered, unresigned. But he set down the little girl with an angry growl and quickly left with his companions.

The little girl stumbled and fell onto her butt. Seeing this, Wulin hurriedly ran over and crouched beside her. "Don't be scared. I'm a man. I'll protect you!"

The little girl raised her head and looked at him with those big, amethyst eyes. She didn't speak, but there was gratitude in her gaze.

Wulin smiled gently. "What's your name?"

The girl opened her mouth, but no sound came out. She shook her head, looking confused.

"You don't remember?" Wulin asked softly.

She shook her head again.

Lin Hao knelt beside them, dismissing his sword. "She might be amnesiac. Lost her memories somehow."

Wulin's expression softened with sympathy. "Where are you from? Do you have family?"

The girl just stared at him, her purple eyes wide and bewildered.

"She's hungry," Lin Hao observed, noting her thin frame. "And she's been living on the streets for a while, by the looks of it."

Wulin made a decision. "Come with us," he said, offering her his hand. "We'll take you home. My mom will make you something to eat, and we'll figure out what to do."

The girl hesitated for a moment, then slowly reached out and took his hand.

---

When they arrived at the Tang family home, Lang Yue — Wulin's adoptive mother — was preparing dinner. She looked up in surprise as the boys entered with the dirty, silver-haired girl.

"Wulin, who is this?" she asked, setting down her cooking spoon.

"We found her on the street," Wulin explained. "Some delinquents were trying to kidnap her. She doesn't remember her name or where she's from."

Lang Yue's expression softened with sympathy. She knelt down and gently wiped the dirt from the girl's face with a damp cloth. Underneath the grime, the girl's features were delicate and beautiful, her silver hair gleaming like moonlight.

"Poor child," Lang Yue murmured. "You must be starving. Come, sit down. I'll make you something to eat."

The girl didn't speak, but she allowed Lang Yue to lead her to the table. She ate ravenously when the food was placed in front of her, as if she hadn't eaten in days.

Tang Ziran came home from work and was told the story. He looked at the girl thoughtfully. "We can't just keep her. We should report this to the authorities. Maybe someone is looking for her."

"But what if no one comes?" Wulin asked. "What if she has no family?"

Tang Ziran sighed. "Then she'll go to an orphanage."

Wulin's face fell. He looked at the girl, who was quietly eating her food, her amethyst eyes downcast. She looked so small and vulnerable.

"Dad," Wulin said firmly. "Can she stay with us? Just until we find her family. Please?"

Tang Ziran exchanged a look with his wife. They were already struggling financially — feeding Wulin's enormous appetite was difficult enough. Adding another mouth to feed would be a burden.

But looking at the girl's thin frame, at Wulin's determined expression, at the way the girl clung to Wulin's sleeve as if he were her only anchor in the world...

"Alright," Tang Ziran said finally. "She can stay. But we'll still report her to the authorities. If her family comes looking for her, we'll return her."

Wulin's face lit up with joy. "Thank you, Dad!"

He turned to the girl and grinned. "You can stay with us! We'll be like brother and sister."

The girl looked up at him, and for the first time, a small smile tugged at her lips.

"Na'er," she said softly.

"What?" Wulin asked.

"Na'er," she repeated, pointing to herself. "My name... Na'er."

Wulin's smile widened. "Na'er. That's a pretty name. I'm Wulin. This is my mom and dad. And this is my friend Lin Hao."

Na'er looked at each of them in turn, her amethyst eyes lingering on Wulin the longest. There was something familiar about him, something that made her feel safe.

"Brother... Wulin," she said hesitantly.

Wulin's heart melted. "Yeah. I'm your brother now. I'll protect you, Na'er. I promise."

---

A few days later, Tang Ziran took Wulin to meet Mang Tian, the blacksmith who had agreed to consider taking Wulin as an apprentice.

Mang Tian's workshop was located on the outskirts of Glorybound City, a large building with smoke billowing from its chimney. The sound of hammering echoed from within.

Mang Tian himself was a broad-shouldered man with blue hair and blue eyes, unusual features that marked him as someone with a beast-type martial soul. He was a 6-Star Rank Blacksmith — one of the best in the city — and a Spirit Ancestor with four soul rings.

"So this is the boy you want me to train," Mang Tian said, looking Wulin up and down with critical eyes. "Tang Ziran, you know I don't take just anyone as an apprentice. He has to pass my test first."

Tang Ziran nodded. "I understand. Wulin is hardworking. He'll do his best."

Mang Tian grunted. "We'll see. Boy, come with me."

He led Wulin into the workshop, where a forge burned hot and anvils stood ready. The air was thick with the smell of metal and smoke.

"I'm going to give you a simple test," Mang Tian said, picking up a heavy iron hammer. "I want you to hit this piece of metal as many times as you can in two hours. Don't worry about technique or precision. Just hit it as hard as you can."

He placed a chunk of raw iron on the anvil and handed Wulin the hammer. It was heavy — far too heavy for a six-year-old child to lift comfortably.

Wulin gripped the hammer with both hands, his muscles straining. He lifted it and brought it down on the metal with a clang.

The impact jarred his arms all the way to his shoulders. Pain shot through his muscles, but he gritted his teeth and lifted the hammer again.

Clang. Clang. Clang.

Each strike sent shockwaves through his body. His arms burned with exertion, his hands blistered and bled. But Wulin didn't stop. He kept hitting the metal, again and again, his determination outweighing his pain.

Mang Tian watched silently, his expression unreadable.

After the first hour, Wulin's arms were shaking so badly he could barely lift the hammer. His hands were raw and bleeding, his clothes soaked with sweat. But he kept going.

*Success is 99% effort,* he reminded himself. *I have to keep going. For Na'er. For my family. For my dream.*

By the end of the second hour, Wulin could barely stand. His arms hung limply at his sides, his hands were a mess of blisters and blood. But he had not stopped. Not once.

"Enough," Mang Tian said.

Wulin dropped the hammer with a clatter and collapsed to his knees, gasping for breath.

Mang Tian walked over and examined Wulin's hands. He clicked his tongue. "You're injured. Your mother will worry."

He reached into a drawer and pulled out a small jar of ointment. "Apply this to your hands. It'll help them heal."

Wulin took the jar with trembling hands. "Did I... did I pass?"

Mang Tian looked at him for a long moment. Then, slowly, he smiled. "You didn't just pass, boy. You exceeded my expectations. Most children would have given up after the first ten minutes. You kept going for the full two hours, despite the pain."

He placed a hand on Wulin's shoulder. "You have the heart of a blacksmith. I'll take you as my apprentice. Come back tomorrow, and we'll begin your real training."

Wulin's face lit up with joy, despite his exhaustion. "Thank you, Master Mang Tian!"

"Don't thank me yet," Mang Tian said with a chuckle. "The real work is just beginning."

---

Over the next few months, Wulin fell into a demanding routine. Every day after school, he would go to Mang Tian's workshop and train in the art of blacksmithing. His hands grew calloused and strong, his arms developed muscle, and his technique improved steadily.

Mang Tian was a strict teacher, but he was also fair. He recognized Wulin's natural talent for forging and pushed him to develop it.

"You have good instincts," Mang Tian told him one day. "Your strikes are becoming more precise, more controlled. With enough practice, you could become a fine blacksmith."

Wulin beamed with pride. He was earning money now — not much, but enough to help his family and save for his future spirit soul.

---

Lin Hao, meanwhile, had his own demanding training schedule.

Every evening after school, he would return home to learn blacksmithing from his father, Lin Wei. The family forge was small but well-equipped, and Lin Wei was a patient teacher.

"Watch the color of the metal," Lin Wei instructed one evening, demonstrating how to heat iron to the perfect temperature. "When it turns cherry red, it's ready to shape. Too cool, and it won't bend. Too hot, and it becomes brittle."

Lin Hao nodded, his Adaptation Talent absorbing every detail. He could see the subtle shifts in the metal's color, feel the heat radiating from the forge, understand the precise moment when the iron was ready to work.

When he tried it himself, his strikes were awkward at first. But his Adaptation Talent quickly corrected his technique, optimizing his grip, his stance, the angle of each blow. Within a week, he was shaping simple iron nails with surprising precision.

"You learn fast," Lin Wei said, impressed. "Faster than I did at your age."

Lin Hao shrugged modestly. "I just pay attention."

But Lin Wei had noticed something else about his son. The Stormbringer Sword martial soul was extraordinary — far beyond a simple Iron Sword. It deserved proper training, not just basic forms.

So he arranged for Lin Hao to study swordsmanship with Master Chen Long, a retired Spirit Ancestor who lived on the outskirts of the city. Master Chen Long had been a renowned swordsman in his prime, and though he was now in his sixties, his skills remained sharp.

"You have a rare martial soul," Master Chen Long said during their first lesson, examining Lin Hao's Stormbringer Sword with keen interest. "Wind and lightning attributes, fused perfectly. I've never seen anything quite like it."

He began teaching Lin Hao the fundamentals — stance, grip, footwork, basic cuts and thrusts. Most students took months to master these basics, but Lin Hao's Adaptation Talent accelerated his learning dramatically.

Within days, his footwork was precise and balanced. Within weeks, his cuts were clean and controlled. Master Chen Long watched with growing amazement as the boy absorbed techniques that would take normal students years to learn.

"You're a prodigy," Master Chen Long told him one afternoon, shaking his head in wonder. "I've taught dozens of students over the years, but none have progressed as quickly as you. Your martial soul is special, but it's more than that. You have an instinct for the sword that can't be taught."

Lin Hao smiled modestly. "I just practice a lot, Master Chen."

But both he and Master Chen Long knew it was more than that. The Adaptation Talent was constantly refining his technique, correcting his form, optimizing his movements. Every practice session made him better, faster, more precise.

---

Meanwhile, he and Lin Hao continued their daily training sessions. Both boys were progressing steadily in their cultivation.

Lin Hao's Adaptation Talent had optimized his meditation technique to an incredible degree. His soul power grew faster than normal — faster than any child in the elite class — gathering with every evening's session like water finding a wider channel.

*I'll reach rank 10 years before most children do,* he realized one evening during meditation. *And then I can absorb my first spirit soul and become a true Soul Master.*

Wulin was progressing more slowly, but he was still improving. His unconventional spiral meditation technique worked well for him, and his soul power was steadily increasing.

Na'er had become an integral part of the Tang family. She was quiet and gentle, always clinging to Wulin's side. She didn't speak much, but when she did, it was usually to call Wulin "brother" or to ask him questions about the world.

She seemed to have no memories of her past, no knowledge of where she came from or who her family was. The authorities had been unable to find any records of a missing silver-haired girl, and no one had come looking for her.

So she stayed with the Tang family, and Wulin treated her like a true sister.

One evening, as the three children sat together watching the sunset, Wulin made a promise.

"I'm going to become a strong Soul Master," he said, his voice filled with determination. "Strong enough to protect both of you. And I'm going to find out who you are, Na'er. I promise."

Na'er looked up at him with her amethyst eyes, and smiled. "I believe in you, Brother Wulin."

Lin Hao grinned. "And I'll be right there with you. We'll become Soul Masters together, and we'll take on the world."

The sun dipped below the horizon, casting the sky in shades of orange and purple. Three children sat together, their futures uncertain but their bonds strong.

They didn't know what challenges lay ahead.

But they would face them together.

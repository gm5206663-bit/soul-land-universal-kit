## Part 1: The Weekly Break

The Intermediate Academy gave one day a week to its students — a single unharnessed Saturday, which the first grade spent in the ways first grades do: sleeping, eating, fighting, and, in the case of two of class five's four, disappearing before breakfast toward the harbor road and the Blacksmith's Association.

They walked together, the way they had walked together since before either could remember — Wulin on the left because he had always been on the left, Lin Hao on the right because that was where he had fallen in step the first morning of the first week of the first year of their lives, when they were four and Glorybound's lanes were wide as oceans. Seven years of the same left and right. The forge boys of the Association had stopped asking which of them was the master; the answer had always been *neither, and both.*

Mu Chen had given each of them a key in the third week — not one key to share, but two, to two closet-forges on the same short corridor, because when he had tried to give the boys one forge they had both, without conferring, politely declined until there were two. "The Association pays for results, not for audiences," Mu Chen had said, handing over the second key with something that, on him, was nearly a smile. "Forge when your class schedule allows. Submit on Fridays. The metal does not care which day you respect it."

So the Saturdays belonged to the hammers. Two sets of them, in two closets, on the same short corridor.

Lin Hao's were the older trade. Not the blood-sacrificed Thousand Refined pair Mang Tian had folded for Wulin in Glorybound — those were Wulin's, drunk from Wulin's blood — but the set his father Lin Wei had put in his hands at six, the year the Stormbringer woke: a matched pair of heavy forging hammers, plain steel, no ceremony, the handles worn to the exact shape of a growing boy's grip because a blacksmith's son does not get new handles, he gets *the same ones* until his hands outgrow them and his father silently makes the next size. Lin Wei had made him four sizes in four years. The fifth was waiting at home, and Lin Hao's hands had not yet earned it.

He was working his own commission. Not a mecha housing — a **blade.** A commissioned short-sword blank, second-rank steel, for a Federation guardsman who had asked, through the board, for an edge that would not chip on the third strike. It was, on paper, a journeyman's task. Lin Hao was ten, and had been at the anvil — really at it, in his father's Glorybound forge, not pretending — since he was six.

He lit the coke. He set the steel in. And while the steel came up to color, he did the thing the Association's older smiths had started making room for and the younger ones had started watching: he *read* the metal before he ever lifted a hammer.

This was the part his father had never been able to teach, because Lin Wei could not do it and had been honest about not doing it. The hawk-eyes — the same eyes that read wind in a fight — read *grain* in steel: the thousand-year weather of compression and direction inside the bar, the places where the metal wanted to move and the places where it would fight. Most smiths found those places by striking and listening to where the ring went wrong. Lin Hao found them before the first strike, the way you see a stair before you climb it, and he struck only where the metal had already said *yes.*

The hammer came down. The steel, which would have fought a journeyman for every inch, opened for him like a door held by a friend.

Strike. Turn. Strike. The forge filled with the particular rhythm — not loud, because loud is waste; every ring that escaped into the corridor was a ring that had not gone into the steel, and Lin Hao's work was quiet the way deep water is quiet. His Adaptation Talent ran under it all, the way it ran under everything: each strike, it took the feel of the grain and *optimized the next angle,* so the hammer fell a fraction truer, a fraction lighter, a fraction closer to the place the metal had already agreed to. A journeyman would have needed three hundred strikes to bring that blank to edge. Lin Hao's count, though he did not count, was running near two hundred.

He was four hundred strikes into the folding — the blank's third turn — when he became aware that his corridor was not empty.

Mu Chen stood at the threshold of Lin Hao's closet. Not behind him this time, not across a public floor: in his doorway, arms folded, watching the work with the stillness of a man recalculating something he had already written down. He had been there, by the look of the coke-bed, for some minutes.

"You fold at a lower heat than the manual allows," Mu Chen said. It was not a criticism; it was the flat register he used when he was writing something down. "Most steel goes brittle there."

"This steel doesn't," Lin Hao said, and did not stop the hammer. "It's got a sulphur pocket near the tang that the board spec didn't catch. Fold it hot and the pocket opens and the edge chips on the third strike — which is what the guardsman complained about on the last one, isn't it. Fold it cool and the pocket stays shut." He turned the blank. "The metal told me. I just listened."

Mu Chen was quiet for a long moment. Then, from two closets down, a sound came up the short corridor — a low, long note, like a held breath let go, the sound of Wulin's four-hundredth strike finding the argument's end on his own piece. Lin Hao's hammer did not falter at it. He had heard that note every Saturday for two months. It was his brother's voice in a language Lin Hao had spoken since the womb, and it did not distract him any more than his own heartbeat did.

"Your father taught you the folding," Mu Chen said.

"My father taught me the folding. The metal taught me the *heat.*" Lin Hao set the blank down to cool and, for the first time, looked at the President. "Lin Wei can't read grain. He's honest about it. He folds by count and prayer and forty years of hands, and he makes honest steel. I—" he shrugged, the shrug of a boy who had been told since he was six that he was different and had never once thought it made him better, only *otherwise* — "I read it. That's all. It's not deeper than his work. It's just a different door into the same room."

Mu Chen looked at him for a long moment. "Your father," he said at last, "is a third-rank smith who folds by count and prayer. You are ten, and you found a sulphur pocket the board's own assay missed, and you are folding around it at a heat that should shatter the bar." He unfolded his arms. "That is not a different door into the same room, boy. That is a different room. But—" and here, for the second time in two months, something crossed the flat register that was almost warmth, "—you carry yourself like it's the same room. I have decided I like that."

He turned to go, and stopped at the corridor's end where Wulin had appeared, wiping his hands, the four-hundredth-strike note still hanging in the air behind him. The two boys fell into step — Wulin on the left, Lin Hao on the right, the way they had since they were four — and Mu Chen watched them walk the short corridor together and thought, though he would not have said it aloud, that he had been given two of them, and that the Association's ledger had no column for what he was actually being handed.

"Both of you," he called after them. "Fridays. Submit together. And Lin Hao." The boys turned. "The guardsman's edge. When it's done — bring it to me before you bring it to him. I want to strike it myself." He paused. "I want to hear if it chips."

"It won't," Lin Hao said.

"I know," Mu Chen said. "I want to hear it *not* chip." And he went back into his office, and closed the door, and sat down, and did not write anything down for a while.

## Part 1-B: The Edge (Skill Shown)

The blank became an edge over the following three Saturdays, and on the fourth, Lin Hao took it to the Association's testing yard to learn what he had made — because a smith who does not test his own edge is a smith who is guessing, and Lin Wei had never once let him guess.

The yard's test-post was a laminated hardwood block banded in iron — the standard the guardsman's complaint had been measured against: a second-rank edge was supposed to bite two inches and not chip on the third strike. Lin Hao set his stance the way Chen Long had drilled it — weight low, the blade an extension of the forearm, the wind already leaning at his shoulder the way it always leaned when he held the Stormbringer's cousin-steel — and he did not simply chop.

He *used* the soul.

The Stormbringer was not in his hand — this was mortal steel, a commission, not his soul — but the hawk was in him, and the wind answered the steel the way it answered everything he held. As the edge came down he let a thread of soul power run the blade's length and *asked* the air to lean into the cut, and the wind — the Gale Talon's wind, the same compressed breath he used to rend at range — folded along the edge in a ribbon of moving pressure that turned a good chop into a *slicing* one. It was not the full Gale Talon; it was the Talon's principle laid along an ordinary blade, the way a swordsman lays intent along a cut.

The edge took the post.

It bit not two inches but four, and the laminated wood parted like wet paper along a line so clean the grain did not even splinter, and the iron band — the iron band that had stopped two prior guardsman-edges and chipped both — *opened,* silently, without a chip on the steel, because the wind had done the work the steel alone would have broken itself against.

Lin Hao stood, and let the blade come down to his side, and listened to the post settle.

Behind him, the yard had gone quiet in the way yards do when a ten-year-old has just done something to iron that iron is not supposed to allow. Three Association smiths stood at the yard gate. One of them, an old fourth-rank with forty years on the anvil, walked over without a word, took the edge from Lin Hao's hand with the care you'd give a sleeping thing, ran a thumb along it, and looked at the cut in the iron band for a long, long time.

"Boy," the old smith said. "Who folded this."

"I did," Lin Hao said.

"No." The old man turned the edge to the light. "Who *read* it. This steel's been folded around its own faults. I've made steel forty years and I fold by count and prayer like the rest." He handed the blade back, and his hand — Lin Hao noticed — was not entirely steady. "Your father's boy. Aye. But not your father's *hands.*" He shook his head and went back to the gate, and the other two made room for him, and none of them said anything else, because there was nothing else that fit.

Lin Hao wrapped the edge in oiled cloth to carry it to Mu Chen, and as he did, he felt the hawk settle — not with leading, not with marking, just the ordinary warmth of a thing that liked, it turned out, to watch him *make* — and he thought, with the particular quiet of a boy who had spent his whole life being told he was otherwise and had finally found the room where otherwise was just *good:*

*This is the part I'm for. Not the watching. The making.*

He carried the edge up the short corridor to knock on Mu Chen's door, and the hawk, on its far water, agreed in the only language it had been using lately — which was none, but which, for the first time, felt less like silence and more like a nod.

## Part 2: The Board

The Association's commission board lived on the ground floor, and beside it — because bureaucracies grow their organs where they fit — the craftsman's ledger: a brass-framed slate where the Association posted, monthly, the ranked output of its registered smiths.

Mu Xi read it the way other people read letters from home.

She was ranked, at ten years old and four months certified, ninth in the Eastsea branch's under-twenty ledger — a number her father's colleagues called astonishing and her father called, in his flat register, "adequate for your age." The number above hers was a nineteen-year-old third-rank with his father's forge behind him. The number below was a fifteen-year-old whose family owned three mines.

And three weeks ago, a new line had appeared at the bottom of the registered-output column — not ranked, not yet; listed, the way the ledger listed associates: *Tang Wulin — second rank — Heavy Silver purifications: eleven.*

Eleven. In three weeks. At ten years old. Heavy Silver was the second-rank *trial* metal — the one that broke wrists — and the ledger counted eleven of them beside a name she had watched, from across a public floor, ring the sixty-third strike like a man twice his age and half his rank.

Mu Xi looked at the line for a long time.

Then she took out her own commission sheet, walked to the intake desk, and requested — in the even voice she had learned before she could write properly — the next available Heavy Silver task.

"Two," said the clerk.

"Two," Mu Xi agreed.

She did not think about the boy. She thought about the sixty-third strike, and about her own fortieth, and about the distance between the two numbers, which was not a distance in strikes at all. She thought about it the way she had thought about it every night for three weeks — carefully, and alone, and without once arriving anywhere.

Her father, from his office window above the intake desk, watched his daughter take two tasks that would take her until midnight, and said nothing at all, which for him was the loudest thing he did.

## Part 3: No Patterns

The night training had acquired a shape, the way all repeated things do: warm laps, Wu Zhangkong's single sentence of the evening, the circle, the disassembly, the walking home.

Xie Xie's shape was different. Xie Xie's shape was Gu Yue.

He had fought her — properly, in the circle, under Wu Zhangkong's eye — four times in the nine nights since the team began. He had lost four times. This was not the surprising part; the surprising part, the part that kept him awake in the bunk above Zhou Zhangxi's snoring, was that he could not find the *shape* of the losses.

"It's the permutations," he said now, walking home between Wulin and Lin Hao under the harbor lamps, with the particular violence of a boy explaining something to himself through his friends. "Everyone's martial soul has a rhythm. Yours is a hammer, Wulin — big, honest, four-four time, I can hear it coming. Yours—" he jabbed a finger at Lin Hao, "—is wind, and wind has grammar, wind *has to*, your hawk can't help itself, I've started reading the feathers. But hers—" He threw up both hands. "Six elements. Six. Do you know how many ways six things combine? I asked Yun Xiao. Yun Xiao did the arithmetic. The number has *digits*, Wulin. There's no pattern. There's no rhythm. She picks, and the picking is the martial soul, and by the time I've found the pattern she's already picked again."

"So stop looking for the pattern," Lin Hao said.

"That's not— that's not how fighting *works*—"

"It's how fighting her works." Lin Hao walked a few steps with it. "You're trying to read the next strike. She doesn't have a next strike until you make this one. So don't make strikes — make *questions.* Feint the light-step left and watch which element answers. The answer tells you more than the strike would have." He shrugged. "That's what my hawk does to me. It doesn't predict the wind. It asks it things and watches which way it leans."

Xie Xie was quiet for four steps. It was, both of the others knew, the longest silence Xie Xie had ever produced voluntarily.

"That," he said at last, "is the most terrifying thing anyone has ever said to me, and I have been called *the one who fought the Elementalist.*" He straightened. "Tomorrow night. I'm doing the question thing. If I die, tell my father I died scientifically."

"You won't die," Wulin said. "Teacher Wu stops it before death."

"Teacher Wu stops it before *injury,*" Xie Xie said darkly. "Death is still entirely on the table."

Ahead of them, walking her own few paces apart as she always did, Gu Yue turned the corner toward the girls' dormitory without looking back — and it was a measure of the nine nights that none of the three boys noticed the not-looking anymore.

Except the hawk. The hawk noticed everything, and the hawk — Lin Hao had stopped asking it why — had begun, in the last few nights, to watch the walking-away with something that was no longer only unease. It was the stillness of a bird watching a horizon it had seen before. He did not have a name for that. He filed it where the other files were: deep, private, marked *later.*

## Part 4: Rank Twenty-Four

It came on the Tuesday of the eleventh week, in the ten minutes before dawn, the way the last one had come — without announcement, without weather, the way the tide comes because it is the tide's hour.

Lin Hao had not been pushing. He had been doing what the mornings were for now: the slow circulation, the spiritual sea laid out under him like a real one, the hawk sitting its far water — and somewhere in the seventh minute, the soul power that had been leaning against the twenty-three ceiling for eleven days simply… stepped over it. Not a breakthrough. A *threshold,* the way a stair is a threshold: already there, only now noticed.

Rank twenty-four.

He lay in the dark of room twelve and took inventory the way he took inventory of everything — honestly, and in order. The Stormbringer, heavier in the soul than it had been at eleven, the way a word you have said a thousand times gets heavier than the day you learned it. The hawk: seven hundred and sixty years of fed cultivation, sitting its water, watching its window, marking its files alongside his. The mutations: the storm-light tips on perhaps a dozen hairs now, invisible unless the sun caught them exactly, and the feather-marks that lasted, after hard nights, until breakfast. The spiritual sea: a hundred and twenty measures and widening, Federation-logged, Wu Zhangkong-silent.

And underneath all of it, where the rank-30 gate would be when he grew enough to see it: the decision he had made in silence six weeks ago, sitting as settled as ballast. *You're family. When the gate comes, you give the ring.*

It was, he understood, an absurd inventory for a ten-year-old. It was also his, every line of it, and he had learned from his brother's forge that the things you can inventory honestly are the only things you can work.

*Three more gates to the big one,* he thought at the hawk. *And a tournament before the first of them.*

The hawk, on its far water, turned its head — and for the first time since the fence, it did not look toward the girls' dormitory.

It looked at *him.*

Not at his face. Not at his eyes. *At* him — with the composed, unhurried attention of something that has finished a long private arithmetic and arrived, at last, at a number it is prepared to share. And in the half-second before the morning bell, Lin Hao felt — not a word, not a picture, not anything the codex of his mind could write down — the unmistakable, undeniable pressure of a *question* being asked of him by something that had never, in seven hundred and sixty years, needed to ask anything at all.

Then the bell rang, and the pressure folded away like weather over a hill, and the hawk was a hawk again, sitting its water, and Lin Hao lay in the gray light with his heart going like a hammer.

*Oh,* he thought, very quietly, in the room of himself where the files were. *Oh. It's not just growing.*

He got up. He dressed. He went to breakfast, where Xie Xie was explaining to Zhou Zhangxi, with a full mouth and both hands, exactly how he intended to die scientifically that evening, and Wulin was eating a pyramid, and Yun Xiao was pretending to read, and the whole ordinary loud morning of class five closed around him like water around a stone.

He did not tell Wulin. Not yet. Some files, he was learning, you keep until you can read them aloud without your voice shaking.

But on the walk to class, under the harbor wind with its long argument, Lin Hao touched — lightly, the way you touch a letter in a pocket — the place under his shirt where the feather-marks lived, and thought at the hawk, with all the steadiness a ten-year-old can assemble at half past seven in the morning:

*All right. When you're ready. I'm listening.*

The hawk did not answer.

It did not need to. For the first time, the silence felt like the silence on the other side of a door that was, at last, being unlocked from the inside.

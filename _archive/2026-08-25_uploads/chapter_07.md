## Part 1: The Golden Scale

It happened on a Wednesday, before dawn, the way important things happened in dormitory three — quietly, and to Wulin.

He woke because his forearm itched. Not on the skin — *under* it, a deep and particular itch, the way a hammer-echo lives in the wrist after a long day. He sat up in the dark and rubbed the spot, and his fingers met something that was not skin.

A scale.

Where his forearm should have been smooth, one scale had surfaced — small as a thumbnail, golden, set into him the way a tile is set into a floor, with no seam and no blood. In the gray light it did not shine so much as *hold* light, the way coals hold heat, and when he turned his arm, the gold moved under the surface like something alive regarding him.

Lin Hao was awake before Wulin finished his first breath of panic — he had been asleep the way hawks sleep, one part of him standing watch — and he was off his bunk and across the two steps between beds before Wulin had decided whether to shout.

"Don't," Lin Hao said, "poke it like that. You're poking it like Zhou Zhangxi pokes bread."

"Hao. There's a—" Wulin's voice came down to a whisper. "There's a scale. On me. A *gold* scale. Old Tang never said anything about scales."

Lin Hao looked. And what his hawk-eyes saw, in the dim, made the breath go still in him for a half-second: the scale was not merely gold. It was *old* — old the way the deep harbor is old, old the way the thing behind Wulin's sternum was old. Around its edges, so faint he would have missed it blinking, the air lay in a different pattern than the rest of the room, the way water lies differently over a wreck.

"It's beautiful," Lin Hao said carefully. "Show me your hand again."

Wulin held out his arm.

The scale was gone.

Not faded. Not sunk. *Gone* — smooth forearm, ordinary skin, the itch already a memory. Wulin turned his arm over and over, pinched the spot, pressed his thumbnail into it until the skin blanched. Nothing. "It was there. Hao, it was *there*—"

"I saw it." Lin Hao put his hand on his friend's wrist, stilling the digging thumbnail. "Golden. No seam. Like it grew there and then decided not to. I saw it, Wulin. You're not imagining it."

Wulin sat very still. In the chest-space where Old Tang lived, something was quiet in the specific way of a teacher who has heard the question and is choosing how much of the answer a student can carry. Finally the old voice came, and Wulin repeated it aloud, slowly, the way he repeated forging temperatures:

"*The seals are not walls. They are doors, and doors have handles. What you saw was a handle. It will show itself when there is something worth holding onto. Do not chase it. Do not fear it. And do not* — " a pause, and Wulin's face grew solemn in the dark, hearing some emphasis only he could receive, "— *do not let it be seen by strangers.*"

Lin Hao nodded in the dark, and said nothing about what his eyes had told him — that the air around the scale had lain like water over a wreck, and that wrecks, in his experience, were things that had once sailed.

Some observations were his alone to carry. That was the whole architecture of their friendship: Wulin's secrets lived in Wulin, and Lin Hao's silence was the room they lived in.

## Part 2: The Listener

On Friday evening, Lin Hao went to the Blacksmith's Association — not to the public floor, but past it, to the private forging rooms, because Mu Chen had sent word through Wulin: *the boy with the hawk-eyes. Friday. Bring nothing.*

The President's personal forge was a plain room with a plain anvil and forty years of silence in its corners. Mu Chen stood behind the anvil with a Thousand Refined hammer in one hand and no ceremony in his face.

"Your friend tells me you hear strikes," he said. "Everyone hears strikes. My fourth-rank smiths hear strikes. I want to know what you hear *between* them." He lifted a bar of common iron onto the anvil. "Ten strikes. After each, you say one word: *true*, or *waste.* That's the test."

The first strike rang. "True," Lin Hao said.

The second rang almost identically — to his ears, to any ears — but under the ring, at the very bottom of the note, a thread of the blow's force had gone sideways into the anvil instead of down into the iron. "Waste."

Mu Chen's eyebrow moved. The third strike: "True." Fourth: "True." Fifth — the note was clean as a bell, and Lin Hao opened his mouth to say *true*, and stopped. Because the hawk inside him — seven hundred and fifty years of a creature whose entire ancestral life had been listening for the wrongness under things — had already gone still and attentive, the way it went still before weather. The note was clean. Under the note, the metal's grain had *flinched.*

"Waste," Lin Hao said. "The sound's right. The iron isn't. It's… flinching. Like you're asking it something it doesn't want to answer yet."

The room was quiet for a long moment. Mu Chen set the hammer down — the first time, Wulin would say later, he had ever seen the man set a hammer down mid-test — and looked at the boy across the anvil with an expression that was not pleasure, exactly. It was recognition. The look one master gives another's work when he understands, precisely, what it cost.

"That was heat-stress in the bar," Mu Chen said. "Invisible. Inaudible to nine smiths in ten, and the tenth tells me after the work is ruined." He picked up the bar and turned it in the light. "A smith who can hear what I can hear is worth three who can't. I said that about you to the boy. I did not expect to be understating it." He pointed at a stool by the wall. "Fridays. You sit there. You listen while I work, and at the end you tell me every waste you caught. You touch no hammer in this room — your friend's hands are his own, and yours are your own, and I will not have the Association saying I collect children." A pause, and then, drier: "Your father's folding weld. The one that should not have worked. I have thought about it eleven years. Sit down."

So the Association acquired something it had never had and did not officially have: a **listener**. On the floor, the smiths learned that on Friday evenings the small Glorybound boy sat by the President's door with his eyes half-closed, and that on Monday mornings the President's corrections were sharper than any of them had ever heard, and arrived, somehow, already phrased in the language of what the metal *wanted*.

And Lin Hao learned something too — that Mu Chen's waste-strikes were never wasted twice, that mastery was mostly the discipline of *hearing yourself honestly*, and that the old man's silence, like his father's, like Chen Long's, like Wu Zhangkong's, was not emptiness. It was a room. The men who mattered in this city all kept one, and they all, one by one, were letting him sit in it.

## Part 3: The Harbor

They took the first soul train to Glorybound on Saturday morning, because the notices needed posting and Wulin had decided — with the particular finality he brought to budgets — that the search would begin where the search belonged.

Glorybound City received them the way it received everything: with salt wind and indifference. The harbor at noon was a forest of masts and a babel of gulls; the notice-boards by the ferry landing were layered three years deep with lost dogs, lost boats, and lost persons, and Wulin added one more, hammered flat with a care that made the harbormaster's boy watch, then help.

The notice was simple. NA'ER. Silver hair. Amethyst eyes. Found in this city, taken from this city, roughly four months past. She is family. **Reward: 500 federation coins for verified word of her.** No questions asked of the bringer. Contact: the Association branch, Eastsea City, ask for Tang.

Five hundred coins was more than most of the men on those docks made in two months, and by nightfall — because Wulin had learned from his father how money talks and from Lin Hao how money *listens* — the notice had been copied by a dozen hands onto a dozen other boards, up the coast road and down, and the harbor's idle architecture of old men had begun, in its slow tide-way fashion, to pay attention.

The night fishermen worked the black water beyond the breakwater, and it was to them the boys went last, with tea in a thermos and the patience Lin Hao's father used on stubborn metal. Old men and the sea keep longer hours than cities. By the second hour, three of them had remembered nothing. The fourth remembered something.

He was called Uncle Gull, which was either a name or a job description, and he mended nets by lamp while he talked, never looking up.

"The night you mean," he said, "there was no wind."

Lin Hao went still. His whole self — the hawk in his chest, the wind in his bones, the air-reading that never slept — went still with him, because he had stood on this harbor every day of his childhood, and there was *always* wind.

"No wind at all," Uncle Gull said, drawing the needle through. "Sea flat as a widow's face. Old men like me, we notice such things because such things are wrong. I said to my brother: the water's holding its breath." The needle paused. "And past the breakwater — out where the lamp doesn't reach — the water was *moving*. Not waves. Movement. Like something under it, going out, taking its own weather with it." He shrugged, resumed the net. "By morning, the wind was back. Nothing in the log. Nothing on the water. But I've fished forty years, boy, and I tell you true: whatever left your harbor that night, it didn't use a boat."

Wulin stood at the end of the pier long after the lamp had gone out.

Lin Hao gave him the whole silence — all the long black minutes of it, because he had learned that his friend carried things the way forges carry heat: slowly, and better when not quenched too fast. When Wulin finally spoke, his voice was not broken. It was *set*, the way metal sets.

"She faced the water," he said. "Every evening, four years, she faced the water, and I thought she was looking at nothing." He looked out at the flat, moving dark. "She was looking *home.* And something came and took her there, and the sea covered the road behind it." He turned to Lin Hao, and his eyes were not wet — they were the eyes of a boy making an alloy, mixing grief and love in exact proportions until the result was harder than either. "Then we'll learn the sea, Hao. Every route, every port, every current. I'll forge the money and you'll read the wind, and between us we'll take her road apart piece by piece until we find which piece she's on."

On the last train back, Lin Hao watched the harbor lights shrink behind them and thought about water that moved without wind. The hawk in his chest was very quiet, and very awake, and — this was new — it felt less like an animal remembering a hunt and more like *someone* remembering a place.

He did not examine the thought too closely. Some doors announce themselves before you knock.

## Part 4: The Sea Inside

That night, back in room twelve, Lin Hao sat in meditation the way he had not sat since Glorybound — fully, without one ear on a sleeping friend or a distant harbor.

His spiritual sea opened under him like a real one.

It had been growing into this for months, though he had never stopped to look: a wide, dark, luminous expanse — Spirit Connection depth, a hundred and twenty measures of it and widening, the surface stippled with the everyday weather of his own thoughts. And out in that sea, where in the first months there had been only a warmth — a sleeping animal-shape folded into the deep — there was now a *presence.*

The Gale Hawk sat on the far water the way hawks sit on high places: composed, alert, entirely itself. Seven hundred and fifty years of fed cultivation had thickened it beyond any hundred-year thing; its edges were less like light and more like *decision*. As Lin Hao watched, it turned its head and regarded him — and he understood, with the wordless certainty of things that happen inside one's own soul, that it had known he was watching before he looked.

*You're not just growing,* he thought at it, the way he thought at his own hands. *You're waking up.*

The hawk made no answer it could have put in words. It did something better: it *showed* him. A flash, wing-beat fast — the training yard last week, Xie Xie's light gathering a half-breath early, and the hawk's attention arriving there *first*, a finger's-width of a second ahead of even his hawk-eyes. And another: Mu Chen's fifth strike, the flinch in the iron's grain — the hawk had heard it before the sound existed. And another, and another — small things, weeks of small things, laid out like a hunter laying out the day's kills: *I have been helping. I will help more. I am becoming able to.*

Lin Hao sat with it — with *him*, the correction came unbidden, and he did not argue with it — and felt, for the first time, the shape of what was coming. Not a calculation. He had done the arithmetic weeks ago and set it aside, because arithmetic was the wrong instrument: a purchasable thousand-year soul cost a million coins his family would never hold, and even held, would come to him a *stranger* — partial compatibility, careful integration, a negotiation. While this one —

This one had been folded inside him since he was eight years old. This one had been fed by his cultivation and fed him back, had rebuilt itself around his meridians and his sword and his very bones, had grown *with* him the way a brother grows with a brother. When the rank thirty gate came, and the third ring wanted a soul to come from —

*It will be you,* Lin Hao thought. *There was never a question. You're not my equipment.* The hawk's presence on the far water stirred, and he felt the answer arrive in his chest warm as a coal: *You're family.*

The sea brightened. Somewhere in its depths a threshold turned over like a sleeper — not waking, not yet, but turning toward the waking — and Lin Hao's soul power, riding the turn the way his body rode all turns, deepened past a line he had been approaching for weeks.

Rank twenty-three came in the silence of room twelve, without sound or spectacle, the way the tide comes: because it was time, and nothing on earth stops the tide.

On his shoulders, under his shirt, the feather-fine marks glowed a little longer than they ever had — and at their edges, if anyone had been watching closely, the faintest new thing: the very tips of his dark hair, where the wind sat even indoors, had gone the color of storm-light. One strand. Two. Nobody was watching. It would not stay nobody for long.

## Part 5: The Announcement

Monday's first class ended with Wu Zhangkong holding the room for ten seconds longer than anyone expected — which, with him, was the equivalent of standing on the desk.

"Two announcements," he said. "The class promotion tournament begins at term's end. Class against class, full roster, the grade's board decides the standings." His pale green eyes crossed the room and rested, with what the class had learned to recognize as his maximum warmth, on the back file. "Some of you have made the worst class's name interesting. See that it stays interesting."

A rustle went through the twenty-odd. Xie Xie's dagger-light was out of his grip and humming over his knuckles before the sentence had landed, and Zhou Zhangxi cracked his knuckles in slow, tectonic sequence, and Yun Xiao — quiet Yun Xiao — allowed himself a single, small, accountant's smile.

"Second," Wu Zhangkong said. "The Academy has assigned a transfer student to this class, effective this week." He let the sentence sit in the silence it deserved — because transfer students went to class one; transfer students went where the silk jackets were; transfer students did not, in the history of Eastsea Academy, come to the ground-floor-end-of-the-building class. "Mid-term. Unusual. Not my decision." A beat. "Her file is sealed."

"*Her?*" Xie Xie said, with the outrage of a boy whose class, whose *streak*, whose entire emerging mythology was about to be walked into by a stranger. "We finally get interesting and they send us a — a *file?* Who does that?"

"The people who sealed it," Wu Zhangkong said, and left, which ended the class in the way his exits always did: instantly, and with thirty conversations detonating behind him.

Walking out with the noise, Wulin fell into step beside Lin Hao, and the two of them said the same thing at the same moment, in the same tone, without consulting each other:

"Our class doesn't get things by accident."

Above them, unseen, unbothered, the gulls of Eastsea turned in the harbor wind — and one of them, for a single beat that no one was watching closely enough to notice, turned *with* the wind rather than against it, the way a bird does only when the wind is, in some small and private sense, its own.

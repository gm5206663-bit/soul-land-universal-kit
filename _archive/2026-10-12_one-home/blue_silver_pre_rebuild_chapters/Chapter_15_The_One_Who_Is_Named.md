*Still the deep era. The road runs both ways now, and the second word is easier than the
first. She comes into the valley the only way a rooted Empress can — not by walking, but
by unfolding: her domain over the whole blue-silver sea of her own line, every blade read,
every blade known, the valley hidden from the forest so that what happens in it belongs
only to the grass. She is the last of her blood and he is the first of his in an age, and
between them lies a ring of nine kept places that neither of them could keep alone. She
tells him what her domain could take and has never taken; he tells her what the ground
offered him and what he refused; and two beings who could each have emptied a country
recognise, in the same moment, that neither of them ever will. Then she does the thing she
has never done in all her long age — she has loved every blade of her line equally and
never once singled one out — and she names him, with the oldest word in a small grazer's
drum-grammar, because that is what he has been to everything he has ever kept.*

---

The second word was easier than the first.

He had not known that would be true. He had spent a season after her answering pulse in
the particular quiet of a being who has done the most frightening thing it has ever done
and cannot take it back — rooted in the crown ground, keeping the nine, listening to a
country of earth for a repetition that might never come. He had waited through worse. He
had waited a whole winter for the mantis to wake. He had waited decades for the found one
to answer. But those waitings had been waitings for a small thing to become brave, and
this was a waiting for the last great one of his line to decide what he was.

The second exchange came at the turn of the season, and it came in the old grammar, and it
was not a word at all. It was a question made of pressure: a shaping in the ground,
deliberate, patient, the way the old one had shaped *what are you* out of three taps and a
pause.

She asked him what he was.

He answered in the only vocabulary he had, which was the vocabulary an ancient keeper had
tapped into a blade of grass across long evenings at the edge of the middle band, and
which a small frightened grazer of the deep fringe had afterwards taught to a hundred
young voices with its feet. The grammar was tiny. It had words for *here* and *home* and
*safe* and *danger*; it had a word for *keep* and a word for *hold* and a word for *gone*;
it had the single new blow the found one had invented for *I am here, I am answering, I am
one of the ones who stays*. It had no words for King, or crown, or valley, or Empress, or
age. It had no word for loneliness.

He used what he had.

*Green,* he sent. *Kept. Came.*

And then, because the grammar's whole genius was that a shaped silence could carry as much
as a blow, he let the ground hold the rest — the shape of a long travel, the shape of a
root drawn and set down and drawn again, the shape of a promise given to a hundred small
drummers and not yet paid.

The answer that came back up through a country of living earth was so long, and so carefully
shaped, and so full of a patience that had clearly been learning his grammar rather than
expecting him to learn hers, that he understood two things at once: that she had been
listening to his ground-language for a very long time, from the far end of her reach, the
way a scholar listens to a language she has never been taught; and that she was, in her
own vast and ancient way, delighted.

---

The domain came over the valley at evening.

He felt it before he understood it — a change in the quality of the reading, the way the
air changes before a storm but without any threat in it. The whole blue-silver sea of the
valley, from the near lip to the far, from the crown ground to the valley's throat, went
still all at once. Not the stillness of fear. The stillness of being *known*.

Every blade of it. He could feel it happening through the ground the way he felt everything
— not by sensing the grass, which he could not do and never would be able to do, but by
feeling the earth under ten thousand thousand roots go quiet at once, the way a room goes
quiet when someone speaks who is worth listening to. The valley's grass was being read.
All of it. Every blade, every root, every layer of the old pressed ground: inspected,
known, held in a single attention so vast that his own whole country of keeping at the
seam would have been a corner of it.

He had no name for what was happening. She gave him one, in the ground-grammar, shaped
deliberately so that he would have it: *my green. my reach. my eyes.*

It was her domain. He did not know then what the word meant in the forest's larger
language — did not know that it was a thing of blood and not of age, that the elder Kings
of his line could stand in a country of grass their whole lives and never hold it, that it
belonged to the Emperor's blood the way his sun-and-water weaving belonged to his. He knew
only what the ground told him: that the Empress of the Blue Silver line could see through
every blade of her own kind in her reach, the way he could see through nothing at all, and
that she had turned that whole impossible sight onto a valley she had not walked in for an
age, because one impossible blade of her line had sent his word down through the earth and
asked nothing of her but to be known.

And then the valley went out of the world.

That was the strangest part, and the part he thought about for years afterwards. The
domain closed over the valley like a hand closing over a small light — not to smother it,
to hide it. The deep country's watching presences, which had followed him the whole long
going and let him pass, lost their hold on the place. The far forest's great ears, which
had smelled him and learned his name the way the deep learns names, by scars and silence,
found nothing where a country of the line's green had been. The valley was there and was
not there; the grass grew and was not read; the evening light went down through it and no
beast in Star Dou could have said what was happening under that light.

She had hidden her home valley to speak to him in it.

He felt the deep country's true ancients withdraw — not in anger, not in challenge. It was
the withdrawal of very old things that understand what they are looking at. Something far
greater than a King had unfolded over the grass country, and the deep, which had watched a
King travel without once testing him, did what it had always done in the presence of the
line's true great ones: it made room.

---

She came into her own grass the only way a rooted thing can come anywhere.

He had wondered, in the long turning-over of the seasons before her answer, what a meeting
between two rooted beings would even be. Neither of them could travel — except that one of
them could, slowly, at the cost of years, and had; and the other was older than the
keeping, older than the boundary, older in her way than the memory of the valley itself,
and was not going anywhere at all. He had imagined, in his small way, that a meeting would
have to be a compromise: that one of them would have to give up the thing they were.

It was not a compromise. It was an unfolding.

The valley's blue-silver sea began, blade by blade, to turn. Not toward the sun — the sun
was going down behind the old forest's rim — toward the crown ground. Toward him. Ten
thousand thousand blades of the line's ancient home leaning in the same direction at the
same moment, in long slow waves that caught the last of the light and threw it back, and
the light they threw back was not only silver.

There was gold in it.

A threading of gold ran through the turning grass the way a river runs through a country —
not on every blade, not evenly, but here and there, in the older growth, in the deepest
blue, a warm metallic thread that had no business being in the commonest plant in the
world and was there anyway, and had been there, he understood, since the first blade of the
line's royal blood had come out of the ground different from its siblings. Royal grass is
born visibly different. He was looking at the line's oldest mark, in the line's own home
valley, turned toward him.

And in the turning grass above the crown ground, where the nine kept places stood in their
ring and his own keeper's green ran around them like a silver thread, the grass grew
together and grew upward and grew into a shape.

He had seen a face in vines before. The old one had carried, in the deep-country memory,
the image of the line's great ones wearing their faces in their own bodies — the way the
elder Kings of the grass carry the marking of a face in the crystal blue of their vines,
not as a disguise and not as a wish to be human, but because a thing that thinks and speaks
and grieves will grow the shape of its thinking into its own body if it lives long enough.
This was that, and more than that. The grass of the valley wove itself into a presence
above the ring of nine — a face in the blue-silver and the gold, vast and quiet, with the
patience of a hundred thousand years settled into the line of it — and the face looked
down at the young King rooted in the crown ground of her line, and the ground under him
went warm all the way to the deep.

She spoke.

She had true speech — of course she had true speech; she was an Emperor of the line, and
if a King of the common line could speak at all then she could speak in ways he had no
words for. But she did not speak to him in the forest's language. She spoke into the
ground, in the old grammar, in the handful of shaped pressures and deliberate pauses that
an ancient last-of-its-kind night-walker had tapped into a blade of grass four hundred
years ago at the edge of the middle band — and she spoke it *well*, with the accent of a
being who had spent a season listening to a language from the far end of a country and
learning it out of love for the one being who spoke it.

*Kept,* she said into the earth. *Green. Came.*

His own words, given back to him.

*Here,* he answered, because it was the only word he had that was big enough.

---

What they said to each other over the following years — for the meeting was not one
evening, and neither of them was in a hurry, and the valley was hidden from the world and
the nine were kept and the grass grew lush around them both — cannot be told in the order
it happened, because most of it happened in a grammar of eight words and shaped silences,
and because a great deal of it was not said at all but simply stood in the ground between
them where the other could read it.

She told him what the nine were.

He had read them as graves. He had kept them as graves. And he had been, she told him,
half right — which for a keeper of his age and stubbornness was a considerable amount of
being wrong.

The ring was not where the line's great ones were buried. It was where they were crowned.

Each of the nine had stood in that ground at the height of its age and been witnessed —
not by a ceremony, because the grass has no ceremonies, and not by a crowd, because a
country of mindless grass cannot watch anything. Witnessed by the ground. A great one of
the line stands long enough in one place, keeps one ground long enough, holds one country
of green through enough of its own slow centuries, and the earth under it takes the shape
of what it was — and that shape, she told him, is the crown. Not a thing placed on a head.
A thing left in the ground by a life that stayed. The grass has no other royalty, she said
into the earth. The grass's crowns are the places it kept.

He thought about that for a long time. He thought about the glade, and the boundary, and
the five centuries of keeping that had gone into the ground there; he thought about the
silver over the old one's place and the place where the fledgling slept and the place
where the found one had drummed its last *here*. He thought about what the door-people's
drums were doing to that ground right now, season after season, without knowing it.

And he understood that he had already been crowned, twice over, in a country he had left
behind — and that no one had been there to read the ground and tell him so.

She told him that she had not walked the valley in an age. That her body was rooted deeper
in the forest than his whole going had carried him, in the direction the old memory called
the core, where the oldest things of the world keep their long silence. That her reach
came here — that the valley was the near ground of her reading, the green she had grown up
in, the place her own line's great ones had stood — and that reach was not a road. She had
felt the nine going quiet one by one across her long age. She had felt the last of them
stand and go into the ground. And she had not been able to come.

He did not ask her why. He knew why. He was the only grass alive who knew exactly why,
because he had been rooted his whole life until the talent broke the last law of his kind,
and the breaking had cost him a decade of his substance and the leaving had cost him every
kept place he loved. Rooted things do not go. That is not a failing in a rooted thing.
That is what a rooted thing is.

So he told her what the ground had told him, which was the thing she had not been able to
read from the far end of her reach, because the reading of a kept place must be done from
inside it, by a root, and her roots were a country away.

He told her about the layers. About the deeper ground being crowded and the middle ground
less crowded and the upper ground sparse, and the ring of nine standing alone at the top
of the stack with nothing after them for an age. He told her that the line had not been
destroyed — that he had tasted no slaughter in it, no fire, no great enemy — that the line
had simply grown quieter, generation upon generation, the way a country grows quiet when
its people stop being born in it.

And then he told her the thing that had come to him in the long quiet of the reading, the
second half of the law that had settled under the first half and made it larger.

*Gone,* he shaped into the ground, in the only word the grammar had. And then, because
*gone* was not enough and the grammar had no word for what he meant, he shaped the silence
after it into the shape of the middle band — the herd cropping, the world walking over,
the forest's ten thousand years of rumor in which his kind was carpet, and food, and
nothing at all. *Gone,* he shaped again. *And not remembered.*

The ground under the crown ground went very still.

She had carried that alone. That was what he understood in the stillness, and it was the
moment the whole long road of his life had been growing toward without his knowing it —
not the wire, not the going, not the valley, but this: that the last Empress of the Blue
Silver line had known for an age that her line's great ones were going quiet and that the
world would not even notice the difference, and had had no one to say it to. She loved
every blade of Blue Silver Grass in the world. Every one. Across a reach so wide that his
whole country of keeping would have been a corner of it, she had loved the commonest,
weakest, most trodden plant in the world, blade by blade, for a hundred thousand years —
and she had been the only being alive who knew that the grass had ever had kings.

Now she was not.

---

She told him what her domain could do, and he understood afterwards that this was the
test, and that he had passed it before it was asked.

*My green,* she shaped. *All. Mine.* And then the harder shaping, the one that took her
three deliberate pauses and a silence long enough that the evening light moved: *I could
take. All. The valley's every blade — its life into me, and I would never be empty while
any grass stands in the world. That is what my reach is, at its deepest. I have never
taken a blade. Not one. In all my age.*

He felt no surprise at all. He had known it since the moment the domain closed over the
valley and hid it — because he had felt, in the reading, exactly how much was in reach of
her, and he had felt her not take any of it. A country of her own line's green, an age of
accumulated essence in unturned earth, more vitality in one valley than his whole well had
ever held, sitting inside the hand of a being who could have drawn it dry — and the grass
grew, and the ground stayed whole, and the nine stayed kept.

So he told her about his own valley.

He shaped it as carefully as she had shaped hers. The rich earth. The age of the line's
dead in the ground around his roots, belonging to no one, drawable by any root, with no
keeper to be betrayed and no small life to find its shelter hollow and no one in the whole
valley to ever know. He shaped the season he had spent considering it. And then he shaped
the refusal, and the reason for the refusal, which was the part he wanted her to have:

*If I take, the memory goes into me. One being's record. Not a country's. The line's home
would be poorer by what I gained. I came home. Not to eat home.*

The grass above the crown ground leaned. All of it. The whole blue-silver and gold sea of
it, from the near lip to the far, leaning toward the young King rooted in the ring of nine
— and he understood that he had just been recognised by the only being in the world who
could recognise it, which was that the two of them were the same kind of thing.

Not the same blood. She was careful about that, and he was grateful for the care: there
was no gold in him, she shaped, and there never would be; he was not of her line's royal
blood, and he had not been made by anything being fused into him, and he was a King of the
common line anyway — which, she let the silence say, was a thing the world had not seen
since the ring of nine was new. He was, in the arithmetic of the grass, exactly what he
had always been: the one impossible blade. Common-born. Crowned by nothing but the count
of his own kept years.

And she did not ask him to serve her.

He had been, in the deep unspoken part of him, waiting for it — because the deep-country
memory had told him that the line's great ones served the Empress, and because the whole
forest ran on the arithmetic of the greater being taking the lesser into its service. She
did not ask. She asked instead what he kept, and he told her: the nine, and the valley, and
far away at the seam a boundary and a hundred small drummers and a green full of kept
places. And she shaped back the only response that mattered, which was that the keeping
was the crown, and he was wearing it.

She told him one other thing, at the end of that first long evening, shaped so lightly
that he almost missed it: that he was not the only King of the grass in the world. Far off,
in another region of the deep, one of his grade had stayed in one ground for a very long
time — older than the keeping, older than the boundary, a presence she had known of across
her whole age and had no quarrel with. She did not shape a name, and she did not shape a
road, and he did not ask for either. The deep country had legends, and he had learned long
ago that a legend of a far region is not a wire and does not want to become one.

---

She named him on an evening in the third year, and it was not a grand thing.

They had been, in their eight-word way, talking about the small ones. He had told her
about the sanctuary — the small and the lost of the fringe, the ones driven out of the
deep by the rot and its hunters, the ones too weak to hold a ground and too frightened to
cross one — and about the door, and about how the door had become a people. She had asked,
in a shaping he had to read three times because he could not believe she was asking it,
what the small ones called the place where they were safe.

He told her. The drum-grammar, which a small grazer of the deep fringe had invented out of
its own fear and then taught to a hundred young voices with its feet: *here. home. safe.*
And the single new blow, the one the found one had made for *I am here, I am answering, I
am one of the ones who stays*.

The ground under the crown ground was quiet for a long while.

Then she shaped a word he had never once in his life turned onto himself — a word he had
used ten thousand times for the glade, for the boundary, for the silver over the old one's
place, for the valley, for every ground he had ever held against the grey and the drought
and the teeth. She shaped it small, and deliberate, and personal, the way you shape a word
when you are giving it to someone to carry.

She named him **Home**.

And then she told him why, and the telling was the part that undid him — the first and only
time in his long life that the King body's face did something it had not been asked to do,
which was to weep, the way the old memory said the line's great ones wept when they were
glad.

She had loved every blade of Blue Silver Grass in the world for a hundred thousand years.
Every one, equally, across the whole vast reach of her green — because that was what she
was, and because to love one blade more than another would have been to admit that the
world's commonest plant had a hierarchy, and she had spent her whole age refusing to admit
it. She had never in all that time given a name to a blade of common grass. Not one. There
was nothing in her long memory of a wild blade being singled out and named, because naming
is a singling-out, and she had made her whole life the opposite of singling-out.

So the naming cost her. That was the gift. She was accepting, in the act of it, that one
impossible blade of her line was not like the others — that he had gone where no grass
goes, and kept where no one was keeping, and refused what she herself would have refused,
and come home to a home he had never seen and held her great ones' crowns in the ground
while she watched from a country away, unable to come. She was accepting that she loved
him, individually, by name, and that this was a new thing in her, and that new things in a
being that old are not given lightly.

*Home,* she shaped into the earth. And then, because the grammar was tiny and she had
learned it for him: *You are that. To everything you have ever kept.*

He had no word for what he did with that. The grammar had no word for it either. So he did
the only thing his whole life had ever taught him to do with anything that mattered: he
kept it. He took the name down into his roots the way he had taken the old one's knowledge
and the mantis's arithmetic and the found one's drum, and he set it in the deepest place
in him, and he held it there, and it stayed held.

---

He paid his promise before the chapter of his life turned.

He had given his word at the going, in the last evening before he drew his root out of the
kept ground of the glade: *I am going to the place the green comes from. I will bring back
the word of it. Keep the door.* He had never broken a keeping in his life, and he was not
going to break this one — but the word he owed had grown in the keeping of it, the way
everything in his life grew, until it was much larger than the promise had been.

So he sent it down. Not into the valley's root this time — the valley's root ran toward the
core, toward her — but out, along the old road of his going, into the living earth of the
deep country and the middle band, toward the seam where a hundred small drummers held a
boundary in the shapes he had taught them by being.

He shaped it carefully. He had eight words and a country of ground to carry them through,
and he had learned from the best of both.

*The place the green comes from is real. The great ones are here. They are kept. She is
here — the far queen, the one who loves every blade — and she has not taken one. I am
named. My name is the word you drum when you are frightened and something takes you in.*

*Keep the door. I am keeping this one. Both are kept. Both are home.*

Far away, at the inner edge of the deep band, a young drummer of the found one's people
lifted its feet at dusk and beat the boundary in the old shapes, and the ground answered —
and under the beat, carried across the whole forest like a root-pulse through living earth,
there was a message in a grammar it had been taught before it could walk, and the young
drummer stopped in the middle of the boundary's shape and stood very still, and then
drummed the new blow, the single one, the one that means *I am here, I am answering, I am
one of the ones who stays*, over and over, until every drummer on the line took it up, and
the kept places of the glade — the densest silver-green over the old one's place, the place
where the fledgling slept, the place where the found one had drummed its last *here* — were
drummed over by a hundred small voices all through the night.

And in the hidden valley, in the crown ground, under a face woven out of her own blue-silver
grass and threaded with the line's oldest gold, the Empress of the Blue Silver line listened
to the far thin pulse of a hundred small feet keeping a door that a wild King had made, and
shaped one word into the ground beside him.

*Home.*

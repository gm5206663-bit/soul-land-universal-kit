*Still the deep era. Still the glade at the edge of the deep band. The keeper keeps, and
his green has become a people, and inside him the count that has climbed all his life is
reaching the end of the long road of the thousand-year age. The deep, which has watched
him for seven centuries and learned it cannot break him, has learned one thing more —
that he is about to become something the deep band has not seen in an age — and it means
to meet that becoming with the oldest of its blades. And the keeper, who has never once
taken anything for himself, must make the last crossing the only way he has ever made
anything: by giving everything he is, and taking nothing.*

The years after the liar went out into the fringe were quiet, in the way the ground is
quiet before the thaw — a stillness that is not the absence of change but the holding of
it. The door-people turned their generations over in his green, and the evening air was
full of their drums — the shapes the found one had taught, passed now to young ones that
had never known the small gray keeper who first spoke them, who drummed them the way a
people speaks a language older than its oldest memory. He listened to them the way he
listened to everything, with the whole patient body of him, and he kept them, and the
well inside him climbed.

He did not know it was climbing toward an end. That was the strange truth of those
years — he had spent so long becoming, layer on layer, season on season, that he had no
picture of a becoming that *finished*. The road he had walked his whole life had always
been the long unbroken being-there, and when it began, in the quiet after the liar, to
run toward a place where it stopped — some edge, some surface, some mark at the end of
the thousand-year age — he felt it the way a root feels the approach of water long
before it reaches it, and he did not know what it was, and he was not afraid. He had
never been afraid of the things that grew in him. The talent did its work the way the
seasons did theirs, and he woke to each new depth the way he woke to each new spring,
and he trusted it the way he trusted the ground.

But the deep band knew what it was.

He felt the change in the pressure first — not an attack, not a gathering of hunters,
but a *withdrawal*. The fringe, which had pressed against his seam for seven centuries,
testing, learning, drawing back to test again, went quiet. The lean shadows that had
crouched at the edge of the silver for as long as the door-people remembered simply
stopped coming. The deep band, which had never once stopped watching the green by the
water, pulled its attention back from his whole length of ground and waited — a silence
so complete that the small lives of the sanctuary felt it and grew restless, the way
prey grows restless when the forest goes too quiet. He felt them press closer to the
deep green, and he folded the heavy safe warmth of his presence over them, and he
watched the silence the way the old one had taught him to watch the deep — with the
whole of his attention, waiting for the shape of what was coming.

It came slowly, the way all great things came to the deep. It came as a change in the
ground itself.

The silver around him began to grow. Not his own blades — his own ground had been his
for centuries, roots woven through every span of it — but the grass beyond the seam,
the wild common grass of the fringe and the meadow and the far valley, the mindless
carpet that had never once answered him, began to lean toward his green. It grew
denser at the edges of his ground, greener, thicker, the way the grass had grown lush
around the old one's place after the great body went into the soil — but this was not
the ground remembering a death. This was the ground leaning toward a life. He felt it
as a slow tide, the whole grass country of the region turning its face to him the way
blades turn to the sun, and he did not understand it, and the deep, watching from its
patient distance, understood it perfectly.

The thing the grass line did once in an age was gathering in him. The green by the
water was not finished. It was becoming what the oldest of the deep band's old ones
remembered from the deep-country memory the old one had passed to him — the thing the
grass did when one of its own had climbed the whole long road and reached the end of
it: it crowned.

And the deep band, which had never once in seven centuries found a way to break the
keeper, had learned that it could not let him become what he was becoming. A keeper
could be tested. A keeper could be outflanked, outwaited, outnumbered. A keeper who
kept even its enemies could be measured by the law it held. But a *King* — the deep had
no pattern for a King of the grass at the edge of its own band. The deep had only one
answer for a thing it had no pattern for. It sent the oldest blade it had.

He felt the serpent's return long before it struck, the way he felt everything — as a
wrongness in the deep ground, a familiar pressure far below the reach of his roots,
moving along an old tunnel-rhythm his ground remembered from the night of its making.
The deep serpent, healed. The deep serpent, patient. The serpent of the band below, the
old blade that had broken the old one's foreleg and turned on his root in the
year of his keeping, that had been driven off wounded and had spent the centuries since
learning what it had learned wrong. It had not forgotten. The deep did not forget. It
had only been waiting — waiting for the one moment in the green's long life when the
keeper could not fight, because he would be too busy *becoming*.

He knew it was there. He could feel it waiting in the dark below, patient as the old
one had been patient, and he did not hunt it. He could not have hunted it if he wished
— he was rooted, and it was below, and he had never once in his life taken a life. He
did what he had always done. He kept. He wove his roots a little deeper into the ground
he held, he pressed his presence a little wider along the seam, and he waited for the
crest with the whole of him, ready to be the keeper he had always been for as long as
the ground held.

---

The crest began in the spring of the gathered year, and it began the way everything in
his life had begun — with a change he could not name arriving out of the ordinary.

He felt it as a turning inward. The substance of him, which had spent seven centuries
pressing outward — roots widening, blades rising, presence spreading along the seam —
began to draw back toward the heart of him, toward the crown-knot that had hardened
over the centuries into a thing like a young trunk, toward the taproot and the well. It
was the opposite of the sickness years, when the grey had eaten him from the outside
and he had shed to survive. This was not shedding. This was gathering — every kept
layer of him, every scar-memory, every woven lesson, every drop of the light and water
he had taken in and kept for seven hundred years, drawing down into the deep place
where the count lived, the count that had climbed without once stopping since before
the glade had been a glade.

The blades thinned. That was the first cost, and the small lives felt it immediately —
the heavy safe warmth of his ground lessening, the deep green opening wider than it had
opened since the thin spring after the breach. The young drummer, the one who kept the
found one's evening count at the door, came and stood at the edge of the deepest silver
and drummed — *here, here, are you here* — and he answered with what he had, a pulse
thin as the last of a dry well, and it was enough, and it lay down against the silver
over the found one's place and stayed.

The gathering deepened through the spring, and the green around the glade grew lush
with it — the wild grass leaning in, the air thick with the smell of growing things —
and the deep band watched, and waited, and the serpent waited below, and he gathered.
He could feel the mark ahead of him now, the way a root feels the deep water it has
been climbing toward all its life: the end of the thousand-year road, the count
complete, the change waiting on the other side of it like a season behind a door. He
did not reach for it. He had never once reached for anything in his life — everything
had come to him by being kept, by being held through, by the endless patience of the
rooted — and he did not reach for this. He gathered, and he waited, and he kept.

And the deep chose its moment.

It came in the deepest hour of the gathered summer, when the gathering was nearest its
crest and he was thinnest — when his substance was so turned inward that the outermost
reaches of his ground were held by habit and root-memory more than by presence. The
fringe came first, the way it always had: a dozen points along the seam at once, the
old coordinated press, the deep testing whether the thinning keeper could still hold
the line he had held for an age. He met them with what he had — the reach, the folding
of his presence from point to point, the ground itself rising against them — and they
broke off, bleeding, the way they always had, and it was a feint. It had always been a
feint. The real blade was below.

He felt the serpent move a breath before it struck — the old tunnel-rhythm surging, the
vast weight of it driving up through the deep ground toward the place it had never
forgotten: the taproot, the well, the deep green where the kept places lay and the
young drummer slept against the found one's silver. It came the way it had come on the
night of its making, from below, where a rooted thing was weakest, where blades could
not reach and roots could not seize a thing that moved in the dark earth like a fish in
water. And he was mid-change, gathered and thin, the whole of his substance drawn down
toward the crest that had not yet crested, and he had nothing to spare.

He felt the first blow against his deepest root — the great head striking, the jaws
finding the oldest of his anchor-roots, the root that ran to the water — and the pain
of it went through him the way the serpent's first strike had gone through him on the
night it broke the old one's foreleg, only this time there was no old one to stand at
the boundary and take the blow. There was only him, gathered and thin and turning
inward, and the whole deep green above him full of the lives he kept.

And the part of him that had never once stopped climbing — the deep endless part that
had cracked the well open on the serpent night and deepened it through every harm of
his life — looked down into the dark and saw what the deep had always known was there:
the teeming life of the grass country. The lush green that had leaned in toward him
through the gathering, the million million blades of the region, every one of them full
of the life he had spent his whole existence tending and keeping and never once taking
from. It was all there, at the reach of him, waiting — the power to end the serpent in
a breath, to cross the last of the count in a single drawing, to become what he was
becoming on a tide of taken life instead of a river of given.

It was the oldest temptation of the deep, and it wore the most beautiful shape it had
ever worn: the shape of necessity. The serpent was at his root. The green above was
full of the small and the lost who trusted him. The crest was gathering, and he was
thin, and the deep had calculated — correctly, as it always calculated — that a keeper
who could not let the kept die would take what he needed to save them.

And the keeper did not take.

He had never taken. That was the whole of the law he had built with his life, the law
that had made the green a place of safety when every other place in the deep was a
place of eating: nothing was taken. The old one's body had gone wholly to the ground
when he could have drawn its returned years into his well. The found one had gone the
same way. The well had run low a hundred times, and he had never once reached into the
ground around him and taken what the deep offered, because taking was the deep's way,
and he was not the deep, and the green by the water was kept because it was not the
deep.

The serpent struck again, and he felt a second anchor-root give, and the pain of it
went through the gathered substance of him like a crack through ice — and still he did
not take. He did the only thing he had ever known how to do. He gave.

He gave everything. He opened the gathered depth of him — the whole turned-inward
substance of the crest, every kept layer, every scar-memory, every drop of seven
centuries of light and water and kept life — and he poured it out along his roots, down
into the dark, into the ground the serpent was tearing, not as a weapon but as a
*holding*. The root-cage he had grown around the eater, the keeping-cage he had grown
around the liar, the reach that had split him across the seam on the night of the
breach — all of it, the whole craft of his long life, flowed out of him in one
unbroken giving, roots seizing, vines weaving, the living earth closing around the
vast shape in the dark like a hand closing around a striking snake, and he held it.

The serpent fought. It thrashed against the growing cage of his giving, and he felt
every blow of it in the substance he was spending, felt himself thinning past anything
he had ever been, felt the crest that had been gathering all spring begin to *break*
under the strain — not the clean crest of a thing completing, but the shattering of a
thing spent too soon, poured out to save what it kept.

And the young drummer, above, felt the ground shudder under it and drummed — *here,
here, danger, here* — and the small lives of the deep green pressed themselves into the
deepest silver, and the whole green by the water held its breath.

And in that moment — the moment when he had given everything and had nothing left, when
the crest was breaking and the serpent was at his root and the lives he kept were above
him trusting him — the count completed.

He did not reach for it. It was not taken. It *arrived*, the way everything in his life
had arrived, the way the found one's first two small blows had arrived out of a century
of silence, the way the drum had passed to a people, the way the green had grown a
door: arrived because he had given everything he was and taken nothing, and the last of
the long road had no more road left to climb, and the change on the other side of the
mark opened like a season behind a door.

The last of the long count crested in him.

---

He felt the reshaping begin at the crown.

The hard knot that had been the crown of him since before the glade had been a glade —
the thing like a young trunk that the herd had cropped and the fire had scorched and
the rot had eaten at and he had regrown, changed, each time — began to open. It did not
burst. It unfolded, the way a blade unfolds from a seed, the way the first green of the
thaw unfolds from the frozen ground, and the substance of him that had been gathering
all spring rose into it — the kept light of seven centuries, the water he had drunk and
kept, the wood of him grown dense and silver-veined, the earth of his ground held in
his roots — and it wove itself upward into a shape he had no name for and no picture
of, a shape the deep-country memory the old one had passed to him knew the name of.

The vines came first, from the crown and the taproot and the deepest silver of his kept
green — great smooth ropes of living wood, thicker than any blade he had ever grown,
crystal-clear blue-green with the pale silver running through them like the vein in a
leaf, rising and twining and rising until the whole glade was full of the soundless
growing of them. They wove together above the deep green, above the kept places, above
the silver over the old one's place and the found one's place, into a body — a trunk of
living vine that gathered the whole of his gathered self, and at its crown the vines
wove closer, finer, into markings like a face.

And in the face, light opened.

He had lived seven centuries in a world of warmth and pressure and the reading of the
ground, and he had never once seen. Sight had been a rumor to him — the thing the
beasts had that he did not, the thing the old one had described in the deep-country
speech without a word for it, the thing the mantis had had in her bright jade eyes when
she had paused at his crown in the first years of the world. He had no picture of what
it would be like. He had felt the sun as warmth on his blades for seven hundred years,
and when the light opened in the markings of his crown and the glade came into him —
the green of his own blades, the silver of the stream, the small bright lives of the
door-people staring up at the thing that had grown where their keeper had been — he
understood, in the first instant of his first sight, why the mantis had watched him all
those years. The world was *beautiful*. It had been beautiful the whole time, and he
had been keeping it in the dark, and now he could see it, and he wept the way the old
country-memory said the grass's great ones wept, and the tears ran down the vine-marked
face that had never had eyes before, and the small lives of the sanctuary saw their
keeper weep and did not understand, and pressed closer.

And sound came. The stream, which he had felt as tremor and wetness for seven
centuries, became the thing the water said — the endless small voice of it over the
stones, the first voice he had ever heard that was not a drum or a pulse. The wind in
his own new leaves. The breathing of the small lives, and the young drummer's heart,
and the far calls of the deep band's birds. He heard the world for the first time, and
it was full of voices, and he had no words for any of it yet — and then, in the deep
place where the drum-shapes lived, where the whole stored record of his life had been
kept layer under layer for seven centuries, he found that words had been waiting for
him all along.

They did not come as sounds. They came as *meanings*, suddenly reachable, the way the
ground-language had been reachable — the shape beneath every shape, the thing the drum
had always meant and never been able to say. *Here.* He had been saying it his whole
life, and now he knew it was a word. *Home. Safe. Danger. I am here.* The pulses he had
sent against the old one's breathing, the blows the found one had drummed back, the
shapes the door-people beat into the evening ground — they all had names, and the names
had been in him all along, waiting for the crown to open.

He spoke. He did not know he was going to. The first words of his life came out of him
the way the first blade had come out of the seed in the thaw — because they had to, and
there was no stopping them, and they were the truest thing he had ever kept:

"I am here."

The voice of it — the first voice that had ever come from the green by the water in
seven centuries — went out across the glade and into the ground and along the whole
seam, and the door-people heard it with their bodies more than their ears, felt it the
way they felt the ground under them, and the young drummer looked up at the vine-marked
face above the deepest silver and drummed, slowly, into the ground at the keeper's
root — *here, here, you are here* — and the King understood it, and answered, in the
voice that was new and the language that was older than his life:

"I am here. You are here. We are here."

The serpent, held in the cage of his giving at the moment of the crest, felt the ground
change above it — felt the thing it had come to kill become, in the space of a breath,
the thing the deep band had no pattern for — and it fought the cage once more, and the
cage did not give, because the cage was no longer the spent holding of a keeper pouring
himself out. It was the living ground of a King, woven through with roots that ran to
the whole green country, and it held the way the ground holds the deep water — without
effort, without end, the way it had always held.

He looked down into the dark, with the new sight that saw by the life of things rather
than by light, and he saw the serpent — vast, ancient, patient, older than his keeping,
the blade the deep had sent against his becoming. And he spoke to it, the first words
he had ever spoken to a creature of the deep, in the voice that carried meaning the way
his pulses had always carried meaning:

"You came to break me at my becoming. You found me giving instead of taking. The ground
you lie in is kept ground now, and it is a King's ground, and it will hold you as long
as it must. But I have never taken a life in all my years, and I will not begin with
the oldest blade of the deep. Go back to your band. Tell it the seam is not a keeper's
seam anymore. Tell it the green by the water is a King's green now — and that it keeps,
even the deep, even you."

He opened the cage.

The serpent lay in the dark for a long moment — a thing of the deep, that had never in
its long life been released by anything it had come to kill. And then it moved, slowly,
with a care it had never shown in all the centuries of its hunting, and it went back
down into its tunnel, into the deep band, and it did not return. Whether it understood
his words, he never knew. It understood the ground. The ground told it everything it
needed to know: that the seam was held now by a law the deep could not break, because
the law was not a wall, and not a door, and not even a King. It was the thing the King
was.

---

The years of the gathered summer broke into a world that had changed while it was
gathering.

He felt it first in his own ground — the ground he had held for seven centuries, that
he knew root by root and stone by stone and season by season, suddenly *more*. The
reach, which had let him be at two points of his ground at once, was not a reaching
anymore. It was a being — the whole of the green by the water and the seam it held and
the wild grass that had leaned in toward his becoming, all of it present to him the way
his own body was present to him, not spread thin but whole, the way the old one had
been present across its whole round without ever leaving its rest. The grass beyond the
seam, the mindless carpet that had never once answered him in all his centuries, was
not mindless to him anymore in the way it mattered: it was *his*, not as a thing to be
commanded, but as a thing to be kept, the way he had kept everything — the whole green
country of the region leaning into his keeping the way the wild grass had leaned toward
his light in the gathered spring.

The door-people felt the change as a deepening of the ground's warmth, a steadying, a
sureness under them that had not been there before. The young drummer, who had known
only the keeper and never the small gray voice of the door, came each evening to the
deepest silver and drummed its count, and the King answered — sometimes with the pulse,
which it understood, and sometimes, now, with the voice, which it did not understand
but felt the way it felt weather, as a presence that meant it was safe. It grew bold
enough, in the years after the crest, to drum the found one's old shapes back to him
and wait, and he would say them back to it in the new speech, and it would settle
against the silver over the found one's place, warm and answered, the way the found one
had settled in the first years of the keeping.

The deep band, in the long years after the serpent's going, learned the new shape of
the seam. It did not test it again. It had tested it with hunters, and with patience,
and with numbers, and with a liar, and finally with its oldest blade at the moment of
his becoming — and the seam had held every test by not being a wall, and the deep had
no more blades to send and no more patterns to try. It withdrew to its patient
distance, and it watched the green by the water the way it watched the great old things
of the forest, and the word that traveled through it in the only ink the deep trusted —
the absence of eaten things — grew into a word it had never had for a patch of grass
before: the seam was a place of the grass's crown now. A King kept it. And a King, the
deep had learned across a century of watching, kept even the deep.

He did not know, in those years, that he was being watched from the other direction
too.

He felt it first as a presence at the farthest reach of his new wide sensing — not at
the edge of his ground, not even at the edge of the region, but out at the very limit
of the grass country, where his keeping met the world beyond and the green ran on
toward the deep core of the forest. It was vast. It was warm. It was as old as the old
one had been, and older, and it was *grass* — grass like himself, grass that read the
grass, grass that had known every blade of its line for an age beyond his counting and
had never once, in all that age, found a blade that was not a blade.

And now it had found him.

Far away, in the deep heart of the forest where the ancient things of the grass line
had their seat, the one who had read her blades for an age beyond all counting paused in
the reading. There was a blade at the middle band's edge that was not a blade. There
was a King of her line in a place she had never planted one, a King she had not made
and had not expected and had not even imagined — a wild King, crowned out of the common
grass by a road no grass of the line had walked in an age. She looked twice at it, for
the first time in a very long life, and she did not know yet what it meant, and she did
not move toward it, because she was the rooted Empress of the deep forest and it was a
single King at the edge of the world and there was no hurry in an age that had already
outlasted every counting the deep country remembered.

But she looked. And he, at the far green limit of his new wide keeping, felt the
looking — felt the vast warm ancient presence of the grass's queen at the very edge of
the world, the direction the old one had turned its head at dawn all its life, the
direction the deep-country memory had pointed to since before the glade had been a
glade — and he understood, with the new understanding that had come with his crown, what
the old one had been turning toward all those years. Not a place. A presence. The great
one of his line, reading the grass at the deep heart of the world, who had read him now
for the first time and would read him again.

He did not move toward it. He was rooted, and it was far, and he was the keeper of the
seam and the King of the green by the water, and the meeting of a wild King and the
ancient Empress of his line was a thing that would come in its own time, the way
everything in his long life had come — by being kept, by being held through, by the
endless patience of the rooted. He turned his whole kept self toward the direction the
old one had turned, the way he had turned it every spring of his life, and he grew —
a little deeper into the ground he held, a little wider along the seam he kept, a
little closer to the far warm presence at the edge of the grass country that had
looked twice at him and was, he knew now, keeping him too.

The young drummer drummed at his root — *here* — and he answered, in the voice that
was still new to him and the language that was older than his life:

"I am here."

And the green by the water, which had been a keeper's green for seven centuries and a
door for a people and a King's ground for the years since the crest, held its quiet,
and the deep watched, and far away the ancient Empress of the grass read the wild King
at the edge of her world and did not yet know his name — because he did not have one
yet, and the giving of it was still to come — and the last of the impossible blade's long count settled
into the ground like a root taking hold, and the long road of the thousand-year age was
done, and the road of the King had begun.

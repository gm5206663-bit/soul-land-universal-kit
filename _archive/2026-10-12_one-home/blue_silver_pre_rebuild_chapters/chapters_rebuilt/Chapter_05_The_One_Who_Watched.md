# Chapter Five — The One Who Watched

```
◆ STATUS — Chapter 5: years 190–240
KIND      : Blue Silver Grass, common line. The bank is his, and the meadow beyond it is
            leaning his way.
AGE       : ~240 at the chapter's close
CLASS     : upper thousand-year class. The deep band has started to notice the seam.
THREAT    : a Spirit Hall surveyor comes into the glade on purpose, following her own
            thirty-year-old report. She stands in him for an afternoon. She does not find
            him — because every instrument she has says grass.
```

---

The forest took forty years to change its mind about the glade.

That is not a figure of speech. The forest has a memory the way soil has a memory, and it
is kept in the same place, and it changes slowly. What changed was this: a several-thousand-
year shadow cat had come out of grid nineteen on three legs, and something had bound it,
and nothing in the forest's long experience of the middle band accounted for it.

The word went around the way word goes around in a place with no words. Not as a story —
as a pressure. The mid-forest half-light hunters began going around the stream bend instead
of through it. The hawk that had worked the meadow for two generations stopped stooping at
the bank. A pair of the deep-fringe dogs that had ranged the ridge in his grandfather's
time — or would have, if grass had grandfathers — did not come down into the low ground at
all after the spring of his hundred and seventieth year.

None of them knew why. None of them could have told you what they were avoiding.

But the ground by the water had done something, and the forest had noticed, and the forest
did what it always did with anything that had done something: it made room, and it watched.

He felt all of it and understood none of it. What he understood was that the glade had
gone quiet around him in a new way, and that the quiet was not empty, and that for the
first time in his life the world was pointing at him from a direction he could not see.

Then the woman came back.

---

She was older. That was the first thing.

Thirty years had gone by since she had stood over a mauled cat on a scree slope and written
up a leg. She was past seventy now and moved slower, and there was a fourth ring at her
wrist — purple, purple, purple, black — and the Spirit Hall issue coat had been replaced by
a better one with a different cut at the shoulder, and she walked into the glade with a
junior behind her carrying an instrument case.

She went straight to the bank.

Not to the stream. Not to the far ridge where the cat had been run. She came down off the
meadow, crossed the trampled ground where the herd came to drink, and walked into the deep
blue grass at the bend, and stood there, and looked at it.

"This is the grid," she said.

The junior was already opening the case. "Nineteen, stream bend, per your thirty-one report.
Ma'am, with respect — the report says vegetation."

"The report says a cat came out of this grid bound." She crouched and took a handful of
the blades and rubbed them between her fingers. "And the vegetation note is a hundred and
fifty years older than mine and it says the same place, and I have spent thirty years not
being
able to stop thinking about that."

"It says vigorous grass."

"It says *tough* grass, and it says blue-silver, and it says worth a look." She let the
handful go and stood up and looked at the whole bank, slowly, the way she had looked at the
cat's leg. "Nobody writes 'worth a look' about grass."

He felt her.

That is the only way to say it, and it does not say enough. Every large warm thing that had
ever come into his ground had come with pressure — the herd's blind weight, the cat's
terrified density, the hunters' casual solidity. She came with pressure and *attention*,
and the attention was aimed, and he was in the middle of where it was aimed, and it was the
first time in two hundred and forty years that anything in the world had looked at him and
meant it.

She stood in him for the whole afternoon.

---

She was thorough, and thoroughness is what nearly ended him.

She took blade samples. Not one — nine, from nine places, cut at the base and laid out on a
cloth in a row while the junior went through the instrument case. She dug a soil pit at the
bank's edge, three feet down, and sieved the silt. She walked the whole forty feet of his
ground counting something under her breath. She had the junior put a palm flat on the
crown and stand there for a quarter of an hour with his eyes shut.

"Nothing," the junior said.

"Say it properly."

"No soul power. No response at all. It's grass, ma'am. It's really just grass."

She did not answer. She was holding one of the nine blades up to the light and turning it,
and the silver veins in it caught the afternoon and ran the length of the blade like wire.

"It's tough," she said.

"Grass is tough."

"This is tougher than grass. Feel the base." She held it out. "Feel where the crown meets
the root. That's not growth, that's *structure*. Something has been laying that down for a
long time." She put the blade down on the cloth with the others. "And it's the wrong colour.
Blue Silver is blue-silver. This is deep blue with silver in the veins, and the colour is
at the base, and the base is where a plant keeps what it's made of."

The junior, who was young and honest and had read the same manuals she had, said the thing
that saved his life.

"Ma'am — plants don't cultivate."

She looked at him for a moment.

"I know that," she said.

"It's the first rule. Plants don't cultivate, they don't hold soul power, they don't have
martial value, and a plant-type soul beast is a hundred-year minimum before it's even worth
registering, and there is no such thing as a cultivated grass." He gestured at the nine
blades on the cloth. "Whatever bound that cat, it wasn't this. It's grass."

She stood there in his ground for a long time.

He felt the whole of it, and he did not know what he was feeling, and afterwards — much
afterwards, when he had words and a self and a name — he would think about that afternoon
more than any other in his long life, because it was the closest he ever came to being
found by someone who was actually looking, and the reason she did not find him was not that
he was hidden or clever or lucky.

It was that nobody in the world had ever been told to look for him.

"You're right," she said at last. "It's grass." She began gathering the samples. "But I'm
taking these, and I'm noting the grid as surveyed-and-unresolved, and if anybody out of the
Hall comes through grid nineteen in the next fifty years I want them to read the note
before they read the map."

"What should the note say."

She thought about it while she tied the cloth.

"That the grass here is wrong," she said, "and that I could not prove it."

---

They went at dusk, taking nine blades of him in a cloth and three feet of his soil in a
tin, and the glade went quiet behind them the way it had gone quiet behind everything.

He was missing nine blades. It was nothing. He had regrown a third of a bank.

What he was not missing, and would never get back, was the anonymity.

Because the note went in. And the note said *surveyed and unresolved*, and in the whole
long history of the Spirit Hall's forest registry there had never been a note like that
about vegetation, and the note sat in the middle-band section of the ledger next to a
four-hundred-year-old entry about vigorous grass and a thirty-one-year-old entry about a
bound cat, and the three of them together made a shape that no clerk copied forward without
a small pause, and the shape was not a shape anybody could name.

Yet.

---

*The deep band began testing him that winter.*

*It was not dramatic. Nothing in the deep band was ever dramatic; the deep band had been
old when the glade was silt, and it did not do drama, it did pressure. What happened was
that the boundary — the soft, unmarked, entirely notional line between the middle depth
where the hundred- and thousand-year families lived and the deep where the great things
moved — began to be leaned on.*

*A low-water hunter from the deep fringe came down to the stream in the cold weeks and
stood in the shallows for two nights looking at nothing. A digger that had no business
being that far out of the deep tried the soft ground under the fallen tree on the far bank
and found it woven shut with root it could not get through, and went away. Something very
large crossed the ridge above the glade at three different times over the following decade,
always at night, always going past, always close enough that the ground under him went cold
with the weight of it.*

*The deep band had smelled him. That was all. It had not decided anything. It was doing
what the deep band did with anything new on the seam, which was leaning on it, season after
season, to find out what it was.*

*He had no idea. He felt the pressure and he did not have a category for it, and the thing
in him did what it always did — took the measure of it, kept it — and he went on growing,
because growing was the only response his body had ever been given.*

*But the keeping had started. That was what those leanings were, in the long view: the
forest asking the seam a question, over and over, and the seam holding, and the asking
going on for a hundred and fifty years until the answer stopped being interesting because
it never changed.*

*The forest would eventually give that answer a name. It would call him the keeper.*

*He was a hundred and forty years from that, and standing in a bank of grass that nine
blades had just been taken out of, and the last light was going off the stream, and the
heron was standing in the shallows where the water had gone dark once, a long time ago, in
the ninety-eighth year of his life.*

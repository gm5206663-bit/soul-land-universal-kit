# Chapter Eight — The One Who Crossed

```
◆ STATUS — Chapter 8: years 360–440
KIND      : Blue Silver Grass, common line. The low ground is his and the seam holds.
AGE       : ~440 at the chapter's close
CLASS     : upper thousand-year class, ≈7,000 carried. Keeper era, alone now.
THREAT    : the rot in the deep band — not a beast, not a man, a wrongness in the ground
            that is pushing everything small out of the deep and toward his boundary.
BOND      : the found one. The first creature of the deep he lets through.
```

---

The old one was dying, and it took him forty years to be sure of it.

That was not indifference. It was that dying, in a thing that old, looked exactly like
living — the same round walked at the same pace, the same head on the ground at the edge of
the bank, the same two blows at dusk. The only difference was the dropped shoulder. It had
walked with the dropped shoulder since the serpent night, and for the first twenty years
the drop had been a limp, and then it had been a favouring, and then it had been a leg that
did not set right at all, and by his four hundredth year the old one was walking its round
on three good legs and a fourth that it carried.

It did not slow down. It did not stop walking the boundary. It tapped him the same keeper's
knowledge at the same four words a season, and it lay down at his edge every night, and
every night it was a little less heavy on the ground.

He had no word for what he was watching. He had four words and a pulse and a vocabulary
built out of taps, and none of them were the word he needed.

So he did what he had always done, which was grow, and hold, and keep the record — and
somewhere in those forty years the record stopped being a record and became grief, though
he would not have known the difference if you had shown him both.

Then, in the winter of his four hundred and second year, something came through the
boundary from the deep side and did not get pushed back.

---

It was very small.

That was the first thing, and it stayed the most important thing: it was the smallest
creature that had ever come at his ground. Not a grazer of the middle band — those were
vole-sized and confident. This was smaller than that and thin, a grey blunt thing with a
coat that had gone patchy at the flank, and it came to the low ground in the deep of winter
and stood at the edge of the woven root-mass with its nose to it, and it did not try to
come through.

It waited.

He had spent eighty years making that boundary impossible. Three places the deep came
through, all three woven shut. The old watercourse door netted with root down to bedrock.
The soft ground under the fallen tree held in layers that a digger could not get purchase
in. He had turned a nine-man Hall expedition by simply not being worth the trouble, and the
deep band had been leaning on his seam for a century and a half and had never once got
through it.

And now something small stood on the far side of the best boundary in the middle band and
waited, and the waiting was not the waiting of a thing looking for a hole.

It was the waiting of a thing that had nowhere else to go.

He let it through.

---

He could not have told you why, and it is worth being honest about that, because the whole
of the rest of his life came out of it and it was not a decision.

He had no concept of mercy. Mercy is a word for a choice made by a being that could have
done otherwise and knew it, and he had four words and a root-pulse and no self clear enough
to hold a principle. What he had was eighty years of holding against *pressure* — deliberate
pressure, the deep band leaning on purpose — and this was not pressure. This was a small
cold thing on the wrong side of the only warm ground for four miles, and the woven mass
under it was not being tested, it was being *stood on*, and the difference came up through
his roots as clearly as the difference between a hoof and a hand.

So the root-mass under it loosened. Not opened — nothing opened, there was no door, there
was no gap a predator could have followed. The top layer simply went from held to
permeable for the length of a small grey body, and it came through, and the layer closed
behind it, and it stood in the middle of his crown in the deep of winter with its patchy
coat and its ribs showing and it did not run.

It stayed for two hundred and twenty-eight years.

---

The rot came through behind it, over the following decades, in the only way it could — as
refugees.

That was the word the ground would have used if the ground had words. The deep band had
something wrong in it. Not a beast. Not a man. A *wrongness* — a stretch of the deep
country where the ground went sour and the small things died in it and the great things
moved off it, and whatever it was it was spreading slowly west, and everything too small to
hold territory against the things fleeing it was being pushed the same direction.

Toward the seam. Toward the low ground. Toward the water.

They came one and two at a time over the next eighty years, and they came the way the first
one had come — to the edge of the woven mass, in the cold season, and they waited. Not all
of them. Most of them tried the boundary the way the deep band had been trying it for a
century and a half, and got nothing, and went away. But some of them stood, and some of
them were small enough that the difference between pressure and standing came up through his
roots clearly, and the top layer loosened for those, and they came in.

By his four hundred and fortieth year there were eleven of them living in his crown.

He did not feed them. That is important and it would be a lie to say otherwise — he was a
plant, he had nothing to give them, and three of the eleven died in their first winter
because there was not enough in a hundred feet of low ground for eleven small things and a
bank of grass. What he gave them was cover, and warmth, and the fact that nothing on the
deep side could get at them and nothing on the middle side wanted them, and that turned out
to be enough for eight.

And in the long quiet the first one — the small grey grazer that had stood at his edge in
the winter of his four hundred and second year — stopped being afraid.

---

It began crossing back.

That was the thing that changed everything, and it happened slowly, over a decade, the way
everything in his life happened. It went out through the loosened layer at dusk and it came
back at dawn. Then it went out and stayed a night. Then two. Then it went out and came back
with another of its kind behind it, and the other one stood at the edge of the mass with its
nose to the root and waited, and the layer loosened.

The door swung both ways.

He had not made a door. He had no idea he had made a door. What he had done was hold a
boundary so completely that the only way through it was by being let, and then let one small
thing through, and the letting had become a *route*, and a route is a different object from
a hole — a hole is found, a route is known, and a known route can be taught.

The old one watched it happen. It walked its round on three legs and a carried fourth and
it tapped, and what it tapped that winter was a new thing, a shaping he had not had before,
and it took him a season to read it, and when he read it he understood that the old one was
not correcting him.

It was telling him he had done something the office had never done in all its long age.

A keeper holds a line. That is the whole of it. The line is the office, and the office is
the line, and every keeper who had ever walked that round had walked it to keep the deep on
one side and the middle on the other, and not one of them in the whole age of the post had
ever let anything *through*, because letting things through is not keeping, it is failing.

The old one had kept that line for longer than the glade had been a glade. And it lay at
the edge of his crown with its head on the ground and tapped out, four words at a time, the
slowest and most deliberate message it had ever given him, and the message was:

*a wall is a wall. A door is a door. You have made a door. Keep it as a door.*

---

*The Hall came back into grid nineteen in the spring of his four hundred and thirty-eighth
year, and they did not come for the grass and they did not come for the walker.*

*They were twelve men with nets and cages and a commission to take as many low-class soul
beasts out of four grids as they could carry, and their foreman was a broad cheerful man
named Pell who had worked the middle band for twenty-two years and had a system.*

*"Rot's pushed everything west," Pell said, on the first morning, standing on the meadow
ridge with the grid map spread over his knees. "Three grids east of here are empty. Empty.
I've never seen empty in the middle band, and I've been doing this since before you were
weaned."*

*"So we work west."*

*"We work west." He folded the map. "And we do not work grid nineteen."*

*A young one at the back — nineteen, first commission, the kind who reads the registry
because nobody has taught him yet that the registry is wrong — put his hand up.*

*"Why not. Nineteen's the biggest low ground on the sheet. There's a stream and a meadow and
it's marked habitat."*

*"It's marked sealed-pending."*

*"What's that mean."*

*"It means there's a thing in it that's older than the Hall's charter and it does not like
company." Pell rolled the map up and hit him with it, gently, on the shoulder. "It means we
work eighteen, twenty and twenty-one, and we do not go near the bend, and if you see blue
grass you turn around and you walk the other way until you can't see it."*

*"Blue grass."*

*"Blue-silver. Tall. Base like rope." Pell shouldered his net. "You won't see it. Nobody
goes that way."*

*They worked nine days. They took forty-one small things out of the middle band, and they
were good at it, and they worked within four hundred feet of the best-kept boundary in the
Star Dou forest on six of those nine days, and not one man in the party looked at it,
because Pell had told them what was in grid nineteen and Pell had been told by the Hall,
and the Hall had been told by a registry, and the registry had been told by a records clerk
with a quota.*

*On the eighth night the young one came back to the fire with his cage light and his mouth
open.*

*"There's a whole population over there. In the grass. I could hear them — dozens. Right at
the edge of the low ground, and they weren't running."*

*"How close'd you get."*

*"Close enough to see them stamping." He sat down hard. "They were stamping, Pell. In a
line. Like they were — I don't know. Like they were asking."*

*Pell looked at him for a long moment, and then he looked at the fire, and then he took the
cage off the boy and set it on the ground.*

*"Get some sleep," he said.*

*"Are we going over there tomorrow."*

*"No."*

*"Why not."*

*"Because whatever's in that grass has been in that grass since before your grandmother was
born, and the Hall put a seal on it, and a seal is the only thing in this forest that I
have never once seen anybody break." He poked the fire. "And because I have been doing this
for twenty-two years and the only job I have ever lost was the one where I got curious."*

*And in the crown of the bank, in the densest blue-silver cover for a hundred feet in any
direction, eleven small refugees of the deep band's rot lay in the warm dark and did not
move, and the ground under them held, and the men with nets went past, and the door stayed
shut, and the keeper kept it without ever knowing they had been there.*

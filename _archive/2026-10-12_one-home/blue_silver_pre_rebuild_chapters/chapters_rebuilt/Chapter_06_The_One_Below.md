# Chapter Six — The One Below

```
◆ STATUS — Chapter 6: years 240–300
KIND      : Blue Silver Grass, common line. The bank, the meadow, and now the first hundred
            feet of the low ground on both sides of the stream.
AGE       : ~300 at the chapter's close
CLASS     : upper thousand-year class, climbing. The consolidation well opens in this
            chapter and cracks in the doing — the first time his growth has ever cost him
            something he could not grow back in a season.
THREAT    : a ten-thousand-class serpent of the deep band comes up through the ground under
            him. Not hunting him. Hunting through him.
```

---

He had been keeping the seam for sixty years without knowing there was a seam.

That is the honest way to say it. The deep band had been leaning on the boundary since the
winter after the surveyor came, and he had been holding, and holding had not felt like a
decision — it had felt like growing, because growing was the only thing his body knew how
to do in response to anything. Pressure came down into his ground. He grew denser against
it. Pressure came again. He grew denser still. Sixty years of that, and by his two
hundred and fortieth year the whole low ground for a hundred feet on either side of the
stream was one continuous woven mass of root, and nothing that came at it from the deep
side had got through it, and he had no idea that this constituted an achievement.

He did not know there was a boundary. He did not know there was a deep. He did not know
that the pressure coming at him from one direction was different in kind from the pressure
of everything else in his life, because it was *deliberate*.

The serpent taught him.

---

It came up out of the ground under the far bank on the fourth night of the cold season.

There was no approach. That was the horror of it and he had no capacity for horror, so what
it registered as was simply that the ground he had been holding for sixty years opened
underneath him and something came through.

It came up through four feet of silt and woven root as if neither were there. It was thick
as a man's body and longer than the stream was wide, and it did not surface the way a
burrowing thing surfaces — it *arrived*, all at once, in the middle of his ground, and the
ground over it went up in a long low ridge that ran the length of the bank.

Ten thousand years. He felt the density of it the way he had felt the cat's, and the cat's
had been a bonfire on a hand and this was the sun.

It was not hunting him.

That was the second thing he learned, and it took him a while. It was moving *through*. It
had come up on the deep side of the low ground and it was travelling, under the soil, in a
line, and the line went straight through the middle of everything he was — and the reason
it was travelling was that something had pushed it out of the deep.

He felt that too, afterwards: the old pressure-signature of men with rings, four ridges
east, going the wrong way, and the whole deep band unsettled behind them the way a pond is
unsettled behind a thrown stone. Hunters. Running something. And what they were running had
gone to ground under a bank of grass in grid nineteen because a bank of grass was soft and
easy and nothing in the world thought anything of it.

They came into his ground the way the cat had come into his ground. Because he was nothing.

The serpent surfaced at the far edge of the meadow, took a breath of cold air, and went
back down.

And on its way back down it turned, because it was a ten-thousand-year thing and it had
been in the ground long enough to know what root-mass felt like, and it had felt a hundred
feet of woven, held, deliberate root under a patch of grass, and it was curious.

---

It bit him at the crown.

Not to eat. He was a plant; it had no interest in a plant. It bit him the way a man tests a
door — hard, once, with the whole weight of it behind the strike, to find out whether the
thing would give.

He did not give.

Sixty years of holding against the deep band, and a thousand years of substance, and a
hundred feet of root woven into silt that had been laid down by a flood when he was thirty
years old — it all went taut at once, the whole bank standing up off the ground like the net
it had become, and the serpent's strike came down into it and stopped.

What happened next lasted most of the night, and he survived it for one reason, and the
reason was not strength.

It was that he could be hurt and go on being.

The serpent was ten thousand years of a thing being alive and careful, and it was stronger
than he was by a margin that made the comparison obscene, and it could have pulled the
whole bank apart root by root if it had wanted to spend the season doing it. But it did not
want to spend the season. It was a predator that had been run out of its own ground by men
with rings, and it was tired, and it was testing a patch of grass, and every moment it
spent finding out that the patch of grass would not give was a moment it was not spending
somewhere more useful.

So it struck him eleven times over four hours, and each strike tore a hole in him the size
of a man, and each hole was still there when the next strike came, and by the ninth he had
lost the whole crown of the far bank and a third of his root mass had come up out of the
silt and would never go back down.

And he held. Not because he was strong. Because he was *still there*.

The serpent went back into the ground at the end of it and did not come back, and it did
not die, and it did not learn anything it would have called a lesson, and the hole it left
under the far bank stayed open for a season before the silt filled it.

He spent eleven years growing the far bank back.

---

What the night did to him, in the long run, was not a wound.

It was the well.

He had been consolidating since the solitude after the mantis — folding the record tighter,
making the root-memory fluent, turning the whole accumulated substance of himself into
something held rather than something merely accumulated. The thing in him had been doing it
for two centuries, slowly, the way it did everything, and by the night the serpent came
there was a depth to him that had no name yet and would not have one for a long time: a
place where the kept essence of two hundred and forty years sat, dense and quiet, below the
ordinary working of the plant.

The serpent cracked it.

Not opened it — cracked it, the way frost cracks a stone, by putting more pressure on one
place than the place had ever held. And what came out of the crack went into the regrowth,
and the regrowth that came back over the following eleven years was not the same growth
that had gone before it.

The new bank came in denser. The blades came in with a harder base. The root-mass laid
itself down in layers instead of in a scatter, and the layers held each other, and the
whole hundred feet of low ground became, over that decade, something that a digger could
not get through and a grazer could not crop and a serpent could not tear open in a night.

That was the well's first true depth, and it was bought with a third of his body, and the
arithmetic of it went down into him alongside everything else:

*what is held can be spent. What is spent can be held again. Neither is free.*

---

*The hunters came into the glade eleven days later, following the serpent's track, and they
did not find it.*

*There were six of them and they were not the Hall. They were private — a contracted party
out of a city two provinces over, working a commission, with the flat efficient manner of
men who were paid by the result and knew exactly what a ten-thousand-year ring was worth on
the open market.*

*They read the ground where the serpent had surfaced. They read it well. Their leader was a
thin man with two black rings at his wrist who spent a long time on his knees at the edge
of the meadow with his fingers in the torn silt.*

*"It came up here," he said.*

*"And went down where."*

*"That's the thing." He stood up and brushed his hands and looked at the far bank, at the
long low ridge where the ground had gone up and come back down, at the hole that was
already half-filled with new growth. "It came up here and it went down there, and between
them it fought something."*

*"Fought what."*

*"I don't know." He walked the length of the ridge, slowly, and then he walked it again.
"There's no blood. There's no tooth mark on anything. There's no body and there's no track
and there isn't a single thing in this grid that could have stood in front of a
ten-thousand-year serpent and lived through the night." He stopped at the crown of the far
bank and looked down at it. "Except the grass is different here."*

*The others looked at the grass.*

*"It's grass."*

*"The grass on this side of the bank is three feet tall and the base is as hard as rope and
the roots are laid in layers." He crouched and took a handful and tried to pull it, and it
did not come. He tried with both hands, braced, and it did not come. He let go and looked
at it for a while.*

*"Hall's got a note on this grid," one of the others said. "Surveyed and unresolved. Thirty
years old."*

*"Unresolved what."*

*"That's all it says."*

*The thin man stood up in the middle of a hundred feet of deep blue grass that would not
come out of the ground, in a grid the Hall had marked unresolved nearly five decades before, at
the near end of a fight between a ten-thousand-year serpent and nothing at all, and he did
the sensible commercial thing.*

*"It's gone," he said. "Track's cold and it went deep and we're not going deep after it.
Pack up."*

*"And the grass."*

*"What about the grass."*

*They packed up. They went east, after the serpent, and they did not find it either, and
the commission went unpaid and the party broke up two seasons later over money.*

*But the thin man wrote a letter first, because he was the kind of man who wrote letters,
and the letter went to a cousin who kept a shop in a city two provinces over and sold
information to whoever paid for it, and one line of it said that there was grass in grid
nineteen of the Star Dou middle band that would not come out of the ground with both hands,
and that the Hall had a note on the same grid, and that he did not know what any of it
meant but he would not have camped there.*

*Letters are not ledgers. Letters get read.*

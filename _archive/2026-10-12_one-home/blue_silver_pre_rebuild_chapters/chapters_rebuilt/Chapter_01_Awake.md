# Chapter One — Awake

```
◆ STATUS — Chapter 1: years 0–1
KIND      : Blue Silver Grass (Lan Yin Cao) — common line. Not yet a soul beast by any
            measure the world would recognise; a sprout with an impossibility in it.
AGE       : 0–1
CLASS     : none. Below the hundred-year mark, below the ten-year mark.
THREAT    : a hunting party of three working the glade's edge. They are not here for him.
            They do not know he exists. That is the only reason he lives through this year.
```

---

Below the frost line, a seed was doing what seeds do.

It did not know it was a seed. It did not know that there was a word for the cold ground
holding it, or that the dark it lay in was dark. It knew water — thread by thread between
the grains of soil, moving past, and drinking was good — and it knew one other thing,
which it had nothing in its short existence to compare it to: that it was *there*.

The soil was full of its kindred. Dozens of them in the space around it, sleeping the sleep
that has no waking in it, patient and mindless, waiting for the same thaw and the same
permission to become exactly what their parents had been. None of them felt the water go
by. None of them knew the dark was dark.

This one felt.

There was nobody in the soil to tell it that this was wrong, so it did not know it was
wrong. It only went on doing it — learning the water's slow paths around the grains,
learning the weight of the cold and how the weight shifted, learning the shape of the dark
it lay in. No seed that had ever lain in that ground had learned a single thing in all the
ages of the world.

Something had been done to it before the thaw. It could not have said so, having no words
and no self to say them with, but the doing was in it, part of the grain of it, working the
way a river works — without thought, without choice, by being what it is.

Then the season turned.

It came down to him as a change in the water: the threads that had crawled slow and cold
began to move faster, warmer, fuller, the whole dark world loosening at once as if the
earth had been holding its breath a long time and had decided to let it go.

The grain swelled. It split along a seam it had always had and never used, and put out the
one thing it had been built to make — a thread of white root, tender as anything alive,
turning down because down was where the water was.

He drank properly then, for the first time, and the drinking was a kind of arrival.

Above him the soil gave way and a blade came up into the light.

---

The glade was a bend in a slow stream, sheltered on three sides by the old growth of the
middle forest, open on the fourth to a stretch of meadow that ran down to the water. In
the deep summer it was the kind of place a traveller would have called pretty and walked
past: green, shaded, a heron's worth of quiet, grass to the knee and thicker by the bank.

The grass was Blue Silver. It was everywhere on the continent. It grew in city cracks and
on grave mounds and in the trampled yards of villages, and nobody anywhere looked twice at
it, because it was the commonest, weakest, most trodden plant in the world, and because it
had no soul power, and no mind, and no value to anyone except as the thing you stood on
while you looked for something that mattered.

That was the world's judgement of his kind, and it was correct.

It was also, in his case, wrong, and it would be wrong for as long as he lived, and the
whole of his long life would be spent inside the gap between those two facts.

He grew. It was all he could do and it was enough: root down, blade up, take the sun, take
the water, hold on. By midsummer he was a hand's width of green in a sea of identical
green, and nothing in the glade — not the heron, not the herd that came down to drink, not
the digging family that lived under the far bank — had any reason to distinguish him from
the ten thousand blades around him.

He had no eyes yet. He had no words. He had the ground, and the water, and the light on his
blade, and the record that was already keeping itself inside him without his knowing what a
record was.

And then, in the last heat of that first summer, three men came into the glade.

---

They came from the meadow side, walking the way people walk in a forest that can kill them:
spread out, quiet, the front man reading the ground and the other two reading the trees.

The one in front was broad and unhurried, maybe forty, with the flat practical face of a
man who had done this a thousand times and expected to do it a thousand more. Two rings
showed at his wrist when he gestured — yellow, yellow. Behind him came a younger pair, a
woman with a coil of thin rope at her hip and a boy who could not have been much past
twenty and kept touching the hilt of his knife for no reason.

"Sign's two days old," the broad man said. "He's not running. He's holding."

"Holding where?" the woman said.

"Water. Everything holds at water." He stopped, looked at the stream, and then at the far
bank where the digging family lived. "There. Soft ground. He'll have gone to ground in the
bank and he'll come out at dusk to drink."

The boy said, "How big."

"Four hundred years, the track says. Could be five." The broad man spat. "Yellow either
way. Clean yellow if we don't spoil it."

*Four hundred years.* The phrase meant nothing to the grass by the water. It had no words
at all yet, and no concept of a number, and no way to know that the men standing in it were
speaking about age the way merchants speak about coin.

It did know pressure. Three large warm bodies in its ground, and the heaviest of them
carrying something the others did not — a weight in the air around him, a density, the way
the stream felt different downstream of the deep pool. He was, without knowing it, feeling
a Soul Elder's cultivation for the first time, and the thing inside him did what it always
did with anything new: it took the measure of it, and kept it.

They spread out and waited.

They were very good at waiting. That was the first thing he learned about human beings, and
it stayed true for the next seven centuries: they were patient in exactly the way a root is
patient, only they were patient on purpose, and for something they wanted.

Dusk came down. The heron went. The light went amber in the meadow grass.

And the burrower came out of the bank to drink.

---

It was a grey-backed thing the size of a large dog, low and thick, with a spade of bone at
the nose and small clever eyes. It came out the way wild things come out — a nose first,
then a long stillness, then the body in one committed movement — and it got as far as the
water's edge.

The woman's rope took it behind the shoulders.

It did not die quickly, and the men did not pretend otherwise. The broad one went in with a
short blade and finished it in a practiced place, and the thing went down in the shallow
grass twenty feet from where he grew, and the water at the bank went briefly dark.

The grass by the stream felt the whole of it. Not as horror — it had no word for horror,
and no self to be horrified with — but as a huge disorderly fact arriving all at once in
its ground: heat going out of a body, weight settling, the long tremor of something very
alive becoming something not, and the roots under it taking all of that in whether it
wanted to or not.

Then the broad man knelt beside the body and did something the grass had never seen
anything do.

He waited.

He waited with his hand flat on the dead thing's flank, and the two others stood off and
watched the trees, and for a long moment nothing happened at all.

And then the ring came up.

It rose out of the body the way a bubble rises out of mud — slowly, and then all at once —
a hoop of pale yellow light, the width of a man's two hands, turning over itself, and the
broad man breathed out through his teeth and said something low and satisfied, and put out
his other hand and *took it*.

It went into him. That was the strangest part. The light did not scatter or fade; it went
into the man the way water goes into dry ground, and he stood there with his head back and
his eyes shut for a count of twenty, and when he opened them again the weight in the air
around him was heavier than it had been.

"Third," he said. "Four-twenty years. Better than the paperwork promised."

"Clean?" the woman said.

"Clean. Give me a minute." He wiped his hand on his leg. "Skin it. Fangs and the plate.
Leave the rest, we're not carrying meat."

The boy was already kneeling. He was quick with the knife and quieter than he had been all
day, and after a while he said, without looking up, "Do they know."

The broad man sat down on a stone to wait. "Do what."

"Before. Do they know it's coming."

"Some of them." The broad man shrugged, not unkindly. "The old ones do. That's why the old
ones are worth something. Four hundred years of knowing is what you're paying for, boy.
You think a white ring costs what a yellow costs? A white ring is ten years of a rabbit.
Anybody can have one. This—" he flexed the hand the light had gone into "—this is four
centuries of a thing being alive and careful, and now it's mine."

"That's four hundred," the woman said. "Not four centuries."

"Same thing. Sounds better."

The grass by the water kept all of it.

---

It could not have told you what it had kept, because it could not have told you anything.
But the record in it — the thing that had been keeping itself since the dark under the
frost, the reason no seed in that soil had ever learned a thing before — took the evening
apart and put it away in pieces, the way his roots took apart the soil.

*The light comes out of the dead.*

*The man took it and became heavier.*

*They counted its years and the years were worth something.*

*Yellow for four hundred.*

And under all of it, deeper than any of it, in the place where a plant keeps what it cannot
use: *things with years get taken.*

He did not know that he had years. He was one summer old. He had no ring and no rank and
no value in the world's arithmetic, and the three men had walked within twenty feet of him
without once looking down, and the only reason the grass by the water was still alive at
the end of that first summer was that it was grass.

That was the whole of his safety, and it would hold for a very long time, and when it
stopped holding it would stop all at once.

The broad man stood up and looked around the glade while the other two finished — the
habitual sweep of a professional checking a place he had just killed in — and his eyes
went across the meadow grass and the bank and the stream, and did not stop anywhere.

Then they went, back the way they had come, taking the plate and the fangs and the four
hundred years, and the glade went quiet, and the heron came back at full dark and stood in
the shallows where the water had gone dark and looked at nothing in particular.

---

Two things happened after that, in the long warm weeks before the first frost.

The first was ordinary. The body they left behind rotted into the bank, and the bank got
rich, and the grass above it grew darker and faster than the grass ten feet away, and by
autumn there was a patch of him — because it was him, the dark patch was him and the blades
in it were his — that stood four inches taller than anything else in the glade and had a
faint blue-silver sheen to it in low light that the other grass did not have.

He had not decided to do that. The thing in him had done it, the way it did everything,
taking the material that was actually there — a dead four-hundred-year body, its years
leaking back into soil that had once been his ground — and turning it into growth. He was,
in the plainest terms available, feeding on the aftermath of the only killing he had ever
witnessed, and he would go on doing versions of that for the rest of his life, and it would
never once occur to him that there was anything strange about it, because a plant does not
choose what falls into its ground.

The second thing was not ordinary.

Frost came early that year. It came down hard in the last week of the turning, a white
night that killed the meadow grass flat and put a skin of ice on the shallows, and in the
morning the whole glade was brown and silver and dead-looking, and the grass by the bank —
his grass, the dark patch, the four inches taller — was still green.

Not dramatically. Not in any way a passing animal would have noticed. But green, when
everything within sight of it was not.

He did not know he had done that either. The thing in him had taken the cold the way it
had taken the water and the light and the dead thing's years, and had measured it, and had
made a small change in the substance of his blades, and the change had cost him — three
weeks of not growing, a third of his root mass given over to something that was not
drinking — and it had held.

The first lesson of his life was not a lesson. It was a habit. *The cold hurt. Do not let
it hurt that way again.*

And the second lesson, which was the one that mattered, was older than the frost and had
been sitting in him since the evening by the water:

*things with years get taken.*

He had no years. That was his whole defence, and it was a good one, and it was going to
last him a hundred and forty years before anything in the world looked down at him twice.

---

*He did not see the second party. They came through the glade eleven days after the first,
walking fast, not hunting, going somewhere — three different men with a Spirit Hall badge
on the lead man's coat, and a rolled map-case, and the brisk irritated air of people doing
a job for somebody else. The lead man stopped at the stream to let the others catch up, and
while he waited he looked across the meadow the way the broad hunter had looked, and then
he looked again.*

*He was not looking at a plant. He was looking at a patch of green in a dead brown field,
and he was doing arithmetic about it, and the arithmetic was small and unimportant and he
did not write it down.*

*But he looked twice. In the whole of the glade's long quiet history, nothing had ever
looked twice at the grass by the water.*

*The man shrugged, said something to the others about the cold, and went on.*

*It was nothing. It was the smallest thing that happens in a forest, a man glancing at a
field on his way to somewhere more important, and it meant nothing at all, and it was the
first entry in a file that would not be opened for four hundred years.*

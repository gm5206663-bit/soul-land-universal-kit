# Chapter 10 — The Second Chair

They came home out of the north in the last of the summer. Spirit City took one look at the woman walking up the basilica steps at his side, with nine rings at her feet and a red one among them, and decided by nightfall that something enormous had happened, and by the next nightfall that no one was going to say what.

He called the Elder Hall for the third morning.

Twenty seats, nineteen of them filled, and he stood in the middle of the floor with the Pope's token in his hand and let the room settle before he spoke, because everything after the first sentence would be shouted about in a hundred corridors by supper.

"I am naming my successor," he said. "Bibi Dong, Saintess of this hall, Titled at twenty, the youngest in any record of the continent, nine rings and one of them red. She takes the chair and the token and the three votes, and I ask the Elder Hall to confirm what I am not really asking."

The silence lasted about as long as a man can hold his breath.

Then the shouting started, and it came from exactly where he had told her it would, the eleven seats whose owners had spent a winter writing letters about a child they wanted dead. He let them shout for a full minute. Then Bibi Dong stood up in the gallery where she had been sitting like furniture, and she came down the stair without hurrying, and she let the nine rings up.

She did not threaten anybody. That was the part he had watched her practise, and the part he loved her for, although he would have been slow to use either word a year ago. She simply stood in the middle of the floor at ninety-five levels of soul power with a hundred-thousand-year ring turning red at her feet and a grey spider breathing over her left shoulder, and she waited, and the shouting died in the order you would expect, loudest first.

"You have three votes," she said, to the eleven, without heat. "Keep them. Use them. I would rather be confirmed by men who voted no than flattered by men who never learned to." She looked around the whole ring. "I was sold to this temple at eight. I know what this chair costs and I know what it buys, and I am not going to worship it. Vote."

They confirmed it nineteen to one, and the one was an old man who said afterwards, to his credit, that he had voted his conscience and would do it again, and she promoted him two offices that same week.

Her first act after the vote took eleven minutes. A grain-tax dispute out of the western diocese came up that same morning, because the hall's machinery does not stop for ceremonies, and she heard both sides and found the clerk's arithmetic crooked in the third column and sent the matter back with the crooked column circled and the clerk's superior named in the margin. The Elder Hall watched a twenty-year-old do in eleven minutes what a committee had failed at for two years, and the last four men who had been doubting the confirmation stopped doubting it, or at least stopped showing.

The token went into her hand in front of the hall, and the chair took her as a chair takes anybody, without comment, and when the room emptied he was still standing where he had stood, and she was sitting where he had sat for eleven years, and neither of them said anything for a while.

"Why," she said at last.

"Three reasons, and I will give you all three because you are past the age of sugar." He sat down on the steps below the chair, which was where he found himself belonging now, and was not displeased about it. "The chair is a tool, and you are the only person alive who will never mistake it for a god. The hall needs a Pope who has been a victim of this hall, because the memory is the only lock that holds. And the third reason is that I am going into the trials, and I want the seat held by someone I would come home to."

"The trials." She said it flat, as she said everything that frightened her. "Your father walked eight of the nine."

"And never passed the ninth, and lives on a hill with a statue and sixty years of patience." He looked up at her. "I am not walking the ninth. I am not asking you to believe that or fear it. I am telling you the door I mean to stop at, before I go in, so that when I come out you can hold me to it."

"Come out at ninety-nine," she said.

"That is the whole plan."

He went up the hill to the Worship Hall the next morning, with a box under his arm, and his father met him in the courtyard with the child on his shoulder, because the child had decided some weeks ago that the grandfather's shoulder was her chair of state and there was no appealing the decision.

She was five months old and she had her mother's grip and his mother's eyes, and she took a fistful of Qian Dao Liu's white hair and crowed like a small drunkard, and the three of them stood in the autumn sun like a painting somebody would have burned a century ago for heresy. The child pulled a white hair loose from the fist she had made of it and studied it at arm's length with the gravity of a scholar, and then ate it, and the grandfather permitted this with the patience of stone.

He gave his father the box at the table afterwards, while the nurse had the child, and the old man opened it as he did everything, completely and without ceremony.

Inside was a book, thin, hand-written, no title. And beside the book, in wax-sealed porcelain, nine pills the colour of storm clouds.

"The book is the core," he said. "The forming of it, the failing of it, the nine nights of pressing and the twin-pull trick, all of it, exactly as I learned it on my own body and taught it to one other person who is now sitting in my chair. It is the only copy. I want it kept here, in this hall, under your hand, and I want it taught to the worship elders one by one, tested on no one, forced on no one, ever. In a hundred years it will be the most valuable book on the continent. Today it is a favour."

Qian Dao Liu turned a page with one finger and read for a long time.

"And the pills."

"For the elders. Three herbs out of the east, ground and bound with whale gum and honey. They will not make anyone extraordinary. They will break a wall or two — a level, maybe two, for a body that has earned its stagnation." He poured the tea. "You have seven titled elders on this hill and four of them have been ninety-one for a decade. Give it to the four. They have waited longest."

The eldest of the four was in the courtyard within the hour, because news travels uphill faster than snow. He had been ninety-one since before the child's mother was born, and he held the storm-grey pill in both hands like a man holding a small bird, and he asked the one question all four of them asked: what does it want from me. Nothing, he told the old man. That is the whole of the medicine. Swallow it, burn two days, and be ninety-two or ninety-three by the new year. The wanting comes out of you, and it always did.

"Why now." The old man closed the book and put both hands on it, and the question underneath the question came out as it always did with him, quiet and straight. "You are giving away a chair, a book, and a fortune in one week, and you are asking me for something. Ask."

"Open the trials for me."

The courtyard went very still, as courtyards do when a decision walks into them.

"I walked eight," Qian Dao Liu said. "I have told you what the eighth felt like. The ninth is not a trial, it is a door, and what stands behind it is not a level. You are ninety-seven with two cores in you and rings no record explains, and I have watched you for a year doing things the angels in that hall do not do." He turned the book a quarter turn, as if offering it back. "Tell me the truth. Are you going to walk nine."

"No," he said. "And I will tell you why I can promise that, which is the only promise of that kind anyone can honestly make. I am not hungry for what is behind the door. I have a chair I just gave away, a woman I intend to come home to, and a daughter on your shoulder who is going to need her father for sixty years. A man with those three things does not walk into a god's mouth. I am going to walk eight, as you did, and take what the eight give, and come down this hill."

His father looked at him for a long moment.

"Then I will open it," the old man said. "At the first snow. And I will be standing outside the statue the whole winter, because that is what the guardian is for, and because I have waited sixty years to see whether anyone in this family comes back down."

He spent his last evening in the city in the Pope's quarters, which used to be his quarters, which was a sentence that amused both of them more than it should have.

She worked late. That was the first thing about being Pope that he had predicted exactly: the chair is mostly reading. He sat by the window with the ledger of the northern dioceses that had followed him home like a loyal dog, and she sat at the desk with the Elder Hall's autumn accounts, and the lamp between them did more work than either.

"You should have kept a vote," she said, without looking up.

"I kept the only one that matters. The one where I get to come home here."

She put the brush down at that and looked at him over the lamp. The red ring was quiet at her feet. The grey spider was asleep on its perch by the window, and Spirit City was doing its ten-thousand nightly sounds below them, and neither of them moved for a while, because there are sentences after which moving would be a mistake.

"Ninety-nine," she said finally. "You come down that hill at ninety-nine, and you come down it walking, and you come down it to me. Those are the terms of this expedition, and I am the Pope now, so they are not requests."

"Terms accepted," he said. "All three. Especially the third."

"There is a fourth term," she said. "The child wakes at dawn. The morning you come down that hill you will take her off my hands before anything else, before your father, before your hall, before your own bath. That is the order of it, and the order is not negotiable."

"The order is the order," he said.

"And when you are done," she said, "you will write this one down too. The book you gave your father. Whatever you learn on that hill that a person can be taught, you will write it down, because you are the only one in this family who writes things down, and I have decided that is the third reason you gave me the chair."

"You have decided correctly."

"One more thing," she said. "I have started writing. The first volume is the hill — what the elders did there, what your father did there, and why you walk it alone in the winter. It will not be flattering to read."

"Write it accurately," he said. "I have never once wanted a flattering copy of anything from you."

"Good," said the Pope, and went back to her accounts, and he went back to his ledger, and the lamp between them burned down to nothing by degrees, and neither of them mentioned the bed, and both of them used it.

The first snow came in the second week of winter.

He walked up the hill before dawn with no cloak, because the cold had stopped mattering to him two ranks ago. His father was waiting in the great hall with the lamps unlit. The angel stood over them both in the grey before sunrise, six wings folded, face quiet, exactly as it had stood for longer than the family had kept records.

Qian Dao Liu did not make a speech. He put his hand on the statue's foot and said one sentence in the old temple tongue that was not a prayer and not a command, and somewhere under the floor of the world a light came on.

The angel's eyes opened. Not a trick of the dawn. The stone eyes opened, and the gold in them was the gold of a sun looked at through closed lids, and the great doors behind the statue, which the hall's own servants believed were a wall, came apart along a seam nobody had ever noticed, and beyond them was not a corridor.

It was a hall with no walls, hung in a night full of stars, six golden pillars holding up nothing, and in the middle of it a smaller angel, grey, and before the grey angel a sword stuck in the floor, grey, six wings folded along the hilt.

The light that came out of that door was cold and clean and enormous, and it lay along the floor of the great hall like water, and it waited.

He stepped through, and the doors came together behind him without a sound, and the star-hall received him as deep water receives a stone. Nine pillars of light stood in a ring around the grey angel and its sword, eight of them near and patient and one of them far, far away at the ring's closed end, and the first pillar was already leaning against his shoulders like a hand made of weather. He walked into it with his nine rings up and his eyes open.

"Eight," his father said, behind him. "The nine pillars will offer you nine. Remember what you promised, and remember that I am standing right here, and go."

He went.

# Chapter 02 — The Codex

Pope Hall smelled of old paper and holy light. The desk was stone and heavy under his hands, carved with the six wings that watched everything in this hall without ever blinking. Behind it the angel statues stood with their swords down, and light came through the high windows in slow shafts with dust turning inside them.

He sat, and let the body settle. Chest warm. Dantian solid — the new core turning with its patient swallow, drinking the world's power in, giving it back stronger. The six wings showed faint behind the chair, white-gold at the edges. The nine rings — two yellow, two purple, five black — moved with his breath. None of it hung on him anymore. It was all inside.

Ninety-six: one level above the man the Hall remembered, and worth that man a hundred times over. The world had no scale for him yet. He did not need one. He needed pages.

He picked up the brush, dipped it, and wrote the first sheet of the first book.

He began with kinds.

Tools: hammers, blades, axes — souls that came to farming families and ended up making history. Beasts: the old, familiar shapes, tiger and wolf and dragon, because a beast soul was only the outline of something that had already lived and been terrible. Bodies: rare, the few whose own flesh became the soul's channel and carried no weapon at all. Elements: fire, water, wind, earth, light, darkness — the plain forces, spread unevenly and understood poorly. And last, up in the thin air where almost nobody reached: the celestial bodies. Sun. Moon. Star.

Then the grades. Low to God, seven steps of a ladder most of the continent climbed blind.

He paused at one name longer than the rest.

The Clear Sky Hammer. Every schoolchild under two empires could recite it like a prayer: strongest weapon soul under heaven. It was not. True rank, Top — and it was what it was because of the men around it. Generations had taught that hammer to be terrible. Technique stacked on technique, art on art, until the metal outlived its own grade. A stronger soul in careless hands would have lost to it. He wrote the truth exactly, and his brush did not hesitate. Somewhere south of Spirit City there was a valley full of that family. The sentence would travel. Let it.

He wrote the rings next.

White for a decade. Yellow for a century. Purple for a thousand years; black for ten thousand; red — the red — for a hundred thousand, which was old enough to be something more than a beast, and was called so: a great soul beast. Past the red: orange at two hundred thousand years, and gold at a million. The books named three beasts in all the ages that had reached a million, and he wrote the number three down, because a number that small teaches more than a legend.

Then he wrote what a ring was worth in skills, which no book in the Hall's library carried. One skill, up to ten thousand years. Two, at a hundred thousand. Four, at a million. A hunter who did not know that would trade a red ring for three black ones and go home believing he had won. Then the mechanism none of the old broken books had ever once explained. When a beast that old died to a successful hunter, it left a bone behind, and the bone did not go to the parts of the body the hunter already owned. It filled what was empty. He wrote it plainly: what you were missing was what you were offered.

Then bones. Six places a bone could sit — head, torso, both arms, both legs — and the rare growths outside the six, which could grow with their owner and never stopped being rare. He ranked the six, because a man who will only ever hold one should know which one to pray for: the torso first, the head next, then the arms, and the legs last. He wrote the odds too. One beast in a thousand leaves a bone at all. A bone once absorbed came out only when its owner died — unless a Titled Douluo paid ten levels to cut a limb and tear it free. He wrote the prices too. A book that teaches without prices is a book that lies.

Then the two columns everyone forgot: body and spirit.

Physique — how much of a ring a body could carry before it tore itself on its own ambition, and why a high ring should be fed to a body that could hold it. And spiritual power — how wide a man's spirit ran, and which wall each width could open. He gave the ranges in plain words. The world had no names for them yet, and would not have believed names. The spirit every child is born with. The spirit that can be held and turned, where control begins. The spirit wide as a sea, where an expert begins and the sixth and seventh rings become possible. The abyss, where most men stop and where a Titled Douluo stands. And above the abyss a domain of its own — the ground a man must stand on before the highest walls will open. He kept the ladder's names to himself. A word nobody has yet is a word nobody believes. He wrote nothing at all about cores.

That was deliberate. There were truths a continent could be handed, and truths a continent would break itself on, and the core belonged to the second kind until the ground under it had been raised. He turned his pages past it silently, not absent — he meant to come back.

What he left out was longer than what he put in, and he knew every line of it by heart. No cores. Nothing of the beasts' own road, or the towers that would one day bargain for their souls. Nothing of the god seats, or how few of them there are, or which of them stood empty and waiting. Nothing of the twenty thousand years he had read to the end of. He wrote the ground and kept the sky, and he did it because a man who hands out the sky on the first day is a man nobody listens to on the second.

He wrote how to choose a ring.

Compatibility first: the Angel's light would have nothing to do with poison, and a poison soul should keep its distance from light. Absorption second — and here he gave the numbers, because numbers are the one part of this art a poor man can use. A first ring from a beast under four hundred and twenty-three years. A second under seven hundred and sixty-four. A third under eighteen hundred. A fourth under five thousand. A fifth at twelve thousand. A sixth at twenty-five thousand. A seventh between thirty and fifty thousand. An eighth between fifty and a hundred thousand. And the ninth from a beast of a hundred thousand years or more. Under the numbers he wrote the shape the ages had settled on: three purple, five black, one red. And last, the rule the Hall itself had been breaking for a century — quality over quantity. The old Hall had swallowed rings for decades and mistaken appetite for strength; the book would teach it to eat with a purpose.

Then three rules that were in no book anywhere on the continent.

A beast soul is the stronger of the two until the true body comes at seventy. After that the tool soul is the stronger, because a tool takes the soul into itself and a beast only wears it.

A plant soul does not have to take a plant beast's ring, and a beast soul may take a plant's. The ring answers to what the man is missing, not to what the beast was.

And a man stopped at a bottleneck has not stopped. Without a ring he cannot step up a stage, but the power keeps filling in behind the wall, and all of it shows on the day the ring arrives.

Near the front, where a poorer writer would have put a preface, he put the thing the Hall had been teaching wrong for a century. A weak soul is not a dead end. A weak soul with the right ring, the right bone and a chosen direction outlives a strong soul that has none of the three. What the continent called a trash soul was almost always a soul nobody had bothered to read.

He wrote one last thing, at the back, where a reader would find it only after he had earned it.

There is a thing above skills. A field of a man's own, which he carries into the fight with him and which no ring gives and no bottle buys. Almost nobody has one. Among Titled Douluo, only a handful ever do. He did not write how it was won, because he did not intend to hand that to anyone yet. He wrote only that it existed — so that men would stop believing the ninth skill was the end of the road.

He wrote until his hand hurt.

Then the ink ran out and he called for more; then the paper stack grew, sheet under sheet, until it stood beside the desk like a small white wall.

Ghost came in near the end of it, black and silent, and bowed.

"His Holiness. The elders are waiting."

He nodded and stood. The papers stayed. Chest warm, dantian solid, core turning — he walked out through the corridors with that quiet radiance around him, and the servants stepped aside twice as fast as they used to.

The Elder Hall was round and cold even in summer, twenty stone seats in a ring, seven raised at the front. Qian Daoliu's seat stood empty; the Great Worship rarely left his statue. Golden Crocodile sat at the front of the front — old and scarred, ninety-eight, one step from the last wall — with eyes like a man who had buried every opponent he ever respected.

Chrysanthemum and Ghost took their places behind the Pope's seat.

He walked in, and the chamber felt him arrive. Six wings faint behind, white-gold; the aura dense and heavy like the Seraphim line at its best. Golden Crocodile lifted his eyes slowly, and something crossed the old man's face that a lifetime of war had taught him to hide at once — but it had crossed. He had never in ninety-eight years felt a holy aura sit like this one sat: a core in the chest, turning, solid. He did not have the word for it, and not having the word disturbed him more than the aura did. He was an old warrior. He wanted to learn it. That want could wait.

Whispers moved around the ring and died.

He sat down in the Pope's seat and let the room come to order.

"I have called you for a while," he said. "I plan to make strict rules, and end the corruption in the Hall. That is the first foundation."

Silence went through the round hall like cold air.

An elder with a gray beard leaned forward. "His Holiness, corruption—"

Another cut in over him, from further down the ring: "Spirit Hall has always—"

Chrysanthemum glanced at Ghost. Corruption touched many seats in this ring. Some elders took offerings from the branch halls; some sold soul-beast sightings to the highest purse; and every man in the ring knew which seats the others meant. The Pope held three votes. Enough elders speaking one way together could unseat him. Everyone had read the same page of the same law.

Golden Crocodile raised one hand, and the hall went quiet.

"Let His Holiness speak," he said. His voice was old and hoarse and used to being obeyed. "What strict rules?"

He laid it out for them.

"Branch halls take extra taxes. Main Halls sell hunting spots. Elders take cuts of both. That weakens the Hall. The Hall is sacred to soul masters because the Pope Hall and the Douluo Hall stand in it — because the dead are honored here and the living are taught here. If the ones teaching are corrupt, the people stop believing. If they stop believing —" He let it sit a moment. "— the Angel God loses faith."

He did not say the other reason. He kept that folded where it lived: in this world, belief had weight. Faith stored up in one place, and a Hall a whole continent truly believed in might, in time, raise something out of it. He did not intend to leave that lying unclaimed. But it stayed in his chest, and what he said aloud was only the truth anyone could check: the Angel God needed pure faith.

The debate took hours. The corrupt seats fought it — some loudly, some by refusing to say anything at all; the clean seats held, because a Hall with its rot cut out is stronger than a Hall that smells of rot, and because they could feel what stood at the head of the room now. At the end of it, the first foundation passed: strict rules, corruption on the block, first step only. No one pretended it was the whole road. A tree that old does not fall in one vote; you take it with axes, over years.

The Pope's three votes had not decided anything. The mood had. And the mood had been decided by the aura standing at the head of the room — that, and one old scarred man on the front benches saying, "Faith. Angel God needs pure faith. Corruption weakens faith. I support."

Azure Luan and Light Feather, two of the seven, glanced at each other and nodded. Six of the seven Worshipers sat in for the vote that day; the seventh seat stayed empty, its owner in the Worship Hall with the statue as always. But his weight was in the room anyway. The Hall knew where the Great Worship's son had just walked, and the Hall had opinions about the light that had come off the Worship Hall roof that month.

He walked back to the Pope Hall through the afternoon, and he wrote again.

The second book was the guild. Open to all — not only the Hall's branches, not only the capitals: any soul master, every city. Branches on the model of the Hall's own, but with their doors turned outward and their purpose written on the wall: true knowledge, shared. The Hall would gain a new name out of it — not only power, but truth; a place where the continent could ask a question and receive an answer that was not a lie. Reputation was a crop that grew slowly, and he had fifty years of practicing patience before he ever got here. And under the guild's honest purpose, the folded reason stayed folded: every soul master who learned from this Hall, and believed it, put one more grain into the granary.

He wrote the guild's shape with the same brush he had used for decrees: headquarters in Spirit City, under the three halls; branches in the two empire capitals, then every major city, then the medium ones; membership open to anyone who came through the gate honestly. In time, more. In time, everything else.

Then he finished the first volume — the stack went still and took the ink like a seal takes wax — and he called Ghost once more.

"Copy this," he said. "Worship Hall first, then the Elder Hall, then the Spirit Holy Halls in both capitals, then the Main Halls. Let them read it. Then let it be public."

Ghost bowed, took the stack under his cloak, and was gone in the manner he had: black folding into black.

Next: Bibi Dong.

He knocked at the locked room and got no answer, and let himself in quietly. She was on the bed with the blanket around her shoulders, knees up again, back to the wall — the same wall, the same posture, a little less pain in the set of her mouth than yesterday, because the healing had settled. She had not eaten much. Yesterday's tray sat on the table by the door, mostly untouched, which he had expected.

He stood in the doorway, three steps short of the bed, and said the only thing there was to say at a doorway like this one.

"You need to eat."

She did not answer. He set the new tray beside the old one — warm soup, bread, another small bottle. Then the pouch, folded small: safe-house money again, more of it, and pills from the Qian family's own quiet stores.

"Resources will be provided," he said. "The guards are for safety, not punishment. No one enters without your permission but food and medicine. If you need anything, tell them."

She looked at the tray, and then at the little bottle, and something crossed her face that was not thanks and not trust — only the first confusion of a person who has been handed an impossible thing and cannot find the drawer it should have come from. The old man in these robes would not have brought soup. The old man in these robes would have brought orders.

She did not say it. She turned her face back to the wall.

He closed the door softly behind him, gave the guards the orders again so they would hear them twice and pass them to the next shift, and walked back under the angel statues to the desk.

He sat down and started the second volume.

It would be about the things the first book only pointed at: how a soul and a ring find their match, what a body must be before it can hold the years it carries, the ranges of the spirit written out to the last point. The pages had been waiting in him for years, in two lives' worth of ways — and the brush moved, and the ink moved, and the light through the high windows went from white to amber to gone. The angel statues went dark. Only his chest light stayed, warm as a banked sun.

The first volume was already moving through the Hall under a black cloak. Before the week was out, the Worship Hall and the Elder Hall would both have read it, and after that the capitals — and after that, the rumor of the book would outrun the book itself, and that was exactly how he wanted it.

Let the continent read it. Let it argue. Let the truth do what truth does, in its own time, while the Hall quietly became a place worth believing in.

He wrote on into the night.

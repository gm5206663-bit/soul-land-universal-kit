# Chapter 04 — The Second Core

The small court behind the Pope's quarters had flagstones, one bare tree, and a well nobody used. He had chosen it for the quiet, and after the first week nobody came to it but the old arms master and, when she chose to, the woman on the wall.

Winter came down on Spirit City early that year. The plains wind turned hard, and the city's smoke ran flat overhead. In the guild's rooms the second list grew longer every week — nine cities, then eleven — and the letters that came with it were shorter than the first letters had been, which he counted as progress. The book had done its work. The work had its own legs now.

He read the letters by lamplight and answered the ones that needed him. Then he sat back, and let himself look at the thing he had not been looking at since the month under the Worship Hall.

The month had left him two things. One was the core, turning low in him, warm and steady as a banked sun. The other was a sentence he had kept folded since the day of the pillar: somewhere past it, faint as a promise, an embryo of the second.

He knew what a second center was worth. In the book he had read to its end, the men and beasts who carried two were few enough to count, and every one of them stood at the top of his own age. A beast-king first, then a boy with eyes that saw power itself, ages away. No one in this age carried even one center. He carried one. Two would make him something the era had no measure for.

The book was exact about the cost, and he had learned to respect exact things. The second center could be laid beside the first, turning the same direction — slow, safe, and half a gift. Or it could be set against it, turning the other way. Then the two would fight from the first breath to the last, and what they threw off in that fight would be his to keep, or to die of. That was the road the strong ones had walked. The book was exact about one more thing: a man who meant to grow a second center needed a piece of himself that was not the first. His angel was warm and round and holy. A second core cut from the angel's own cloth would only double what he already carried, and doubling was not the thing.

He needed an edge. He had one. He had always had one, the same as he had always had wings. The holy sword came out of its golden spiral and lay in his hand, light that had learned manners. And it was a skill. It answered when he called, cut where he sent it, and went out like a lamp when the call ended. A skill was obedient and dead. He needed a thing that was his past the end of his own arm.

The old lives agreed on the road, which was rare. In the book's later years there had been an old man of the Seven Treasures who walked his blade like a road, until the world gave up and named him for it: Sword Dao, because nothing else fit. The Hall's oldest sword hand said it in worse words, standing in the cold of the small court with a stick under his arm.

"A man and a sword are a marriage, Your Holiness," he said. "Use it like a tool and it stays a tool. Feed it your mornings and it becomes yours."

"You will teach me the mornings, then," the Pope said.

"Ten thousand times," the old man said. "Or don't bother."

So the court filled with his mornings. The old man taught him the plain work, the work that had no light in it at all: the ten cuts, the footwork, the breath. Out with the edge, and back with the recovery. He corrected the Pope's feet with the stick for eight days. Then he stopped coming, because there was nothing left in the feet to correct, and because he had never once liked watching religion. When the Pope asked, the old man said, "You have stopped needing the stick, and I have stopped liking the cold." That was the end of the lessons, and by then the lessons did not matter.

The holy sword came when he called it. He cut with it until the cutting was boring, and then he cut some more. The first thousand cuts were easy, and the light went out at the end of every one of them, punctual as a clerk, leaving nothing behind. The problem was never power. He had more power than the sword could drink. The problem was that the sword was there only while he was there. It began and ended with him, and a thing that begins and ends with you cannot hold the far side of a fight against you.

The year's cold deepened. Frost came into the court and stayed. His breath stood in the air with the light and both went out together. He kept the Hall and the guild moving through it — the third list began, then the fourth; two branch halls sent back money nobody had caught them taking; the academy wrote twice. He read the reports at night with his wrists aching, and slept, and was in the court again before the bells.

One morning the physician came and stood at the edge of the court until he finished the tenth cut. The Saintess was well, the physician said. The child was well. "Due when the cold breaks," he said. "Warm room, clean water, and no politics standing by the bed. The Saintess will do the rest." He said it in the flat voice of a man who had delivered a thousand children in a thousand villages and had no politics in him at all. The Pope paid him from the family purse, as always, and the physician went away, and the ten cuts went on.

The Saintess walked the wall some mornings. She had the whole length of it these months, and her color had come back, and she kept to the same slow loop she had walked in the first weeks when the loop was all she had. One morning she stopped above the court while he was cutting. He went on with the form. The sword did its punctual work. The light died and came back ten times. Then, from the wall, in her flat academy voice, she read a sentence off the morning.

"It is not the light," she said.

"No," he said.

She watched one more cut, and then she went on with her loop, and neither of them said anything else. It was more conversation than most weeks gave them, and he put it away with the other things he did not spend.

It ended on an evening of wet snow.

He was on the ninth cut, tired past tiredness. He was doing the plain work with nothing in his head but the edge and the breath. Then he felt the blade decide. That was the only word he had for it afterward, and he did not like the word much, but it was the true one. His hand took the stroke where every other day it had taken it. The light came with it, and then the light went on a hand's width past the end of the stroke, and finished it — a half-breath late, exactly right.

He stood in the wet and looked at the golden sword hanging where his stroke had ended. It did not go out. He counted to ten. At ten he reached and took the hilt, and the sword lay across his palm with the light steady along its whole length, and it had weight. Not the weight of metal. The weight of a thing that is still there when you are not looking.

He gave it the only test that mattered. He set the point on the flagstone and let go.

The sword stood. Snow landed on the light and melted and ran off the edge in a line so straight it looked drawn. He walked around it twice. It had no eye and no voice, and it needed neither. It knew the next cut before his hand did, and it stayed where he left it, aimed at the world, patient as a held breath.

That night he learned what he had actually done. The strength he carried between his brows — the deep sea of it, higher than any Qian before him had carried — did not all come home when he set the sword down. A thread of it stayed in the blade. He could feel the thread like a warm wire running from the sea behind his forehead, down his arm, out along the light. The sword was no longer a skill he spent. It was a place part of him lived.

The old man from the book had spent sixty years arriving at that and had, in the end, called it nothing but the sword. He understood the man now. He did not have a better word, and he did not need one.

He went down to the secret chamber under the Worship Hall on the first day of the last month of the year. He told no one. He took water, and he took the sword.

The first center sat low in him, warm and wide, turning its one way since the month it was born. The thread he drew up slowly, hand over hand, into the sea between his brows. There he began the second — small, and cold, and set to turn against the first. The book had marked this road for the strong, and had not pretended it was kind.

The first day was pressure. His bones rang with it, and his heartbeat learned to arrive twice, once for each center, close together and wrong. The second center did not want to exist. He kept it turning anyway. In the evening his nose bled and he did not stop.

The second day it nearly came apart. For one breath he was two people with two heartbeats, and the world split into two versions of itself an inch apart, and the taste of blood came up hot and wrong. The thread in the sword pulled straight. He took the hilt in both hands — not to use it; to be told the line — and the sword gave him its straightness, the plain line a beam gives a roof, and he poured the second turn along it, and the breath passed, and he went on.

On the third day the two centers began to fight in earnest, because that was the design. The warm one pushed down and out; the cold one pushed up and in; between them, at the crossing, the strength of him ground against itself. He sat in the middle of the grinding with his eyes open and his jaw set and kept both turns true. Twice he thought of stopping. Both times he counted to ten and did not, and afterward he could not have said which ten it was.

On the fourth day the fighting changed sound. The two turns began to shed something at the crossing that was neither warm nor cold, and the shedding fed him through channels that no one had ever fed. The old blood in him, proud as it was, pushed once at the little cold thing and did not like it. Then the stream reached down and fed the warm center too. Proud things eat. The angel stopped arguing.

On the fifth day the second center stopped moving. It was small, the size of a plum pit. It was cold, like the far side of a fire. It turned the wrong direction, and the wrongness was the whole point. He stood up, slowly, in a room that smelled of salt and holy light, and both centers answered. Warm under the ribs. Cold between the brows. Between them, a circulation that had never once existed in this world.

He sat down again with the sword across his knees until his heart found its single beat. His nose had bled at some point and dried. He had not noticed when.

He came up at night. The Hall was asleep, and the snow was coming down in the dark, quiet and busy. For five nights a light had stood over the Worship Hall. The priests had counted it and written it in the day-book, and no one had claimed it, and the Elder Hall had decided, by the particular silence it kept, that the matter was the Worship Hall's business and not theirs.

The Great Worship came to the courtyard door once, his hands behind his back. He stood and looked at the sword where it rested against the chair, plain now as a walking stick, for a long time. He asked nothing. He did not cross the threshold. He had done the same in the month of the first core, and the Pope had expected it; some doors in that family were never crossed, only stood at. When the old man was gone, the light in the courtyard seemed to him no dimmer, and the silence no heavier, and both of those were answers too.

The rest of the month went back to its ordinary shape: lists, ledgers, letters, and the sword by the chair.

On the last evening of the year he sat at the desk with the window open to the cold and did the sum, once, coldly.

The angel at ninety-six, six wings, purer than any of the blood had ever run. The first center, low and warm. The second, between his brows, thin and straight as a blade's edge. Both turns of one world, and the whole of him strengthened twice over by the argument between them. In the book, a man with two centers was called a thing his age had not seen, and no wall stood in front of him until the heights. He looked for the man in this era who could come at him now, below the gods. He could not find him. He did not say so aloud, and he did not write it down. It was not for saying. It was for being true.

The door at the end was still a door. He had not walked through it, and nothing that winter had opened it, and he did not pretend otherwise, even alone. But the door no longer had anyone else's footprints in front of it. The stairs up to it were his now.

Outside, the year was ending. The cold would hold for a while yet. The child was due when it broke. On the desk the fourth list had reached thirteen cities, and the guild's readers were in half of them, and somewhere in a noodle shop a teacher was putting six characters on a board for children who would otherwise have had nothing.

He put out the lamp and left the sword where it stood. Under his ribs and between his brows, turning against each other, the two centers kept their long argument, quiet as a household. He slept in the middle of it. That, he had decided, was the whole art: to sleep in the middle of it, and be the house.

# Chapter 03 — The First Cut

Pope Hall kept its own weather. Autumn came down the plains and shook Spirit City; inside, the corridors held the same warm stone, the same lamp oil, the same slow light on the angel statues' wings.

Five weeks since the first volume went out.

The book had left the Hall quietly — grey binding, plain title, no seal on the cover — and it had come back loud. In the two capitals, men read it in academy rooms and argued until the lamps died. In the Main Halls, men read it behind closed doors, and the letters that followed had careful hands and wet seals. Three hundred li east of Heaven Dou City, the family whose hammer the book described sent no letter at all. The Hall took the silence for an answer, and left it alone.

The letters that did come were plain business. The Star Luo capital wrote for three more copies and paid in gold. The Heaven Dou academy wrote for one and argued about the price. And one branch hall in the west sent back money it had not been caught taking — an old tax, collected twice in a hungry year, returned now without a confession attached. Chrysanthemum read that one twice and put it on the desk without a word.

In Spirit City, the guild's pilot room had opened in a side hall under the Elder Hall: ten rows of chairs, a stove, and a lectern cut down from an old door. He had opened it to three people and no ceremony, on the theory that a thing which starts loud must keep shouting to stay alive. It had started quiet. By the fifth week there was a list on the door and a line on the stairs.

One morning in that room, a master with gray in his braid asked how a man should choose a fifth ring when his body was old and his spirit still young. The clerk at the lectern leafed through the first volume, found the page, and read it out — no flourish, no commentary, just the page. The old master sat a while with his hands on his knees. Then he stood, and bowed to the book, which embarrassed everyone in the room, and then he bowed to the clerk, which rescued them.

That scene was argued about for a week in the tea houses, by men who had not been there. The book had done something to people's arithmetic. A man who had been quiet for twelve years was about to prove it.

He watched the room once from the high window and did not go down. A Pope's face changes a room. That room had been built to stand with no one at its head; the whole point of it was that he could stand at the window instead.

On the thirty-third night, someone left a packet at the guild's door.

The night watch brought it up to Ghost, because the guild had one rule after dark: anything left at that door went through black hands before it went to paper. Ghost laid it on the desk and stood back from it like a man stepping away from a letter with no seal.

"No seal," he said. "No name on the string. Scholar's ink. Cheap paper."

He untied the string himself.

Inside was one leaf of paper, copied in a careful hand, and a note folded twice.

The leaf was a page of numbers. Every hall kept two sets of them: the ones that traveled up to Spirit City, and the ones that happened. This leaf claimed to be a copy of one page from the receipt book of the Main Hall at Chenghe — a real page, copied exactly, down to a small crooked figure in the corner where the hall's clerk kept his private tally.

The note was three sentences under a plain beginning. "The true book has three columns. The book you were sent has two. I copied the first page of the true one." Signed: "A teacher. Twelve years at that hall. I am not brave. I am old, and I am finished being quiet."

Three columns.

The first was taxes. The second was the hunting rosters — the good soul-beast grounds, listed by name and sold to families ahead of the public lists, a price written in the margin beside each one. The third had no heading at all. It was fees: paid by the families of children who had failed their awakening, so the names would not be read out at the gate.

The three columns added up to twenty-one percent more than the number that had traveled up to Spirit City. For eleven months. In the same direction, at the same size, every month.

Of the difference, two parts went to the hall's steward. One part went to the seat above.

The seat above the Main Hall at Chenghe belonged to an old elder of the round hall.

He sent for the file the Hall of Accounts kept on Chenghe — eleven months of clean summaries, every column adding to the last copper the city had sent up. He read the file beside the leaf. Both sums were honest. Only one of them had happened.

He sat with the leaf a while. Not with the numbers — fifty years in this Hall had taught the body where rot gathers, and the memory had kept every lesson. It was the third column he sat with. A failed child's name had a price, and the Hall had been selling the quiet for years.

Then he did the thing he would do every time after this, so that the Hall could learn the shape of him: he did not move alone, and he did not move angry.

The law came first, because the law was the whole game. The Pope could not cut a steward, or a Main Hall, or an elder. The round hall of the Elder Hall decided — and the round hall held the seat he was aiming at. He had three votes of his own, a stack of paper, and one night's head start.

He used the night.

"Seal the books of the Main Hall at Chenghe before the criers go out," he told Ghost. "Two clerks from the Hall of Accounts as witnesses. Take nothing but paper. Frighten no one except the steward, and not him much."

The black was already thinning at the edges.

One more thing went into the dark with him: if the books had been cleaned, the leaves would be old and the thread new. Thread remembers.

Then he was gone.

To Chrysanthemum he gave the road: ride before the criers, tell them nothing, and bring the clerk — the one who kept a tally in the corner of his pages. The Hall was asking, not accusing. He was to make that true.

Chrysanthemum rode at first light.

The Elder Hall met at noon, and by then everyone in the ring already knew. That was the point of the night. Seals do not whisper; they only have to be seen.

The round hall was cold even in autumn, and the ring filled without sound. Twenty stone seats; the seven raised at the front; the Great Worship's seat empty as always, its owner in the Worship Hall with his statue. The whispers that had been running all morning died when the leaf came out, wrapped in plain paper. He laid it on the round stone and sat down.

The elder was built low and wide like a wall, and he did not hurry his face. "A copied page is a story," he said. "Any clerk with a grudge and a steady hand can write a story."

"Then read the true books," he said. "Your hall's books are on the table. Protocol had them carried in. So read them."

The books came out — two heavy volumes in their cloth wraps, laid beside the leaf with the care of men who wanted everything on the record. They were clean. The totals on the pages matched the totals that had traveled up, to the last copper.

Golden Crocodile leaned forward. He was the oldest body in the ring and had been sitting in it since before most of the seats had titles. He turned the copied leaf in his fingers, then opened the volume to the same month and held the two pages up to the light, one in either hand, reading the paper with his thumb as much as with his eyes.

"This copy's paper is five years old," he said at last. "The ink has gone brown at the edge. This book's leaf is five years old too — and the ink on it is five days old. Someone taught an old book new numbers."

He set both pages down and folded his old hands.

Then he called for the clerk.

The clerk was in the corner of the hall with his hands inside his sleeves, because a seat's books travel with their keeper, and the keeper of these books had come up from Chenghe on a borrowed horse and no sleep. He was a thin man of forty with a spot of ink on his cuff, and he stood like a man who had spent the ride rehearsing being struck.

He asked the clerk the small questions first. Every page his? Yes. The small tight figure in the corner — did he draw it?

"I do," he said. "Every page. A clerk counts twice in his life — once for the book, and once for himself."

Then he told the clerk to find his month in the book and say which page was his.

The clerk crossed the round floor and looked down at the clean page for the fifth month of the year — the month he had kept, the month he had signed with his own small mark a hundred times over eleven years.

He looked a long time. Then he straightened, and his voice came out steady, which was the bravest thing done in that hall in years.

"That page is not mine," he said. "I never made it. I have never seen those numbers."

The ring went quiet.

The elder spoke once more, and it cost him something to do it evenly. "The steward is a thief," he said. "I am a man who trusted one. The ring may judge that as it likes."

The ring judged. Nine hands rose for the audit, the removal, and one new page of rules. Four stayed down. The rest studied the ceiling and let the arithmetic pass without them.

The ring had also watched how it was done — slowly, in daylight, on paper, with the accused seat's own voice given its turn. Whatever else the seats took home that noon, they took home the method. The next cut would cost less to make than this one.

The steward's robe was taken; he kept his life and his name and nothing else. The fields would take him, as they had taken every thief the Hall had ever stripped of a branch. The seat stayed — no hand had been proven, and the Hall was not yet in the business of punishing men for the crime of trusting. What the seat lost was quieter. The Main Hall at Chenghe would keep its books open to guild readers for five years, and the elder would sign every page of that arrangement with his own name, once a year, in front of the ring.

Three days later the guild posted its first reader to Chenghe — a young clerk with a satchel of blank paper and four orders that fit on one page: count honestly, write plainly, answer every question, take no silver.

The new page of rules was short. Any seat whose hall was found falsified answered for the falsification, whether or not a hand could be proven on the pen. It passed in the same hour, while the embarrassment was still fresh enough to vote with. The elder abstained. He did not put his hand against a rule that already had his name on it.

He had wanted the law to be a blade that could cut the hand holding it. That was the only kind that lasted. A blade that cuts only downward is a whip, and whips make noise; they do not make halls clean.

The teacher took four days to find — not guards' work, clerk's work. A note that says twelve years at one hall is a note a good clerk can walk. Ghost found him in a rented room above a noodle shop: an old man with his robe already on, reading by a window, easy to find on purpose.

He had expected a cell. He was brought up the long stairs to the Pope Hall, through two wings and a courtyard, and delivered — which frightened him worse — not to a judgment seat but to the guild's pilot room, where a lectern had been cut from a door and a chair had been set behind it for the first time that morning.

He came in with his copy-bundle held against his chest: the rest of the true book, eleven years of it, copied page by page in his own careful hand and carried every step of the road like a sick child.

He was told that his twelve years were counted, and asked whether he would teach here now.

The old man asked what he would teach.

"Truth," he was told. "And how to write it down."

The old man stood with the bundle in his arms. Then he put it on the table and sat down behind the lectern, and began — page by page, in a dry, even voice — to read his city's numbers into the record.

Afterward, the guild's first teacher wrote two things on its first board. His name, and the date. Under them, six characters: "I was quiet for twelve years." Then he sat down again and let the room fill.

That evening the Pope crossed the yard between the two halls.

The plum trees along the east wall were bare. The Saintess was walking the loop of the yard — one turn or four, he did not count, and the guards kept their distance and did not count for him. He walked the same direction she did, which was chance, and they passed at ten steps.

She did not stop. He did not stop. She said one thing into the middle distance, flat, to no one he could see: "The pills work."

He nodded once. That was all. Words across that distance were doors, and doors wanted handles; none had been offered, so he kept walking. Behind him her steps went on around the yard. He did not slow down to listen, and he did not let himself count the turns.

He had paid the doctor that week — an old country man with no seat in any hall's factions, paid from the Qian family's private purse and not from the Hall's. The child was due when the cold broke. The paper in the drawer said nothing else about her, and he left it at that.

Back at the desk, he read the rest.

Eleven years of numbers from one city. He copied them out into his own columns, on his own paper, because a man who means to cut should measure first. Then he set them beside the files the Hall of Accounts kept for three more cities — and the shape came up again, the same each time. A steward's share. A seat's share. A quiet column with no heading.

The last page of the bundle ended without a name.

"The one at the top," the old teacher had said, when Ghost asked. "I know it. I will not put it on paper until the first one has been done properly."

He squared the pages against the desk and lit the lamp. Then he took a fresh sheet from the pile, drew three columns, and began the second list.

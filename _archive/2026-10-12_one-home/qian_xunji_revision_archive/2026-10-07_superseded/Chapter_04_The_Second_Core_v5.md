# Chapter 04 — The Second Core

The small court behind the Pope's quarters had one bare tree, a well nobody drew from, and flagstones older than the Hall above them. He had taken it for the quiet, and by the second week of winter nobody came there at all — except, some mornings, the woman on the wall, when she chose to walk it.

Winter came down on Spirit City hard and all at once, as it always did, and the letters came down with it. Nine cities, then eleven. The guild's rooms smelled of ink and stove smoke, and the reports from the readers had grown shorter than the first ones, which he counted as progress. A reader who could say little was a reader the cities already trusted. The book had done its work, and the work had legs of its own now.

He read the letters by lamplight and answered the ones that needed him. Then he sat back and let himself look at the thing he had not looked at since the month under the Worship Hall.

That month had left him two things. One sat low in him, warm and steady, like a fire banked against the night. The other was a sentence he had folded small and kept ever since: past the first core, faint as a promise, there was an embryo of the second.

He laid the two roads out in his mind, plainly, as the book laid them out.

The second core could be set beside the first, turning in the same direction — slow, safe, and half of what it could give. Or it could be set against the first, turning the other way, and then the two would fight from the first breath to the last, and the strength the fight threw off would be his to keep, or his to die of. Every strong one in the book had walked the second road, and the book had not pretended that road was kind.

It was exact about one more rule, and the rule was the whole problem. A man who meant to grow a second core needed a piece of himself that was not the first.

He tested the rule before he trusted it. One night, alone in the court, he drew on the warm turning of the first core and spun a second turn out of the same stuff — small, a seed, nothing more. It took, which surprised him. It stood apart for one long count, which pleased him. Then it slid. It did not fight him. It simply would not stand, and it settled back into the first core's turning like water finding an old channel, and all it cost him was an hour and a nosebleed.

That road was closed. The book had said so, and now his own body had said the same, and bodies did not lie.

He needed an edge — a piece of himself that was not the angel. He had one. He had always had one, the same as he had always had wings.

The holy sword came out of its golden spiral and lay in his hand: light that had learned manners. And it was a skill. It answered when he called it, cut where he sent it, and went out like a lamp when the call ended. A skill was obedient, and a skill was dead. What he needed was a thing that would still be his past the end of his own arm.

There was no teacher for it in this world. No swordsman alive had walked where the second core would have to be walked, none of them could be told about it, and the book could not be argued with. So it came down to the book and the court, and to the same road every swordsman had ever walked — mornings. Many mornings. The plain work, the work with no light in it at all.

The ten cuts. The footwork. The breath. Out with the edge, back with the recovery. The feet had never been his problem; he had carried a blade his whole life, and the book's flat words had little to teach him about them. He set his feet against them anyway, and he cut.

He cut until cutting was boring, and then he cut more, because boredom was not the enemy. Boredom was the door. The first thousand cuts went easily, and at the end of every one of them the light went out on time and left nothing behind. Power had never been his problem either — he carried more spiritual power than the sword could drink.

The problem was that the sword lived only while he lived inside the moment of it. It began with his call and ended with his hand, and a thing that begins and ends with you cannot hold the far side of a fight that has not happened yet.

So he stopped letting it end.

It began as a test on a dull morning: one heartbeat of light, held past the close of the stroke. The stroke finished and he did not let go. The light stood an inch beyond where it should have died, his arm sang with the effort, and he released it and stood there breathing like a man who had just set down a loaded mule.

Three holds that morning. Seven before the week was out. At seven his arm rang like a struck bell, so he stopped at seven, came back in the afternoon, and did it again.

The holding left something behind that plain cutting never had. Not strength — he was no stronger than he had been. It was a warmth that stayed near his hand after the light went out, the warmth still in a cup after the tea is drunk. He could find the sword in the dark now without calling it. Through the deep middle of the winter the warmth became a thread, and the thread ran from the spiritual sea between his eyebrows, down his arm, out along the light — and it stayed two breaths after every light in the court was gone.

The sword had stopped being a thing he spent. It was becoming a thing he kept.

The guild came to him twice that month, in the side room under the Elder Hall, with the fourth list in its hands.

"Thirteen cities," the reader said. "Six with our own people in them. Chenghe wants a second room and offers to pay for the stove."

"Give them the stove," he said. "Not the room. One room to a city until the readers can read."

The reader wrote it down and did not argue. He had stopped arguing in the autumn, which was its own kind of progress.

The Hall moved through the cold on its own momentum. Two branch halls sent back money nobody had caught them taking. The academy wrote twice. He read the reports at night with his wrists aching and his arm ringing, and slept, and was back in the court before the bells.

One morning a doctor came and stood at the edge of the court until he had finished the tenth cut.

"The Saintess is well," the old man said. "The child is well."

"Good."

"Due when the cold breaks," the doctor said. "A warm room. Clean water. Nobody else in it. She does not need the Hall standing over her, and she knows it."

"She will have the room," he said.

The Pope paid him out of the family purse, as he always did, and the doctor went away, and the ten cuts went on.

The Saintess walked the wall some mornings. She had the whole length of it to herself these months, her color had come back, and she kept to the same slow loop she had walked in the first weeks, when the loop was all she had.

One morning she stopped above the court while he was cutting. He went on with the form; the light died and came back ten times. Then, from the wall, in her flat academy voice, she read out what the morning had shown her.

"It is not the light," she said.

"No," he said. "It is what stays after."

She watched one more cut and went on with her loop, and neither of them said anything else. It was more conversation than most weeks gave them, and he put it away with the other things he did not spend.

It ended on an evening of wet snow.

He was on the ninth cut, tired past tiredness, doing the plain work with nothing in his head but edge and breath. The stroke came up as it always had. And at the close of it, before he could decide anything, the light finished the cut for him — a hand's width past where his hand had stopped, half a breath late, exactly right.

He stood in the wet and watched the golden sword hang where the stroke had ended. He counted to ten, and it did not go out.

At ten he took the hilt. The sword lay across his palm with the light steady along the whole of it, and it had weight — not the weight of metal, but the weight of a thing that is still there when you are not looking at it.

He gave it the only test that mattered. He set the point on the flagstone, and he let go.

The sword stood. Snow landed on the light and melted, and ran off the edge in a line so straight it looked drawn. He walked around it twice. It had no eyes and no voice, and it needed neither. It had held the line of the cut after his hand left it, and it stayed where he left it, point to the world.

That night he learned what he had made. The spiritual power he carried between his eyebrows — more than any Qian before him had ever carried — did not all come home to him when he set the sword down. The thread had found its place. It was no longer a thing he held; it was a thing that stayed. He could feel it run out of the sea behind his forehead, down his arm, out along the light.

The sword was no longer a skill he spent. It was a place where part of him lived.

He went down to the secret chamber under the Worship Hall on the first day of the last month of the year. He told no one. He took water, and he took the sword.

The first core sat low in him, warm and wide, going about its one slow turn since the month it was born. He drew the thread up, hand over hand, into the spiritual sea between his eyebrows — the piece that was not the angel, won by a winter of held cuts — and there he began the second. Small. Cold. Set to turn against the first. The book had marked that road for the strong.

The first day was pressure. His bones rang with it, and his heartbeat learned to arrive twice, once for each core, close together and wrong. The second core did not want to exist. He kept it turning anyway. In the evening his nose bled, and he did not stop.

The second day it nearly came apart. For one breath he was two people with two heartbeats, and the world split into two versions of itself an inch apart, and blood came up hot and wrong in his mouth. The thread in the sword pulled straight. He took the hilt in both hands — not to use it, but to be given the line — and the sword gave him its straightness, and he poured the second turn along it. The breath passed. He went on.

On the third day the two cores fought properly, because that was the design. The warm one pushed down and out. The cold one pushed up and in. Between them, at the crossing, his own strength ground against itself. He sat in the middle of the grinding with his eyes open and his jaw set, and he kept both turns true. Twice he thought of stopping. Both times he counted to ten and did not, and afterward he could not have said which ten it was.

On the fourth day the grinding changed sound. At the crossing, the two turns began to give off something that was neither warm nor cold, and that something fed him through channels nothing had ever fed him through before. His old blood, proud as it was, pushed once at the little cold thing and did not like what it found. Then the new stream reached down and fed the warm core too.

The angel stopped arguing.

On the fifth day the second core stopped moving.

It was small, the size of a plum pit. It was cold, like the far side of a fire. It turned the wrong direction, and the wrongness was the whole point of it. He stood up slowly in a room that smelled of salt and holy light, and both cores answered him — warm under the ribs, cold between the eyebrows, and between them a circulation that had never once existed in this world.

He sat down again with the sword across his knees until his heart found its single beat. His nose had bled at some point and dried. He had not noticed when.

He came up at night. The Hall was asleep, and the snow was coming down in the dark, quiet and busy.

For five nights a light had stood over the Worship Hall. The priests had counted it and written it in the day-book. No one had claimed it. The Elder Hall had decided, by the particular silence it kept, that the matter was the Worship Hall's business and not theirs.

The Grand Worship came to the courtyard door once, his hands behind his back, and stood a long time looking at the sword where it rested against the chair — plain now as a walking stick.

"Five nights," he said at last. "The Elder Hall will ask what stood over the Worship Hall."

"It was mine," the Pope said.

The old man nodded once, asked nothing more, and did not cross the threshold. He had done the same in the month of the first core, and the Pope had expected it. Some doors in that family were never crossed, only stood at. When he was gone the light in the courtyard seemed no dimmer and the silence no heavier, and both of those were answers too.

The rest of the month went back to its ordinary shape — lists, money, letters, and the sword by the chair.

On the last evening of the year he sat at the desk with the window open to the cold, and he counted what he had. Once. Coldly.

The angel at ninety-six, six wings, purer than the blood had ever run before. The first core, low and warm. The second, between his eyebrows, thin and straight as a blade's edge. Both turns in one body, and the argument between them had made the whole of him stronger twice over. In the book, a man carrying two cores was called a thing his age had not seen, and no wall stood in front of such a man until the highest ranks.

He looked for the man in this era who could come at him now, below the gods.

He could not find one.

He did not say it aloud, and he did not write it down. It was not for saying. It was for being true.

The door at the end was still a door. He had not walked through it, nothing that winter had opened it, and he did not pretend otherwise, even alone. But there were no other footprints in front of it now. The stairs up to it were his.

Outside, the year was ending. The cold would hold for a while yet, and the child was due when it broke. On the desk the fourth list had reached thirteen cities, the guild's readers were in half of them, and somewhere in a noodle shop a teacher was putting six characters on a board for children who would otherwise have had nothing at all.

He put out the lamp and left the sword standing where it stood. Under his ribs and between his eyebrows, the two cores kept their long argument.

He slept in the middle of it. That was the whole art.

# Chapter 11 — The Ninth Door

The first pillar was not a fight and not a vision. It was weight.

It came down on his shoulders out of the star-filled dark like a hand the size of a house. It pressed on his soul power as a millstone presses on grain, and it did not stop pressing. After the first hour he understood the whole design of the place. The nine pillars did not test what a man could do. They tested how long a man could hold what he already was.

He held. Days went by, or what the hall let him feel of days. There was no sun in the star-field and no hunger that mattered, only the weight, the nine lights, and the grey angel watching from the middle with its sword in the floor. The second pillar pressed his body instead of his soul. The third pressed his spiritual sea. The fourth took everything at once.

It was under the fourth pillar that the wall at ninety-seven gave.

He had been carrying that wall since the spring, and the pills and the hunt and the long northern summer had loaded power behind it as a reservoir loads behind a dam. Now the pillars squeezed the reservoir. It broke on the ninth day, in the middle of a breath, without ceremony. One more level of soul power: ninety-eight. The body paid — a week of ache, a month of eating like a growing boy — and he counted the cost as it came, because he had learned what uncounted costs do.

The fifth pillar showed him the night.

Not a vision of some other man's fear. His own, exactly. The corridor of the basilica. The door he had walked through as somebody else. The thing that had been done with his hands before his hands were his. It showed him that night for nine days and asked him nothing. On the ninth day he said out loud, to the empty hall, the only sentence that had ever survived the showing: *I did not do it, and I will spend the rest of my life not doing it, and both of those are true.* The pillar let him pass.

The sixth pillar was an incense stick, and his father standing at the other end of the hall.

千道流 came in through the seam in the world, where the light had come. He carried a stick of incense and no weapon, and he lit the incense on nothing and set it burning between them.

"One stick," the old man said. "The house has always asked the sixth of us this much. Hold me to the ash."

He held him for most of it. That is the honest accounting. His father at ninety-nine was not a man any more but a weather system with a face. Six wings of the true body folded down to two, out of courtesy. Holy fire ran off his hands like water off a roof, and every exchange between them shook dust out of the stars. He gave ground and took it back. He used the sword as a man uses a boat in a storm: for staying upright. Twice he was on one knee. Once his father's palm stopped an inch from his chest and waited there for three full heartbeats, and both of them knew what the inch meant.

The ash fell. His father looked at the burned stick, and then at him, for a long moment.

"Eight will let you through," he said. "Nobody in this family has ever heard me say that to a son."

The seventh pillar was the sword.

It stood in the floor at the centre of everything, grey as old winter, six wings folded along the hilt and a pale gem in the pommel, and it had stood there longer than the continent had kept count of years. The pillar's light lay on it and asked him, without words, to pick it up.

He wrapped both hands on the hilt and pulled. Thirty-six thousand jin of it came out of the stone as if the stone had been waiting sixty thousand years to let go.

The recognition happened in the blood before it happened anywhere else. Something in him that was older than his name answered something in the steel, and the answer was fire. 太阳真火 — the sun's true fire — woke along the blade in a line of white and ran up his arms and into his chest, like a river changing its mind about direction. It burned. That is not a metaphor and not a complaint; it burned the sleeves off him and burned a mark into both palms in the shape of a six-winged thing, and he held on through it because letting go was not an option the sword had offered.

When the fire settled, the blade was grey no longer. It was the gold of a sun seen through closed eyelids. It weighed nothing in his hands and thirty-six thousand jin to everything else in the world, and it was his.

The eighth pillar opened under the grey angel's feet.

Four bones rose out of the floor in a slow ring: an arm and an arm, a leg and a leg. Each one was 99,999 years old and each one hummed like a struck bell. He knew them before he was told. He had known them since he was nine years old in another world — the other four of the six, the Angel Set, the hall-guarding treasure of Martial Soul Hall, two of which had been sleeping in his own skull and chest for three years.

His father was standing at the edge of the light when he turned, and the old man spoke the house law before he could ask it.

"There is a condition. It is older than the records, and it is the reason no one in this family has ever stood where you are standing." 千道流's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man reading a funeral rite. "When the last of the six is taken in, the body must stand at a hundred. Not ninety-nine and a promise. A hundred. Every ancestor who tried it with less is a name on a wall. I stopped at eight because I am many things, but I am not a fool."

"A hundred," he said, and did the arithmetic out loud, because the codex had taught him that laws are answered with numbers. "Ninety-eight levels when I came in, ninety-nine when the bones go in. That is one short, and the law does not accept short. But the law says the body must stand at a hundred, not that the rank must read one. I carry two cores no era has seen. I carry a sword that drinks the sun's own fire. And I carry blood that came down from the thing this hall was built for. Count it with me, father: ninety-nine, and a blood core, and a sword core, and true fire, and the angel's own blood."

His father counted. It took him a long time, and his face did not change once.

"The law may accept it," he said at last. "The law may also eat you. There is no record either way, because nobody has ever brought this much to the door."

He took the bones one at a time, right arm first. Each one went in like a key going into a lock made for it.

The right arm gave him the Angel's Wing, a shield of folded light that could turn aside anything the world threw. It gave him the Golden Vortex too, a wheel of blinding gold that ground whatever it caught. The left arm gave him the Angel Light Blade: fifteen small blades of light that gathered into one great blade able to open a mountain. Its second gift had no name on the bone and named itself in his hand, a blessing on steel, so that whatever sword he held afterwards held the sun a little better. The left leg gave him flight that was not flight so much as the air agreeing to carry him, and the Swift Advance, which was speed with the waiting taken out of it. The right leg gave him the Angel Domain, golden radiance opening off his body like a dawn that would not finish. Behind it, folded small and unfinished, sat the prototype of something enormous: a sky turned red-gold, a sun standing at his back, meteors that were somehow also him.

Eight skills. Four bones. Ninety-nine levels of soul power. The two older bones in his skull and chest woke to answer the new four, and all six of the Set sang the same note for the first time since the Angel God died.

And then the fusion asked for its price, and the first attempt failed.

He had expected the six to melt together at a touch. They began to. The bones went liquid at the edges and ran toward each other in threads of gold. Then they froze, every thread, and the recoil came back into him like a door slamming in a small room. He went down on one knee with blood coming out of his nose and both ears, the two cores in him spinning out of alignment. He understood the law's real shape in the same moment. A hundred was not a number the door checked. It was fuel the fire burned, and his ninety-nine and his cores and his fire were enough to *start* the melting and nowhere near enough to finish it.

"I am the guardian." His father was kneeling beside him, and his voice had the terrible calm of a decision made sixty years ago. "There has always to be a loss for a gain like this. My existence is for the inheritance — that is the whole of what I am for. Let me be the fuel."

"No."

"Son—"

"No." He got one arm under him and looked at the old man, and his voice came out of a ruined throat but it did not wobble. "You have already burned. Sixty years on a hill with a statue. Watching a family die by inches. Raising a child alone. Waiting for a door that never opened for you. That was the fuel, father, and you paid it before I was born. The debt is paid. What is left is mine to carry, and I am carrying it."

He paid with the blood instead.

He opened the marks in both palms with the sword's own edge and poured the blood of the Angel God's line onto the six bones. The blood went gold where it left him — the gold of the rings that ran on blood and not on soul power, the gold that no sealing could touch — and red patterns came up through the gold like veins through marble. The nine rings rose around him, beast rings on their honest ladder. Over all of them the gold overlay blazed. And his soul cracked.

That is the plain word for it. His soul cracked along the seam where the first core sat against the second, because three natures were trying to become one body. The pain of it was a country he had never visited. He walked through it alone, and he did not let go of the fire.

The hall answered. All nine pillars lit at once and the star-field went white. Outside in the cold, the grey eyes of the statue over the Worship Hall shone like lamps for a night and a day. Spirit City looked up and saw a pillar of gold standing on the highest ground, and the elders came out of their houses into the snow and knelt. In the Pope's quarters a woman at ninety-five put down her brush and stood. A child a year old laughed in her sleep.

In the middle of the light the six bones finished what they had started. They melted, all six at once, the two old and the four new, every one of them crossing to a hundred thousand years on the way down. The liquid gold ran over him like a second skin and cooled into the Angel Sacred Armor: winged at the shoulders, quiet as a held breath, drinking the sun's true fire as a lamp drinks oil. The sword in his hand went from gold to something past gold. The fire in his chest, fed and turned and pressed by two cores' worth of will, gathered itself. It concentrated and hardened. Where the codex in his memory had always shown a third seat, in the chest, an element core took shape: warm as noon, solid as a held word. The Holy Solar Core, third of three.

Blood in the dantian. Sword between the brows. Sun in the chest.

He stood up in the armour of a dead god at ninety-nine levels. The seam in his soul ached with every heartbeat. The fire wanted feeding and would want feeding for the rest of his life. At the ring's far end the ninth pillar was still dark, and the door behind the grey angel was still shut.

He walked to it, because a man should look at the thing he is refusing.

The dark behind the ninth pillar was not empty. It was the shape of a question held for ten thousand years, and it leaned toward him as water leans toward a wound. Everything in the armour leaned back. He stood there with his hand on the hilt. He thought about a chair in Spirit City, a brush, a ledger, a woman who had said *terms*, and a child who had laughed in her sleep. He turned around.

"Eight," he said to the hall. "As promised."

The doors let him out at the thaw.

The ice was running in the gutters of the Worship Hall. His father was standing exactly where he had stood all winter: thinner, older, alive. The old man looked at the armour, and the sword, and the gold still fading in his son's eyes, and he said the only thing he had been waiting four months to say.

"You came down."

"I promised," he said. "There is a fourth promise waiting in the city, and the dawn is early, so walk with me."

They came down the hill together, the guardian and the god-in-armour. The Pope was already up, because she was always already up. The child was awake and furious about it. He took her off her mother's hands before he did anything else: before the hall, before his father, before his own bath, in the order the terms had set. She closed on his thumb with a grip that had opinions.

She had been named in her second month. Bibi Dong had chosen the name alone and had never once explained it, and he had never once asked. 千仞雪: snow and the thousand, a name with a hill in it.

"I felt the light," Bibi Dong said, watching him with the child. "The whole city did. Your elders are writing hymns about it. I have forbidden three."

"Forbidden is good." He shifted the child to his other arm. "I owe you a book. What I learned up there that a person can be taught, I have been writing all night: the forming of the cores, all three seats, blood and sword and element, one body. It has a name already. The Tri-Unity Soul Core Forming Method. It goes in the hall's book beside the one I gave your father, and it will outlive every hymn."

"Write it accurately," the Pope said, as she had said it in the autumn.

"I am writing it accurately," he said. "The last line of it is a warning. It says: the third core is not a crown, it is a furnace, and the ninth door does not care how bright you are."

Out the window the hill stood white in the new sun, the statue quiet on it, its eyes stone again. Behind the statue, where no eye in the city could see, nine lights stood in their ring in the star-filled dark. Eight of them burned low and patient. One of them, at the end, was exactly as closed as the day the Angel God died.

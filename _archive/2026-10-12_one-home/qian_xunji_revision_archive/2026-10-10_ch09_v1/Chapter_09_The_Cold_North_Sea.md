# Chapter 09 — The Cold North Sea

The ship was a northern trader out of the ice ports, wide in the beam and slow, and the captain took their gold without asking questions and put them aft with instructions to the crew that the two passengers were not to be spoken to, served, or looked at for longer than a breath.

On the ninth day out the water changed colour, going from green to the black grey of slate, and the air went hard with cold. The captain came aft once and said that his ship went no further north, that nobody's ship went further north, and that whatever the two of them were hunting out there had eaten three of his grandfather's boats.

"We are getting off here," the Pope said, and paid him the second half of the gold. The trader came about and ran south with every sail it had. Two people stood on the cold water with nothing under them, one on a floor of golden light and one on eight rings of black spider silk laid over the swell like a raft, and the wind came down out of the north and found them.

"Three days," he said. "It moves slow. It is hurt, and hurt things circle."

"How hurt."

"You will see."

They found it on the third morning, and she understood the word hurt the moment the sea opened under them.

It came up out of the grey water without hurry, a back first, and the back kept going, a cathedral ridge of blue-black hide that shed the sea in sheets. Then the head, blunt and barnacled and wider than the trader's beam. Then the eye, the size of a carriage wheel and the colour of winter milk, and it looked at them with the flat patience of something that had been alive since before the current temple had a roof.

And across the whole starboard flank of it, from behind the eye to under the water, ran a wound.

It was a burn. Not a cut, not a tear, a burn. A branching white scar the width of a road where the hide had been cooked and sloughed and was growing back wrong, slow and pale. The smell of it came off the wind even at a hundred paces, the smell of a body that had been struck from above and had lived.

"The spring storms," he said. "The first tribulation of a hundred thousand years comes down out of the sky, and most of them die in it, and the ones that live carry the receipt. That scar is three months old. It will take it a decade to close, and until it closes, that is the door."

Bibi Dong did not answer. She was looking at the leviathan and the leviathan was looking at her, and her two spiders came up over her shoulders without being called, the death one black and heavy on her right, the devouring one grey and lean on her left, and the eight black rings of her first soul turned slow under her feet.

"It is the size of a mountain," she said.

"It is a hundred thousand years old," he said. "Size is what time looks like. Listen to me. I am going to hold its attention and its water. I cannot hurt it anywhere that matters, not in a week of trying, and I am not going to try. You are going to go in through the burn, you are going to climb inside the receipt the sky wrote on it, and you are going to put the death into the place where it is already dying. When it goes down, the ring is yours, and you will sit down in the water and take it, and I will stand between you and the sea. Do you understand the shape of it."

"I understand the shape of it."

"Then go around. I will go in front."

He went in front.

The gold came up around him the instant the leviathan decided they were real, eleven rings of it, no year on any of them, and the layer of light over the water went out from him in a circle half a mile wide and lay down on the sea like a lid on a pot. The Angel Domain. Under it the water went still, and the stillness was the point, because a whale-class leviathan fights with the sea itself, turns the ocean into a hammer, and a hundred thousand years of that is not a fight two people survive. So he took the sea away from it, and it felt the theft at once, and the flat patience went out of the winter-milk eye and it came for him.

The tail came over the water first and it was not fast and it did not need to be, because it was the size of a city wall and the whole sky behind it was moving with it. He did not dodge. He put both hands up and took it on the domain, and the domain rang like a struck bell and bucked under him and held, and the shock of it went down through the light into the water and came back up through his boots and sat in his teeth. The second blow took him under. Under the grey water in the cold dark the leviathan turned with a sound like grinding and its mouth came open. He burned a road of holy light along the roof of that mouth and went up through the blowhole spray into the air again. The domain was still there, still flat, still holding the sea down, and it was costing him, and he could feel the cost.

He could also feel her, out on the flank, working.

She went in through the burn as silk goes through a torn sleeve. The devouring spider went first, because that soul had been made for entering, grey and flat and hungry. It laid itself against the pale wrong-growing flesh of the scar and drank. A hundred thousand years of leviathan noticed the bite and shuddered, and the shudder was a wave that stood up out of the still water and broke over the domain and over her. She did not come out. Under the cover of the shudder the death spider put eight legs into the wound and lowered her into it, and she climbed inside the receipt the sky had written, into heat and stink and the deep red dark of a body that size, and she let the death spider give her what it had.

The venom of the Death Spider Emperor is not a poison that kills fast. It is a poison that convinces a body to stop repairing itself, and she poured it into the one place where a hundred thousand years of flesh was spending all its strength on repair, and the two of them together, the venom and the wound, began to do what neither could do alone.

The leviathan screamed.

It was not a sound a person makes or a beast makes. It was a sound the weather makes, and it went out over the grey water for a day's travel in every direction. Under the domain the sea fought the lid and lost, and the great blunt head came around to its own flank to find the thing inside it. He would not let it. He went down the long curve of that turning like a stone off a sling, both hands full of the holy light sword. He put the sword into the winter-milk eye and turned it, and the eye went to ruin. The scream changed register and the head came for him instead. The third blow of the tail caught him square across the chest and put him through the air into the water a quarter mile away. The domain flickered. He came up under it spitting blood and laughing, because the water was still flat, and her soul was still burning steady out on the flank, and the leviathan was turning in a slow circle now, and every turn was slower than the one before.

It took another hour. He kept the eye side. She kept the wound. The devouring spider drank and the death spider poisoned, and the great body spent itself turning between the two of them. At the end it simply stopped, mid-turn. The flat patience came back into the one good eye for a moment, almost like understanding, and then it went down out of the world without a wave. The sea closed over the cathedral of it, and the grey water was just grey water again as far as anyone could see.

The ring came up out of the deep a minute later.

It rose slow, a hundred thousand years of it, red as a coal held in the dark. It turned once over the place where the body had been, looking for the one who had done it, and it found her, because she was the one whose venom was in the wound when the heart stopped. It came to her over the still water and stood waiting above her head like a question she had to answer with her whole life.

Behind it, pale and heavy, a second thing rose. A bone. A torso bone, longer than she was tall, the colour of old ivory. It turned toward her as well, because the law of a hundred thousand years is that the body leaves its best piece with the killer, for the part of them that is still empty. There was nothing on this water that could argue with the law.

She looked at the red ring for a long moment.

"Three days ago," she said, "I would have called this a myth."

"Sit down," he said. "It will not wait politely forever."

She sat down on the silk raft over the cold water and the ring came down onto her, and the first thing that failed was her body.

He saw it from ten paces, saw the red light sink into her and saw her meridian system light up under her skin like a map drawn in fire, and he saw the map start to tear. The whale gum had done its work, muscle and bone and channel all rebuilt harder than they had ever been, and it was not enough. Nothing that eats gum is built to take a hundred thousand years through the front door. Her channels, built by nature to carry black, were asked to carry red, and they began to let go along their seams. Blood came out of her nose and both her ears. Her back arched. The new scar beside her spiritual sea, nine days old, opened again, and the second failure followed the first into the light, because her core, her newborn grain, began to spin too fast for itself and heat up like a bearing with no oil.

He was inside the light before she fell. The gold rings came up and the holy light went into her. What he could not repair he held, both hands in the burning map of her, holding the seams of her channels shut from the inside while the red river went through. He held the spinning grain still with his own two cores set against it like hands on a wheel. It was the third time in nine months he had put his hands inside another person's cultivation, and it was the worst of the three. Somewhere in the second hour she screamed. Somewhere in the fourth she stopped. And somewhere in the grey of the third morning the light over the water went from red to gold to nothing, and it was done.

The phenomena had not been quiet. For three days a pillar of red light had stood on the northern sea where no light had ever stood. The water within a mile of it steamed. The sky kept a bruise in it long after the pillar went down, and sailors in the ice ports would tell their grandchildren about the summer the north caught fire. Under the pillar, for the whole three days, two spiders had stood over the burning woman without moving, the black one and the grey one, eight rings turning slow around the first and the new red one settling over the second like a crown coming down onto a head.

When she opened her eyes she was lying on silk over still water and the sky was empty and her whole body ached in a language she had no words for yet.

"Ninety," he said. He was sitting beside her with his sleeves burned off and his hands wrapped in linen, and he looked like a man who had gone ten rounds with a city. "The wall opened. You took the ring and the rank came with it, as the rank always comes with the ring. You are ninety."

She lay still and felt it. It was true. The liquid in her was a finger deeper than it had ever been, and the grain between her eyebrows turned slow and heavy and hot, and over the whole of it sat a weight that had not been there before, a red weight, like a second heart kept somewhere outside her chest.

"Two skills," she said. "It gave two. A hundred thousand years always gives two." She was quiet, finding them, and then her voice changed, because she had found what they were. "The first is a web. Not silk, pressure. I lay it on the water or the air and it becomes the bottom of the sea for anything inside it, and everything in the web is slower and heavier and I can feel every thread. The second." She stopped. "The second is hunger. I can put it in a thing through the web and take its power out of it, a little at a time, and it does not stop until I stop."

"A web and a drain," he said. "A hundred thousand years of deep water and your spider, and the two of them shook hands. There is no name in any book for those two skills, because nobody alive has ever had them. You will have to name them yourself."

"Later." She pushed herself up onto her elbows, and her face went grey with it, and she lay back down and breathed. "The bone."

The torso bone lay on the water beside the raft, patient as a fallen column. She took it in on the second day, when she could sit up, and it went into her back with a cold that made the red ring feel warm, and its two skills did not wake.

"They are sealed," he said. "A hundred-thousand-year bone does not open for a passenger. It will take years of your power to feed it before it gives you anything, and until it does you are carrying the heaviest thing in your body for nothing. That is the ledger. The scars on your channels are permanent and they will ache in the cold, which is a joke the north will keep making at your expense. Your core needs years. The bone needs years. And you are ninety at twenty years old, with two skills nobody has ever had, and when we come back into the harbour at Spirit City you will have to decide, every day, what a person does with all of that, because I am not going to decide it for you and nobody else alive can."

The wind came down out of the north and found them.

"The child," Bibi Dong said.

"Yes."

"When we get back. When she is old enough to be told. Will you tell her who I am."

He looked at her for a long moment, and she looked back with the face that held nothing he could push against, and the red ring settled over her second soul like something that had always lived there.

"No," he said. "I will tell her what she asks, when she asks, and no more than that, because that is the only honest way to hand a child her own mother."

"Good," said Bibi Dong. "Then it will be me who tells her."

And she turned her face to the south, where the long grey road of the sea went home, and she did not say anything else for two days, and he did not ask her to.

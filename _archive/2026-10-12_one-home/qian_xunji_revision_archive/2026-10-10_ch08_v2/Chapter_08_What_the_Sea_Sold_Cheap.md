# Chapter 08 — What the Sea Sold Cheap

The watch on her door came off on a morning one month after the child was born.

He did it himself, in the corridor, with the two Elder Hall men who had stood there since autumn. He told them the watch was ended. They looked at each other as men do when an order arrives that they will have to explain to someone above them, and then they bowed and went down the stairs. He took their chair and sat in it for a moment with his hands on his knees, because he wanted the fact in the world before he said anything else to anybody. The Saintess of Spirit City was no longer watched. The lock had always been the watch, and the watch had been his, and he had taken it off.

He knocked, and she said come in without waiting, and when he came in she was at the window in full white robes, dressed for the city and not for a room.

"You took the men off my door," she said.

"I did."

"Why."

"Because the reason for the watch died with the child's birth, and because I am done pretending that guarding you is the same thing as keeping you." He set the box he had carried on the table. "Come down to the cellar. There is a great deal of it, and it is easier to show you than to say."

The cellar under the west tower had been his since the third month. Fourteen crates, each the length of a man's arm, sealed with wax and the stamp of a different merchant house. He broke the nearest seal and lifted out a slab wrapped in oilcloth, grey and faintly yellow, heavy as wet stone, smelling of the deep sea and something under it.

"Whale gum," he said. "You know it?"

"Everyone knows it." There was a note in her voice close to amusement and not warm. "The coast sells it to old lords who want to believe things. A brothel drug. That is the only thing in this cellar the merchant houses know how to price."

"It comes out of the brain of a whale-class soul beast, and it is food for the body itself. Muscle, bone, meridian. It rebuilds a person so they can carry what would have torn them open the week before. There was a child in a book I read once who ate it from the time she could walk, and she took a thousand-year ring at level twenty, and everyone thought she was simply gifted." He rested his hand on the crate. "Four months of buying, nine houses, less than one season of the basilica's incense budget. When the coast learns what this is, one brick of the good grey will buy this tower. I learned first. That is the whole of my cleverness."

"And you are telling me this."

"I am giving it to you. All of it." He said it as he said everything to her now, flat and without arrangement. "And I am going to need you to eat a great deal of it, starting tonight, because the gum is not the gift. The gum is the preparation."

She looked at him then with something sharper than curiosity.

"Preparation for what."

"For a medicine that would kill you without it," he said. "I have to go east first. Six days. Do not eat anything from a stranger's hand while I am gone."

He left that night, and this time he did not walk.

He had walked east in the autumn, in a grey coat, a Pope pretending to be a clerk, because the basin was a secret and secrets are kept on foot. But the child was born, and the woman in the tower was eating whale gum by the spoonful, and there was no more room in him for the slow roads. He went up over the city after midnight with the six wings out and the cloud layer under him like a second sea. The crossing that had taken him a season of walking took him three nights of flying. On the fourth morning he stood again on the rim of the basin, sulphur in his teeth, the line of steam rising straight up between the two waters.

Six herbs stood where he had left six standing. He had written down where each of them grew, in a book he kept for a boy who was not born yet, and he stood a long while over the book before he opened his knife.

Two orchids and a lily. The eight-petalled orchid that lifts a soul master's power five whole levels when it is ground into medicine, the tulip that lifts it six, and the fragrant immortal that cures no poison but defeats all of them, which is what a body needs standing next to a surge that big. He had left them for the boy. The boy would find three fewer fortunes growing here in twenty-three years, and would never know what had been standing in their places.

He weighed a child he had never met against a woman he had wronged, and the scale did not move for long, and he did not pretend to himself that it was only medicine.

He cut the three at the base with the knife, carried them downwind in separate folds of cloth, drank one cup from each spring where he stood, and was in the air again before the steam had closed over the rim.

The making of the pill took nine days, and she was there for all nine.

That was not his arrangement and it was not hers; it simply happened, as a room fills when two people keep choosing to stay in it. The eight-petalled orchid had to be ground at dawn while the dew-weight was on it. The tulip had to go into warm oil and no hotter, and she learned to hold the pan at the height where her wrist said yes and the oil said no. The fragrant immortal went in last, a single petal at a time, and the refined whale gum went in under it, three ladles of the pale gold wax, and wild honey to bind, and nothing else, because immortal things do not forgive company they were not invited with.

"What will it do," she said, on the eighth night, stirring.

"The orchid lifts five. The tulip lifts six. That is what they did for two children at the level of soul ancestors, in the book." He watched the pan. "You are ninety-two and past the age where medicine is generous. My guess is three. It will put you at ninety-five, which no pill has ever put anybody, and it will burn for five days, and the fragrant immortal is the only reason the burning stops at your skin instead of going through it."

"Three levels." She said it slowly, the spoon still turning. "I have not had one level in fourteen months."

"I know."

"You keep doing this." She was not looking at him. "You keep handing me things that should be handed to your enemies never, and you keep standing there like a man waiting to be told to leave."

"I am not waiting to be told to leave."

"Then what are you waiting for."

He did not have an answer that was not also a confession, so he watched the pan, and she let him, and the ninth morning the medicine came off the fire as one pill the colour of old ivory with three faint gold veins in it where the gum had folded through.

She swallowed it at noon, in her own room, and asked him to stay.

That was the first thing. In seven months she had never asked him to stay anywhere. She said it flatly, looking past him at the wall. *Sit there. I do not want to be alone for this.* He sat, and within an hour he understood that she had not been managing her pride when she asked, she had been managing her fear. The burning started like a furnace door opening inside her ribs and did not stop opening.

What failed first was the place she had been stuck. Fourteen months of ninety-two had packed the bottleneck solid, as silt packs a sluice, and the medicine came down on that silt like a flood on a dam and the dam did not want to give. The pressure piled behind her breastbone until the veins stood out along her throat, and then the silt let go all at once, and the letting go tore. Blood came out of her nose. A capillary burst in her left eye and half her vision went red. Her back arched off the bed and a sound came out of her that he had heard once before, in the basin, out of his own mouth, and his hands were already glowing, already on her, the holy light going in along the tear and holding it while the flood went through.

The room shook. Not a figure: the lamp jumped, the pitcher walked off the table, and out over the city a pressure rolled off her in a ring that set dogs barking for six blocks. The nine rings came up through the floor of her soul one behind another: yellow, yellow, purple, purple, black, black, black, black, black. The last black one was the one she had killed for at eighteen, and it turned faster than it had ever turned. Behind her the Death Spider Emperor reared to its full height, carapace steaming, and the room was too small for it and it did not care.

It ran five days. He did not leave the room in five days. She came out of the fever on the sixth morning with her hair stuck to her face and her eyes clear. The first thing she did was count her own soul power with her eyes closed. The second thing she did was laugh, a sound he had never heard from her and did not recognise for a moment.

"Ninety-five," she said. "I can feel all of it. Every level. It is sitting there like it was always mine."

"Three," he said. "The medicine promised five and six to children and gave three to you, and that is the honest arithmetic of being extraordinary. And the cost is on your body now, not in a story: a capillary gone in that eye, a tear along the old channel that will ache in cold, five days you will not get back. That is the whole ledger."

"You say that every time." She was still smiling, and then she stopped smiling and looked at him properly, at the five days in his face, the beard, the eyes gone hollow, the chair he had not left. "You stayed."

"You asked me to."

"I asked you to sit." She held his look. "Nobody has ever stayed."

The core took another nine days, and it failed the first time exactly as he had told her it would.

He taught it in the cellar at night, off his own body and his own mistakes, because there was no one alive to teach it and he remembered every error he had made in the basin. The core is what happens when the liquid of a person's soul power is pressed until it refuses to be liquid and turns to a seed, and from that hour the power runs refined through the seed instead of splashing raw against the channels. But she was two souls in one body, and when she pressed, the Death Spider Emperor pulled one way and the Soul Devouring Spider Emperor pulled the other, and the half-formed grain between her eyebrows was torn off the press by two hands reaching for it at once. It burst inside her head with a sound like a wet branch breaking. Blood, her nose and mouth together, the channel beside her spiritual sea letting go along a hand's length of itself, her arm out of the world, and him across the floor before she finished falling, holding what he could not mend.

The second press he built with her over nine nights, and the trick was not force. She stopped fighting the two pulls and let each soul pull its own stream, and where the streams met she gave them one narrow throat, and the friction of the two pulls did the pressing her hands never could.

When the grain set, the note of it was felt in the teeth. The wave of it shook dust off the cellar beams and set all fourteen crates humming against each other like struck bells. Over her right shoulder the Death Spider Emperor rose. Over her left the grey devouring one rose with it. The two of them stood in the world at the same hour for the first time in her life, nine rings circling the one and none the other, and she sat between them breathing hard with her hair coming loose.

"Both," she said, and her voice was not steady at all. "Both of them. They have never been in the world at the same hour. I stopped letting the second one out when I was twelve years old, because it frightened the other children, and now it is standing there, and it is not frightened of anything."

"One furnace," he said. "Both drinking."

She let the spiders down and the cellar was just a cellar. She sat in the low light with her hands still shaking, and he reached over and put his hand over hers to steady them, the first time in eight months he had touched her without light in his fingers. She turned her hand over under his and held on.

"Do not make this pretty," she said. "I have not forgotten what you did. I want you to know that I have not. And I want your hand there, and I have wanted it there for weeks, and apparently I am a person who can hold both of those at once."

"Apparently you are."

"Then do not sit there like a man at a funeral." She pulled him down by the hand, and she kissed him first, hard, like everything else she did, and the lamp went low over the two of them and the fourteen crates of whale gum stood around them like an audience that had known before they did.

They came up out of the cellar near dawn, and the plan was made over cold bread, because neither of them was good at mornings and both of them were good at work.

"North," he said. "To the cold sea. There is a whale-class leviathan out past where the ice ports stop, just past one hundred thousand years. It took its first heavenly tribulation in the spring and it is still paying for it, with a burn along its flank the width of a road, and that burn is the only reason two people can stand in front of it at all."

"A hundred thousand years." She said it as she had said ninety-five, tasting it. "And the ring."

"Is red, and it carries two skills, and it is yours, and it will not go on the Death Spider Emperor." He watched her understand. "It will go on the sister. The first ring of the Soul Devouring Spider Emperor, a hundred thousand years, as a certain hammer family will learn to do it in twenty years if the book I read is still right. The red ring lifts people three to six levels when it lands. You are ninety-five. Do the arithmetic."

She did, and for a moment the woman who had been sold at eight and frightened children at twelve and taken at fourteen sat very still with the arithmetic in her hands.

"Ninety-eight," she said. "Twenty years old. Second on the continent under your father."

"Second on the continent," he agreed, "and first in that room, and I will not be pretending otherwise to make myself comfortable." He poured the last of the water into her cup. "The bone comes too. A hundred thousand years always leaves one, for whatever part of you is still empty, and a bone that old carries two skills of its own and rebuilds the body around them. It will be the worst nine days of your life, worse than the pill, and I will be holding you through it as I held you through the pill."

"Stay," she said, "will be the word, not sit."

"Stay," he said. "That was always the word. I was just slow."

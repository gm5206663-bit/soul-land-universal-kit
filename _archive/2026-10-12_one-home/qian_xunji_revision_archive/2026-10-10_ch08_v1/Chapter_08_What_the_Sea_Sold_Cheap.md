# Chapter 08 — What the Sea Sold Cheap

The watch on her door came off on a morning one month after the child was born.

He did it himself, in the corridor, with the two Elder Hall men who had stood there since autumn. He told them the watch was ended. They looked at each other as men do when an order arrives that they will have to explain to someone above them, and then they bowed and went down the stairs. He took the chair they had been sitting in and sat in it for a moment with his hands on his knees, because he wanted the fact in the world before he said anything else to anybody.

The Saintess of Spirit City was no longer watched. There was no lock on her door. There had never been a lock she could not have broken with one hand, but the watch had been the real lock, and the watch was his, and he had taken it off.

He knocked, and she said come in without waiting, and when he came in she was at the window in full white robes with her hair done up, dressed for the city and not for a room.

"You took the men off my door," she said.

"I did."

"Why."

"Because the child is born, and the reason for the watch died with the reason for the room." He set the box he had carried down on the table between the chairs. "And because you are not a prisoner and I am done pretending that guarding you is the same thing as keeping you."

She looked at him for a long moment with the face she had worn for seven months, the one that held nothing he could push against. Then she looked at the box.

"What is that."

"Come to the cellar," he said. "It is easier to show you."

The cellar under the west tower had been his since the third month. Fourteen crates, each the length of a man's arm, sealed with wax and the stamp of a different merchant house. There were nine houses in all, three of them coastal, and not one of them knew what they had been selling him, and that had been the entire trick of it.

He broke the wax on the nearest crate and lifted out a slab wrapped in oilcloth. It was the size of a brick, grey and faintly yellow, heavy as wet stone, and it smelled of the deep sea and something under it.

"Whale gum," he said. "You know it?"

"Everyone knows it." There was a note in her voice that he had not heard from her before, close to amusement and not warm. "The coast sells it to old lords who want to believe things. It is a brothel drug. It is the only thing in that crate that the merchant houses know how to price."

"It comes out of the brain of a whale-class soul beast," he said. "And it is not what they sell it as. It is food for the body itself. Muscle, bone, meridian. A person who eats the refined working of it becomes able to carry what would have torn them open the week before, and the higher the beast, the more it carries. There was a child in a book I read once who ate it from the time she could walk. She took a thousand-year ring at level twenty, and everyone thought she was simply gifted."

The amusement went out of her face.

"How much did you pay for this."

"Four months of buying, nine houses, and less than one season of the basilica's incense budget. When the coast finds out what this is, one brick of the good grey will buy that tower. I found out before the coast did. That is the whole of my cleverness."

"And you are telling me this."

"I am giving it to you." He said it as he said everything to her now, flat and without arrangement. "Thirteen crates refined and sealed, and the fourteenth open for work. It is yours. The houses do not know who bought, the ledgers say a temple fund, and by the time the price moves the stock that matters will be in this cellar with your name nowhere on it."

She was quiet for a while, looking at the grey brick in his hands.

"Why," she said, "do I keep getting that question wrong. I ask why, and the answer is never the one I braced for."

"Because you braced for seven months in one room," he said. "And the answers kept coming from a direction you had decided was impossible. That is not a flaw in your judgment, Bibi Dong. That is me."

The working of whale gum is not subtle and it is not clean. He showed her over the next nine days, in the cellar, with a brazier and a shallow bronze pan and a length of raw silk stretched over a frame. The gum melts slow. The scum is most of what the coast sells and most of what makes it a drug, and it lifts off in grey sheets that stink like a low tide. What stays under the scum, strained twice through the silk and cooled, is a pale gold wax that goes into the body like warm water and rebuilds as it goes down.

She learned it with her sleeves tied back and her hair tied up, skimming scum off a pan of melted whale brain with a wooden blade, and she learned it fast, and she did not comment on what it looked like, the Pope of the continent and his Saintess cooking brothel drug in a temple cellar at midnight.

On the ninth day he set a small clay pot on the brazier with a third of the nine-grade fungus in it, the last of it, the two thirds of the mushroom that had survived the making of the nine pills. The fungus had come out of the basin country in the second month, and it had already paid for one rank of his and nine safe births. What was left went into the pot with a ladle of refined gum, because the fungus needed no help and the gum needed no company.

It cooked for six hours. It came out as one pill the colour of old ivory, faintly warm in the hand, and when he set it on the table in front of her she looked at it and then at him.

"You took the working of this yourself," she said. "In the first month. The city said the Pope had taken a rank in seclusion."

"The city was right for once."

"And you are giving me the last of it."

"I am." He sat down across from her. "You are eighty-nine. You have been eighty-nine for two years, and the wall in front of you is not a wall a pill can break, because the wall of ninety opens for one thing only and that thing has teeth. What the pill can do is take you to the razor edge of eighty-nine, where nothing is left to give and everything is ready to take. It will burn for three days. You will lose what you are carrying now, most of it, and you will get it back harder. And when you can stand up at the end of it, I am going to teach you something no one else alive knows how to teach, and you are going to hate the first week of it."

She took the pill without another word, which was the most Bibi Dong thing that had ever happened in front of him, and swallowed it with cold water, and the burning started before the cup was back on the table.

He did not stay in the room. That was a line he had drawn in the first month and had not moved since. He sat outside her door as the Elder Hall men had sat all winter, on a hard chair, listening to the hours and to the low sounds behind the door that he could do nothing about. He had ledger books from the northern dioceses and a reading lamp for company, and at some point in the second night he stopped reading and simply sat.

On the third morning she came out of the room on her own feet.

She was thinner, her skin grey with three days of burning its own reserves, and she was holding the doorframe, and her eyes were steady.

"Eighty-nine," she said. "Packed to the seam. I can feel the wall. It is right there, and it is the first time in two years it has felt like a door instead of a cliff."

"The pill buys no ranks," he said. "You know that. The rank comes with the ring, and the ring comes with the teeth. What you are standing on now is the shore of it, and now we build what lets you swim."

He taught her in the cellar, at night, after the lamp hour, and he taught it the only honest way he had, which was his own body as the blackboard.

"There is a thing you can make in the spiritual sea between the eyebrows," he said. "A core. Soul power is liquid in a person, even mine, even yours, and it stays liquid your whole life and it sloshes, and every rank simply means more sloshing. A core is what happens when you compress the liquid until it refuses to be liquid any more and turns to a solid, a grain, a seed, and from that hour your power is refined through the seed instead of splashing raw against your meridians. I made mine in the first month, in the basin, because my blood demanded it, and I very nearly died of it. There was no one in the world to teach me, which is why I can teach you. I remember every mistake because I made all of them."

"And what is it worth."

"Recovery. Endurance. Refinement, so the power you spend buys more than it did. And it is the threshold, in a law that is not written yet, of everything above ninety-five." He paused. "But none of that is why you are going to make one. You are going to make one because you have two martial souls."

Her face changed. It was the first time he had named them both in front of her.

"They have been pulling at one purse your whole life," he said. "That is the sickness of the twin, and why twin-souled masters cultivate one soul and let the other sleep. It is why you are eighty-nine at twenty and not ninety-five. One purse, two hands in it. When the wall of ninety opens and you reach for a ring worth a hundred thousand years, it will come through you like a river through a gate, and two hands in one purse will tear you open from the spiritual sea down. The core is the third thing. One furnace that both souls drink from instead of both souls fighting over the pot."

"You said there is no one alive to teach this."

"There is me, and I am barely that." He shrugged. "First attempt tomorrow night. It will fail."

It failed exactly as he had told her it would, and worse.

She sat in the centre of the cellar floor with the brazier low, and he sat three paces off with his hands ready. She gathered her soul power as he had shown her, drawing it up out of the body into the point between the eyebrows. It came, a great weight of it, eighty-nine levels packed to the seam by the pill, and she began to press it together.

The Death Spider Emperor pulled.

The Soul Devouring Spider Emperor pulled back.

He felt it happen from three paces away, felt the whole shape of her soul power go from one turning wheel into two wheels grinding against each other, and the half-formed grain between her eyebrows did not so much fail as get torn off the press by two hands reaching for it at once. It burst inside her head with a sound like a wet branch breaking. She went over sideways, blood out of her nose and mouth together, and the meridian beside her spiritual sea let go along a hand's length of itself. The backlash poured down into her shoulder and put her arm out of the world.

He was across the floor before she finished falling. The gold came up around him unbidden, as it did now, and the holy light went into her head and found the torn meridian and held it as you hold a rope, not mending it, holding it, keeping the tear from running while her own body crawled after his hands. She was rigid, shaking, her teeth shut on her own tongue. He held her head in his lap on the cellar floor and pressed light into her until the shaking stopped, which took until the grey of morning.

When she could speak she spoke through a swollen mouth.

"You said it would fail."

"I said it would fail. I did not say it would fail like that." His voice was level and his hands were not. "I made my core with one soul. One soul pulls one direction, the press holds, the grain forms. You are the first two-souled person this has ever been tried on, and now we know the thing I could not know. The press itself has to be built for two hands. You will not try again for nine days. That meridian needs nine days and not one less."

The nine days were the strangest of his life, and he had had a strange life.

She recovered in her quarters, and from the fourth day in the cellar, because the work of the gum did not stop for anybody. He cooked and she skimmed, and they talked as two people talk when neither of them is performing, which was new for both of them. He learned that she had been eight when her mother sold her to the temple school, and that she remembered the price, and that she had never told anyone the price. She learned that he read ledger books for pleasure, which she refused to believe until he showed her, and that he had never in his life been taught anything by anyone, which she also refused to believe, and then believed, and then went quiet about.

On the sixth day she asked the question he had been waiting for since the birth, and she asked it over a pan of scum, not looking at him.

"The child."

"In the Worship Hall," he said. "Qian Dao Liu has her. She is warm, she is fed, she is hidden. She will be told she is a Qian and taught the sword and the statue, and she will be six before anyone looks twice at her, which is exactly long enough."

"Is she." Bibi Dong skimmed a grey sheet off the pan and watched it curl. "Like you."

"She has a grip," he said. "That is all anyone knows yet. She will not wake until she is six, and when she does the whole hall will feel it. I would rather not say more, because everything I could say is a hope, and hopes said out loud about children are a kind of theft."

She was quiet for a while. The brazier ticked.

"I am not going to thank you," she said. "For the room. For the pill. For any of it. I need you to understand that nothing you have done has been forgiven or forgotten, and that I have simply decided the hatred can sit in the corner while other things happen, because I am twenty years old and eighty-nine and there are other things happening."

"I never asked you to thank me. I need you alive on a deck in the north, and I would take your hatred for that trade any day of the week."

"Then it is a trade."

"It is a trade."

On the tenth night she sat down on the cellar floor again, He sat behind her this time, close enough to put his hands on her shoulders, and the press they had built over nine days of talking was waiting in her.

It was a small thing and it was the whole trick of it. She did not gather her soul power into one wheel and press it. She gathered it into two streams and let each soul pull its own stream, Where the two streams met, at the point between the eyebrows, she did not fight the pulling. She let them pull against each other through one narrow throat, and the friction of the two pulls did the pressing her hands could never have done.

The Death Spider Emperor pulled. The Soul Devouring Spider Emperor pulled. Between them, in the throat of the meeting, the liquid went slow, then thick, then silent, and then it was not liquid any more.

The sound the core made when it set was a single low note, felt in the teeth more than heard, and the pressure wave went out through the cellar and shook dust off the beams and set all fourteen crates humming against each other like struck bells. She came up out of it with her eyes open and both hands full of light. Over her right shoulder the great black carapace of the Death Spider Emperor rose into the air. Over her left, grey and lean and hungry, rose the Soul Devouring Spider Emperor. The two of them stood in the cellar together for the first time in her life, eight rings circling the one and none at all around the other, and she sat between them like a woman at the head of her own table.

"Both," she said. Her voice was not steady. "Both of them. At once. I have never. They have never been in the world at the same hour."

"One furnace," he said. "Both drinking."

She held them for the count of a hundred breaths. The devouring one leaned its eyeless head toward its sister, and the death one did not strike. Then the light went thin at the edges and she let them down, and the cellar was just a cellar again, and she sat breathing hard with her hair coming loose.

"How long can you hold it."

"A stick of incense," she said. "Maybe two, by the end of the week. The grain is newborn. It will take years to grow up." She looked at her own hands. "I am still eighty-nine."

"You are still eighty-nine," he said. "The core buys no rank, the pill bought no rank, and the wall is still the wall. What you have now is a body on the razor edge, a furnace that can feed two souls at once, and a meridian with a scar in it that will ache in cold weather for the rest of your life. That is the whole ledger. Now go and sleep, because the rest of it has teeth, and the teeth live in the northern sea."

She stood up, and at the door she stopped.

"The northern sea."

"A whale-class leviathan," he said. "Just past one hundred thousand years. It came through its first heavenly tribulation in the spring and it is still paying for it. That is the only reason two people will be able to stand in front of it at all. When we kill it the ring is yours, because the ninth ring of the Death Spider Emperor is the door of ninety, and it is going to be red."

She stood in the doorway with her back to him for a long moment.

"When," she said.

"When you can stand on a deck," he said. "Nine days for the meridian, nine for the gum to finish your body, and then we go and buy you a hundred thousand years."

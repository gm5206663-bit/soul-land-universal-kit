# Chapter Eleven: Four Hours

```
◆ STATUS — Chapter 11: years 1–1
KIND      : a boy who said yes for good reasons and then found out what he had bought.
AGE       : seven. Wulin is seven. Na'er is six and a half and is not at the academy at all.
CLASS     : rank one. Eleven chapters and one number. Nothing has moved and nothing can.
THREAT    : Lin Ximeng, who has watched him for three years, who keeps a small black book with
            a six-month hole in it, and who has just been given four hours a week of him.
BOND      : Su Wan, who asks the question nobody else in the house would ask. Lan Yue, who has
            been in seven chapters and is given a scene of her own at last.
LAW       : being on the books is not the same as being in the yard. Also: a man who does not
            raise his voice is not thereby a man who is not dangerous.
DIALOGUE  : standard 13-30% — a table, a storeroom, a yard, a bank of coming-back grass.
```

They did the arithmetic at the table, the way they did everything at the table, and Lan Shen sat at his end and let them do it.

"The fee's already off," said Lan He. "It came off in the spring. That's not what he's offering."

"He's offering to keep it off."

"He's offering to keep it off," said Lan He, "and to pay us in hours instead of in jars, and I have thought about which of those is cheaper and I have not got an answer."

Su Wan put the pot down.

"It's four hours a week," she said.

"Four hours a week."

"Of a seven-year-old."

"Of a seven-year-old who reads notices," said Lan He.

That was the whole of the argument. It was over in about four sentences. Lan Shen learned something from it that he would keep for a long time: that in his house a thing was decided by whoever described it most accurately first.

Lan Yue said, from the doorway, where she was standing with her coat half on because it was a Wednesday and Wednesday was her whole week, "What's he actually doing. The four hours."

"Carrying crates," said Lan He. "Washing jars. Standing where a man tells him to stand."

"That's what I do here," said Lan Yue. "And nobody's offering me a place in a book."

"You're eleven."

"I'll be on the books when I'm seven, then," said Lan Yue, and went out. The door did not slam. She did not slam doors, and that was somehow worse.

Su Wan looked at her son.

"Do you want to," she said.

Lan Shen had been waiting for that question for the length of a meal.

"No," he said.

"All right," said Su Wan. "Then don't."

"There isn't a don't," said Lan Shen. "There's a yes, and there's a yes that my father says for me. If he says it for me then it's his. I'd be doing it because he asked and not because I agreed. Those are different lines in a book." He had heard the phrase used at a table in front of Yan Song a year ago and he had kept it, and it landed. "I'll do it."

"Why," said Su Wan.

"Because the fee comes off properly instead of coming off subject to valuation. Because four hours a week is four hours a week. And because if I don't then Dad will, and Dad can't wash jars for four hours a week and run a shop."

Su Wan looked at him for a while.

"That's a very good answer," she said.

"I know."

"That's what worries me." She picked the pot back up. "A boy who has an answer that good at seven has been keeping it somewhere. Where have you been keeping it."

"Everywhere," said Lan Shen.

---

The new term started nine days after Awakening Day.

He went in through the gate with the other children and he was in the Soul Master class, sixteen of them again, one fewer than last year because a family had gone to the harbour, and Lin Ximeng was at the front of the room in the same place he had stood a year ago, saying the same sentences in the same order.

The east wing had tall windows and a floor that had been swept so many times the boards had gone soft in the middle.

The storeroom was at the end of the same corridor.

Lan Shen stood in the doorway of it on the first afternoon with a crate of jars in his arms and understood, all at once and completely, the thing he had not thought to ask about.

"Fourth-form store," said the man with the list, who was not Zhao and who had a key on a nail. "You'll wash in the sink there. The crates go on the rack. Don't stack above the line, the line is there for a reason, and the reason is that a boy stacked above it in my first year and I have had the line ever since."

"Is this the Soul Master class's store."

"It's the east wing's store."

"Is the classroom—"

"Through there," said the man. "You'll hear them. You'll get used to it. Everybody does."

Lan Shen put the crate on the rack, below the line. He washed eleven jars. He counted them. Through the wall on his left he could hear sixteen children being told what a martial soul was, by a man who had been telling them for three years, and who had told him for three years.

*Four hours a week,* he thought. *I bought four hours a week and I did not ask where the four hours were.*

He was not angry about it. He had made an error of a kind he recognised — he had priced the cost correctly and located it wrongly, which is the more embarrassing of the two, because the arithmetic was fine and the map was not.

He washed the jars.

---

The garden came back in the second week.

He went on a holiday, because the holiday rhythm was a thing that existed between him and Su Wan and had existed for eight months, and he stood at the gate and looked at a bank that was green all the way up now instead of green at the roots.

He had not touched the sense since the autumn. He had not needed to. There had been nothing to touch it with.

He took ten seconds.

It was not the field it had been in the summer. The summer field had been forty thousand strands going in every direction at once, a whole garden shouting. But this was a field. The bank was in it. The mound was in it. The bench with its new slat was in it. And the thing he had lost twice was not.

He let go at ten, on purpose, before the ten became an eleven, because ten was what he had paid nothing for once before, on a street, in the third week of everything. Eleven was the number that had taken a week out of him. And he had a rule now, and the rule was that the price was width, and a bank of grass in the second week of spring was not wide.

Nothing happened. No white line. No nosebleed. He walked home and ate supper and was fine.

He wrote it down anyway. *Bank. Ten seconds, let go before eleven. Nothing. Count resumed at nineteen thousand four hundred and the number is going up because the grass is going up and not because anything is under it.*

And then, underneath, in the smaller hand he had started using for the things that were not counts:

*The bench has a new slat and I have stopped measuring from it. Whatever was thirty-one paces from the broken one is thirty-one paces from nothing now.*

He did not dig. He had decided in the autumn that he would not dig. The decision had held through a winter. It held through a spring. Holding was beginning to feel like a kind of answer.

---

Na'er was at the gate on the second holiday.

She had not been at the first. Su Wan had walked him to the garden and stood where she could see him and asked nothing, and on the way back he had said that Na'er had not come, and his mother had said that Na'er's mother had wanted her at home, and he had understood from the way she said it that this was going to be true for a while.

The second holiday she was there.

She was already inside the gate when he got to it, which meant she had come on her own and had been let in by somebody, and which meant she had been standing there for as long as it takes a six-year-old to decide whether to go and look at a bank of grass.

"You didn't come," he said.

"I wasn't allowed."

"Are you allowed now."

"I came," said Na'er.

They went in. He counted. It took longer than it had in the autumn, because there was more to count, and because the grass had come up unevenly, and because the hole in the mound had filled with water and drained and filled again, so that the shape of it was no longer the shape he had written down.

Na'er did not ask what he was doing. She had asked once in the autumn and been answered and had not asked again, which was more restraint than most grown-ups in his life had managed.

She stood where she could see him and watched the counting, and about halfway through she said, "You say some of them out loud."

"Do I."

"Some of them."

He had not known that. He thought about it and decided it was true, and that it had started in the winter, when there had been nothing to count and he had gone on counting anyway to keep the shape of it in his mouth.

"When I'm losing my place," he said.

"Are you losing your place."

"Not now."

Na'er looked at the bank.

"I'm not going to be in his class," she said.

Lan Shen went on counting. He did not stop, because stopping was the thing that would turn it into a conversation about it, and he had learned in four months of standing at a pump with his mother that the way to keep a person going was to not stop what you were doing.

"I know," he said.

"Wulin goes."

"Wulin was six last year."

"I'm six," said Na'er. "I'm six and a half. I was six and a half at the thing."

"At the ceremony."

"I know what it's called." She said it flatly, without heat, in the voice of a person who has heard adults use a word a great many times and has decided to own it. "Everybody goes. My mum says everybody goes, it's free and it's compulsory, and I go next year." She kicked at the root of the bank without bending down to do it. "To the ordinary one. With the little ones."

"You're not a little one."

"I'll be the biggest one in it," said Na'er. "Because I'll be six and three quarters and they'll be six. That's what being behind means."

He said nothing, because she was right, and because she had clearly been told it by somebody and had worked out the rest herself.

"Would you want to be in his class."

Na'er did not answer for a while.

"That's a stupid question," she said. "Nobody asks me if I want to be in anything. They ask if I have one."

He finished at nineteen thousand six hundred, which was more than the autumn and less than the summer, and he wrote it down, and he wrote the date, and then he wrote *Na'er came* underneath it in the smaller hand, because that was the sort of thing that mattered later and never seemed to at the time.

"What are you writing."

"The count."

"What's the other bit."

"The other bit isn't the count."

She looked at the page. She could read a little; she had said so in the autumn and then had not read anything for four months, which he had taken to mean she could read rather less than she had claimed.

"That's my name," she said.

"Yes."

"Why's my name in your book."

Lan Shen closed the book.

"Because you came," he said.

---

Lan Yue got her scene on a Wednesday, because Wednesday was hers.

She came in at eleven in the evening with a bowl of whatever was left under her arm and her hair coming down and the specific exhaustion of a person who has been on her feet for five hours in a room full of people eating, and she put the bowl on the table and sat down at it without being asked.

"You're on the books now," she said.

"Somebody told you."

"Everybody told me. There's a boy from the noodle street whose cousin washes at the academy. He says you're the one who did the deal about the jars last year. He says you're seven, and he doesn't believe the second part."

Lan Shen ate a mouthful of the bowl. It was noodles and it was mostly broth.

"What else does he say."

"He says you don't talk." Lan Yue put her chin on her hand. "He says the other boys talk and you don't. You just do the work and then you go. You stack below the line without being told. The man with the key likes you. And nobody knows what you're like, because you've never once said anything that wasn't about jars."

"That's true."

"I know it's true." She looked at him. "That's the problem. I've been telling people about your balm for four months and I've never once told anybody anything about you. I've noticed that. I want you to know I've noticed it, because everybody in that street thinks my brother is a rumour."

Lan Shen put the spoon down.

"What do you want me to say."

"I don't want you to say anything." Lan Yue was eleven and she was tired and she was, he understood in the middle of that sentence, considerably sharper than anybody in the house had ever given her credit for. "I want you to tell me one thing. Any one thing. Not the jar thing, not the garden thing, not the count. One thing about you that isn't about work."

He could not think of anything.

That was the alarming part. He sat there with a bowl going cold in front of him and searched for a single fact about himself that was not a measurement or a plan or a lie, and he could not find one, and the not-finding was so fast and so total that it felt less like a failure of memory than like an answer to a question he had not known he was being asked.

"I like the canteen," he said.

Lan Yue waited.

"The Soul Master class eats as much as it wants, and what it eats is better than what the ordinary class eats. It was the first thing about the academy that I genuinely loved. I have thought about it about once a week for a year."

"That's a thing about work."

"It's a thing about soup."

His sister looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed. It was not a kind laugh, and it was not an unkind one either.

"All right," she said. "Soup. I'll take soup." She got up and took the bowl to the pot and poured the rest of the broth back in, which was a thing their mother would have objected to on principle. "Next Wednesday you get a second one and I'm not asking for it. You just have it ready."

"I'll have it ready."

"You won't."

"I will," said Lan Shen. "I'm good at keeping things."

---

*Lin Ximeng had a small black book and he had had it for three years. In three years he had filled about half of it. The half he had filled was almost entirely Tang Wulin.*

*That was not a failing. That was the work. A homeroom teacher in a small city academy who watched sixteen children for three years and wrote down what he saw was doing more than most men in his position did, and he had seen a great deal: a boy who meditated on a schedule, who improved on a schedule that was not the schedule he had been given, whose spiritual power sat so far above his classmates that the gap was visible in the way he tired and did not tire.*

*He had written that down. He had written down the dates. He had gone to the office twice and asked what the procedure was for a child whose cultivation speed could not be explained by his rank, and both times he had been told, courteously, that there was not one.*

*The other half of the book was thinner.*

*There was a section in it for a boy called Lan. The section had begun in the boy's first year, when the boy had been six. It had run for about four months. Then it had stopped.*

*Not because anything had happened. Because nothing had.*

*Lin Ximeng had walked the rows every day for six months. He stopped at the same desk for the same length of time. He got nothing. He wrote down that he had got nothing, because a gap with a name beside it is a fact and a gap without a name is a lapse. He had six months of that. Six months of nothing is a shape. He had drawn the shape, and then he had stopped drawing it, because there was no more of it to draw.*

*Then the new term started, and the boy went on the books.*

*He learned about it from the storeroom, in the third week, in the ordinary way one learns anything in a small building: the man with the key mentioned that the Lan boy was reliable, that he stacked below the line, that he washed eleven jars and did not need to be told twice.*

*Lin Ximeng had gone on marking his register.*

*And then he had put the pen down, because he had realised that a boy he had watched for three years and got nothing from was now standing forty feet away, on academy time, four hours a week, doing a task that required him to stand still and be within earshot.*

*He got the black book out that evening.*

*He opened it to the section. He looked at the six-month hole with the name beside it. He wrote one line at the top of the new page in the hand he used for dates.*

*Then he sat for a while with the book open, because he was an honest man in the specific way that men who keep records are honest, and he wanted to be quite sure, before he wrote anything else, that what he was feeling was interest and not relief.*

*He could not tell the difference. That was itself worth writing down, and he wrote it down. Then he closed the book, put it in the drawer with the register, and went to bed.*

*He did not think of himself as a man watching a child. He thought of himself as a teacher with two pupils he could not explain, in the same room, in the same year, and one of them was on his own books.*

---

Su Wan asked him on the fourth week.

She asked it while he was drying his hands at the yard pump, which was where she asked things, because a boy with wet hands cannot leave.

"How many hours have you done."

"Sixteen."

"And how many have you got left."

"A lot."

"How many, exactly."

"Nine years' worth," said Lan Shen. "If I keep doing it. Which is four hundred and sixty-eight weeks, which is about a thousand eight hundred and seventy hours." He had done it in his head, standing at the pump, and it had taken him about four seconds, and he noticed that his mother was not looking at his hands.

"You've already counted it."

"I count things."

"I know you count things." Su Wan took the cloth off him and dried the back of his neck, which was a thing she had stopped doing when he was five. "Have you counted what you're getting for it."

"The fee."

"The fee," she said. "Right."

He did not answer. There was an answer and he had it: a thousand eight hundred and seventy hours in a storeroom at the end of a corridor was a price he had agreed to without asking where the corridor led. He had found out where it led on the first afternoon. He had stayed anyway — and he had stayed anyway for reasons he had not written down.

"Say it," said Su Wan.

"If I say it you'll ask the next one."

"I'll ask the next one anyway."

Lan Shen looked at the pump, and at the yard, and at the two doors down where a lamp was on in a house with a girl in it who had no martial soul and who had asked him for a made-up thing.

"Four hours a week is cheap," he said. "Cheaper than the jars. If I wash jars my family is a supplier and a supplier can be dropped, and if I'm on the books I'm on the books, and a boy on the books is harder to drop than a jar is. That's what I bought."

Su Wan was quiet for a moment.

"That's a good answer too," she said.

"They're all good answers."

"That's the problem with them," said Su Wan, and let him go.

---

On the last afternoon of the month he washed eleven jars and stacked them below the line. The man with the key signed the sheet and gave it to him to take to the office. The office had a book in it. The book had a column in it. The column had his name in it, with a number against it.

He looked at it. He was allowed to look at it; it was his own hours, and the clerk turned the book round without being asked, the way you turn a book round for a person who has earned a look at it.

Sixteen hours. Written in a hand that was not Yan Song's.

There was nothing else in the row. No note, no dash, no mark of any kind — just a name, a number, and a date, in a book that did not care. He stood there longer than a boy needs to stand to look at his own name. The clerk did not say anything about it.

*That's me,* thought Lan Shen. *That's what I look like from the inside of a building.*

He took the sheet back. He washed his hands. He went home through a city that was going green. He did not think about the other book — the one with a tray of coins beside it, in a yard, where a man had written something in a rank column that had no right to hold a number. He had decided in the autumn that he was not going to look at things until he was ready, and he was not ready.

Not yet.

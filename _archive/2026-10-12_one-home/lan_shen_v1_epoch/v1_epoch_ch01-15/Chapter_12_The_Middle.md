# Chapter Twelve: The Middle

```
◆ STATUS — Chapter 12: years 1–2
KIND      : a boy who has got so good at being average that his average is starting to look
            like a thing no child has ever produced before.
AGE       : seven, and then eight. This chapter runs about fifteen months, from the first term
            of Year 1 to the winter of Year 2.
CLASS     : rank one. Twelve chapters and one number. Nothing has moved and nothing can.
THREAT    : Lin Ximeng, who has stopped walking the rows and started measuring them, and who
            needs a control for a boy he cannot explain.
BOND      : Lan Yue, on a Wednesday, owed a second answer. Wulin, being guided, and not
            knowing that he is being compared.
LAW       : a refusal is more visible than an acceptance. Also: the way to be good at something
            is to not do it when you are not supposed to.
DIALOGUE  : standard 13-30% — a Wednesday, a classroom, a ditch, a doorstep.
TURN      : ch11 he was on the books with sixteen hours against his name and nothing else
            anywhere. Ch12 he has a number in a black book, put there by a man comparing him to
            Tang Wulin — and he lands in the middle every single time, for a whole term, and
            does not understand why that is the worst thing about it.
```

The rest of the first year went the way the first year had gone, only faster, because he knew where the edges were now.

He washed jars on Tuesdays and Fridays. He stacked below the line. He signed his own sheet at the end of each month in a box that was there for the purpose, and by the summer his hours were in four books and the number in all four of them agreed.

He counted the bank every holiday. The count went up all spring and peaked in the middle of the summer at forty-one thousand two hundred, and then went down again through the autumn the way it had the year before, and he wrote every one of them down and did not touch the grass once.

He learned what the canteen soup was on Wednesdays and what it was on Fridays, and which of the two was worth queueing twice for.

He did not find a second fact about himself.

---

Lan Yue asked on the second Wednesday. And on the third. And on the fourth. He had an answer ready each time. Each time she took it and looked at it and put it down.

"The soup," he said, on the second one.

"You gave me the soup."

"The soup on Fridays is better than the soup on Wednesdays and I have not worked out why, and I have thought about it for a week."

Lan Yue counted on her fingers.

"That's about work."

"It's about soup."

"It's about soup at your work." She put her chin on her hand. "Next."

On the third Wednesday he said that he liked the smell of the storeroom in the morning before anybody had opened the window, and on the fourth that he had a way of walking home that was two streets longer than the short way and he had never once taken the short way, and she wrote both of those down on the back of a noodle ticket in a hand that was worse than his.

"What are you doing," he said.

"Keeping a list."

"Why."

"Because you're not going to say the real one," said Lan Yue, "so I'm going to write down all the ones you say instead, and one day there'll be enough of them that it'll add up to a person, and I won't have to ask."

She was eleven. He was seven. He went to bed that night and lay there working out whether that was a threat, and decided it was not, and then decided that the reason it was not a threat was worse.

---

The measuring started in the second year, in the first term.

Lin Ximeng came in one afternoon with a watch in his hand, which was not unusual, and a piece of paper, which was.

"We're going to do something different," he said. "Everybody sit where you are. Don't move your desks."

There were fifteen of them that year. Sixteen the year before, and seventeen the year before that. Two gone since, one to the harbour and one to a cousin in the next city. One new boy come in at the winter, who sat at the back and had a cough.

"Hold your state," said Lin Ximeng. "As long as you can. When you come out, stay out."

"Don't try to go back in," said a boy at the back, "or you'll get a headache."

"I was going to say that."

"You say it every year."

"And it is true every year," said Lin Ximeng. "Ready."

He had done this before, in the first year, in the ordinary way, with the rows and the shoulders and the wrists. What was different was the paper.

Lan Shen looked at the paper from four desks away and saw that it had fifteen lines on it and a column at the left for names, and that the column at the right was narrow, and that narrow columns were for numbers.

*He's timing us,* thought Lan Shen.

He closed his eyes.

---

The grass under the east wing was not like the grass in the street.

The street grass was thin and broken and grew in cracks. He had spent five years learning to sit on top of it without going down into it. The academy's grass had been sown. It ran along the ditch, under the wall, across the whole of the clean grounds. It was even. It was thick. It was directly beneath a ground-floor room with tall windows, and it had been sown two years before he ever sat above it, and he had sat above it every afternoon for a year and had not touched it once.

He did not touch it now.

He sat with his hands on his knees and held the shape of a meditative state, which is a thing you can do without doing anything, the way you can hold your breath. The blue thread in him was coiled and asleep and he left it alone. He breathed. He counted his breaths, because counting was what he did with time he could not spend, and he got to sixty and started again.

Somewhere to his left a boy came out of it and was told, quietly, to stay out.

He could feel the room going. One at a time they were dropping away, and each one made a small change in the air, and he had been in enough rooms full of people to know what a room of fifteen sounded like when eleven of them were still trying.

*Middle,* he thought. *I want the middle.*

The middle of fifteen was eighth. He would need to come out after seven and before nine, and he would need to do it in a way that looked like running out rather than deciding.

He got to two hundred and forty breaths and let his shoulders go.

He opened his eyes on the floor and let them lose their focus, which was the trick, because a boy who has come out of a meditative state looks at nothing in particular for a second or two before he remembers where he is. He gave it two seconds. Then he looked up.

Lin Ximeng was watching the watch.

He was not watching Lan Shen. He was watching the watch. His thumb was on the stem. There was nothing at all in his face. Lan Shen understood, with a small cold drop in his stomach, that the man had been watching the watch the entire time, that everybody in the room had been under it, and that the only reason to feel singled out was to have been doing something.

"Out," said Lin Ximeng, to the room. "Everybody out. Hands on your knees. Breathe."

He went down the rows with the paper.

He stopped at Wulin for a while. He wrote. He said something very quiet that Lan Shen could not hear and Wulin answered with a shake of his head, and Lin Ximeng wrote again.

Then he came to Lan Shen.

"Four minutes," said Lin Ximeng, and wrote it.

That was all. He did not put a hand on the shoulder. He did not move a wrist. He wrote a number in a narrow column and went on to the boy behind, who had been out for some time and was pleased about it.

Four minutes. Eighth of fifteen. Two boys behind Wulin, who had gone seven and a half and knew it, and who was already being told by the whole room without anybody saying so that seven and a half was a good number.

Lan Shen sat with his hands on his knees and breathed, and thought: *that was easy.*

And then, because he had been doing this for a year and because a year is long enough to develop a habit of checking, he thought: *why was that easy.*

Wulin found him at the door afterwards.

"How long did you get."

"Four minutes."

"I got seven and a half," said Wulin. "Jiang Ning got two."

"Everybody knows. Everybody heard her come out."

"She came out and then she went back in and then she came out again," said Wulin. "You're not supposed to do that. He said so before we started."

"He said so before we started," Lan Shen agreed.

"And he wrote two down and then he wrote two down again." Wulin worked at this on the way down the steps and arrived somewhere. "That's not right. You can't put the same number twice."

"You can if it happened twice."

"It didn't happen twice. The second one was shorter. I was watching."

"Then he wrote the wrong number the second time," said Lan Shen.

"See," said Wulin, satisfied, and went off down the yard to tell somebody.

---

He timed himself on Tuesdays and Fridays, in the storeroom, with the clock on the shelf that the key man wound on Mondays.

It was not difficult. It turned out that it was not difficult at all, and that was the first thing that should have told him something. He could hold a shape for four minutes with the grass under the floor, the ditch outside the window, and sixty thousand strands going about their business beneath the foundations of the building. It cost him nothing. It had always cost him nothing. The reason was that he had been practising not-doing for five years.

Four minutes was comfortable.

Six was comfortable.

He tried nine on a Tuesday in the second week of timing himself, with the door shut, standing up, and he came out of it at nine with his hands shaking slightly and no nosebleed and no white line, and he wrote it down.

*Nine minutes. Nothing. The street was eleven seconds and it took a week out of me. This is a floor and a ditch and it is nine minutes and there is nothing.*

He did not go higher. He had a rule and the rule was that the price was width and that a rule you only tested in one direction was not a rule. But nine was enough, and nine was more than twice what the class was doing, and four was the number that put him eighth.

So he had a choice, and the choice was not whether he could do it.

The choice was that he had to keep doing four.

---

He kept doing four.

Third week: four minutes. Fourth week: four. Fifth week: four. Sixth week: four and a half, because a boy two desks over had improved and the middle had moved, and he had moved with it. Seventh week: four. Eighth week: four.

He came eighth, or seventh, or ninth, in a class of fifteen, every single time, for a whole term.

Lin Ximeng guided them on Wednesdays.

It was not called guiding. It was called the afternoon session, and it was the session everybody had — except that Lin Ximeng now came to two desks instead of walking the rows, put his hand on two shoulders instead of fifteen, and stayed.

Wulin got most of it. That was correct and everybody could see that it was correct. Wulin was seven and a half and then eight and then eight and a bit, and he held his state like a stone in water, and there was a thing about him that came out when he was sitting still which did not come out when he was standing up.

Lan Shen got the rest of it.

Lin Ximeng would come and put a hand on his shoulder, and turn his wrist a quarter of the way round, and say something. *"Your breathing's high."* Or: *"You're holding your jaw."* Or once, in the summer, *"You're counting,"* which had made him go cold all the way down, until the man went on and said *"that's fine, keep counting, count slower,"* and moved on.

They were good notes. They were the notes of a man who knew what he was looking at.

And they were, Lan Shen understood sometime in the second term, notes about form. Every single one of them was about how he was sitting. Not one of them, in a whole term, had been about what he was doing.

Because there was nothing to say about what he was doing.

He was four minutes. He was the middle. He was a boy with a rank one Bluesilver Grass and a form that could be improved and a number that did not change.

---

Na'er started at the ordinary school in the autumn.

He saw her go on the first morning, because he was on the step with a crate, and she came out of the Tang door with a bag that had been somebody else's and her hair done up by somebody who had got better at doing hair up.

"How many are in it," he said.

"Twenty-nine."

"That's a lot."

"They're all six," said Na'er. "And I'm seven and three quarters and I'm taller than the teacher."

She was not taller than the teacher. She was going to say something else and did not, and went.

He watched her go down the street with a bag that had been somebody else's. He thought — because he could not stop thinking, and because it was the sort of thing he thought now — that in four months somebody in that school would work out that the girl who was seven and three quarters in the sixes' class had no martial soul at all. And that the working-out would be worse than the ceremony. The ceremony had been one afternoon. The school was every day.

He put the crate in the cart. He did not say any of it to anybody.

---

*Lin Ximeng had a black book and he had had it for four years, and the first half of it was Tang Wulin, and the second half of it had begun, in the winter of the second year, to be a comparison.*

*That was not how he had thought of it at the start. At the start he had thought of it as a control, which is the word a man uses when he wants to feel that he is doing something other than staring.*

*The numbers were on the left page. Fifteen children, once a week, in a narrow column, and the column had the shape he expected: a scatter. A boy who had done six one week and two the next. A girl who improved for a month and then fell off when her brother was ill. The new one with the cough, who was getting worse in a way that Lin Ximeng had put in a letter to a man he did not know, because that was a thing you could do something about.*

*Tang Wulin was at the top of the scatter, and above it, and going up.*

*The other one was in the middle.*

*He had noticed it in the fifth week of the term. He had not written it down for three weeks, because he wanted to be sure he was not seeing what he wanted to see. Then he had written it down. Then he had stopped writing anything else in that section for a month, and simply kept taking the number.*

*Four minutes. Four. Four and a half. Four. Four. Three and three quarters — once, in the week the boy had a cold. Four. Four. Four. Four.*

*Lin Ximeng was an honest man in the specific way that men who keep records are honest, and he knew perfectly well what four minutes meant and what it did not mean. It did not mean the boy was slow. He had watched him wash jars for a year and a half, from a doorway, on the ordinary afternoons when a man has nothing better to do than look at a corridor. The boy washed eleven jars in the time it took the other boys to wash seven. He stacked below the line without being told. He signed his own sheet.*

*It did not mean he was fast, either. Fast would have been a number.*

*What it meant, and what Lin Ximeng wrote at the top of a fresh page in the hand he used for dates, was that in fifteen children measured once a week for a whole term, there was exactly one whose number did not move.*

*Children scatter. Sick children scatter more. Well children scatter less. Nobody holds still by accident.*

*He had been to the office twice about Tang Wulin and had been told, courteously, that there was no procedure. He did not go a third time, because he had nothing to say, and because a man who goes to an office three times about two children in a small city acquires a reputation that has nothing to do with either child.*

*He put the pen down. He looked at the two pages for a while.*

*On the left, going up and away from everything, a boy whose spiritual power he suspected and could not prove, because proving it needed a helmet and a hall and a hundred Federation Coins that nobody in Glorybound City was going to spend on a child who was already doing perfectly well.*

*On the right, staying exactly where it was, a boy who washed eleven jars in the time it took other boys to wash seven.*

*He wrote one more line under the date.*

*Then he closed the book, and put it in the drawer with the register, and went and found his coat, because it was the second year of three and there was a great deal more of this to do.*

---

Su Wan caught him at the pump in the winter of the second year, in the dark, on a Tuesday.

"You're eight," she said.

"I'm eight."

"You've been eight for a month and you haven't said."

"I didn't think—" He stopped. "No. I didn't say."

She turned the handle and let the water run over his hands, which was the way she asked a question without asking it.

"How's the academy."

"Fine."

"How's the man with the book."

Lan Shen looked at her.

"He hasn't got a—" He stopped. She was not looking at his hands. The water was running. And he had learned at a table in this yard that in his house a thing was decided by whoever described it most accurately first, and that his mother had just described it more accurately than he had.

"He's got a book," he said.

"What's in it."

"Everybody."

"What's in it about you."

Lan Shen thought about four minutes.

"A number," he said.

"What number."

"It doesn't mean anything."

Su Wan turned the water off.

"That's what your father says about the fee," she said, "and it's what your sister says about her job, and it's what you say about the garden, and I have noticed that all three of you say it in the same voice." She wiped her hands on her coat. "I'm not asking what it means. I'm asking what number."

"Four minutes," said Lan Shen.

"For what."

"For holding still."

She looked at him for a moment.

"You can hold still for longer than four minutes," she said.

"Yes."

"How much longer."

"I don't know," said Lan Shen.

"Guess."

"Nine."

"Why nine."

"Because nine is what I've done," he said, "and that's not the same as what I could do, and he only writes down one of them."

"Which one."

"The one on the paper."

Su Wan turned the water back on. The first true thing he had said at that pump in two years had been *I don't know*, and neither of them had noticed it, because it had gone by under a number the way everything else did.

---

That night he got the book down off the shelf, second row, square to the edge, and turned to the back, where the smaller hand was, and he wrote the week's number in.

Then he looked at the column above it.

Four. Four. Four and a half. Four. Four. Four. Three and three quarters. Four. Four. Four.

He had written every one of them himself, on the evening of the day, because he had learned in his first year that a thing not written down had not happened. He looked at the ten in a column in the smaller hand and felt nothing at all. He turned back a page to the counts of the bank, which were ragged and went up and down and had a summer peak in them. He looked at those for a while. Then he looked at the column again.

He wrote underneath it: *why is this straight.*

He did not have an answer, and he did not think it was a dangerous question, and he went to bed.

Outside it was the second year of three, and the ditch under the east wing was full of grass that had been sown, and a man in a coat was walking home with a book in his bag in which fifteen numbers scattered and one did not.

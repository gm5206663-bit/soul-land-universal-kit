---

The first morning he put his hand flat on the floor before he was properly awake.

There was nothing under it. He lay there with his hand flat and his eyes open. He worked out two things in the same breath: that he had not decided to do it, and that he was going to do it every morning for a month whether he decided to or not.

He got up. He did the wall. He did the floor. He counted two hundred and six. He put the last two down square. His hands ached in the place they ached now, which was lower down than it had been a fortnight ago. He thought that was progress. He wrote it in the book.

Then he sat on the edge of the bed and tried to remember the garden.

That was the first mistake of the day. He did not know it was a mistake until about an hour later.

---

He remembered the bench. That was easy. The bench had a slat missing on the left side as you faced the grass. The gap was about a hand wide. There was a nail head sticking up at the other end, where the slat had been nailed. He remembered the mound. He remembered the tree at the top of the slope. He remembered that from the bench, if you stood up, you could probably see the harbour, and that you were not supposed to stand up.

He wrote those down.

Then he tried to write down where the strand was.

He wrote *middle of the bank.* He looked at it.

Middle of the bank was not a place. The bank was forty paces long. It went up a slope and it curved. Middle of the bank was a region the size of his father's whole shop. He had four seconds of knowing exactly which one of forty thousand things it was. He had written down a phrase that could have meant any of about four hundred.

He crossed it out. He wrote it again, longer.

*The bank curves. The mound is at the south end near the gap in the wall. The tree is at the top. I was on the bench. It was in the middle third, closer to the mound than the tree, and it was pulling at me in a straight line, and the straight line went from the bench toward the mound, and it was left of the line, and—*

He stopped.

He could not remember whether the tree was left or right of the mound.

He sat on the edge of his bed with the pen in his hand. The tree was there. It was at the top of the slope. He had seen it with his own eyes the night before, in the last light. He could not put it on either side of anything.

He had been sitting down. He had been facing the bank. The tree had been at the top of the bank, and he had not needed to know which side of the mound it was on.

So he had not learned it.

He wrote: *the tree is somewhere at the top.*

He looked at the page. There were eleven lines on it. Eight of them were things anybody in the district could have told him without going. The three that were his were the three he was least sure of.

That was the first thing he learned in the blind week. He learned it on the first morning. It was not the thing he thought he was going to learn.

He had thought the problem was that he could not feel the garden.

The problem was that he had felt the garden for four seconds, and then written it down the next morning from a bedroom. The writing down had made it feel like more than it was.

---

School on the fifteenth day was Soul Master topics. There was nothing to feel there either. He had expected that. It was worse than he had expected.

He had got used to the street. That was the thing nobody had told him about, because nobody had told him about any of it. He had spent two weeks walking to school with forty things under his hands. He had complained about the number. Now there were none. It was the same walk. He kept putting his hand out at the corner by the drain to check.

Lin Ximeng walked the rows on the fifteenth day, and on the sixteenth. On the sixteenth he stopped at his desk for about as long as he stopped at any desk, which was not long. She said nothing. She went on.

He did not know what was in the black book. He had stopped guessing about that in the second week. What he knew was narrower than that. Whatever he had written about the fourth day, he had not written anything about the fifteenth or the sixteenth, because there was nothing to write.

A record with a gap in it is a different object from a record without one. Somebody who reads the second version will ask about the first.

He put that in the book too. It was the second line in it that day.

---

On the eighteenth day his mother came home from the market with grass seed on her hem.

He noticed it at supper. He noticed things at supper. It was the wrong sort of thing to be on a person.

Glorybound City was paved. He had worked that out four days ago in a yard with his back against a wall, and it had felt like a discovery about himself. Now it was a discovery about his mother's skirt.

There was no grass in the district that had gone to seed. There was grass in the district. There was grass at the base of walls, and in the gutter outside the noodle place, and in the crack by the Tan family's step. All of it was the sort of grass that got trodden, or swept, or eaten by a goat before it got tall enough to make seed.

That is what a city does to grass.

The seed on his mother's hem was from grass that had been left alone long enough to flower, and set, and dry.

He ate his supper.

His mother ate her supper. She said the fish was dearer than last week. His father said it would be dearer next week too. Lan Yue said she had got through two baskets and a half on the Wednesday, and her hands were all right because of the balm. His father said good. His mother said good. His sister went back to her bowl.

Nobody said anything about a garden.

Lan Shen finished his supper and helped stack the bowls. He went out to the yard to do the wall again. He stood in the yard with his hand on the bricks for a while without doing the wall.

He could be wrong. There was hay. There were carts coming in from outside the walls with vegetables packed in straw and dry grass. A woman standing at a market stall all morning could get seed on her hem from a cart. That was a perfectly good explanation. It was available to anybody who had not spent four seconds in a garden full of forty thousand things two doors past the Tan place.

He thought about how long it would take to walk there from the market.

He thought about whether his mother had ever had a reason to walk past the Tan place.

He thought about the fact that he had told her he was going to see whether Zhao had any more jars. Zhao's yard was in the other direction. His mother knew where Zhao's yard was, because she had bought jars from Zhao for nine years.

He went in and got the book down and wrote: *she went.*

He looked at it. Then he wrote underneath: *or a cart. I cannot check.*

That was the second thing he learned in the blind week. It was the same as the first thing with different words on it. He had an observation and he had no instrument. Writing it down did not make it an observation.

It made it a sentence.

---

On the nineteenth day Wulin came to the gate at the end of school.

"Tomorrow," he said.

"Tomorrow."

"You said six days." Wulin counted on his fingers. He always counted on his fingers, slowly, with his mouth moving. "Five, six. That's tomorrow. You're coming, aren't you."

"I'm coming."

"Na'er's coming. There'll be other kids. There's always other kids, it's a holiday, everybody goes." Wulin looked at him in a way that was not the way he usually looked at him. "You all right."

"Yes."

"That's your all-right face."

"I know it is."

"You've got three faces," said Wulin. "You've got your no-reason face and your all-right face and your thinking-about-the-shop face. My mum says everybody's got one face and they use it for everything. I said no, Lan Shen's got three and I can tell them apart, and she said well that's very useful for you, isn't it."

Lan Shen did not say anything, because there was nothing in that to say.

Wulin shifted his weight from one foot to the other. He was nine. He had been told for nine years that he was not a subtle person, and he was not. He was also not stupid. The two things were not the same.

"My mum came round to your mum's," he said. "Yesterday."

"I know."

"I wasn't listening," said Wulin. "I was in the yard. I couldn't hear hardly anything."

"Right."

"She said you had a nosebleed."

"I had a nosebleed."

"In the garden. On your own. At night."

"Yes."

Wulin looked at the ground. Then he looked at the wall two doors down. Then he looked back.

"My mum said was it the heat," he said. "She said it's been warm and she said boys get nosebleeds when it's warm and my dad said that's not true, and then they argued about it for a bit."

Lan Shen thought about that for a second.

He thought about the fact that his brother had just handed him the entire thing already wrapped up, with the paper on it, and that it would be ungrateful not to take it, and that taking it would cost him nothing at all.

"It was the heat," he said.

It was not the heat. It was twenty seconds of forty thousand living things in a place where there had been forty for two weeks. He had known that going in. He had done it anyway. It had cost him a month of the only thing he had that nobody else in the city had.

Wulin beamed.

"See," he said. "I told her. I said it was the heat, and she said how would you know, and I said because I was there."

"You were there."

"After," said Wulin. "But I was there."

He went off down the street. Lan Shen stood at the gate with his bag on his shoulder. He thought about how many people in Glorybound City now believed that a six-year-old had bled from the nose in a ruin because of the weather. He thought about how long it had taken. Four people, five days, and he had not had to say a single untrue thing to any of them except once, to a nine-year-old, who had wanted to be told.

He went inside.

---

His mother was in the back room.

She was not doing anything. She was standing at the window that looked out on the yard, which was a window that had never in six years had a reason to be stood at. When he came in she turned around.

"Tomorrow's the holiday," she said.

"Yes."

"You're going to the garden."

It was not a question. There was nothing in it he could take hold of. He stood in the doorway with his bag on his shoulder and he thought about three answers. He threw away two of them.

"Yes," he said.

His mother looked at him for a while. She had her hands in front of her, one inside the other, the way she stood when the fish was dear.

"Wulin's mother came round yesterday," she said.

"I know."

"She says there's always children in it. She says it's not a secret, she says everybody in the district knows it's there, she says there's a bench and a bit of a hill and you can see the harbour if you stand up."

"Yes."

"She says her Tang Wulin takes the little one every second holiday and has done for two years."

"I know."

His mother looked back at the window. The yard was on the other side of it. There was nothing in the yard.

"I'll walk you there," she said.

"Mum—"

"I'll walk you there," she said, "and I'll walk you back. You can go and be with Wulin. I'll be somewhere I can see you." She turned around again. "That's not a question either."

"Are you cross."

"No."

"Then what."

His mother picked something up off the sill and put it down again in the same place.

"I went and had a look at it," she said. "On the way back from the market. I thought I'd see what it was."

He did not say anything.

"There's a bench," she said. "There's grass. There's a pile of dirt somebody's tipped and left. There isn't anything there, Shen. There's a hole in the wall and there's grass and there's a bench with a broken slat, and I stood in it for about ten minutes and I could not work out what it was about that place."

She was not looking at him.

"You came home with your nose bleeding," she said. "You told me you were going to Zhao's for jars. Zhao is the other way. You have known where Zhao is since you could walk, and I have known where Zhao is for nine years, and you told me you were going to Zhao's."

"I know."

"So I went and had a look at it."

"Yes."

"And there is nothing there."

"No," said Lan Shen.

That was true, and it was the truest thing he said to her all month, and she took it for an answer because it sounded like one.

---

The holiday was warm. There were people on the street, which there always were on a holiday. The walk down past the Tan place took about the time it took.

His mother walked on his left. She did not hold his hand. She had not held his hand in about a year and he had noticed at the time. Now she walked on his left with her hands in front of her and looked straight ahead.

She said two things on the way. One was about the price of fish. One was about a step that was uneven. Neither of them was about anything.

Wulin was at the gap in the wall with Na'er on his hip.

"You came."

"I said I'd come."

"Mum, this is Lan Shen's mum." Wulin tried to put Na'er down. Na'er stayed where she was. "Na'er. Walk. You've got legs, you're six soon."

"I'm five and a half."

"You're six soon, that's what I said. Walk."

Na'er walked. She walked about four steps and then did not walk.

Lan Shen's mother said, "She's like her brother."

Wulin's mother came round the corner behind them with two other women he did not know, and said something that made all three of them laugh, and Wulin said, "What. What did she do."

"Nothing," said his mother. "Carry your sister."

"I am carrying my sister."

"Then stop telling her to walk."

---

The garden had about thirty people in it.

That was the thing he had not accounted for. He stood in the gap in the wall for a second and looked at it.

There were children running on the bank. There was a man asleep on the bench with his hat over his face, on the end with the missing slat. There were two older boys kicking something. There was a woman spreading a blanket near the mound and putting bowls on it. There was grass going to seed everywhere, knee-high and shoulder-high. It had been trampled flat in about six directions. There were paths in it that had not been there six days ago.

He had come to find one strand out of forty thousand.

Somebody had been standing on it all afternoon.

His mother said, "Go on."

He went.

---

He started at the bench, because the bench was the only thing in the garden that could not move.

The man was asleep on the left-hand end. That was the end with the missing slat. He was asleep on the gap. The nail head was sticking up into the small of his back through his jacket. He did not seem to mind.

Lan Shen stood in front of the bench and looked at it and worked out which way he had been facing.

He had been facing the bank. He had been sitting on the right-hand end, because the left-hand end was where people sat, and the right-hand end was where a boy who had come alone would sit. He turned around. He faced the bank. He stood exactly where he would have been sitting.

The bank was in front of him. The mound was at the south end. The tree was at the top.

The tree was left of the mound.

He had not been able to remember that for five days. He could see it now, from the bench, in about half a second. He thought about the fact that he had spent eleven lines of a book failing to do what standing in the right place did in half a second.

He put that in his head to write down later. Then he started counting.

Forty paces along the bank. He walked it. He walked it with small steps, because his legs were short and he did not trust his own stride. He counted. He got to forty at a place about two thirds of the way along, nowhere near the mound.

Forty paces was his paces. A man's pace was longer.

He walked it back. He walked it in his father's stride, which he could do, because he had been walking behind his father for six years.

He got thirty-one.

Thirty-one paces from the bench. Then left of the line to the mound. Then the middle third. Then one of about four hundred.

He stood in the grass at thirty-one paces with his hands at his sides and looked at it.

There was grass. There was grass. There was a place where two children had been wrestling, and the grass was flat and the dirt was showing. There was nothing on any of it that he could feel. There was nothing on any of it that he could see.

He had four seconds of a straight line. He had a place on a bank. They were not the same thing. He had known going in that they were not the same thing.

It made no difference to knowing it.

---

He got a stick.

It was not a good stick. It was a piece of something that had come off something else, about as long as his forearm, with a knot at one end. He picked it up off the path near the mound. He carried it to thirty-one paces. He pushed it into the dirt with his foot.

It went in about a hand's depth and stood up crooked.

He stood back and looked at it.

It was a stick in the ground in a garden full of grass, on a holiday, with thirty people in it. It meant one thing to one person in Glorybound City. That person was six years old and could not currently feel anything at all.

He took three more steps back and looked at it from where the bench was.

He had brought the book. He had not been able to think of a reason not to. He wrote the numbers down: *thirty-one from the bench in father's steps. It is forty in mine. The stick is in the bank. The tree is left of the mound. The bench slat is missing on the left and there is a nail head.*

He wrote the date. He had learned the date in cultural class in the second week, and it had seemed like a waste of an afternoon at the time.

Then he shut the book and stood there.

A boy went past at a run. He pulled the stick out of the ground on the way, without slowing down, without looking at it. He threw it into the bank about six paces to the left and kept going.

Lan Shen watched it land.

Six paces to the left. He looked at the place the stick had been. He looked at the place the stick had landed. He looked at the grass. He thought about writing down *six paces to the left*, and about what six paces to the left of a hole in the dirt meant in a garden that had been trampled flat in six directions since he was last in it.

He did not go and get the stick.

He stood there a while longer. Then he walked back along the bank in his father's stride, counting. He found the place where the children had been wrestling. He put a second stick in at the edge of it, because the flat grass was a mark too, and marks were the only thing he had.

Somebody pulled that one out as well, about ten minutes later. It was a girl. She used it to hit her brother with. Her mother told her to put it down. She put it down in a completely different place.

Lan Shen watched that too.

*I am not going to be able to hold this,* he thought.

*It is not the grass that moves. It is everything else.*

---

"Oy. Lan Shen."

Wulin came up the bank with Na'er by the hand and three other children behind him, all of them about his sister's size, all of them out of breath.

"What are you doing."

"Nothing."

"You put a stick in the ground," said Wulin. "I saw you. What's that for."

"For nothing."

"You've got your book out."

"I've always got my book out."

Wulin crouched down and looked at the hole where the first stick had been. He put one finger in it. He looked up.

"There's a hole here."

"Yes."

"Did you dig that."

"I pushed a stick in it."

"Why."

Lan Shen looked at him. Wulin looked back with his finger in the dirt, entirely happy, entirely without suspicion, waiting for an answer that would satisfy him whichever way it went.

"Because I wanted to know where it was," said Lan Shen.

Wulin thought about this for a moment.

"That's a stupid reason," he said. "You know where it is. You're standing in it."

"I know where it is today."

Wulin took his finger out of the dirt and wiped it on his trousers. He stood up. He looked at the bank, and at the mound, and at the children on it, and then he shrugged, because he was nine and this was not interesting any more.

"Na'er found a beetle," he said. "Do you want to see it. It's got legs that go backwards."

"No."

"They go backwards. Look."

He turned around and started down the bank. Na'er went with him. The three other children went with him. Lan Shen stood in the grass at thirty-one paces in his father's stride and watched them go, and did not follow, and after a while Wulin stopped and looked back.

"You coming."

"In a bit."

Wulin said something to the other children. They went on without him. He came back up the bank.

"You're being weird," he said.

"I know."

"Are you still bleeding."

"No."

"Are you going to bleed again."

Lan Shen thought about it. He thought about twenty seconds and the white line and the bank collapsing out of forty thousand things into one, and about the fact that the honest answer was that he did not know, and that he had done the arithmetic three times and got two answers.

"I don't think so," he said.

"That's not the same as no," said Wulin.

"No," said Lan Shen. "It isn't."

Wulin stood there with his hands on his hips. He looked at the grass. He looked at the hole. He looked at the bench, where the man was still asleep, still on the nail.

"You can just tell me," he said.

"I know."

"You told me it was the heat."

"I know."

"And it wasn't the heat."

"No."

Wulin nodded slowly, the way he nodded when he had been given a thing he did not want and had decided to carry it.

"All right," he said. "Then it wasn't the heat."

He went back down the bank to his sister.

---

His mother was standing by the mound with the woman who had the blanket. She had a bowl of something in her hand. She was not looking at him.

He went over, because he had been out for a while and because he could not think of anything else to do.

Wulin was on the bank with Na'er and the three other children, showing them something about a beetle. Na'er was standing very still in the middle of the grass, not doing anything, with her hands at her sides. Lan Shen looked at her for a second. Then he looked away, because there was a thing about her standing like that which he had noticed before and had decided was nothing.

His mother said, "Sit down."

He sat down on the edge of the blanket.

He was tired in a way that was not the tired of the wall or the floor. He had not done anything all afternoon. He had walked about seventy paces. He had written five lines. He had watched two sticks get taken away by strangers.

"Did you find what you were looking for."

He looked up.

His mother was looking at the bank, at the children on it, with the bowl in her hands. She had said it the way she said most things, without turning her head.

"No," he said.

It was true. It was the first true thing he had said to her about the garden. She nodded, as though he had answered a different question.

"That's all right," she said. "You can come back."

He thought about the month. He thought about the arithmetic he had done twice and got the same answer, and done a third time and got a different one. He thought about grass going to seed and dropping. He thought about a bank that had been trampled flat in six directions. He thought about a straight line that had been in his hands for four seconds, and had been gone for six days, and had not been written down properly in between.

He thought about the fact that if he came back in a month and found nothing, he would not know whether there had been nothing, or whether he had lost it.

"Yes," he said.

---

They walked home in the late afternoon with the light going off the harbour behind them. That meant the harbour was behind them. That meant they were going up.

Wulin came with them as far as his own gate. Na'er was asleep on his shoulder with her fist in his collar.

"You were quiet," he said.

"I was counting."

"What were you counting."

"Steps."

Wulin considered this for about four steps' worth.

"Steps to where."

"Nowhere," said Lan Shen. "Just steps."

"That's your thinking-about-the-shop face."

"I haven't got a shop."

"Your dad's got a shop," said Wulin, and went in through his own gate with a sleeping girl on his shoulder and a beetle in his other hand.

His mother went on up the street. He walked on her left this time. She did not say anything for about as long as it took to get from the Tan place to the corner by the drain.

At the corner she stopped, because there was a cart there.

While they waited for the cart she said, "There's a bench with a slat missing."

She was looking at the cart.

"Is that the one."

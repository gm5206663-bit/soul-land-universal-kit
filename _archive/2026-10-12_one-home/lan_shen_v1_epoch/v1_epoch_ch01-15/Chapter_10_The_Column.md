The forty-three days between the notice and the ceremony went the way forty-three days go when you are seven and nothing is being asked of you. He counted them because counting was what he did with time he could not spend. There was a week of rain that came in off the sea and stood in the street. There was a holiday. There was the thaw, which arrived all at once in a single afternoon and made the whole district smell like a cellar with the door open.

He walked to the garden twice and did not go in.

The first time, the grass was still down and the bank was still bare and there was nothing under his hands to work with. The second time, three days before the ceremony, the bank had gone faintly green at the roots, the way a thing goes green when it has decided to and has not yet told anybody.

He stood at the gate and felt it, and let go.

Not because it would have cost him. It might not have. He let go because the width of a whole bank coming back at once was not a thing to take with forty days of good behaviour behind him and three days to go, and because he had learned what width meant in a garden in the middle of the summer with two children walking toward him.

He wrote it down when he got home. *The bank is coming back. I did not touch it.*

Then he put the book on the shelf, second row, square to the edge, where his mother could reach it, and went to bed.

---

Awakening Day was a clear one.

The street in front of Red Mountain Academy was full the way it had been full a year ago, and it was full of the same people in the same coats, and Lan Shen walked up it with his hands in his pockets and understood, for the first time, that he was on the wrong side of the queue.

He was not in it. He had been in it. He had come out of it with a rank and a weed and a sentence from a man who had been crying without noticing, and now he was seven and he was walking past.

Parents stood in their best coats and did not say what they were thinking. Somebody had put up a second table, further in, and there was a line of children at it holding jars.

He recognised the jars.

They were his family's jars. They had the batch number on the slip and the measure on the slip. There were four crates stacked behind the table with the straw still in them, and a boy from the bursar's office was counting them down a list.

*Four dozen,* thought Lan Shen. *They kept four dozen back.*

"Are you coming in or are you standing there."

Na'er was two paces behind him, in a dress that had been let out at the sleeve, with her hair done up by somebody who was not good at doing hair up. Lang Yue was behind her with Tang Ziran, and Wulin was beside her, wearing an expression of enormous importance on her behalf.

"You're supposed to be early," said Wulin. "Qin Wu does them in order and if you're late you go at the end and the end is after lunch."

"How do you know that."

"Because I was there," said Wulin. "Last year. I was in the middle and it was still after lunch for the ones at the end."

Na'er said nothing. She looked at the crates.

"They're yours," she said.

"Some of them."

"They've got your writing on them."

That was not true and it was not untrue. The slips were in his father's hand and the numbers on the slips were the numbers Lan Shen had made, and a six-year-old who had been counting jars since the autumn would not have drawn the difference. He let it stand.

"That's the measure," he said.

"What's the measure for."

"So everybody gets the same."

She thought about that for the whole of the rest of the walk, which was about forty paces, and then she said, "Same as who."

---

Inside the gate the yard had been swept.

There was a chamber at the far side with a door wide enough for two men to carry a table through. There was a queue at the door. There was a table inside the gate with a register open on it and a shallow tray beside the register, and the tray was the reason the queue for the table was shorter than the queue for the door.

The man behind the register had ink on one cuff.

He had not changed. Lan Shen had expected him to have changed, in a year, and he had not. He was forty-ish and thin and cleanly dressed and he had the flat pleasant expression of a man who had been saying the same sentences since before sunrise and had made his peace with it. There was a new register, because the old one was full. That was the only difference.

Yan Song looked up.

He looked up at Lang Yue, at Tang Ziran, at Wulin. Then his eyes came down to Na'er and stayed there, because she was the one who mattered, and then they went past her to the boy standing half a pace behind her on her correct side.

"Lang," said Yan Song. "You're bringing her in."

"She's ours," said Lang Yue.

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the answer," said Lang Yue.

Yan Song wrote a name. He wrote it in a hand that was neither large nor small and did not hurry, and Lan Shen watched him do it and remembered the margin hand, which was smaller, and which he had been given one line of a year ago.

"You're taller," said Yan Song, without looking up.

"I'm seven."

"You were six." He turned the register a fraction, not toward Lan Shen, just enough that a person standing where Lan Shen was standing could see that it was turned. "Rank one. Bluesilver Grass. I have it here. You're not on the list for today, you know that."

"I know."

"Then you know more than most of them." He nodded at the queue. "Your family supplied the balm."

"Four dozen."

"Four dozen," said Yan Song. "At the spring price, thirty days, and we paid on the twenty-ninth. I want you to know that I checked." He put the pen down. It was not a friendly gesture and it had not been a friendly gesture last year either. "Your father's fee is off the account. That's done. What's not done is the other thing."

Lan Shen did not say anything.

"The seventh line," said Yan Song. "Materials in lieu of coin, at the academy's valuation, subject to the needs of the class. You read it to me. You were six and you read it to me and you told me what it meant and then you told me what you wanted out of it, and I want to be honest with you, because the honest version is cheaper for everybody."

"That's what you said to my mother."

"That's what I say." He did not smile. "The class did not need a working student last year. The class needed medicinals and it was buying them grey and you were correct about that and I have not enjoyed being correct-ed by a child, but you were correct. So this year the bursar has specified a measure. It is your measure. It is on four dozen jars in my yard. And now the class does need one."

Behind them a child came out of the chamber crying, and his mother took him by both shoulders and said his name twice.

"Need what," said Lan Shen.

"A working student." Yan Song picked the pen back up. "Not what your father did last year. Last year he brought jars against a fee and I valued them and we were finished with each other in the afternoon. This is different. This is four hours a week. Carrying crates, washing jars, standing where a man tells you to stand. And it is your name in my book, in a column, with a number of hours against it." He put two fingers on the open page, in the column, without covering anything. "A jar with a number on it goes in a storeroom. A boy with a number on him goes in a register. Those are different things, and I want you to understand which one you are agreeing to before you agree to it, because I am not going to explain it twice."

Lan Shen understood the difference.

Being in the yard was a thing that happened to you on a day when your neighbour was being written into a book. Being on the books was four hours a week, every week, in a building, in front of a man who kept a register, with your name in a column.

He had spent a year not being in a column.

"There is one thing," he said. "I am not going to do it yet."

Yan Song's pen stopped.

It stopped for perhaps a second, which in the life of a register is a long time.

"That's not a no," said Yan Song.

"It's not a yes."

"It's a boy with a rank-one weed and a mouth on him, and I have four hundred people to get through this year and I am telling you that the offer stands until the term starts and then it does not." And then he did the thing that Lan Shen would think about for the rest of his life, which was that he looked up, all the way up, and looked at him — not through him, not past him to the queue, not down at the page — and looked at him, for about as long as it takes to say a name.

"Go and stand with your neighbour," said Yan Song. "She's the one who matters today."

---

The chamber was long and had a stone floor and smelled of lamp oil and warm children.

There was a dais at the far end with a low stool on it. A man stood beside the stool in the plain good robe of somebody who was paid by an institution. A second man sat at a table with the register. That register was not Yan Song's register, and Lan Shen noted the fact and filed it, because there were two books in the room and only one of them had a tray of coins beside it.

Qin Wu was a fourth-rank man with a face that had gone soft at the edges from saying the same thing all day for twenty-three years.

He put his hand on the child's head. He said something short. The child stood there. Something happened or did not happen. Qin Wu looked at the second man. The second man wrote. The child went out one door while the next one came in the other.

It was fast. Lan Shen had not expected fast.

The grass in the ditch outside the chamber wall came through the floor to him whether he wanted it or not. The yard beyond that. The street beyond that. He stood with his hands in his pockets and did not go looking for any of it, because a chamber full of children whose souls were being lit was the widest thing he had ever stood in, and he had learned what width cost.

He counted the room instead. Twenty-two children in the queue. Three adults at the front. Two at the door.

He got twenty-five.

He subtracted one, because the one was always there. He got twenty-four. He looked round the room and counted again the slow way, with his eyes, and got twenty-four.

*Right,* he thought. *For the first time in eight months I am right, and all it took was deciding that I was always going to be wrong in the same direction.*

It was a small thing. He let himself have it for about four seconds and then he put it away, because Na'er was six children from the front and her hands were at her sides and she was not fidgeting, and a child who does not fidget in a queue is a child who has decided something.

Wulin leaned across him.

"She'll get a good one," said Wulin.

"You don't know that."

"My dad says everybody gets one. He says the only question is what it's shaped like." Wulin said it with the enormous confidence of a boy who had been told a thing by a man who loved him. "If she gets a pot, I'll still let her be my sister."

"She's already your sister."

"That's what I mean," said Wulin.

---

*Qin Wu had awakened thousands and tens of thousands of children.*

*He did not think of it that way, in those words, at that moment; he thought of it as a queue, a stool, a hand, a look. But the number was true and he knew it was true, and he had the specific calm of a man who has done a thing so many times that his body does it while his mind is somewhere else.*

*Six children. He put his hand on the first. A tool soul, some kind of awl, rank two, and the boy's mother made a noise at the back of her throat that she immediately pretended had been a cough. The second produced nothing at all and Qin Wu said the sentence he always said and the child went out, and that happened perhaps four times a year and he had a manner for it.*

*The third was the small one with the silver hair.*

*He put his hand on her head.*

*He said the short thing.*

*He waited.*

*Qin Wu had felt martial souls come up under his palm for twenty-three years. There was a shape to it. It was not a thing you could describe to a person who had not felt it, and he had stopped trying, but it was as reliable as a hand in warm water — something arriving, something unfolding, something that had been folded up very small now taking up the room it was entitled to.*

*Nothing arrived.*

*He waited longer than was polite. Then he waited longer than that. The child stood very still under his hand with her eyes open and did not fidget, and Qin Wu had the distinct and unwelcome sensation of a man leaning on a door that is not there.*

*He took his hand away.*

*He looked at her. She looked back. She was six and a half and she had amethyst eyes and something behind them that he had no business noticing, and he noticed it, and he put that in the same place he put everything else he had no business noticing.*

*"Does it hurt," he said.*

*"No."*

*"Have you ever felt anything in your chest, when you were frightened, or running."*

*"No," said Na'er.*

*He looked at the second man, whose name was Bo and who had the register, and Bo had his pen in the air, and the pen stayed in the air.*

*There was a column. There was a column for martial soul and there was a column for innate rank and there was a column for element where an element applied and there was a column for notes, and the notes column was narrow, because the notes column was for things like a child who fainted and a child who had to be brought back in the afternoon.*

*Qin Wu had filled an anomaly form twice in twenty-three years.*

*"Write it," he said.*

*"Write what."*

*"Nothing," said Qin Wu. "Write nothing."*

---

It took four minutes and it was the loudest silence Lan Shen had ever stood in.

He was at the side, because he was not family and he was not being written into anything. From there he could see the second man's page. He could see that the man had written the name and the age, then gone to the next column and stopped, then gone to the column after that and stopped there too.

"There's a form," said the second man.

"I know there's a form."

"There isn't a line for this on the form."

"Then there's a line for it on the other form," said Qin Wu. "Go and look. Bring both."

Lang Yue had come forward at some point. Lan Shen did not remember her coming forward. She was standing at the edge of the dais with her hands together in front of her, in the posture of a woman who has been told something and is waiting to be told the rest of it.

"Is she all right," said Lang Yue.

"She's perfectly well," said Qin Wu.

"Then what—"

"She doesn't have one." Qin Wu said it in the voice he had for the four-times-a-year version, and Lan Shen heard the voice and understood what it was, because a year ago he had stood in a queue and heard a man use the same voice on somebody else. "She has no martial soul. Not a weak one. Not a hidden one. There isn't one there."

Lang Yue said something that was not a word.

"It's rare," said Qin Wu. "I want you to understand that it's rare and that rare isn't bad. I've never seen it. I've been doing this twenty-three years and I've never seen it, and I'm going to have to write to two offices about it, and neither of them is going to know what to do with it either."

Wulin had come forward too. He had not been invited.

"What does she get instead," said Wulin.

"Wulin," said Lang Yue.

"What does she get," said Wulin. "Everybody gets one. My dad says everybody gets one."

Qin Wu looked down at a boy of seven who had rank one and a hammer at home and a father who had told him the world worked a certain way.

"Everybody does," said Qin Wu. "Go and sit with your sister."

*He's not wrong,* thought Lan Shen, from the side, where he was not family and was not being written into anything. *Everybody does. She doesn't. Both of those are true and he is going to be a long time finding out that a thing can be true and still not cover the case.*

He looked at Na'er.

She had come off the dais by then and was standing where she had been told to stand, with her hands at her sides. She was not crying. She was not looking at her mother. She was looking at the floor in the specific way of a child who has been in a room while adults discussed her.

Lan Shen had been that child for a year.

He went and stood next to her, on her correct side, because she had asked him to in the street and because he had said he would, and he did not say anything at all.

---

Yan Song took the second register when Qin Wu brought it out.

He did it at the table in the yard, with the tray of coins beside him, and he read the page once and then he read it again, and then he turned it and read it from the other side, as though the writing might be a mistake that would resolve itself if approached differently.

"There's no rank," said Yan Song.

"There's no soul," said Qin Wu.

"There's a column." Yan Song put one finger on the page, in the space, without covering anything. "There is a column here. It is the third one. It has had a number in it for every child who has walked out of that chamber in the nine years I have kept this book, and it has had a dash in it for the ones who came back with nothing, because there is a dash for that, and there is no dash for what you have brought me."

"Then leave it empty."

"I can't leave it empty." Yan Song said it patiently, the way a man explains arithmetic to somebody who would like the answer to be different. "An empty column is an unfinished register. An unfinished register goes back to me. If I put a dash in it I am saying she came back with nothing, and she did not come back with nothing, she did not go in with anything, and those are different facts and the second one is a fact that has no column."

"Write it in the notes."

"The notes column," said Yan Song, "is four words wide."

They looked at each other for a moment over the book.

Then Yan Song picked up the pen and wrote, in the third column, in the hand that was neither large nor small, the only thing a register can hold when it has been handed something it was not built for.

He wrote a number.

Lan Shen could not see it from where he stood and he did not move to see it, and later, for a long time, he would think about that — that he had stood still, that he had let it be a thing written in a book by a man he did not like, that he had not taken one step to look.

*Zero,* he thought. *He's written zero. There isn't anything else he can write.*

But Yan Song had not written zero. Zero was a dash, and Yan Song had just explained, at length, that a dash was the wrong fact.

He had written something, and he had put it in the rank column, and the rank column was the column that went upward.

---

They came out at the middle of the afternoon.

The sun had gone round behind the academy and the yard was in shade and the queue had thinned to the ones who were going to be there after lunch. Lang Yue had Na'er by the hand. Tang Ziran had Wulin on his shoulders, which was too old for Wulin and Wulin knew it and stayed up there anyway with his arms round his father's head.

Lan Shen walked on Na'er's correct side.

Nobody said anything for the whole of the walk home, and at the corner where the drain was, Su Wan was standing with a basket, because it was a holiday and because she had said in the autumn that she would be there on holidays, and she looked at the five of them coming round the corner and she did not say anything either.

At the Tang door Lang Yue stopped.

"Will you eat with us," she said, to Lan Shen.

"My mother's got the—"

"Will you eat with us," said Lang Yue.

So he ate with them. It was rice and a green thing and fish that had been salted. Wulin ate three bowls and talked about the ceremony, the awl, and the boy who had cried. Tang Ziran laughed at the right places. Lang Yue put food into Na'er's bowl twice without being asked, and Na'er ate it.

She did not say anything.

It was not the silence of a child who has been told she is nothing. Lan Shen had spent a year inside a silence like that and he knew the difference. It was the silence of a child who has been in a room while adults discussed her and who has come out of the room with the discussion still going on inside her head.

Afterwards he walked her to the step.

"Can I tell you something," he said.

"All right."

"Not a true thing," he said. "A made-up thing. My brother says I'm good at them and my mother says I'm a liar, and both of them are right, and I want you to know which one you're getting before I start."

Na'er looked up at him.

"A made-up thing," she said.

"Yes."

"All right," said Na'er.

So he sat down on the Tang step, on her correct side, and he told her a story, out loud, in the dark, with no paper anywhere near him, about a man who kept a shop at the top of a mountain where he sold nothing at all.

People came up the mountain and asked him what he had. He said nothing. They said that was what everybody said. He said no, he meant nothing: he had nothing, his shelves were empty, his jars were empty, his purse was empty, and he had been keeping the shop for forty years.

And they said, then what do you do up here.

And he said, I keep it.

"That's a stupid story," said Na'er.

"I know."

"He doesn't have anything."

"No."

"Then what's he keeping."

Lan Shen looked at the street. Two doors down his own lamp was on and his mother was moving past it. The bank in the garden three streets away was coming back green at the roots and he had not touched it.

"The mountain," he said.

Na'er thought about that.

"You can't keep a mountain," she said. "It's already there."

"Yes."

"And he can't sell it."

"No."

"And nobody's going to take it away."

"That's right," said Lan Shen. "Nobody's going to take it away. So he just keeps it. He's kept it for forty years, and everybody who comes up the mountain goes back down again and tells the same story about the idiot at the top of it. He doesn't mind, because the keeping isn't for them."

"He's an idiot," said Na'er.

"He is."

She sat with that for a while.

"Did you make that up just now," she said, "or did you already have it."

Lan Shen had already had it. He had had it for four years and another lifetime, in a drawer with everything else, and he had never once told it to anybody, and he had not known until that moment on the step what it had been kept for.

"Just now," he said.

"All right," said Na'er.

She got up and went inside, and the door shut, and the lamp in the Tang front room went on and then did not go off.

Lan Shen sat on the step for a while longer with his hands in his pockets.

There was a form in a drawer at the academy with two sheets in it already and a third one going up in the post, and a register with a number in a column that had no right to hold one, and a man with ink on his cuff who had looked at him, all the way up, for about as long as it takes to say a name.

And there was an offer standing until the term started.

He went home and he did not write any of it down.

*Not yet,* he thought. *There is one thing, and I am not going to do it yet.*

He put the book back on the shelf, second row, square to the edge, where his mother could reach it. He went to bed. He lay there in the dark with the year turning over outside, and he did not sleep for a while.

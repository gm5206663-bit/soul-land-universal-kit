# Chapter Fourteen: The Empty Case

The paper shop kept its door shut all winter, with a board across the inside of the glass that made the glass a wall. He had passed it a hundred times and never been in. There was a bell over the door that had forgotten what it was for, and when he pushed the door it remembered, late and rusty. The man behind the counter looked up at it as if it had bitten him.

The shop was a small room that smelled of glue and dust. Quires. Ledgers. Sewn account books with dark spines. Ink in two sizes. Nothing a boy could afford, and the boy did not look at it.

"We're not open for looking," said the man.

"I'm not looking. I'm asking."

The man put his glue brush down into the pot it lived in. "Ask, then."

"The man with the case," said Lan Shen. "The one who brings the bound books. When does he come."

"Twice a year. When the roads dry, and before they freeze."

"Which was the last one."

"Before they freeze," said the man. "Six weeks gone. He stayed a day and a half and he always stays a day and a half. You'll have remembered that for next time by the time you're my age. Nobody remembers it the first time."

"When the roads dry," said Lan Shen.

"Late spring. Ask me again in the thaw and I'll give you the same answer." The man picked the brush back up, and the conversation was over in the way shop conversations were over, which was without anything being said about it.

Six weeks. He stood on the step and let the cold find the gap in his cuff. The bell forgot him before he was three steps down the long street.

---

Lan Yue brought the first letter home in the same week, inside her coat against the frost, folded into the sleeve the way she carried noodle-ticket stubs.

"Mrs Guo," she said. "You know Mrs Guo."

"Her hands went bad," said Lan Shen.

"Her hands are fine now, she's back at the washhouse, she's got a daughter on the coast in a dye house and not a word of writing in the house either way." Lan Yue held out the folded paper. It was not paper. It was a coin, wrapped in a bit of rag so it would not chime. "One copper a letter. Two if you read it back. She's told two other women at the washhouse and they'll have work for you if you don't ruin the first one."

He looked at the rag. "You told her I can write."

"I told her you write fair."

"We don't write at school."

"You write fair," said Lan Yue, "because I've seen your father's slips." She said it the way she said everything that she had decided was already true, which was not fast and not slow, and left no room at either end. "The letter's tomorrow, at the washhouse, after the third bell. Don't ruin it."

At the bench Lan He said, without taking his eyes off the scales, "A copper for a letter."

"Two if I read it back."

"A copper for a letter," his father said again, weighing something that was not on the scales. "A man who keeps books on the long street gets two for a page of figures, and the figures tell him whether the shop is dying. A letter tells a mother whether her girl is alive. One copper." He set a weight down. "The washhouse is doing you a kindness, and it does not know it. Whatever they pay, keep the hand fair."

---

Mrs Guo dictated with her hands in the water.

She was a square woman gone grey at the front, with red knuckles and a way of holding her elbows out from her sides so the wet apron would not touch her dress. The washhouse yard was grey light and steam, and the letter-paper was the back of a laundry list, because he had not yet thought of that side of it and she had. She stood him at the edge of her trough where his sleeve would not trail in the water and she did not once stop working.

"To Fen," she said. "Start with her name so she knows it's hers. Tell her the cold came early this year and the pipes burst at Mrs Lu's and she sends her love and her wages came through. Tell her — " Her hands went on working. Something in the middle of the telling came up against something in the middle of her, and the telling stopped for the length of one wrung sheet. "Tell her her cousin had a boy in the autumn and they're calling him after the grandfather."

He wrote it.

"Ask her when she's coming home."

He wrote half of it, and stopped, because he had learned the shape of a stopped sentence by listening to them. This one was not finished, it was abandoned.

Mrs Guo looked at the half line, and at his hand. For a moment her face did the thing faces did when a person read their own words back in somebody else's ink. "No," she said. "Take that out. Write that the winters are shorter than they used to be. They aren't, but write it."

He took it out. He wrote the winters. Her hands had never left the water. The sheet she was wringing was wrung tighter than a sheet needed to be by then, and he wrote on.

"Read it back," she said.

He read it back. She stopped him once, on the cousin's name, and he had it right. She said "huh" the way a person says it when a thing costs two copper and turns out to be worth two copper. The letter forgot it had ever been a laundry list. She paid the two without unwrapping the rag from anything else, and the coins were warm from being carried inside her coat. That was the first money the writing hand had ever made, in any life, here or there.

It was the easiest writing of his two lives. The sentences arrived already true. All he had to do was be legible, be there, and keep his sleeve out of the trough. Nobody crossed anything out of him. Nobody reading it could ever guess the hand was a child's, because the women of the washhouse did not know his hand from a grown clerk's, and knew no reason they should.

Three letters some weeks, after that. None, other weeks. A washhouse was not a post office. The coins went into a jar that had held winter balm once and weighed nothing like balm now, and the jar sat on the ordinary shelf in plain sight, because a hideable jar was a jar somebody looked for and a boring jar was furniture.

---

Su Wan caught the jar's existence the way she caught everything, by not looking at it until she had finished counting it from across the room.

She was at the pump on the first warm morning of the spring, sleeves up, and he came out with the bucket and she took it from him in the way that meant stay.

"Words are paying now," she said.

"Coppers."

"That's not a no."

"It's not a no," he said.

The pump handle had remembered his weight some time in the winter and lifted his feet off the ground when he hung on it. He hung on it now, because a boy hanging on a pump is a boy, and she watched him be one for a moment and did not say what she was measuring. Her eyes went to the window over the sink the way they went to the rain barrel. Then she said, "Keep the hand fair," and it was his father's phrase in his mother's mouth. He understood they had talked about it at night in the low voices and decided the jar was a good jar. That was the end of the conversation in both directions.

At school the Wednesdays kept saying four minutes. The column in his book kept its shape, *why is this straight* kept its page, and the jar got heavier by coins that weighed nothing each.

In the winter his soul power had passed six.

He knew it the way you know a stair in your own house in the dark, by putting your foot where it was and finding the next one already there. There was no measurement for it that did not cost a hundred Federation Coins, so there was no measurement, and there was no writing it down either, because a number written was a number findable. He had one book, and the book was not a safe.

Six, then not six. The street did not change because of it. The street had its own numbers and they were all older than his.

---

He reported two in the late spring, on a Wednesday, after the measure.

He had spent a week deciding the size of the lie, because it was a lie of direction and lies of direction collected interest. The list the school kept was an honest list, and an honest list wanted to stay honest. A boy listed at one for two and a half years was either a slow boy or a hidden one, and the office knew what a slow boy looked like because slow boys scattered. Nobody held still by accident. A small move, on a small day, said plainly: that was what a slow boy's list did. It moved late, it moved a little, and it apologised for neither.

"Teacher."

"Lan Shen," said Lin Ximeng.

"I think I'm rank two now."

The man looked at him over the register. It was not a long look. It was not a short one either. In it Lan Shen could see the arithmetic a careful man did when a number arrived at his desk without being asked for. Compare the memory. Compare the Wednesdays. Note the difference, note that the difference was noted, and file both.

"Two," said Lin Ximeng. It was not a question. He wrote it in the register, and then he wrote something in the small black book that lived in his coat. The pen moved in the slow even way it moved when he was writing a fact and not a thought. "That's good. Keep at it."

"Yes, teacher."

That was all. It was, Lan Shen knew, nowhere near all, because the register now said two and the Wednesdays still said four minutes, and whatever had been written in the black book had put those two numbers in one place for the first time. He had made his file one layer thicker and one layer duller, which was the only kind of thick a file was allowed to get.

"You're two," said Wulin, on the walk home.

"I'm two."

"That's good. You'll make it. You've got years."

"Make what."

"Ten," said Wulin, as if it were obvious, which it was, which was the trouble. "Before the door. There's two of us already there. Bao made it last term and Jiang made the cultural school hear about it for a week." He said it without any victory in it. Victories over Lan Shen were not a currency Wulin carried. "I'm nine and a bit. Teacher says a bit is polite for nearly."

"You're nearly."

"I'm nearly," Wulin agreed, and went back to balancing on the kerbstones, which remembered him and let him, as if ranks were weather and the weather was good, which for him, that spring, it was.

---

He went back to the paper shop when the roads were dry, with the jar in his satchel and the jar's whole weight resting at eighty-one copper.

The bell remembered him this time. The man behind the counter, whose name turned out to be Qiao, or whose name was at least what the case man called him, looked at the coins counted out on the glass and then at the boy who had counted them.

"The rough quire," said Lan Shen.

"Fifteen."

"Fifteen," Lan Shen agreed, and pushed them over. Twenty-five sheets, in a block, rough as sacking. The good lay beside it, white and limp and out of the question, priced within a coin of what the good balm went for at the shop at home, which was the only price in the world he had ever watched being made.

"You'll want the rough anyway," said Qiao. "The good is sized and the ink sits on it. The rough drinks. You've been told."

"I've been told."

"And the case man is in the back," said Qiao, as if that were nothing, nodding at the curtain, and the room changed temperature in the specific way a room does when a thing you have been waiting six weeks for turns out to have been standing behind a curtain drinking tea.

Old Pei came through the curtain with the tea still in his hand. He had a back that had chosen a direction, and it had chosen toward the case. The case itself stood on two straps against the wall, leather gone the colour of strong tea, as tall as the boy was, with corners rubbed pale by cities.

"This yours," Old Pei said, of the quire.

"Mine," said the boy.

"You count like a man I owe money to," said Old Pei, which made Qiao laugh once through his nose, and that was the strongest anybody's face moved in the whole of that afternoon.

The case opened along its middle like a book. Lan Shen had prepared himself to be unimpressed, because a trader's case was a shop and he knew shops. He was unimpressed, and it was fine that he was, because the case's lesson was not in its stock.

Ledgers. Primers, two kinds. Sewn account books. A flat box that rattled. A stack of bent things with printed covers, eight of them, their corners soft from being read by people who had paid for them and intended to get their six copper's worth. The covers remembered other cities.

"Wonder tales," said Old Pei, following his eyes. "Six copper each. Coast price. I add nothing for the road because the road's coming anyway. If I added for it, the children here would stop buying, and a stopped customer never starts again."

"Who writes them," said Lan Shen.

"Men in the coast cities."

"Who buys them there."

"Shops," said Old Pei. "Every city on the coast has a shop with a boy in the back who does nothing but copy what sells. What sells is what does not come back. A man pays six copper for a wonder tale and does not bring it back, that tale is a trade, and the shop will pay for the next one like it." He drank his tea. "Nobody writes them here. Nothing is written here. This is a place where paper is bought. It is not a place where paper is made into anything. I've carried from this city for nine years and the only thing that has ever travelled out of it in my case is empty space."

"Can I read one," said Lan Shen.

"Read it here. A read book is not a spent book." Old Pei waved the tea at the stack, and watched, with the mild interest of a man watching weather through a window, as a boy stood in a paper shop and read a wonder tale from one end to the other without sitting down.

It was not bad. That was the thing he had not expected to know with such certainty. It was not bad. It was easy, it moved, its people said true-shaped things. Its ending stopped instead of arriving. It was finished in the way that mattered to the road, which was that a man who had paid six copper for it did not bring it back. He could feel, the way you feel a draught, the places where the writer had cared and the places where the writer had had to post the thing and had posted it.

He set it back on the stack, square, the way he set everything.

"Well," said Old Pei.

"It's finished," said Lan Shen.

"That," said Old Pei, "is exactly what it is. Most people say it's good or it's rubbish. It's finished. There's more money in finished than in good." He took the cup one-handed and looked at the boy the way he had looked at the quire. "You write."

"Letters," said Lan Shen. "For the washhouse."

"Letters." A trader's nod, pricing something. "What do you have that is finished."

"Nothing you'd carry."

"Then write faster," said Old Pei. That was the whole of the chapter's question, said in a trader's voice over a cup of tea, and it detonated the way things detonate when they are both true and simple. "Because if you ever have something that is finished, properly finished, covered and sewn, with nothing left to write in it, I will carry it to the coast. A shop there will look at it, and if it is new, and if it holds, they will give between twelve and twenty copper for it and ask if there is another. One part in three is mine. The case comes home empty either way. I'd rather it came home with your book in it than with nothing."

"Twelve to twenty," said Lan Shen.

"It does not sound like much," said Old Pei. "It is not much. But a thing that holds gets carried twice. The second time at their cost, not yours, because the shop fronts the paper when the shop wants the book. The first one you lose on. Nobody keeps making the first one." He set the cup down. "There is one rule. I do not carry a book twice. If it comes back, it comes back, and we do not speak of the next one. A book that comes back was not finished, whatever it says on the cover."

"When do you come back," said Lan Shen.

"Before the frost," said Old Pei. "When I always come back. Bring it then."

Lan Shen looked at the ledgers, the primers, the flat box that rattled, the eight bent wonder tales, and the trader with the bent spine drinking tea in a paper shop. He saw the road laid out under the whole of it like a vein under skin.

"What do they charge," he said, "for a spirit soul."

Old Pei looked at him for a moment with the tea halfway to his mouth.

"Wrong trade, boy. I carry paper." He drank. "But I will give you this free, because it is the only thing in any trade that is free. Prices are the one thing nobody charges you for. Go and ask at the Pagoda. They will tell you their prices in a bored voice to a child with no money, because children with prices grow into men with money. They learned that before I did."

"That's the whole answer," said Lan Shen.

"That's the whole answer to everything," said Old Pei, and went back to his stock-list. Qiao sold the boy a small bottle of the ink that the rough sheets drank best, for two copper. The jar that had weighed eighty-one weighed sixty-four. The bell remembered him kindly on the way out.

---

The Pagoda had a hall like a bank's idea of a temple, and he went there on the long way home from the jar-washing, on a Tuesday, with no purchase and no appointment and nothing but Old Pei's truism under his arm.

Stone. A brass rail polished at hand height. A desk like a shop counter, only cleaner. Doors with quiet behind them. And on the far side of a wall of glass was a room with machines in it, and a metal chair. A child he did not know sat in the chair with its head inside something that hummed, while a man in a long coat read a screen the way a man reads a letter he already knows the end of.

His head remembered being six. It remembered asking what else the ceremony measured, and being told there was a test for that if there was a reason. He watched the child in the chair for three breaths, and then he went to the desk, because a reason was another word for money and he had come to learn what the reason cost.

The clerk was a middle-sized man with a stamp he had clearly forgotten could make an interesting noise, because he used it the way other men tapped a desk.

"Prices are posted," he said, before Lan Shen had finished arriving at the counter.

"I'm asking," said Lan Shen, and something about the sentence, or about being outmanoeuvred by a child at his own counter, made the man actually look at him rather than at the space above him.

"Ask, then."

"The draw," said Lan Shen.

"Thirty thousand Federation Coins," said the clerk, in the bored voice Old Pei had promised. "Random spirit soul, white or yellow, no choosing. Defectives in the pool. That's the draw."

"And choosing."

"Seventy thousand, for a guaranteed ten-year. A hundred-year is not a number we say to boys at counters." He said it without any cruelty in it. Cruelty would have meant interest. "The examination is a hundred Federation Coins, and no, it is not being waived for you. It is waived when a Spirit Master says it is waived, which is when he feels like it, which for you is never."

"Does anybody buy the draw."

"Everybody buys the draw," said the clerk. "Nobody here has seventy thousand. That is why the draw exists." He picked the stamp back up. "Anything posted is free to ask about. Anything not posted, you come back with your father."

"Thank you," said Lan Shen.

"Form an orderly queue of one," said the clerk, to the empty hall, which was as near as that building was ever going to come to a goodbye.

He walked home the short way, and did the arithmetic on the steps while the lamp lighter went past, because a number walked with was a number half-digested and this one needed digesting.

Thirty thousand bought a throw of dice. Seventy thousand bought the choosing. He had known it was a ladder since the spring of the second year; what he had not had until today was the rungs. Seventy thousand was six thousand three hundred and sixty-four days of Lan Yue's mornings. Seventeen and a half years of getting up in the dark. It was thirty-five hundred letters to the coast and back, if a washhouse could produce thirty-five hundred daughters who had gone anywhere. It was, at twelve copper a finished tale after the road had taken its third and the paper had taken its due, something like four hundred finished tales, if a boy could write four hundred, which no boy could, which was the point the arithmetic was making.

The ladder stood. It was weather with a name now, but it stood.

---

What he wrote that summer, in the long evenings, on the backs of his father's account sheets, was not a letter and it was not about anyone in the street.

That was the first decision, and he made it the way he had made the last first decision, in about two seconds. The washhouse got his true sentences and paid a copper each for them; the coast would get something else. He wrote a boy who wanted a sword and was given a hammer, a smithy at the end of a street that was not what it seemed, and a gate that would open for the boy when the boy stopped needing it to. He wrote it slow, because slow was the speed the hand went, and the sentence on the page kept being better than the one in his head and he kept resenting it and it kept being true.

The draft grew along the backs of the accounts. Three pages written to keep one. The old rule, the only rule the other life had left him that he still respected. On read-back, in the middle of the seventh kept page, the boy with the hammer was described as the kind of boy who thanked the anvil.

He knew that boy. He had lived two doors down from that boy for three years.

He thought about striking it out. He had struck out worse sentences. He kept it. He did not think about why for very long, on purpose, and the page stayed where it was.

Lan Yue found the quire on the table in the eighth month, came round it the way she came round everything, and read three sheets standing up before she said anything at all.

"This isn't a letter."

"No."

"This is a made-up thing."

"Yes."

"You're copying it out fair," said Lan Yue, "for a reason."

"A man carries books to the coast, twice a year. I want him to carry one of mine." It was the true answer. It was the first true answer he had ever given her, and he found, giving it, that he had braced for the usual thing and the usual thing did not come.

She waited for the rest of it. There was no rest of it. She got up, went to the tin over the stove, wrote something on the ticket with the pencil, and came back and finished the sheets.

"What did you write," he said.

"*Says a man can carry his book to the coast*," said Lan Yue. "I underlined *says*."

And she had. He did not tell her there was nothing else to tell. That was the sixth one. It was true, it satisfied nobody, and it stayed on the tin.

The fair copy went slower than the draft, because a fair hand is a third hand, neither the counter's nor the story's, and it has to be taught on the job. The rough quire drank the ink the way Qiao had promised. A second quire cost another fifteen in the ninth month, when the jar had recovered to sixty. The sewing would cost three more, Qiao said, if the edges came to him untorn. The deadline was before the frost. Old Pei had said when I always come back, and meant it.

The bank that year had come back to twenty thousand six hundred in the spring and peaked in midsummer at forty-three thousand seven hundred, . He had counted it on the holidays, every season, and touched none of it.

In the third month of the warm term he shut the storeroom door and stood the way he had stood the winter before, and held the meditation out past the old edge and down the far side of it. Nine had stopped being the edge some time while the letters were being written. Twelve held, clean, with nothing left over and nothing owed. He did not write twelve down. He came out of the storeroom and went back to four minutes on Wednesdays, because four was the number on the list, and the list was the whole of the camouflage.

His soul power had passed seven by then too, or that was the shape it had. He was losing the right to the integer. He did not mind. Integers were for the school's book.

The frost came, on the calendar's schedule and not on his.

---

He was nine before the frost. Nobody mentioned it. It was becoming the custom.

He went to the paper shop in the second week of the cold with the fair copy at a quire and a half of two quires and the draft's last page still wrong under his hand at night, . The bell remembered him. Qiao did not look up from the glue pot.

"He was here on Tuesday," said Qiao. "Stayed the day and a half, like always. I told him there was nothing for the case yet. He said there never is, from here, and he went." The brush went round the pot once. "He'll be back when the roads dry."

"Nothing for the case," said Lan Shen.

"He asked what," said Qiao. "I didn't tell him. It's not mine to tell. He said good, keep it quiet, a promised book that's late is just a book." He looked up then, briefly, at the boy and the weather outside him. "Finish it slow. A returned one is worse than a late one. He means it about the twice."

He finished it in the cold months, by lamplight, in the fair hand, because a thing finished was a thing that existed, and a thing that existed could wait on a shelf better than a boy could. The lamp remembered oil the way the jar remembered balm. Qiao sewed it in the first week of the cold for the three copper, trimmed true, with a cover the colour of dishwater that would not impress anybody into opening it and would not warn anybody off either.

It sat on the shelf like a tin of wicks. His mother dusted it once and moved it nowhere.

His father picked it up once, read the first page and the last, set it down square, and said, "It ends."

"Yes."

"Letters end too," said Lan He, "and they pay in the same week." But he said it without any weight on it, turning the sewn thing over once to see the stitching the way a man turns over any piece of work. Then he let it be a thing his son had made. That was, in his way, a large gift, and neither of them remarked on it.

---

Wulin came on a night in the late autumn, after dark, in the cold, and did not knock so much as arrive against the door.

Na'er was a step behind him, holding her coat shut with one hand, saying nothing, eyes going once round the room the way they always went once round a room, counting the people in it.

"Thirty thousand and two hundred," said Wulin, in the doorway, before anybody's greeting. Short sentences. Colour high in his face. Both fists shut like a boy holding himself onto the ground. "Three years. I counted it twice, with mum, on the table. Thirty thousand and two hundred. Two whole hundred past the number."

"Come in out of the cold," said Su Wan, and meant it. They came in, and the news came in with them and stood in the middle of the front room the way a stove stands.

"Thirty thousand and two hundred," said Lan He, from the bench, slowly, the way a man says a number to weigh it rather than repeat it. "Three years of hammering."

"Two hours a day," said Wulin. "Every day. Mang Tian says I'm nearly there. Nearly ten. He asked me straight and I said nearly and he said good luck, which he never says, he says do the work." The words were falling out of him in the wrong order and neither of them cared about the order. "As soon as I make ten we're going, all of us, dad's taking the whole day off, the whole family, Na'er's coming, you'll come, your mum and your dad and Lan Yue, it's a whole day, the Pagoda lets the family in."

"You'll be measured," said Lan Shen.

"There's a thing they put on your head," said Wulin, incurious, at the height of his own news, waving it away with one shut fist. "It tells them your number for the record. It doesn't cost if you're buying. I heard the part about the buying." And then, with the absolute faith of a boy who has done the arithmetic and the arithmetic has come true: "A hundred coins, Na'er. Good food, from the shop with the glass front. I said so on the way here."

"Two hundred was for saving," said Na'er, quietly, the first word out of her.

"A hundred's for eating," said Wulin.

Lan Shen stood in his own front room and watched his friend's news occupy it, and did the only arithmetic he had.

Seventy thousand, minus a jar, minus a sewn book on a shelf that would not travel until the roads dried. Twelve to twenty, if it was new, if it held, one part in three to the road. Four hundred tales, or thirty-five hundred letters, or seventeen and a half years of mornings that were not his to spend.

And under all of it, quiet as the second hand nobody winds, the thing in him that had no integer any more, close enough now that he could feel the door's lintel the way you feel a wall with your hand flat out in the dark. He did not say near. He did not say it anywhere.

"Well done," he said, to Wulin, and meant every part of it, because the counting had been his friend's and the work had been his friend's and three years of two hours a day was a thing nobody in either family could do the sums to set against. "You should say it to Mang Tian first thing. A man who never says good luck will want the rest of the sentence."

"He asked to see the grass when I tell him," said Wulin. "After. He said bring the martial soul out and let me look."

That sat down in the room for a moment, small and warm and ordinary, and nobody there could hear what was in it, because there was nothing in it yet.

Then Su Wan put bowls on the table without being asked, because news of that size in that house got fed the same as people. Lan Yue was sent through the cold two doors down to say come and eat. Lan Shen walked out with her as far as the gate, into the frost, and stood there after she had gone in, with the dark all the way to the end of the street and the lamp of the Tang house coming on across the way.

Seventy thousand was the number. The carry man came back when the roads dried. The book on the shelf was finished and the story in it held. He knew it held, the way he had always known, as a cessation of arguing. And under the writing hand the stairs were running out, one at a time, quiet, unpaid for, unrecorded.

The roads would dry.

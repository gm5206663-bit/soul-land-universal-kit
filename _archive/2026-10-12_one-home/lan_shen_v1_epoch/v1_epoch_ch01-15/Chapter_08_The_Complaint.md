"Yes," said Lan Shen. "That's the one."

His mother did not move. The cart went past. The man with it said something to the horse. The corner by the drain was empty again, and she was still looking at the place where the cart had been.

"The one with the nail sticking up," he said.

"I saw the nail."

"He was asleep on it."

"Who was."

"A man. On the bench. On the broken bit." Lan Shen put his bag on his other shoulder. "He had a hat over his face."

His mother turned around then and looked at him. He could see her working something out, and he let her, because he had learned in the last four days that his mother working something out took about as long as it took and could not be hurried.

"There were thirty people in that garden," she said.

"Yes."

"You were in it for two hours."

"Yes."

"And you spent the whole of it walking up and down the bank counting."

It was not a question. He did not answer it, which was the same as answering it, and she let that go too.

"Shen."

"Yes."

"You don't go there on your own."

He thought about this. He thought about the four seconds and the straight line and the twenty seconds and the white line at the edges of his eyes and the fact that he had been alone in a garden full of forty thousand things with nobody within a hundred paces who could have carried him home.

"All right," he said.

"That's not a punishment."

"I know."

"It's because you bled," she said. "There was nobody there. I was two doors away eating supper, and I am going to have that in my head for a while."

He looked at the drain. There was water in it, going along slowly.

"You can come," he said.

His mother made a noise that was almost a laugh and was not.

"On the holiday," he said. "Every month. You can come and stand where you can see me and I'll do the counting and we can come home. That's what we did today."

"That is what we did today."

"So we can keep doing it."

She looked at him for a long time. He was six and he had just offered his mother a job. He could see her deciding whether to be cross about it, and then not being cross about it, and then noticing that she was not cross about it.

"All right," she said.

They walked the rest of the way home. At the gate she put her hand on the top of his head for about a second, which she had not done in a while either, and then she went in to see about the fish.

Lan Shen stood in the yard.

He put his hand flat on the wall, because he was passing it, and there was nothing.

He thought: *I have just made my mother part of the instrument.*

He thought: *that is not a bad thing. That is what a bench is. That is what a stick in the ground is. That is a thing that does not move, standing where you need it to stand.*

He thought: *and she is going to ask me every month what I am counting. I am going to say steps. She is going to let me.*

He went inside.

---

The complaint came home with Lan Yue on the twenty-fourth day.

She came in at the end of the eleven o'clock, which was the wrong end of it, because the eleven o'clock went to eleven and it was not yet a quarter past, and she came in through the yard with her sleeves wet to the elbow and her jaw set the way it got set.

"What," said his mother.

"Nothing."

"Lan Yue."

"It's Mrs Guo," said Lan Yue. "She says the balm doesn't work any more."

The back room went quiet in the way the back room went quiet.

His father put down the pestle.

"What do you mean doesn't work," he said.

"Her hands have gone bad again. She says she's been using it every night the way I showed her and her hands have gone bad anyway. She said it to me at the door with her coat on. She says she's not paying for a jar that doesn't do anything, and then she gave it back."

Lan Yue put a jar on the table.

It was one of theirs. Lan Shen could tell from across the room. It had a slip on it. The slip had his father's handwriting on it, and the batch number, and the line that said which stick the batch had been made against — the line his father had started putting on the slips in the second week.

His father picked it up and turned it over and read the slip.

He said the number under his breath. Then he got up and went to the crock by the bench and took out the dip-stick. He came back and sat down and unscrewed the jar.

Everybody watched him.

He dipped the stick. He held it up to the window. He looked at the wax line against the two cut marks. He turned the stick a quarter of the way round and looked again. Then he set it down on the bench and wiped it on a rag.

"It's right," he said.

"She says it isn't."

"It's right," said his father. "It's above the lower line and below the upper line and it's the same as every jar that went out that week, and I wrote the number on the slip myself."

"Then why are her hands bad."

Nobody said anything for a while.

Lan Shen was looking at his sister's sleeves. They were wet to the elbow. The water on them was cold. There was a bit of something grey in the cuff that was lye.

"It's the water," he said.

His father looked at him.

"Mrs Guo washes," he said. "So does Lan Yue. The water's colder than it was. Her hands are in it the same number of hours and the water's colder, so her hands are worse, and it isn't the balm."

The back room stayed quiet.

"How do you know she washes," said his mother.

"Lan Yue washes."

"That's not—"

"It's the same job," said Lan Shen. "Isn't it."

Lan Yue looked at her sleeves. She pulled one down over her hand.

"It's cold," she said. "It's cold every day now. In the morning you can't feel your thumbs for the first hour."

His father picked up the jar again. He looked at it for a long time. Then he screwed the lid back on and put it on the table. He did not put it back on the shelf, and Lan Shen noticed that he did not put it back on the shelf.

"The balm's all right," his father said. "That's not the same as it being enough."

---

They talked about it for a while after that, in the way his family talked about things, which was in short bursts with long gaps.

His mother said they could not make it stronger, because stronger meant more wax and more wax meant dearer and dearer meant nobody bought it. His father said it was not the wax, it was the comfrey, and the comfrey was what it was. Lan Yue said Mrs Guo had asked whether there was something for the cold and she had said she didn't know.

Lan Shen sat at the table and listened, and thought about a thing he had been thinking about for ten days.

He thought about a man in a garden asleep on a nail head who did not mind the nail head.

He thought about eleven lines in a book, eight of which were worthless because they described a bank that had since been trampled flat in six directions.

He thought about forty things under his hands for two weeks, then none, then — in eleven days — forty again. He thought about how he had no way at all of knowing whether forty was the number of grasses or the number of grasses that week.

He thought about the three thin things at the base of his own wall. Nobody swept them. Nobody trod them. They had been three for as long as he had lived in the house.

He said, "Can I have the stick."

His father looked up. "What."

"The one with the two lines. Can I make one for something else."

"There isn't anything else."

"There is," said Lan Shen. "It's just not in here."

His father looked at him across the bench for a second. Then he got up and went to the offcut rack and took down a piece of the same wood. He put it on the bench in front of his son, along with the knife and the nail and the hammer.

He did not say anything. He went back to the pestle.

Lan Shen made a stick.

He put two lines on it and two on the other face. He cut the numbers in with the nail. He did it slowly, because his hands were small and the wood was hard, and it took him most of an evening. Nobody in the back room said a word to him about it except his mother once, who said mind your thumb.

He did not put weights on this one. He put three marks on one face and the same three on the other, and he did not number them, because he did not know what the numbers were yet.

He put it under his bed.

---

For nine days he counted grass.

He did it on the way to school and he did it on the way back, and he did it with his eyes, because that was the only instrument he had.

He counted the same stretch. It went from the corner by the drain to the step outside the noodle place. It was about ninety paces in his father's stride and about a hundred and forty in his own. There was grass at the base of the wall on the left for most of it. There was the gutter. There was the crack by the Tan family's step.

The first day he counted thirty-one.

The second day he counted thirty-three. He stood there for a while trying to work out whether he had missed two or whether two had appeared, and decided that he could not tell.

The third day he counted thirty.

The fourth day it rained and he counted from under the eaves and got twenty-eight and did not trust it.

The fifth day he counted thirty-two.

He wrote every number down. He wrote the day next to it and he wrote the weather next to that, because by the fifth day he had understood that the number was not a number, it was a number and a day and a weather, and that the same stretch of gutter was not the same stretch of gutter from one morning to the next.

By the ninth day he had nine numbers and they went from twenty-eight to thirty-four and they did not settle, and he looked at them in a row on the page and understood something that made him put the pen down.

He had been treating forty as a fact.

Forty was a number the sense had given him once, in the second week, on a street, in weather. It was not the street. It was the street on a day.

And he had spent a fortnight grieving an instrument that had never been accurate in the first place, and had not once asked himself what it was accurate to.

He wrote: *the street is not a number. The street is a number per day. I have been measuring myself against a thing that moves and calling the movement my own.*

He looked at that. Then he got the second stick out from under his bed and went into the yard.

---

The three thin things were at the base of the wall, in the angle where the wall met the ground, in about a hand's width of dirt that the rain got to and the broom did not.

He counted them by eye.

Three.

He counted them every morning after that, before school, and every evening. He wrote it down. Three, three, three, three. It rained twice and they were still three. Somebody's goat got into the yard on the thirty-third day and ate the leaves off the middle one. It was still three. The middle one was a stump, and he wrote *stump* next to the number.

He did not open the sense on them, because he could not.

He put his hand flat on the ground beside them every morning anyway. There was nothing. He did it anyway. By about the thirty-sixth day he had stopped noticing that he was doing it.

School went on. Lin Ximeng walked the rows every day and stopped at his desk every day for exactly as long as he stopped at any desk, and said nothing, for three weeks.

On the thirty-eighth day he wrote in the book: *he has three weeks of nothing in his book about me. Three weeks of nothing is a shape. Somebody reading it later will see a gap that is exactly three weeks long, and a gap that is exactly three weeks long is not the same as no gap.*

He thought about writing something down that would fill it. He thought about it for most of an afternoon.

He did not, because he could not think of anything that would be worth the risk of being wrong about, and because he had spent three weeks learning what happened when you wrote a thing down before you could check it.

---

The sense came back on the fortieth day, in the evening, while he was doing the wall.

There was no moment to it. He had his hand flat on the floor and there was nothing. Then he had his hand flat on the floor and there was something, and the something was three thin things in the angle of the yard, one of which was a stump.

He stayed where he was for a while.

He did not open it wider. He did not reach. He sat on the floor of his bedroom with his hand flat and felt three things in a hand's width of dirt. That was all. It was enough. It was exactly what had been there when it went away.

He counted them.

Four.

He counted them again.

Four.

He sat on the floor with his hand flat and counted four things in the angle of the wall. He had counted three with his eyes every morning and every evening for eleven days. He had written down three eleven times, and one of those three was a stump.

He got up. He went into the yard. He knelt down in the dirt with the wall at his back and looked.

There were three. Two with leaves and one that was a stump. There was no fourth. He put his face about a hand from the ground and looked at the angle from every side he could manage. There were three. He could have sworn to it in front of anybody.

He went back inside and put his hand flat on the floor.

Four.

He wrote it down. He wrote *eyes: three. sense: four. same corner, same evening, one minute apart. I do not know which is wrong.*

Then he sat on the edge of the bed and thought about it for a long time, and what he thought about was not the fourth.

He thought about the fact that he had found a place in the entire city where nothing moved. He had looked for it for three weeks. He had found it in a hand's width of dirt in his own yard, where the broom did not go and the goat had been once. He had counted it eleven days running and got the same answer. He had been right to trust it.

And the first time he could check it, it disagreed with itself.

He thought: *there is nothing that stays still. Not the street. Not the garden. Not the wall.*

He thought: *so the answer is not to find something that stays still.*

He thought: *the answer is to keep counting the thing that nearly does, and to write down what it did, so that when it moves you know it moved and you know which one of you it was.*

He looked at the second stick on the shelf, with its three unnumbered marks on both faces.

He had made it for a thing that did not exist yet. He picked it up and looked at it and put it back.

---

Zhao came on the forty-first day, in the morning, with a handcart, for the jars.

He came about twice a month and always came for the jars. He always stood in the doorway of the back room rather than coming in. He always asked about the price before he asked about anything else. This time he asked about the price, and Lan He gave it to him, and Zhao wrote it on his clipboard. Then he did not go.

He looked at the shelf.

There were eleven jars on it and nine of them had slips, and every slip had a batch number and a line under it.

"What's that line," said Zhao.

"Which stick," said his father.

"Which what."

His father got up and took the dip-stick out of the crock and laid it on the counter between them. It was about a hand's length longer than his hand. It had two cut lines on one face and two on the other. The numbers were cut in rather than written, and the cutting was good.

"That's the nine," he said. "And that's the eleven. Anything between those two lines went out of this room right. Anything outside them went in the pot."

Zhao picked it up. He turned it over. He looked at the numbers, and he put his thumbnail in one of the cuts to see how deep it was.

"You made this."

"I made this."

"How long have you had it."

"About a month."

Zhao put it down on the counter and did not let go of it straight away.

"The bursar's been at me about the medicinal line since spring," he said. "She wants to know why one jar of yours does something and the next jar off somebody else's shelf doesn't. I've been telling her it's because yours are yours. She's been writing that down, and neither of us has been happy."

Lan He did not say anything.

"How much for the stick."

Lan Shen was in the doorway of the front room with a broom he was not using.

His father looked at the stick on the counter. He looked at Zhao. He looked at the eleven jars on the shelf, and at the slips. He looked at the nine years of jars that had gone out of that doorway without a single one of them having a line on it.

He picked the stick up and put it back in the crock.

"It's not for selling," he said.

"Everything's for selling."

"Not that," said his father. "That's how I know. You want one, I'll make you one, and I'll show you where the lines go. It'll be yours and you can put your own numbers on it. But you don't take this one, because this one's the one my jars were made against, and if it goes to the academy then the academy's got my jars' measure and I've got nothing."

Zhao looked at him for a while.

"That's a new way of talking," he said.

"It's a new way of doing it."

"Who taught you that."

Lan He's hand was on the crock. He did not turn around.

Lan Shen stood in the doorway with the broom and watched his father's back. He thought about the second stick under the bed with three unnumbered marks on it. About a garden he could not currently find. About four things in a corner where there were three.

He thought: *he is going to make Zhao one. Zhao is going to take it to the bursar. The bursar is going to write it down. In a year there will be a stick in the academy storeroom with two lines on it and somebody's name cut into the end.*

He thought: *and nobody will know it came out of this room.*

He thought: *that is exactly how it is supposed to work. I am the only person in this city who knows that. I am six years old, and I cannot feel my own wall from the front room.*

He put the broom down and went and got the second stick out from under his bed.

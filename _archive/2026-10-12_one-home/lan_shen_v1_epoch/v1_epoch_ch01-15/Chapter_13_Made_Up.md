# Chapter Thirteen: Made Up

```
◆ STATUS — Chapter 13: years 2–2
KIND      : a boy who has spent five years learning to have nothing in public, asked what to do
            with a girl who has nothing in public, and giving the only answer he has.
AGE       : eight throughout. About nine months, from the last of the Year 2 winter to the
            autumn of Year 2.
CLASS     : rank one. Thirteen chapters and one number. Four minutes, term two and term three.
THREAT    : Madam Zhou, who has twenty-nine children and one question, and who cannot expel
            anybody and cannot make it stop either.
BOND      : Na'er, who brings a note two doors down before she brings it home. Lan Yue, owed a
            fifth answer, who is given something that is not an answer at all.
LAW       : give a child a job and nobody looks at the child. Also: he may write anything
            except the truth.
DIALOGUE  : standard 13-30% — a doorstep, a classroom, a pump, a table, a bed.
TURN      : ch12 he had ten fours in a column and a question he did not think was dangerous.

```

The bank came back in the last week of the winter, the way it had come back twice before.

He counted it on the holiday and wrote it down. Twenty thousand one hundred. The year before it had been nineteen thousand four hundred at the same point in the season, and nineteen thousand six hundred on the holiday after that. The year before that he had not been counting, so there was no number for it, and the hole where the number should have been was a thing he noticed and then filed.

He did not touch it.

That made four seasons running. He had a rule, and the rule was that the price was width. A bank of coming-back grass in the last week of winter was not wide. He had proved the rule in both directions now and it held. Holding it had stopped feeling like discipline somewhere in the autumn, and started feeling like weather.

On Tuesdays and Fridays he washed jars. On Wednesdays Lin Ximeng came to two desks instead of fifteen. Four minutes. Four minutes. Once, in the third week of the second term, four and a quarter, and he wrote the quarter down and looked at it and it meant nothing, and the week after it was four again.

He was eight. He had been eight since the winter and he had not mentioned it, and by the spring not mentioning it had become the ordinary way the household handled the fact that he was growing.

---

Na'er brought the note two doors down first.

He was on the step with a crate because it was a Monday and the crate went out on Mondays, and she came up the path with her school bag on both shoulders, which was not how she wore it, and stood at the bottom of the step without coming up.

"You're standing in the wrong place," he said.

"I know."

She had been standing on his correct side since the autumn. She had worked out which side that was in about four seconds, in a chamber, on a day when she had been written into a register as having nothing at all, and she had never once got it wrong since.

She held out a piece of paper. It had been folded twice and unfolded twice and folded again by somebody who had been carrying it for a while.

"What is it."

"It's for my dad."

He took it. It was academy paper, except it was not academy paper, it was ordinary-school paper, which was thinner, and it had a printed line at the top with the school's name on it, and it had been written on by hand in a woman's careful hand.

*Please could Na'er's father or mother come in on Thursday after the bell. It is not a trouble. It is a question I have not been able to answer and I would rather ask it than guess at it.*

"Have you given it to him," he said.

"No."

"How long have you had it."

"Since Friday."

He looked at her. She was eight, and tall for eight, in a coat that had been let out at the sleeve twice. She was not crying. She had not cried once since the chamber. She was standing at the bottom of his step in the wrong place, because the wrong place was where you stood when you were not sure you wanted to be let in.

"What do you think it's about," he said.

"Thursdays," said Na'er.

---

He gave it to Tang Ziran himself, at the door, in front of Na'er, because leaving it in a house for three days was a thing that got worse and because he had worked out that a note handed over by a neighbour was a note that had already been read, and a note that had already been read could not be a surprise.

Tang Ziran read it twice.

"It's not a trouble," he said. To nobody. He read it a third time. "It's a question I have not been able to answer."

"Is that good or bad," said Wulin, from behind him.

"It's a teacher," said Tang Ziran. "Ask your mother."

"Mum's at the dye house."

"Then Thursday," said Tang Ziran, and folded the note into the shape it had arrived in, which was the shape Na'er had been carrying it in, and put it in his coat.

Lang Yue had gone to find work in the winter, because a household with one wage and two children and a third child who had arrived with nothing had done the arithmetic and the arithmetic had only one answer in it. She was at a dye house four mornings a week. She had good hands and she had a way of standing at a bench for five hours that the forewoman had noticed, and she came home smelling of indigo, and Na'er had learned what indigo was.

Lan Shen had watched the Tang household do the thing his own household had done two years earlier, which was to spread one income across a shape it did not fit, and he had not said anything, because there was nothing to say that would not have been arithmetic, and people did not like being given arithmetic about their own house by a child from next door.

"Can I come," he said.

Tang Ziran looked at him. It was not a long look. It was the look a man gives a neighbour's child when the child has said something that is not quite a question.

"You're not her brother."

"I walk her to it."

Tang Ziran thought about that in the doorway, with the door open and the cold coming in, and said yes, and Lan Shen understood from the way he said yes that he had been given permission for a reason that had nothing to do with the reason he had asked.

---

The ordinary school was four streets away and it was a long low building with a yard that had been paved and then unpaved by weather.

Madam Zhou was small and quick, with chalk on her left cuff. She met them in the classroom after the bell. Twenty-nine chairs in six rows. A blackboard wiped in the middle and not at the edges. A shelf of slates. She did not sit down behind a desk, which Lan Shen noticed, because a woman who sits behind a desk has decided something already.

"Thank you for coming," she said. "I wrote that note on Friday and I read it four times before I let her take it home."

"She gave it to me on Monday," said Tang Ziran.

"I know," said Madam Zhou. "She told me she had given it to a boy first, and I thought, well, that is a start." She did not smile about it. "Sit down. This won't take long and I'd rather it took short than took comfortable."

They sat. Lan Shen sat at a desk in the second row, because there was nowhere else, and put his hands on the top of it, and found that the top had a groove in it worn by elbows, on both sides, by two generations of children leaning.

"It's Thursday afternoons," said Madam Zhou. "Thursday afternoon is martial soul. That's the timetable. It has been the timetable since before I was born. On Thursday afternoon the sixes bring theirs out, we go round, and I write down what I see. It takes about an hour, and it is the one hour of the week they all sit still for."

She had a register open in front of her. It was not a black book. It was a school register, grey, with a spine that had been repaired with cloth tape.

"Twenty-nine children," she said. "Twenty-eight bring something out. A spade. A length of cord. A cat, once, which was a bad afternoon for everybody. Two of them are rank one and I don't pretend otherwise and their mothers have both been in to see me about it, and I have said the same thing to both of them and I'll say it to you, which is that rank one at six is not a verdict, it's a weather report."

Tang Ziran said nothing.

"I have one child who brings nothing out," said Madam Zhou. "I have had her for five months. I have been letting her sit and watch, and I have watched her sit and watch, and I have worked out that I am not teaching her anything at all on Thursday afternoons. She knows it. The other twenty-eight know it."

She turned the register round so that they could see it.

"There's a column," she said. "A column for the martial soul, a column for the rank, and a column for what I saw. I have put a dash in the first two for five months. A dash is correct. I have left the third one empty for five months, and the third one is the one that goes up to the district."

"You can't put a dash in it," said Tang Ziran.

"No," said Madam Zhou. "Because a dash in the third one means nothing was seen. And something is seen. She's there. She sits. She watches. She's the quietest child in that room for one hour a week and I don't believe for one minute that's nothing, but I can't write *quiet* in a column that goes to the district, because the district has a list of words it accepts, and *quiet* is not on it."

She closed the register.

"So," she said. "That is the trouble. The trouble is small, and I could keep papering it until she is nine. But I have a mother coming in on Friday about the seating. And I have a child who is eight years old and has worked out that Thursday afternoon is the hour she does not count. I would rather ask the question than guess at the answer." She looked at Tang Ziran. "What do I do with her on Thursdays."

Tang Ziran opened his mouth.

He was a good man. Lan Shen had lived two doors down from him all his life in this street. He had watched him carry a daughter home from a chamber with a queue in it and say *she's ours* to a man conducting a ceremony. He was a good man, he did not have an answer, and the not-having was written all over the front of him.

"She could sit out," said Tang Ziran.

"That's the fifth month."

"She could read."

"They read on Tuesdays. Half of them can't."

"Then I don't know," said Tang Ziran. "I'm sorry. I don't know what you do with her. Tell me what the other children do and I'll tell you whether she can do it."

"That's the best answer I've had all morning," said Madam Zhou, "and it isn't an answer."

And Lan Shen said, "Give her a job," because he had not decided to.

---

They both looked at him.

He had said it in the ordinary voice he used in that house — the voice of a child contributing to a conversation he had been sitting inside for years. It came out at the wrong volume for the room. The two adults turned their heads at the same time. He understood, in the middle of the turning, that he had just spent something.

"Say that again," said Madam Zhou.

"A job," he said. "In the hour."

"What kind of job."

"Calling the names."

She waited. He could feel the waiting, which was a thing he had learned to feel at the academy, where a man called Lin Ximeng had spent three years waiting at children until they said the rest of it.

"When you go round," he said, "somebody has to say whose turn it is. You're at the front and the child is at the desk and you have to walk between them every time, and that's twenty-nine walks in an hour, and that's why it takes an hour. If somebody says the names, you just go."

"And she says the names."

"She'd have the list. She'd have to know which one's next. She'd have to say it loud enough." He stopped, because he could hear himself going. Going was what he did when he was interested, and interest was the thing he had spent five years not showing. "And you could write down what she does in the third column. Calling twenty-nine names in order is a thing. It is a thing you can see. It is on the list."

Madam Zhou had not taken her eyes off him.

"Is it on the list," she said.

"It's a thing she'd be doing."

"That's not the same as being on the list."

"It's nearer than *quiet*," said Lan Shen.

And the room went quiet in the specific way a room goes quiet when a child has said something that an adult is going to have to deal with, and Tang Ziran looked at him for a long moment with an expression Lan Shen could not read at all, and Madam Zhou wrote something on the inside cover of her register, in pencil, quickly, the way a person writes a thing down before they have decided whether to keep it.

"Thursdays," she said. "From Thursday. She brings the list home on Wednesday and she brings it back on Friday and if she loses it we go back to the fifth month and I will know whose fault that was." She stood up. "It'll take her a fortnight to be any good at it. I'd better start her before she's nine."

She walked them to the door. At the door she said, to Tang Ziran, "She's a good girl and she's not behind, and I want you to know that the column isn't the child."

"I know that," said Tang Ziran.

"Nobody knows that," said Madam Zhou. "That's what makes it a column."

Then she looked at Lan Shen for about as long as it takes to say a name, and went back in.

---

Wulin was at the gate when they came out, because Wulin had finished early and had been told to wait and had not waited where he had been told to.

"What happened," he said.

"Nothing," said Tang Ziran.

"You were in there for half an hour."

"Twenty minutes," said Lan Shen.

"How do you know."

"Because she'd have stopped at fifteen if she'd got an answer at fifteen."

Wulin considered this on the walk home, the way he considered things, out loud and in the wrong order. "Na'er gets to call the names," he said. "Everybody in the sixes gets called and she does the calling. That's better than being in it."

"It's not better than being in it."

"It is if you're the one holding the list."

"That's not what the list is for."

"It's what it's for now," said Wulin, and ran ahead to tell her. Tang Ziran let him. Lan Shen walked the four streets home behind a man who said nothing at all until the second corner.

"You didn't think that up in the room," said Tang Ziran.

"No."

"How long have you had it."

Lan Shen looked at the paving.

"A while," he said.

Tang Ziran nodded once, in the way a man nods when he has been given an answer that is not an answer but is not a lie either. He said nothing more about it. At the Lan gate he put his hand briefly on the back of Lan Shen's neck, which was a thing nobody in that street did, and went in to his own house.

---

Su Wan caught him at the pump on the Saturday.

"You went to the school."

"I went to the school."

"You're not her father."

"I walk her to it."

Su Wan let the water run into the bucket and did not say anything for a while, which was the way she asked a question without asking it.

"And what did they want."

"They wanted to know what to do with her on Thursdays."

"And what do you do with her on Thursdays."

"She calls the names now," said Lan Shen.

His mother turned the tap a quarter of the way shut, which cut the noise down, which was the only reason anybody in that yard ever turned the tap a quarter of the way shut.

"Where did you get that," she said.

"Get what."

"That." She did not move her hand, so it was not a gesture, it was just a word with weight on it. "A man with twenty-nine children and a register and a district office could not get that, and you got it in twenty minutes in a second-row desk. Where did you get it."

"It's obvious."

"Nothing is obvious," said Su Wan. "Obvious is what people call a thing when they cannot see the road it came down." She filled the bucket, lifted it off the stand, set it on the ground, and looked at him over it. "I am not asking again today. But there is a road. I would like to know where it starts. And one day you will be old enough that you will want somebody to ask."

He did not answer, and she did not wait for him to, and the bucket went in.

---

Lin Ximeng had said something in the second term, on a Wednesday, about spirit souls, in the voice he used for things fifteen children of eight were not going to need for years.

He said that quality mattered more than age. He said that the price followed the age and not the quality, and that this was a fact about markets rather than a fact about souls, and that a man who could not tell the two apart would end up paying for a number.

Lan Shen wrote it down in the front of his head and carried it home.

He had thirty thousand.

Thirty thousand had been two thousand seven hundred and twenty-seven days of his sister's mornings since he was six years old. He had worked that out on the step at the back of the shop, in the summer, with his hands in a bowl, and he had known ever since that twenty-seven hundred days was seven and a half years, and the arithmetic had been hard but it had been a shape a person could hold.

If thirty thousand bought the young one, and the old ones cost more, and the price followed the age rather than the quality, then thirty thousand was not the price of a spirit soul.

It was the price of the smallest spirit soul there was.

He sat on the step and turned it over. What he arrived at was not a larger number, because he did not have one. Lin Ximeng had not given one. There was no case-carrying man from out of the city in Glorybound in the second term to ask. What he arrived at was a direction. He had spent two years working out how to afford a floor.

He had a rule about numbers, and the rule was that a number you could not hold was not a number yet, it was weather. He wrote this one down anyway, at the front of the book, in the counting hand, because it was a count and not a thought: *thirty thousand is the cheap one.*

---

He asked his father about paper on the Sunday, at the bench, while the balm was cooling.

"What for."

"Writing."

"You've got paper."

"I've got the back of your accounts."

Lan He looked at him the way he looked at a batch that had come out wrong and might still be usable.

"There's a shop on the long street," he said. "They sell it by the quire. Twenty-five sheets, and it costs as much as a good jar of the winter balm, and you want the cheap stuff, because the cheap stuff takes ink better and the good stuff is sized and the ink sits on top of it and smears."

"How much is a book."

"A book."

"A bound one. With a cover."

"That's not a shop on the long street," said Lan He. "That's a man who comes from out of the city with a case, twice a year, and he'll charge you more for a plain one than for a good jar of anything I make, and he'll be right to, because he's carried it." He stirred the balm. "Why do you want a book."

"Because the backs of your accounts run out."

"They run out in the spring," said Lan He, "and it is the spring."

---

That night he got the notebook down off the shelf, second row, square to the edge, and turned past the counts and past the column of fours and past *why is this straight*, and went to the back, where there were a dozen pages he had never used, because a dozen pages at the back of a book is where you put a thing you have not decided about.

He had read a great deal in the other life. He had written a great deal in the other life, and sold some of it. Twice, two different people had told him that a particular paragraph of his was the reason they finished a thing. He had known both times exactly which paragraph they meant. He had said nothing either time. He had not put any of it on paper in this one. Not once. Eight years of a skill carried like a coin sewn into a lining.

What he wrote was not about a girl with no martial soul.

That was the first decision. He made it in about two seconds. It was the right one, and he knew it was right because he had spent five years learning what a thing looked like when it was aimed at a child who was being pitied. A girl with no martial soul is a girl with a hole in the middle of her. You do not write that. You write the other thing.

So he wrote about a girl who could feel it.

He wrote that in a city of nine million people a great many things are happening at any one time, and that most of them are small. That one girl in one street could feel all of them. That she could not switch it off, and had learned to stand very still in doorways so fewer came through her at once. He wrote that everybody thought she was slow. He wrote that she was not slow at all. She was, in fact, the only person in the city who had ever noticed a particular thing about a particular man on a particular Tuesday. She had not said so, because saying so would have meant explaining how she knew.

He wrote it in a hand that was worse than the hand he used for counts, because the counts had to be legible to a man in a coat and this did not have to be legible to anybody.

The trouble was the speed. He could get a sentence in his head complete, with its shape and its ending already in place, and then he had to move a piece of wood across a piece of paper at the speed of a child's hand, and by the time he had reached the middle of it the next one had arrived and was standing behind the first waiting. In the other life he had typed. He had not thought about typing once since he got here, and he thought about it now, at the back of a notebook, at eleven o'clock, with a cramp coming into the web of his thumb.

He wrote slower than he thought. That was the whole of the difficulty, and it was not a difficulty at all, because writing slower than you think is a large part of what makes the thing good. He had learned that in the other life somewhere in his thirties and had not been grateful for it. He was not grateful for it now. He was eight, his hand hurt, the sentence on the page was better than the sentence in his head, and he resented every part of that.

He wrote four hundred words on the first night. Six hundred more on the second, and five hundred on the third. He counted the first four pages and then stopped counting them. On the fourth night he wrote the ending, and it was wrong. He crossed it out and wrote it again. The second one was wrong too. The third one was the first one with two sentences taken out of the middle.

He sat back and looked at that and thought: *that is what it always was. You write three and you keep one and the one you keep is the one you thought of first with the rubbish removed.*

It was good. He knew it was good in the specific way he had known it in the other life, which was not a feeling so much as a cessation of arguing.

And then he read it back from the top.

---

Lan Yue asked on the Wednesday.

"Have you got one."

"I've got one."

"Say it."

"It's not a saying one," he said. "It's a giving one."

He put the notebook on the table between them, open at the back. Ten pages, in a hand that was not his counting hand. She looked at them the way she looked at everything anybody handed her, which was without moving first.

"What is it."

"It's a story."

"You've written a story."

"I've written a story."

Lan Yue picked it up and read the first page standing. Then she sat down. Then the second page, the third. By the fifth she had stopped eating. His mother came to the table twice and was waved off twice by a daughter who did not look up.

It took her about twenty minutes. When she finished she put the pages down square, in a stack, and looked at him for a long time.

"Who is she."

"She's made up."

"She's Na'er."

"She's made up," said Lan Shen.

"She can feel everything and she can't turn it off and everybody thinks she's slow." Lan Yue put her finger on the fourth page, in the margin, on the paragraph about doorways. "And you've never once in your life said a word about Na'er having anything at all. You've said the opposite. Everybody in this street has said the opposite. There's a whole ceremony about the opposite."

"I know."

"So how do you know about the doorways."

He did not have an answer, and he found that he was not, for once, reaching for one, and that the not-reaching was so unfamiliar that he had to look at it before he could speak.

"I made it up," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got."

Lan Yue looked at him for another moment. Then she got up, went to the shelf above the stove where the noodle tickets were kept in a tin that had held something else, came back with the ticket that had four things on it in a hand worse than his, and picked up the pencil.

"What are you doing," he said.

"Writing it down."

"It's not one of those."

"It's a fifth one," said Lan Yue, and wrote, at the bottom of the list, under *the soup* and *the storeroom in the morning* and *the long way home*, the words *wrote a story about a girl who could feel everything and says she made her up*.

She put the pencil down.

"There," she said. "That's five. You'll run out of not-answers eventually and then I'll have the lot of you on one ticket and I won't have to ask you anything ever again."

It was a category error. A list of facts about a person is not the same as a list of things a person has done, and a story is a thing done, and she had filed it with the soup.

He did not tell her so.

---

Na'er read it on the Saturday, in the Tang yard, on the step, with Wulin sitting on the wall above her being an audience.

She read slower than Lan Yue. She read the doorways paragraph twice.

"She stands in the doorway," she said.

"Yes."

"So it goes through her less."

"Yes."

Na'er looked up at him from the step with the notebook open on her knee, and her mouth did something that was not a smile and not the opposite of one either.

"Did you make her up," she said.

"Yes."

"Right," said Na'er, and went back to the third page, and did not ask him anything else about it all afternoon.

On the Thursday she called twenty-nine names, got two of them out of order, and was told to slow down. On the second Thursday she got all twenty-nine. On the third she did not need the list. Madam Zhou noticed that she did not need it, and wrote something in the third column that was four words long and was not *quiet*.

---

The bank peaked in the middle of the summer at forty-two thousand six hundred, and came down through the autumn the way it had twice before, and he wrote every count down and touched nothing.

He kept doing four minutes. Second term, third term, the whole of the year. Once, four and a quarter. Every other time, four.

And at the back of the book, on ten of the dozen pages, there was a story in a hand that was not his counting hand, about a girl who could feel everything and could not switch it off, and he had read it back once, on the fourth night, from the top.

He had got to the doorways.

He had written, in the middle of the fifth page, that the girl could feel it when a person in the same room was working out whether to say a thing, and that she had learned to leave before they finished working it out, because the finishing was worse than the not-saying.

He had not put that in for Na'er.

He had put it in because the paragraph before it needed a paragraph after it. And because he was good at this. And because a man who is good at a thing will supply the thing without being asked. He had supplied it. Then he had read it back, recognised it, and sat in the dark at the back of the front room with the notebook open on his knee, working out that he had written the only true sentence in two lives — about a girl who was not him, in a doorway.

He had thought about burning it.

He had thought about burning it for about as long as it takes to decide not to, and then he had closed the notebook, put it on the second row of the shelf square to the edge, and gone to bed.

Outside it was the second year of three. The ditch under the east wing was full of grass that had been sown and counted by nobody. In a house four streets away a girl who was eight had learned all twenty-nine names. And on the second row of a shelf in a front room there was a book holding a count of grass, a column of fours, a question about a straight line, and ten pages of something made up. One true sentence in it. About somebody else. He had not counted that one either.

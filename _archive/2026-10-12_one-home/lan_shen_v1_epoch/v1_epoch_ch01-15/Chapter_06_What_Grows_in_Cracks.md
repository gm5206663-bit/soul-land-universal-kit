# Chapter Six — What Grows in Cracks

```
◆ STATUS — Chapter 6: years 0–0
```

---

The week went the way weeks go when you are six and waiting for a part of yourself to come back.

He went to school. On the fifth day they sat on the soft boards again and Lin Ximeng walked the rows again. Lan Shen sat with his hands on his knees and felt the blue thread coiled in him and did not ask it a single question. It did not offer anything either. That was the whole of it. He could feel the thread. He could not feel anything the thread could feel. It was like having a hand in a thick glove.

He spent nine seconds on nothing at all and then he stopped, because there was no point spending nine seconds on nothing.

On the sixth day they did cultural class and he wrote three sentences about the way to school and they were three correct sentences and nobody said anything about them.

On the seventh day his father took him into the back room.

"Hold this," said Lan He.

It was a stick. About the length of his forearm, smooth, with one end squared off, and somebody had burned a line into it near the top.

"What is it."

"It's a dip. You put it in the pot and you read where the wax comes to on the line, and then you know whether it's reduced enough." His father held it up and turned it once, the way he turned a jar when he was looking at a jar. "I've been doing it by eye for eleven years. By eye is why it's wrong in the winter. By eye is fine when it's the same day and the same fire and the same pot and I'm the one standing there, and by eye is no use at all to anybody else."

Lan Shen took the stick.

He held it for a moment and did not say anything, because there was a thing arriving in his chest that he did not have a name for and did not want to have a name for in front of his father.

*He made a gauge.*

*A man who has never heard the word made a gauge.*

"Say something," said his father.

"The line's in the wrong place."

"What."

"It's burned near the top. If the wax comes up to there the pot's already gone too far, you've got to watch the whole time it's coming down to it." He turned the stick over. "You want two. One where it's right and one where it's ruined, and then you know what the middle looks like. And you want them on both sides, because if it rolls on the bench you lose it."

The back room was quiet. The candle was going, because it was the fifth day of the ninth month and the light was going early, and the pot was not on.

"Two lines," said Lan He.

"Two lines."

"On both sides."

"On both sides. And write the weights on it. Not on the paper — on the stick. The paper's in the drawer and the drawer's shut and the stick's in your hand."

His father looked at him for a while. It was the same look as the one at the gate, the one about the good answer, and Lan Shen braced for the second half of it.

It did not come.

"Give it here," said Lan He.

He took the stick back and went to the bench and got the knife. He put two lines on it, then two more on the other face, and wrote eleven and nine beside them in the large slanted hand that was confident about characters and less confident about sums. Then he held it out at arm's length and squinted at it.

"That's no good," he said.

"What."

"The wax hides the numbers. You'll never read it." He set it down and got a nail and a hammer from the shelf. He put the point of the nail on the wood and tapped it, and tapped it, until the figures were cut in rather than written on. "There. That'll take wax. That'll take water. That'll last as long as I do."

Lan Shen watched him do it.

He watched the four cuts and the way his father turned the stick a quarter each time, and he understood, watching, that this was not a man following an instruction. This was a man who had spent eleven years being wrong about a thing and had been given, in one afternoon, the idea that he could be right about it in a way that would still be true tomorrow.

*That's the whole of it,* Lan Shen thought. *That's all it is. It isn't the measure. It's that the measure is the same tomorrow.*

"It'll change in the cold," he said.

"What will."

"The stick. Wood moves. It'll swell and the lines will be off by the new year."

His father stopped tapping.

"So we make two," he said. "One for the warm and one for the cold, and we check them against each other in the fourth month and the tenth. And if they've moved we know by how much." He looked at his son over the stick. "You've thought about this."

"I think about everything."

"That's your mother's answer."

"It's my answer."

Lan He put the nail down. He did not say the other thing. Lan Shen noticed that he did not say the other thing. Both of them went on with the stick.

---

The batch went out on the ninth day, and it went out right.

Nine jars of the winter balm and three of the old one. This time his father wrote the numbers on the slips himself instead of asking, and this time there was a line on every slip saying which stick the batch had been made against. Lan Shen had suggested it and had expected an argument and had not got one, and that was worse, because it meant his father had already been thinking the same way for a week and had simply been waiting for somebody to say it out loud.

On the tenth day, which was a Monday, Lan Yue went into town.

They heard about it at the pump in the evening, because everything was heard about at the pump. She had got the washing job. Six to eleven, eleven copper a day, a bowl of whatever was left. She was starting on the Wednesday, and she had cried in the noodle street, which she was cross about.

"She's got hands like a washerwoman already," said Su Wan, to nobody, wringing her own cloth out. "And she's never washed a dish for money in her life."

"Is the balm any good."

"I gave her a jar and she cried about that too." Su Wan set the bucket down. "She says it lasts. She says she put it on Sunday and her hands were all right Monday, and she's never had anything that did that. She asked me what was in it. I said wax and comfrey and her own family's guesswork, and she said it isn't guesswork any more is it."

Lan Shen was at the other side of the yard with a bowl.

He did not turn around. He did not need to.

*She asked what was in it,* he thought. *That's how it starts. Somebody asks what's in it, and then somebody asks who made it that way, and then somebody asks whether there's more.*

He had a jar of something on a shelf in the front room, made against a marked stick. It was in a washerwoman's bag two doors down. It had taken eleven copper a day to make her want to know about it.

He thought about thirty thousand, because he had been thinking about thirty thousand since the afternoon his brother had stood at his door with his hands in a bowl, and because he had worked out that thirty thousand was two thousand seven hundred and twenty-seven days of eleven copper.

Seven and a half years of washing dishes.

*A random one,* he thought. *For a random spirit soul. Seven and a half years of a woman's mornings, and you get a lottery.*

He did not know that yet. He had not read chapter seventeen properly either. What he knew was the number and the stipend and the fact that the maths was worse than any of it looked, and he stood at the pump with a bowl and did the sums again and got the same answer and washed his bowl.

---

The sense came back on the eleventh day, at night, exactly when he had said it would.

He knew it the way you know a room has somebody in it. He was lying on his back with his hands flat and the window open, and the street came back — not all at once, not dramatically, just the way a smell comes back when you stop being ill. The ditch behind the shop. The crack in the pavement outside the gate. The three thin survivor things along the base of the wall, which was where they had been all along and which he had not been able to feel for seven days.

He lay still and counted to twenty and did nothing at all.

Then he did ten seconds.

Ten seconds of the street, both sides, the whole length he could get, which was about the length of two houses and a bit. He held it. He felt the weave, the sameness, the one-organism thing that he still had no word for. And at ten he let go, on purpose, before the ten became an eleven, and he lay there and waited for his head.

Nothing came.

Nothing at all. No white line, no pressure behind the eyes, no warmth at the nostril. He lay in the dark with his heart going and checked himself twice, and both times the answer was the same, which was that he was a six-year-old boy in a bed and his head was his own.

*So it's the stopping,* he thought. *It was never the ten seconds. It was the eleventh.*

He had spent seven days being afraid of a thing that turned out to be a limit he could stand inside of, and he had found the edge of it by having been pushed off it once, hard, in a way he had not chosen.

He did it again. Ten seconds. Let go. Nothing.

He did it a third time and then he stopped, because three was enough for one night and because he had learned something about himself in a herb shop four days ago.

*Two lines,* he thought. *One where it's right and one where it's ruined. You can't find the middle without both.*

He went to sleep feeling the street, which was the first time in a week, and it was like sleeping in a house with people in it.

---

The garden was Wulin's idea, in the sense that Wulin said the word and did not know he had said it.

He was at the gate on the twelfth day with Na'er on his hip, because Na'er walked and then did not walk and then walked, and he was telling Lan Shen about the holiday.

"We're going to the garden," he said. "Me and her. On the holiday. We always go on the holiday. There's grass and there's a bit of a hill and there's a bench and if you sit on the bench you can see the harbour if you stand up, and you're not supposed to stand up."

"What garden."

"The garden." Wulin said it as though there were only one, and there was, in his world. "Down past the Tan place. Where the wall's fallen down. Everybody goes, it's not a secret or anything, there's kids in it on a holiday, there's always kids in it."

"Is it big."

"It's big enough." He shifted Na'er to the other hip. "Why."

"No reason," said Lan Shen.

"That's your no-reason face."

"I've only got one face."

"You've got your no-reason face and your all-right face and your thinking-about-the-shop face." Wulin shifted Na'er back again. She had gone to sleep against his shoulder with her fist in his collar, which was how she went everywhere. "You can come if you want. On the holiday."

"When's the holiday."

"Eight days," said Wulin. "It's every month, it's the second one."

Lan Shen said he would think about it. Wulin said that was his all-right face, and went down the street with a sleeping girl on his shoulder.

He did not wait six days.

He waited two, because two was as long as he could stand. He went on the evening of the fourteenth day, at about the time the light went off the harbour, and he went alone, and he told his mother he was going to see whether Zhao had any more jars — which was a lie, but a small and checkable one. She did not stop him, because she had stopped stopping him in the third week of the month.

The garden was down past the Tan place, where the wall had fallen down.

It was bigger than he had expected and worse than he had expected, and both of those things turned out to be the same thing.

There was a low bank with grass on it. There was a bench with a slat missing. There was a path that had been gravel once and was now mostly dirt with gravel in it. There was a bit of a hill, which was a mound of something that had been dumped and had grown over, and Wulin was right that if you stood on the bench you would probably see the harbour.

And there was grass.

Not the three thin survivor things along the base of a wall. Not the crack in the pavement. Not the ditch behind the shop, which was the best place he had and which had four strands in it.

There was a stand of it.

He stood at the gap in the wall for a while and did not go in, because his body had stopped and he had not told it to.

The whole bank was grass. Shoulder-high in places, knee-high in others, gone to seed at the top, some of it in the shade of the one tree and some of it in the last of the light. There were dozens of kinds in it. There were things with flat blades and things with round ones and things that were not grass at all and were growing in the middle of the grass anyway, and under all of it, in the ground, the roots of all of it, going into each other.

He could not feel any of it.

He was standing at the edge of it with his hands at his sides and his heart going, and the sense was switched off, because he had switched it off, two days ago, on purpose, at ten seconds.

*Ten seconds,* he thought. *In the street.*

*This isn't the street.*

He went in.

He walked to the bench and sat down on it. He put his hands flat on the wood the way he put them flat on the floor of his bedroom, and he let it open.

It was not like the street.

The street was a thread he could hold — a thin weave, sparse, forty strands if there were forty, mostly growing where nothing else would, and it came to him in about a second and sat in his head at a size he could manage.

The garden arrived all at once and it was not a size he could manage.

It was not louder. That was the thing he would remember afterwards and would get wrong for years — it was not louder, it was *more*, and more was worse than louder, because louder you can brace against and more just keeps coming. Every strand on the bank. Every root under it. The tree. The things that were not grass. The whole slope of it going down away from him and the whole of it alive and the whole of it one thing in the way the street was one thing, except that the street was forty and this was forty thousand.

He got four seconds.

In the four seconds he found it. He did not look for it, and that was the part that frightened him.

There was a strand in the middle of the bank that was not the others.

Not brighter. Not bigger. It was the size of the ones around it and it was growing in the same dirt and it had gone to seed the same way. But everything on that bank was pulling at everything else in a tangle he could not sort. This one was pulling at *him*, in a straight line, and it was the only thing in forty thousand that had a direction.

He knew what it was without being told and without seeing it, the way he had known the shape of a thing under a light in a room two doors down.

And then the four seconds were over and the fifth one started, and he did not stop.

He should have stopped. He knew he should have stopped, and he knew it in the same part of his mind that had counted two hundred and six and put two hammers down square. He did not stop, because there was a straight line in a tangle and he had four years of wanting to know what the straight line was.

Six. Seven.

The white line came in at the edges of his eyes.

Nine. Ten.

The bank went from forty thousand things to one thing, which was the thing he was holding, and he understood in that moment that the cost was not the length of time and never had been. The cost was the *width*. Ten seconds of a street was nothing. Ten seconds of a garden was a great deal. He was eleven seconds into it, the straight line was still there, and something about it was—

"LAN SHEN."

He let go.

Not gradually. Not the way he had practised, four times, in a bedroom, at ten. He let go the way you drop a hot pan — all at once, with the whole arm. The bank went out like a lamp. The world came back as a bench and a slope and a gap in a wall and a boy standing in it with a girl on his hip.

Wulin was twenty paces off. He had come up the path from the harbour side. Na'er was awake on his shoulder now and looking at him with her fist still in her mouth.

"What are you doing," said Wulin.

Lan Shen's hand went to his face before he had decided to put it there, and came away with the wrong colour on it.

"Nothing," he said.

"You're bleeding."

"I know."

"From your nose."

"I know." Wulin came up the slope with Na'er on his hip and his face doing the thing it did, which was worry arriving in stages. "Did you fall over."

"No."

"Are you ill. Mum says if you're ill you shouldn't come out. Is it your head. You look like your head."

"It's not my head."

"You're white." Wulin sat down on the bench beside him, which made Na'er have to be moved, and put her on his knee instead. He looked at him properly. "Is somebody after you. Is it that boy again. I'll go and get dad, dad's at home, I can run—"

"No."

"Then what."

Lan Shen looked at the slope.

It was just a bank. Grass. Seed heads. A tree at the top of it and a bench with a slat missing and the light going off the harbour behind the houses. There were forty thousand things on it and he could not feel one of them, and was not going to be able to for a while. There was a girl on his brother's knee who had woken up and was looking at him without any expression at all.

"I came here to sit down," he said.

"To sit down."

"On my own."

Wulin looked at him for a moment. Then he reached over with his free hand and took a grip on Lan Shen's wrist, which was the thing he did when he had decided something, and held it while he got up.

"You're coming to ours," he said. "Mum's got the ginger and she'll wash your face and she'll make a fuss and then she'll stop making a fuss and give you something to eat. And I'm not carrying you, you've got to walk, but I'll walk slow."

"I'm all right."

"You're bleeding on yourself." Wulin picked Na'er up onto his hip again. "And you came and sat in a garden on your own, and that's not all right, that's the opposite of all right, that's what I do when I'm upset."

They walked back up through the commoner's district in the dark, the long way, because Wulin walked the long way when he had something to think about. Na'er fell asleep again against his shoulder. Lan Shen walked slow beside them with his head tipped back and his hand under his nose.

He could not feel the street.

That was the thing he kept checking, every few paces, the way you check a tooth with your tongue. The ditch behind the shop. The crack at the gate. The three thin things along the base of the wall. He had had them for three nights and he had slept with them and it had been like sleeping in a house with people in it.

There was nothing. Not a thread. Not a shape. He had the blue coil in him, rank one, asleep. It was the only grass within a hundred paces that he could feel at all, and it was his own.

*Seven days last time,* he thought. *Ten seconds.*

*This was twenty. In a garden.*

He did the arithmetic twice and got the same answer both times and did not like it, and then he did it a third time and got a different answer, which was worse.

*Seven days for ten seconds of a street. Fourteen for twenty of a garden. Twenty-eight for forty.*

*A month.*

He walked along Feng Street behind a boy carrying a girl, with his head back and his hand under his nose. He thought about a straight line in a tangle of forty thousand things — how it had been the only one with a direction, and how he had found it in four seconds without looking.

And he thought about the fifth second, and the sixth, and the seventh. And about the fact that when the width got bad enough the bank had stopped being forty thousand things and had become one thing.

The one he had been holding.

He took his hand away from his face. It came away clean at last. He wiped it on his trousers and looked up. Wulin had stopped at his own gate and was turning around to see whether he was still there.

"How long do you think you'll be able to come and play," said Wulin.

Lan Shen stood on the street with his hand at his side.

He could have said a week. He could have said nothing, or that he would see, or that he had to help at the shop, and any of those would have been believed and none of them would have been checked.

"Six days," he said. "There's a holiday. You said."

"I did say." Wulin beamed, and shifted Na'er, and went in through his own gate.

Lan Shen went two doors down and let himself into the yard and stood in it for a while with his back against the wall of his own house, in the dark, not feeling anything at all.

He had a month, more or less, and he had spent the first evening of it finding out that the reason there was so little grass in Glorybound City was that Glorybound City was paved.

That was not a small thing. He stood in the yard and turned it over and looked at it from the other side, and it stayed being the same thing. Every strand he had felt for two weeks had been growing in a crack. He had been measuring what he could do by how much of it survived under a city, and the answer had been almost none, and he had thought the answer was about him.

It was not about him. It was about the paving.

He looked down at the base of his own wall, where the three thin things grew, and could not feel them. He understood that he was going to be a long time learning how wide his own reach was, because there was nowhere in this city to stand.

Except one place, on the second holiday of every month, in a garden where a boy brought his sister.

He went inside and got the book down. He did not light the candle. He wrote: *the garden is not like the street. The street is what survived. Cost is width, not time. Twenty seconds here is not twenty seconds there. Found one strand out of forty thousand in four seconds without looking. It had a direction. Everything else was pulling at everything else.*

He stopped. He looked at that in the dark, which meant he did not look at it.

Then he wrote: *I did not stop at four.*

He shut the book and put it back on the shelf square to the edge, second row, where his mother could reach it. He got into bed and lay on his back with his hands flat.

There was nothing under them.

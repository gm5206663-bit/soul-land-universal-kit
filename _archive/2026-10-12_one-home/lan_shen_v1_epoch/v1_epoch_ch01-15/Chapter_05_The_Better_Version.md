# Chapter Five — The Better Version

---

He woke before his mother and lay still and did the arithmetic, because the arithmetic was the only thing that ever helped.

Six years old. Rank one. Two days gone out of seven. Five to go. The grass under the paving of Glorybound City was still a rumour and would be a rumour until Tuesday night, and there was nothing at all to be done about that, and the nothing was the worst part.

Then he turned his head and put his hand flat against the wall, because that was the habit now, and he got nothing, because the habit was not for the grass.

The habit was for the house.

Two doors down there was a house with a boy in it whose hands had been red at the base of the fingers at seven o'clock last night. A man in it who had gone quiet behind a door. A girl in it who had eaten four bowls of something and smiled for the first time since she arrived. Lan Shen had lain against this wall until midnight and got out of it a murmur, a chair, and the shape of a voice going up and coming down.

He got up and dressed and went out into the yard, because he had decided at some point in the night that he was going to look at those hands.

Wulin was already out.

He was at the pump with a bowl, in his school clothes, singing — which he only did when he had been told he was good at something. Lan Shen crossed the yard and stood next to him and looked down.

Both hands. Clean. Bright. The skin at the base of the fingers was pink in the way a child's skin is pink, and the knuckles were knuckles, and there was nothing swollen about anything.

"There," said Wulin.

"Where."

"On me. Look." He held them out, palms up, and waggled the fingers. "They don't hurt at all. Not a bit. Mum said Uncle Mang Tian's medicine is really good and dad rubbed it on me after I went to sleep and I slept right through and now they're fine and I'm going to be fine every day and I've got to go every day for three months."

Lan Shen looked at the hands.

He had held one of those wrists at seven o'clock last night, in the doorway of a hall that smelled of metal, while a big man crouched in front of it and a woman stood behind with her hands over her mouth. He had counted the red. He had counted it, because counting was what he did instead of feeling things. The count had been: both hands, base of all eight fingers, darkening, the left worse than the right — and the left was the hand that steadied.

Fourteen hours ago.

"That's good," he said.

"Isn't it." Wulin beamed. He put the bowl down under the pump and worked the handle with his whole body, because he was still small enough that it took his whole body. "And there's other things. Guess other things."

"Na'er."

"She's my sister," said Wulin. "Proper. On paper. Dad said it last night — he didn't say it to anybody, he just said it, out loud, to himself, and mum said *mnn*, and that means it's done, that's how they do it, you don't have to go anywhere. She's a Tang now. Na'er Tang. Doesn't that sound good."

"It sounds good."

"And mum's going to get a job." The pump squeaked. Wulin did not seem to notice the pump. "In town. They decided last night, after you went home. She says it's because Na'er eats more rice than me and I eat more rice than anybody and one wage won't do it, and dad says we're going to be poor forever but in a good way."

He let go of the handle and the water stopped and he picked up the bowl and drank out of it and spilled some down his front and did not care.

Lan Shen looked at the hands again while he drank.

*Fourteen hours,* he thought. *A nine-year-old's arms would take four days. A grown man's would take a week, and he would not be able to hold a bowl.*

He had read this. Not this — he had read the sentence about the medicine, the sentence about the mother crying, the sentence about the father rubbing it on. He had read all of it in the other life, in a bed, on a phone, at two in the morning, at the point where he had stopped paying attention to the family and started skimming for the fights.

He had not read this.

He did not remember the hands being fine the next morning.

*No,* he thought. *He remembered the opposite.*

There had been a whole chapter about it — the father going in at dead of night with a bottle and finding nothing to put it on.

He caught himself, standing there in a yard in Glorybound City at six years old, and he pulled the thought back in and looked at it properly, the way you look at a thing you have just dropped.

*He doesn't know about the lamp. He can't know about the lamp. The lamp is in the dead of the night behind a closed door and there is nobody in that room but a father and a sleeping boy.*

So there was a thing happening two doors down that was not in the book.

Not a big thing. Not a dragon. Not a pagoda. A thing about a pair of hands.

And for the first time since he woke in this body with a man's mind in it and a weed for a soul, Lan Shen stood in a yard with a bowl of water going cold next to him and understood that he had missed something. That he would go on missing things. That the four hundred chapters in his head were a map of a country that had already started moving.

"Aren't you going to eat," said Wulin.

"Yes."

"You look funny."

"I'm six," said Lan Shen. "This is what six looks like."

---

The fourth day of school was cultural class, which meant reading and writing and arithmetic and the names of the provinces. It was held in the room with the hard benches rather than the room with the soft boards, and Lan Shen liked it immediately and did not examine why.

Twenty-two children. Six could already read properly. The rest could do their own names, the numbers to a hundred, and the twelve animals of the year.

Lin Ximeng taught it himself, which he had not expected. He had expected somebody old for the cultural class, somebody with a stick. Instead the man who had spent nine seconds on his brother's wrist yesterday stood at the front of the room with a piece of chalk and wrote four characters on the board and said, "Copy this. Then underneath it, write what you saw on the way to school."

"What we saw," said a girl called Feng.

"What you saw. Three sentences. If you can't write a word, draw it, and I'll tell you the word after."

Lan Shen copied the four characters. They were about the season. He wrote them correctly the first time, and then he sat for a moment with the pen in his hand, and he thought about the three sentences.

He had walked to school with Wulin. That was the walk. On the walk there had been: the pump, a dog, a cart with a broken slat that a man was hitting with a hammer while the horse stood still and looked bored, the smell from the noodle place, and a woman shouting at somebody about a basket.

He wrote the cart.

*The cart had a broken slat and the man was hitting it and the horse was not moving.*

He looked at that. True. Also nothing. It was a list with a full stop at the end, which is what he had been writing into the book on the shelf every night for four years, in the handwriting of a careful child, in a country where nobody was ever going to read it.

He looked at the board. He looked at his three sentences. And then something happened that he did not decide to do.

*The man was hitting the slat and the horse stood still and watched him, the way a horse watches, which is not the way a person watches, and every third hit made a different noise because the wood had already split further than the man thought.*

He stopped.

He looked at the sentence. He counted the words in it. He had written twenty-eight words about a cart and he had been in this world for six years and he had never once in his life, in either of them, put a thing like that on a page on purpose.

He had put things like that on pages by accident in the other life, at three in the morning, in a folder on a machine, for nobody.

*The horse watched him the way a horse watches.*

That was good. That was a real sentence. He knew it was good in the same instant he wrote it and in the same way he knew a hammer had come back a fraction behind the beat, which was to say that a part of him he had never been introduced to had done the knowing and then handed it up.

Lin Ximeng was coming down the rows.

He had about four seconds and he used three of them deciding and the fourth one doing.

He put a line through the horse. He wrote, underneath, in the round careful hand of a child who has been taught: *The cart was broken. A man was hitting it. The horse did not move.*

Then he stopped, because stopping there was worse than either of the other two things, and he did not have time to work out why, and he was at his bench.

He read the four characters, and the three sentences, and then the crossed-out line — which was legible, because he had crossed it out with a child's single stroke rather than a man's careful blacking-out, and because he had not actually expected anyone to look.

Lin Ximeng did not say anything for a moment.

"Who taught you that word," he said.

"Which word."

"Further. Split further than the man thought." He tapped the page with one fingernail. "That's not a word a six-year-old has. Where did you get it."

"I heard it," said Lan Shen.

"From who."

"My dad. He says it about the wood in the shop. He says it splits further than you think and you have to cut it off."

That was true. His father said it about bloodroot every autumn, and he had said it three days ago.

Lin Ximeng looked at him. He let him look. He had learned in four years that the trick with being looked at was not to look away and not to look back too hard — to let the face do whatever it was doing, because the face was six and had nothing on it.

"He's a herb seller," he said.

"Yes."

"Does he read."

"He reads the accounts."

He made a noise. It was not a word. He wrote something in his own book — a small black one he carried from room to room — and moved on down the row. Lan Shen sat with his hands flat on the bench and felt the whole exchange replay itself at speed, and found four places he would have done it better.

He had never had that happen before.

He had spent four years being careful and being careful had worked, and this morning he had been careless in a direction he did not know he was careless in — and the woman who was going to find his brother's secret in three years' time had written something in a small black book.

*She found Wulin's because Wulin moved,* he thought. *She's going to find mine because I wrote.*

Behind him at the other end of the row a boy was crying quietly about the twelve animals.

---

At the pump in the afternoon his mother and Lan Yue were talking. They stopped when he came up, and then started again, which told him the whole of what he needed to know.

"There's a place on the noodle street," Lan Yue was saying. "Washing. Mornings, six to eleven, they said. Eleven copper a day and a bowl of whatever they've got left."

"When do they tell you."

"Monday." Lan Yue wrung the cloth out. Her hands were red at the wrist from the water. "But it's something and it's in town and I've been asking since before she came and nothing else has come up, and Tang Ziran says with two children eating the way those two eat we can't do it on one wage."

"Two children," said Su Wan.

"The girl eats like she's got a furnace in her." Lan Yue laughed. It was not a happy laugh and she knew it and she did not stop. "I don't mind. I don't mind at all. She's a good little thing, she says thank you, she sits still. It's just that she eats four bowls and then she looks at the pot, and I've never seen a child look at a pot like that."

"Has she grown."

"Not a bit. Five and a half and she's the size of a four-year-old, and she eats more than Lin Lin, and Lin Lin eats more than his father."

Su Wan said something under her breath that Lan Shen did not catch, and then she said, louder, "You'll want the ginger again."

"I'll want the ginger."

"On account," said Su Wan. "Same as before. And if you do end up with your hands in hot water six to eleven you'll want something for them, and I've got a batch with a quarter more wax in it that'll last you till the new year, and I'm not charging you for that either."

"You can't not charge me for it."

"I can. I've charged somebody else for it." Su Wan filled the second bucket and set it down with the noise that meant the subject was finished. "Lan He's been in a mood all morning about a six-star blacksmith telling him his winter ratio's wrong, and he's been at the back room since four o'clock with the scales and a candle, and if I don't give the balm away he'll boil the whole lot down and then we'll have nothing to sell and I'll have to listen to it."

Lan Yue laughed properly, that time.

Lan Shen stood with his bucket and did not say anything, because there was a great deal to say and none of it was his to say.

*A quarter more wax,* he thought. *He's doing it. He's actually doing it.*

His father had been told his balm was wrong by a man who wore his bench into a dish, and instead of being angry he had gone to the back room with the scales. His mother had taken the batch and given it away. Lan Yue was going to wash dishes for eleven copper a day with a jar of it in her bag.

Three things had happened and none of them had needed him.

"Are you all right," said his mother.

"Yes."

"You've been all right at me three times today."

"I'm all right."

"You've got your father's face on." Su Wan looked at him for a second longer than the sentence needed. "Go and put that bucket down. And eat something. You've got a bit of a colour."

He put the bucket down and ate something, and he thought: *a bit of a colour. She's noticed the colour. Last week she noticed the shelf and the week before she noticed the rag.*

He thought: *this is what it is. This is the whole thing. You don't get caught. Everybody just slowly acquires a version of you that is more accurate than the last one.*

---

His father was in the back room with the scales and a candle, exactly as described.

The window was open because it was always open when the pot was on, and the smell of it was the smell Lan Shen had been born into and would have recognised in a foreign country. His father was doing something with a small brass weight and a strip of wax and a spoon, and there were three jars on the bench and a piece of paper with numbers on it, in his father's handwriting, which was large and slanted and confident about every character except the ones that were sums.

"Come in or don't," said Lan He.

He came in and sat on the upturned crate that was the crate he always sat on.

"I've got the ratio wrong in the winter," said his father, to the pot.

"Somebody said."

"A blacksmith said. Six stars." His father put the spoon down. "Do you know what six stars is."

"No."

"Neither do I, exactly. I know it's the top of what there is in a city this size and I know your mother's friend's husband's cousin once had a knife made by a four-star and they still have it and it's forty years old and it's better than anything I've ever cut with." He picked the spoon up again. "And he says my wax is wrong in the winter."

"Is it."

"It is." His father did not look pleased or annoyed. He looked like a man doing arithmetic. "I've known it's wrong for two years. It goes soft in the cold and it goes gritty in the heat and I've been putting more beeswax in from the ninth month and I've been guessing at how much and I've not written down how much because I've never had a reason to. Now I've got a reason."

"What's the reason."

"A man who's better than me at the thing I do told me I was wrong, in front of my son, and then asked my son what my name was." Lan He looked at him then. "That's the reason. Because if I don't fix it, then the next time somebody from that shop comes in here I'm going to be the man who was told and did nothing, and I'm not that, and neither are you."

Lan Shen did not say anything.

"Did you say thank you," said his father.

"No."

"Why not."

Lan Shen thought about it, because he had thought about it already, on the walk home, and he had not arrived at anything he was willing to say out loud.

"Because he told me there was nothing there for me," he said.

His father was quiet for a moment. The pot ticked.

"That's fair," he said at last. "That's a fair reason and it's a bad one, and you can have it until you're ten and then you can't. Write down what I do with this batch. Write it proper — the weights, not my sums. Your mother says you've got a hand for it."

"I've got a hand for it."

"Then use it."

He used it. He sat on the crate for an hour with his father's paper on his knee and wrote down eleven weights, three temperatures, and the length of time the pot sat off the flame, in his own hand, in his own book. His father did not look at any of it. Once he said "say that again," and Lan Shen said it again, and his father nodded and went on.

At the end his father corked the fourth jar and wrote a number on a slip and put the slip under the lid.

"That's for her," he said. "The one at the pump. Your mother's already given it away so we may as well make it good."

"She's washing dishes."

"I know what she's doing. I was listening to your mother, which is the same as listening to the whole street." He set the jar by the door. "Six to eleven in the morning with her hands in hot water and then a boy at a bench all afternoon. Everybody's hands are going in that family."

Lan Shen looked at the jar.

*One jar,* he thought. *Eleven copper a day is eleven copper a day and a jar of balm is not money and it is not a job and it does not put rice in a pot.*

And then, unasked, in the same voice as the sentence about the horse:

*But it's the first thing anybody on this street has ever made that was measured.*

He did not write that down either.

---

He could not sleep, which was not new.

What was new was that he knew why, and knowing why did not help. By the time the yard had gone properly dark he had given up on the bed and gone to the wall — the third place he had tried, and the one he always ended up at.

He put his hand flat and got nothing. Two days gone, five to go.

He lay there with his ear near the boards and did what he had done every night this week, which was to sort the sounds of the house two doors down into a list, and tonight the list was: the front room, quiet. The kitchen, quiet. A door, once, at about the hour his own father went to bed. And then nothing at all for a long time.

He was nearly asleep when the light came.

It was not in the room he was in. It was on the other side of the yard, through the gap between the two houses, where the Tang family's bedroom window faced onto nothing much and had no shutter because nobody in Glorybound City had ever needed a shutter on a window facing a wall.

A light came up past the sill. Not a lamp. He knew what a lamp did — a lamp is held at arm's length and it swings and it makes the whole room go round in a slow circle. This did not swing. This moved in short, decided steps, and it stayed at one height, about the height of a man's chest, and then it went up a little and stopped.

And it was on him. It was on the man.

There was a small steady light sitting on Tang Ziran's shoulder.

Lan Shen watched it for a long time.

He watched the man go in and kneel, because a light at that height moving in short steps is a man crossing a floor on his feet and then going down. It stayed down. It turned slightly, and stayed turned slightly for as long as it would take to look at one thing carefully.

Then it stood up and went to the door and stopped there for a while.

And went out.

He lay against the wall with his hand flat on it and his heart going, and he worked out what he had seen, and he could not work out what he had seen.

*A lamp on his shoulder.*

He had never seen a lamp on a shoulder. He had seen lamps. He had seen the oil one in the shop and the candle in the back room and the street one at the corner of Feng Street that somebody lit at dusk with a long stick. He had seen the light in Mang Tian's hall, which came down from somewhere and was even and did not move.

He had never seen a light that a man wore.

*It's a machine,* he thought. *It's a bit of a machine. He's a machine man — he fixes them, Wulin said he fixes them, that's what he does, he'd have bits. He'd have a bit he kept.*

That was the answer and it was a good answer and it fitted everything, and he lay there with it for a while and it did not settle.

Because a man does not put on his work light at dead of night to go and sit in his son's bedroom.

He went over what he knew. He had the light. The kneeling. The staying down for a looking-at length of time. The stopping at the door. And he had, from fourteen hours earlier, a boy in a doorway with both hands red at the base of the fingers — and a boy at a pump this morning with both hands clean.

He did not have the arms. That was the whole problem. Whatever the man had gone to look at was in a room behind a wall two doors down, in the dark, under a sleeve.

*Something's the matter with him,* thought Lan Shen. *Or something was.*

And then, coldly, all at once, in the way things arrived:

*There is a chapter about this. I have read it. I read it at two in the morning and I skimmed it and it was about the family and I was looking for the fights.*

He lay still.

He had the shape of it. A chapter title, maybe, or not even that — a page he had turned quickly, a family being warm, a father being worried, a paragraph he had read without reading. He had four hundred chapters in his head and had read perhaps sixty properly, and this was somewhere in the thirty he had moved through at speed with his thumb.

He could not get it.

He tried for a while, lying against the wall with his eyes shut. What came up was the smell of the hall and the weight of the hammer and the number two hundred and six, which were all yesterday; his father saying *that's a fair reason and it's a bad one*, which was today; and a horse standing still watching a man hit a cart.

Nothing about a lamp.

*So,* he thought. *That's what it is.*

*Four hundred chapters and I remember the ones I liked.*

---

He got the book down. He did not light the candle, because the candle was in the front room and the front room was where his mother slept, and he had done this enough times to know the shape of the dark well enough to write in it.

He wrote what he had seen. He wrote it the way he always wrote it, which was the worst version, which was the safe version, which was the version that would be true in ten years' time when he could not remember any of it.

*Dead of night. A light through the gap between the houses, at the Tangs' bedroom window. Not held. Worn — on the shoulder. Steady. He went in, knelt, stayed down a looking-at length, stood, stopped at the door, went out. Wulin's hands were red yesterday at seven and clean today at six. Fourteen hours. That is not possible for a nine-year-old or for anybody. Something is in that room.*

He looked at it in the dark, which meant he did not look at it at all. He put the pen down and picked it up again, because it was bad.

Not wrong. Bad. It was a list, and it was the correct list, and he had been making it every night for four years without it ever bothering him. This evening it bothered him so much that his hand had already started before he decided anything.

What came out was:

*There is a light on his shoulder and it does not swing, and he kneels down beside the bed like a man who has done it before, and the boy does not wake, and the light goes onto the arms and stays there. He is not putting the medicine on. He has the bottle in his hand and he is not putting it on, because there is nothing to put it on.*

He stopped.

He sat on the floor of his own room in the dark with the book on his knee and went through it a second time, slowly, and found four things in it that he had not seen.

*Like a man who has done it before.* He had not seen that. The light had moved in short decided steps, and he had written that down as fact in the first version, and in the second version he had turned it into a man who had done it before.

*And stays there.* He did not know how long.

*He is not putting the medicine on.* He did not know about any medicine. Nobody had said anything about medicine to him. Wulin had said his dad rubbed it on and his mum had said the medicine was good, and both of those were this morning, and both of them were about the arms being fine.

*Because there is nothing to put it on.*

He had not seen the arms.

He had been in the yard at six in the morning looking at a pair of clean hands in daylight. It was now past midnight, and he had written a man kneeling in a dark room looking at a pair of arms with nothing wrong with them, in a hand that did not shake. Every word of it was a guess.

And it was better.

That was the thing. That was the thing that made him sit very still on the floor with the window open and the yard going cold. It was better. It was a scene. It had a man in it, a light, a boy who did not wake, and a reason for the light to be there. If he had read it in a book he would have gone on reading and believed every word of it, with no idea that three sentences had been invented by somebody standing in a yard two houses away.

He looked at the first version. Then he looked at the second.

He thought about crossing the second one out. He got as far as putting the pen near it.

He did not cross it out.

He shut the book and put it back on the shelf, square to the edge, second row, where his mother could reach it. Both versions were in it, one above the other, the second one underneath — which meant that anybody reading the page would read the true one first and the good one second, and would not know which was which. Neither, he understood, standing there in the dark, would he.

That was not a small thing, and he knew it, and he put it in the place he put things.

*The horse,* he thought. *It started at the horse.*

He got into bed. He lay on his back with his hands flat, because his hands had been aching since yesterday and the ache was the one thing in the day that was exactly what it appeared to be.

He had four hundred chapters in his head and had read sixty of them properly, and the one he needed was in the other three hundred and forty. He had skipped it because it was about a family.

He had a book on an open shelf with two versions of the same night in it, and a father in the back room measuring wax because a better man had told him he was wrong.

He had a brother two doors down whose arms had healed in fourteen hours, and whose father had gone in at dead of night with a light on his shoulder to find out about it.

And he had a sentence, in a school exercise book, in a crossed-out line, about a horse standing still watching a man hit a cart, that a teacher with a small black book had read and written something down about.

He lay there and added them up, the way he added everything up, and the sum came out the same as every night this week.

*Nothing is happening to me,* he thought. *Everything is happening to everybody else and I am writing it down.*

He did not get to sleep until late. In the morning he would not have been able to say which of the two pages he remembered. By the end of the week he would have thought about the light on the shoulder four times, and each time it would be a little more like the second version. By the end of the year he would not have been able to tell you whether the boy had woken up.

He had started keeping a record because he did not trust his memory.

He had not thought about what would happen to the record.

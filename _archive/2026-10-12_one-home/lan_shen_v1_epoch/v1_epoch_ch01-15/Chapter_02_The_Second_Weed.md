# Chapter Two — The Second Weed

```
◆ STATUS — Chapter 2: years 0–0
KIND      : the second weed in a room of sixteen, on the first day of nine years of school.
AGE       : six
CLASS     : rank one. The classroom is told aloud that the door to the title Soul Master
            opens at rank ten. He is nine ranks from having a name at all.
THREAT    : Niu Bao — rank five, a knife, and a father who has already told him what he is
            for. Also the room, which ranks everybody out loud before lunch.
BOND      : Tang Wulin. In canon he walks home from this day alone and resolves himself by
            himself. Here he does not, and that is the butterfly.

            Masters. There are two doors and the room only hears one.
```

---

The jars went over on the second morning, in a handcart, before the light was properly up.

Lan He had packed them himself and would not let anybody else touch the lids. There were nine of them. Three of dried bloodroot, turned twice over the stove in the back room across two days. Two of silverleaf. One of something grey and fibrous that Lan He called old-man's-beard and did not explain. And three of a powdered root he had wrapped in cloth inside the jar, so the powder would not settle and look like less than it was.

"You did that on purpose," said Lan Shen, watching him wrap the third one.

"Did what."

"The cloth. So it looks like more."

"It looks like what it is," said Lan He. "It just doesn't look like less."

He tied the cloth with two knots and a half. The half was the part that held.

Lan Shen watched his father's hands do it and understood, for about the fourth time in his life, that everything his father knew he had learned by doing it slowly.

The academy's storeroom was behind the kitchen, which was behind the canteen, which meant the whole corridor smelled of pork fat and steamed bread at six in the morning, and Lan Shen spent the walk down it with his mouth open.

The storekeeper was a heavy woman with a clipboard and no patience and a name badge that said ZHAO. She opened the first jar, put her nose in it, and stopped being impatient.

"Where's this from."

"Third shop past the well," said Lan He. "Off Feng Street."

"I know Feng Street." She took a pinch of the bloodroot and rubbed it between two fingers and held it up to the window. "This isn't grey."

"No."

"The harbour stuff is grey."

"The harbour stuff is dried in the sun."

"Everybody knows that," said Zhao, "and everybody buys it anyway, because it's four coppers and this is nine." She wrote something on the clipboard. "Nine is fine. Nine is better than fine, the bursar's been at me about the medicinal line since spring." She looked at Lan He over the top of the clipboard. "Who told you to bring nine jars."

"My son."

Zhao looked down at Lan Shen, who was still looking at the corridor, and then at the cloth-wrapped jar, and then back at Lan Shen.

"He's six."

"He's six," said Lan He, "and he's right about the bloodroot."

They got a receipt. The valuation would follow. The account stood at nothing until it did. The arrangement was subject to the needs of the class. Lan Shen read the whole of it in the corridor while his father loaded the empty jars back into the cart, and he noticed that the receipt had a number in the top corner and no name anywhere on it.

"What's the number for," he said.

"Accounts."

"Whose account."

"The academy's."

"Then why doesn't it say our name on it."

Lan He thought about this while he tied the last jar down.

"Because if it goes wrong," he said, "they want to be able to lose it."

They walked home. Lan Shen thought about that for the whole of Feng Street and decided his father was right and that this was worth knowing, and he did not write it down yet, because he had not worked out what the sentence was.

---

On the first day of school the sky came up grey and stayed grey. Su Wan put him in the good coat. The good coat was too big.

"It'll be right by winter," she said.

"It'll be right by the year after winter."

"Don't," she said.

Wulin was waiting at the corner of the wall. He had obviously been waiting at the corner of the wall for some time. He was standing very straight.

"You've got a coat," he said.

"It's my dad's."

"It's huge."

"It'll be right by winter."

Wulin considered this, decided it was a thing grown-ups said, and let it go. He was wearing his school clothes, which were new, which his mother had made, and which he was trying extremely hard not to be proud of.

"There's a teacher at the door," he said. "For us. There isn't one for the other class."

"There isn't?"

"There's just a bell." He was pleased about this and ashamed of being pleased. "Shen, are we going to be good at this."

"I don't know."

"I've been thinking about it since the soup."

"What did you decide."

"I decided I'm going to try really hard," said Wulin, "and that if that doesn't work I'm going to try harder, and that if that doesn't work I'm going to ask you."

"That's a good plan."

"It's not a plan, it's just trying."

"It's a plan with three steps. Most plans have two."

They went in through the gate together. The teacher at the door was a young woman with a clipboard who smiled at them and told them which way, smiled at the next family and told them which way, and by the ninth family had stopped smiling. By the fourteenth she was simply pointing.

There were sixteen children in the Soul Master class of Red Mountain Academy that year, which was a good number for a small city, and one of them was a boy from a herb shop who had been standing at the back of a queue the day before yesterday.

---

The classroom was on the ground floor of the east wing and had tall windows and a floor that had been swept so many times the boards had gone soft in the middle.

Sixteen children stood in it in a loose half circle, brimming. Every one of them was six. Every one of them had been told by somebody at home that they were special, and the arithmetic of that had not yet been done by anybody.

"Hey," said a boy beside Wulin. He was chubby and not too tall and had the confident posture of a person who has been stood in front of relatives. "What's your martial soul."

"The man who founded the Tang Sect had the same one," said Wulin, and turned his hand over.

A blue light flickered in his palm and a single strand of Bluesilver Grass came up and danced there, thin and pale and slightly ridiculous, and Wulin held it out at arm's length the way you hold a thing you have been told to be proud of.

Lan Shen looked at it.

He had been looking at grass for four years. He had grown it in cups on a windowsill and written down what it did. He had learned the difference between a blade that had been in the dark and a blade that had not, and between one that had been watered and one that had been left. He had looked at the roots of forty of them through a piece of his father's reading glass, because the roots were the interesting part and nobody else cared about the roots.

Near the root, this one was not pale.

It was faintly, unmistakably, the colour of the inside of a coin. It ran about the length of a fingernail's worth of blade and stopped. Above it the grass was the ordinary blue of every weed on the continent. Below it, where the blade met his palm, there was gold.

It was so slight that it did not look like a thing that had happened. It looked like the light. It looked like the window.

Lan Shen looked at it for perhaps two seconds longer than anybody looks at another child's hand.

"Pff," said the chubby boy. "Grass. Out of everything there is to wake up holding — grass."

And he turned away and walked off, coldly, the way a person does when they have decided something is beneath them.

Wulin's hand stayed out at arm's length with the grass still dancing in it. Nobody had told him to put it away.

"Then what about you," said Wulin, to the boy's back. "What's yours."

The boy turned round. He waved his right hand and a cold light came up in it and there was a small knife in his fist, short and plain and very real, and the half circle went quiet in the specific way a room of six-year-olds goes quiet when there is a weapon in it.

"Look at it properly," he said. A knife came up off him and he held it the way a boy holds a thing he has been told is impressive. Rank five innate soul power, he said. And a Title when the cultivation got there, and a second word inside the Title, and both of them about the knife. Nothing about what the knife had done. He glanced at Wulin's hand. "I could chop that grass of yours into tatters without trying."

"You could try," said Lan Shen.

Everybody looked at him. He had not decided to say it. It had come out in the same place decisions come out of, and he noticed, distantly, that his own grass was not in his hand, because he had not called it.

The boy with the knife looked at him for the first time.

"Who are you."

"Lan Shen."

"What's your rank."

"One."

The half circle made a sound. It was not a roar, it was worse than a roar — it was the small delighted noise sixteen children make when somebody has been given a number out loud and the number is bad.

"One," said the boy with the knife. He said it slowly, the way you say a thing you want to be sure of. He let the knife go and it went back into him, and he came two steps closer, and he was not being cruel, which was the confusing part. He looked genuinely interested, like a boy at a well. "And you've got grass too."

"Same grass."

"So you're the same as him, but worse."

"That's the shape of it."

"What's the point of you."

There was a pause. In the pause Lan Shen worked out three answers and rejected two of them, and noticed that Wulin had gone very red and very still beside him.

He noticed something else too. The question was not an insult, or not only one, because the boy had asked it with his head tilted, like a dog at a new smell.

"I'm the control," said Lan Shen.

"The what."

"If you chop his grass into tatters, I've got grass too, so now you've got two of us and you've still only got one knife." He shrugged. "It's not a good plan. It's the only plan in the room."

Somebody at the back laughed. It was not a friendly laugh exactly, but it was on his side, and the side it was on was the interesting part.

The boy with the knife looked at him for a long moment.

"You're weird," he said, and went back to his place, and that was the whole of it, and it was the first time in four years that Lan Shen had spent any of the invisibility on purpose and gotten change back.

---

"My name is Lin Ximeng," he said, "and this room is mine." He said the rest of it in the voice of a man who has said the same thing to a room before and will say it to another room in three years' time: what he was going to teach them, how long he was going to teach it for, and then the request that they go round the room and give their names, their martial souls, and their ranks.

There were sixteen of them, so it went quickly, and by the seventh introduction the format had settled and by the eleventh it was a game.

"Niu Bao. Knife. Rank five."

"Jiang Ning. A pot. Rank four."

"A pot," said somebody.

"A clay pot," said Jiang Ning, with the flat dignity of a person who has already had this conversation twice at home. "It's a Utility soul. My grandmother has the same one and she can make soup in it without a fire."

"Can you make soup in it."

"I'm six."

"Right," said the somebody, and the room laughed, and Jiang Ning's ears went pink and her chin stayed up, and Lan Shen, who had been about to say something about soup, discovered that his mouth had closed itself.

"Tang Wulin. Bluesilver Grass. Rank three."

Lin Ximeng had been writing. Lin Ximeng stopped writing. He looked up, and he was startled — there was no other word for it, his whole face did it — and the room saw his face do it and the room roared.

Wulin stood in the middle of it with his hands at his sides. His little face went a deep red. He looked as though he was holding a ball of fire in his chest and was not certain how much longer he could.

*Lan Shen,* he thought. *Were these the challenges your dad meant.*

"Lan Shen. Bluesilver Grass. Rank one."

The roar came back, louder, because it had somewhere to go now. Lin Ximeng looked at the second grass boy, then at his register, then back at the second grass boy. His mouth opened.

"Two of them," he said.

"Yes, teacher."

"In the same year. In the same city. With the same soul."

"We're neighbours."

Lin Ximeng closed his mouth. He looked at the two of them for a moment with an expression Lan Shen would think about for eleven years, which was the expression of a man recalculating something he had already written down. Then he recovered his composure, and he smiled, exactly as he was going to smile at this class every morning for three years.

"Right," he said. "Well. Sit down, both of you. Together, if you like, there's a bench."

They sat on the bench.

---

"Everybody in the world has one," he said. He went on in the same register: it awakens at six, it can be anything at all, and if soul power wakes up alongside it then the child who has it may cultivate, and become a Soul Master. Lan Shen had known this since before he could read, in either life, and he listened anyway, because the man was saying it to a room that had not.

"Now." A pause with a shape in it. "Somebody tell me how many kinds of martial soul there are."

Four hands went up. Lin Ximeng took the tallest one.

"Beast souls and tool souls."

"Good. Say the difference."

"Beast souls appear on the body. Tool souls appear separately from the body."

"Correct." Lin Ximeng wrote it on the board in two columns and then explained the columns: soul power feeds the martial soul, the martial soul grows, and at rank ten the child who has a soul becomes a Soul Master. He explained it the way a man explains a thing he has never once wondered about.

The room did something. It was not a sound, it was a settling, sixteen children at once doing arithmetic they had not been told they were doing.

Lan Shen did his arithmetic out loud, because he had not yet learned not to.

"Nine," he said.

Lin Ximeng turned round. "What was that."

"I've got rank one. The door's at rank ten. That's nine." He was aware that the room had gone quiet and aware also that this was a thing he already knew and had known since the day before yesterday, and that saying it out loud had made it a different object entirely. "Is that how many years."

"That," said Lin Ximeng, "depends entirely on you. Some children take four years. Some take six. Most of you in this room will reach rank ten before you leave this academy, and some of you will reach it in the first year, and one or two of you will not reach it at all, and I would rather tell you that now than have you find out in front of an examiner." He put the chalk down. "Rank is not the whole of it. It's the part that's easy to count."

Niu Bao turned round from the row in front.

"You're nine ranks from being a person," he said, to Lan Shen, in a stage whisper that the whole bench row heard.

"I know."

"You know?"

"I counted it before you did."

Niu Bao considered this, decided he had won anyway, and turned back.

"Two classes," said Lin Ximeng. "Battle Soul Masters." He let the word sit in the room. "And Utility Soul Masters." Those who cultivated in order to fight were the first kind, and everybody else was the second kind, and regardless of what a child's martial soul turned out to be, he said, that child was still a Soul Master. "And there are many minor classifications, which you will spend three years being bored by."

The room had stopped listening at *Battle*.

Lan Shen had not stopped listening. He was listening very hard, and he was listening to the second word, and he was the only child in that room who was.

He would not understand until much later that this was the moment his life turned — in a ground-floor classroom with soft boards, on a Tuesday, while everybody else was thinking about knives.

---

*Jiang Ning had a pot. Niu Bao had a knife and a father with a plan. Sixteen children in that room had gone home the night before and been told what they were, and fifteen of the sixteen had been told it in the same language: strong, famous, first, best.*

*Niu Bao had been told it at four, sitting on a counter in his father's stall while his father sharpened things. A knife is a trade. A trade is a life. If you've got soul power then you're not carrying the stall, you're owning it. His father had said it kindly, because he said everything kindly, and he had said it every week for two years.*

*By the time Niu Bao was six the sentence had stopped being a sentence and had become the shape of the inside of his head.*

*He had rank five. Rank five was the best in the room. He had checked during the introductions, the way he checked everything, counting under his breath with his thumb against his trouser seam. Rank five was the best in the room. He had known it since the fourth introduction, and he had waited until the sixteenth to enjoy it.*

*And then the weed boy had said* I'm the control.

*Niu Bao had never heard anybody say a thing like that in a room full of people. He had heard his father haggle. He had heard the fish woman lie. He had never heard a six-year-old who had just been given the worst number in the room stand there and change what the room was about. He had not liked it. He had liked it. He had spent the entire classification lesson with his thumb against his trouser seam, counting, and getting the same answer every time.*

*Two weeds. One knife. Sixteen children.*

*At lunch he sat three benches away and watched the grass boys eat. He did not know that he had started something. Neither did they.*

---

The canteen fed the Soul Master class without limit and the food was better than the ordinary class's food, and this was the first thing about the academy that Lan Shen genuinely loved.

There was pork braised in soy with the fat left on. There was a green thing with garlic. There was a soup that had been on a fire since dawn and had a bone in it, and the bone was the reason. Whoever had made it had known what they were doing, and had not been in a hurry. There was rice. There was more rice. There was a woman at the end of the counter whose job was to say no, and she had stopped saying it to Wulin at the second bowl.

"You're going to be sick," said Lan Shen.

"I'm not."

"You've had four."

"This is five." Wulin's mouth was full. "It's really good, Shen. Have you had the soup. Have the soup. There's a bone in it."

"I can see the bone."

"So have it."

Lan Shen had the soup. He had it slowly, and he worked out what was in it — three things he could name and one he could not, and the one he could not was probably the reason it was good — and then he had a second bowl and went and stood at the counter while the woman watched him.

"Is it the same every day," he said.

"Every day."

"Who makes it."

"Canteen." She looked at him. "You want to complain about something, you tell your teacher."

"I want to know what the fourth thing is."

The woman at the counter laughed, and it was a surprised laugh, and she leaned down.

"Ginger," she said. "Old ginger, and it goes in at the start, and if you put it in at the end it's no good at all. Now go and sit down."

He went and sat down.

"What did she say," said Wulin.

"Ginger."

"That's it?"

"That's the fourth thing." He picked up his spoon. "It goes in at the start."

Wulin looked at him with his bowl in both hands, his face going soft at the edges the way it did when he was full and happy.

"You're really weird about soup," he said, and went back to his rice.

Lan Shen ate the rest of his second bowl in the company of the only other child in Glorybound City who had a weed for a soul. It was the best meal he had had in four years.

In the afternoon they learned to meditate.

---

Lin Ximeng taught it to sixteen children in a room with soft boards, and he taught it well. He walked the rows. He put a hand on a shoulder here and moved a wrist there. He said *breathe out longer than you breathe in* four times. He said *don't force it* nine. And he spent the most time on the children who were doing it wrong in an interesting way.

He spent the least amount of time on Tang Wulin.

It was not unkindness. Wulin was doing it correctly, the first time, in the ordinary way, with his eyes shut and his small fists loose on his knees. There was nothing to fix. A teacher with three years and sixteen children spends his hands where the hands are needed.

Lan Shen watched it happen from a bench and a half away, with his own eyes shut, and he counted the seconds, because counting was the only thing he could do with his face that nobody had ever called strange.

Eleven seconds on the first row. Four on the second. Two, and a hand on a wrist, on Niu Bao. Nothing at all on the bench by the window, twice.

He did not resent it, which surprised him. He had read this day. He had read the whole of this year, and most of the next one. He had read the part where a man with a register spends his hands efficiently. He had read it in a chair, in another life, and it had been sad then in the way a thing in a book is sad.

It was not sad now. It was eleven seconds and four seconds and two seconds, in a room that smelled of chalk, happening to his brother, three feet away.

He opened his eyes and meditated properly. The grass in him did nothing at all. At the end of the afternoon he could not have said whether he had felt anything, or whether he had decided that he ought to have.

---

They walked out together into a grey afternoon. The street was doing what the street did. Wulin was quiet.

He was quiet all the way to Feng Street, which was a long time for Wulin, and Lan Shen let him be quiet, because he had learned a great deal about silence in two lives and most of it had been about not filling it.

At the corner of the well Wulin stopped.

"Shen."

"Mm."

"My martial soul. Is it really that bad."

It was the question. It was the question the whole of the first day had been arranged around, and he remembered it being asked to an empty street by a boy who then answered it himself and went home and ate and fell asleep and burned with gold behind a closed door.

Lan Shen had an answer for it. He had three, and two of them he could never say.

The first was: *no. And here is the proof. It is in your hand, at the root, the colour of the inside of a coin, and nobody in that room saw it except me, because I am the only child in this city who has spent four years looking at roots through a piece of reading glass.*

The second was: *yes, and it stays bad for a long time, and there is a thing in you that is going to try to kill you on a schedule, and I know the schedule.*

He said the third one, because the third one was true and was not either of the others.

"The teacher gave us two doors today," he said. "Everybody in that room heard one."

"What do you mean."

"Battle and Utility. He said both, and he wrote both, and then he said there were minor classifications and the room stopped listening." He kicked a stone along the gutter and watched it go. "Your dad's right that it's going to be difficult. He's wrong about why. It's not because grass is weak. It's because everyone who looks at grass is looking at a sword and doing a subtraction. Niu Bao's got a knife and he's going to be a Battle Master and his dad has already told him what the stall's going to be called."

"And me."

"You've got a weed," said Lan Shen, "and a weed grows everywhere, and nobody has ever had to think about where."

Wulin looked at him.

"That's not an answer."

"It's an answer. It's just not the one you wanted."

"You looked at my grass for a long time today," said Wulin. "When I showed it. Before Niu Bao said the thing. You looked at it longer than anybody."

The street went on being a street. Somebody two doors down was beating a rug.

"No," said Lan Shen.

He had never lied to Wulin before. He found that he did not enjoy it, and that he had not decided to do it, and that he was certainly going to do it again.

"Come and eat," he said. "My mum made the ginger thing."

---

He wrote it down anyway, at night, by the window, because he had never once in two lives been able to keep a number in his head without putting it somewhere.

*First day. Gold at the root, about a fingernail's length, colour of the inside of a coin, stops where the blade starts. He didn't see it. Nobody saw it. It wasn't there yesterday.*

He looked at the sentence for a while and then wrote under it:

*Two cups, day three, no change in either. The weeds are weeds. That's not the same as the weed being rubbish and I need to be careful which one I mean.*

And then, because his hand was already moving:

*Niu Bao counts with his thumb against his trouser seam. Jiang Ning has a pot and can't make soup in it yet. The canteen woman said ginger goes in at the start. Lin Ximeng spent two seconds on me and none on Wulin and it was the correct allocation and I want to hit him.*

The book was a school exercise book from the shop. It went on the shelf by the window, on the second row, where anybody in the family could reach it — because his mother had asked him when he was going to mention it, and he had told her the truth, and she had said don't stop.

He put it back on the shelf.

Then he took it down again and wrote one more line at the bottom of the page, in the small hand he used for things he did not want to read twice.

*If anybody ever finds out what I know, the gold is the first thing they will ask me about, and I will have to say I saw it on the first day, and then they will ask why I didn't tell.*

He shut the book. He put it on the shelf. He left it where anybody could reach it.

---

Glorybound City smelled of salt and hot metal, and nobody who lived there thought either smell was worth mentioning.

On the morning of Awakening Day the street in front of Red Mountain Academy was so full that a dog could have crossed the square without touching the ground. Parents stood in their best coats, which for most of them meant their only good coat, and did not say what they were thinking.

Two boys sat on the low wall of the herb shop because they had arrived early and had already been told twice to stop climbing it.

"If I get a hoe," said Tang Wulin, "I'm going to be very good at it."

"You'd be terrible at it," said Lan Shen. "You cried when you dropped a spoon."

"That was a good spoon."

"It was a spoon, Wulin."

"My mum likes that spoon."

"Your mum likes you. She'd like you if you dropped the whole bowl."

Wulin thought about this, decided it was true, and brightened. He was six, slightly tall for it, with short black hair and large clear black eyes and lashes the older women on the street had remarked on more than once. He wore commoner's clothing his mother had washed the night before. He did not seem to notice any of it.

Lan Shen was six and small for it, with dark hair his mother had stopped fighting about and a face that gave people the impression, unfairly, that he had already heard the end of the joke. He had a callus on the heel of his right hand from a staff he had been turning for two years. A knife scar on his left thumb from a ginger root he had been in a hurry about. And he had been counting the street since dawn.

Four hundred and eleven people. Sixty-two of them children. Fifteen of those children were the right age, which meant roughly one of them, statistically, would come out with soul power, and the whole street was arranged around that one the way iron filings arrange themselves around something you cannot see.

He had been doing arithmetic like this since he was two. He had never thought it was strange, because he had never met anybody who did not.

"What are you doing," said Wulin.

"Nothing."

"You've got the face."

"What face."

"The one where you're counting." Wulin kicked his heels against the wall. "My dad says you count like a tax man. He says it in a nice way."

"Your dad says everything in a nice way."

"He does, doesn't he." Wulin was pleased about this, and then, in the middle of being pleased, went quiet. "Shen. What if it's grass."

"There's grass all over this street."

"That's not the point."

"It's a little bit the point." Lan Shen slid off the wall. "Come here. Look at it."

He took Wulin by the sleeve and walked him three paces to the academy wall. Bluesilver Grass came up through the base of the stone, along the ditch, in through the gate, and across the whole of the clean grounds beyond — the flat patient way that weed grew everywhere on the continent. It grew in city cracks and on grave mounds and in the trampled yards of villages. There was more of it in Glorybound City than there were people.

Wulin looked at it, because Lan Shen was making him, and he was a boy who looked at things properly once he started.

"It's a weed."

"It's a weed that eats sunlight," said Lan Shen. "Did you know that. It doesn't need meat. Everything else you can get — a tiger, a hawk, a snake, a knife — you have to feed it. This one stands in the sun and drinks water and makes itself."

"So?"

"So it's the only one that never gets hungry."

Wulin looked at the grass for a while longer.

"That's a very small good thing," he said.

"It's a good thing," said Lan Shen. "You're just counting it wrong."

---

Su Wan came out of the shop with her sleeves still pushed up and her hands still wet, because she had been washing radishes since before the light, and she was not a woman who dried her hands for anybody.

"Off the wall," she said.

"We were on the wall," said Wulin helpfully.

"You're both on it in spirit. Off." She looked at her son, and her face did the thing it did about once a week, which was to go careful. "Lan Shen. Where have you gone."

"Nowhere."

"You've been gone since you were two and I have never once caught you at it." She crouched down, which she did with some effort, and took his chin in one wet hand and turned his face up to hers. "Today of all days. Stay here. With the street. With me."

"I'll stay," he said.

It was the truth, and it was also the first lie of the day. He had planned it. He would have felt worse if the alternative had been anything other than her standing in a crowd for eleven hours being frightened.

Lan He came out behind her with the account book under his arm, because it was Awakening Day and he did not trust a holiday, and he put a hand on his son's head without looking at him.

"Whatever happens," said Lan He, "we're proud of you."

"Dad."

"Yes."

"If I come out with a hoe, I want you to know I'm going to be very good at it, and I'm going to be furious about it the entire time."

Lan He laughed, which was a rare enough event that the cooper across the street looked up, and shoved his son gently toward the gate.

A girl in a green coat fell into step beside them, and Lan Shen immediately discovered an urgent interest in the bricks.

"That's Meilin," said Wulin. "She's in our year."

"I know who she is."

"She asked me if I knew what your martial soul would be and I said probably a rock."

"That's not funny."

"It was a little funny."

"She asked you about me."

"She asked me about everybody. She's got the list." Wulin nudged him. "Your ears are red."

"They are not."

"They're the colour of the radishes."

Lan Shen walked faster. The green coat walked faster. By the time they reached the gate he had worked out precisely four things to say and rejected all of them.

This had been his condition for the whole of his previous life, and he had rather hoped not to bring it into this one.

---

Inside the gate there was a table, and behind the table there was a man, and the queue for the man was much shorter than the queue for the gate, which told Lan Shen everything he needed to know about what the table was for.

The notice pinned to it said SOUL MASTER CLASS — REGISTRATION in letters somebody had painted by hand, and underneath, in smaller letters, the things nobody painted by hand ever bothered to make small enough.

Yan Song was forty-ish, thin, cleanly dressed, with ink on one cuff and the flat pleasant expression of a man who had been saying the same sentences since before sunrise and had made his peace with it. There was a register open in front of him and a shallow tray of coins beside the register, and the tray was the reason for the queue's length.

"Tang Wulin," he said, and wrote. Then, without looking up: "Rank three. Bluesilver Grass. You'll be in the Soul Master class. There is a materials fee at the start of term and a book list. Your father's trade?"

"Machine repair," said Wulin. "Soul machines."

"Good trade." Yan Song wrote it in a column that was not the rank column. "Next."

The next family came up, and the one after, and Lan Shen stood at the end of it with his mother's hand on his shoulder and listened, because listening was free.

He heard the word stipend four times, and every time it was the same sentence in a different coat: your number is too low for a stipend. He heard one father ask what the fee covered and get an answer that took forty seconds and said nothing. He heard the tray take coins.

Then it was his turn.

"Lan Shen." Yan Song's pen was already moving. "Rank?"

"One," said Lan Shen.

The pen stopped. It stopped for perhaps a second, which in the life of a register is a long time.

"Martial soul?"

"Bluesilver Grass."

"Rank one Bluesilver Grass." Yan Song said it the way a man reads out a weather report, and then he did look up, and his expression was not unkind, which was worse. "You have soul power. That's more than most. But I want to be honest with your mother, because the honest version is cheaper for everybody. The Soul Master class costs money. There is no stipend at rank one, and there is no stipend at rank two, and by the time you'd reached the age where a stipend was possible you'd have spent three years of a poor family's coin on books and materials and a body that can't carry it. Most children at rank one go to the normal class and take a trade. That is not a punishment. That is arithmetic."

"He's six," said Su Wan.

"He's six for about ten minutes of the day, madam, and after that he's seven, and the arithmetic doesn't get friendlier."

The queue behind them did not shift, but Lan Shen felt it settle the way a room settles when somebody has been told something true in public.

Four years of listening in a herb shop. Four years of standing at his father's counter.

Men came in and asked what a thing cost. His father said a number. The man said a smaller number. They met somewhere in the middle, and every single one of those conversations ended the same way: somebody found a line in a ledger that both of them had forgotten was there.

Lan Shen looked at the notice on the table.

He read it again. The painted letters said SOUL MASTER CLASS — REGISTRATION. Underneath, in the small hand nobody bothered with, there were nine lines, and the seventh of them said: *materials may be supplied in lieu of coin at the academy's own valuation, subject to the needs of the class.*

He had read that line four times in four years, on four different notices, in four different shops on this street, because his father made him read notices aloud, and he had never once had a reason for it.

"Sir," he said.

Yan Song's eyes came down to him.

"Does the academy buy its medicinals in the city."

"It buys what it buys."

"From the street, or from the harbour merchants."

There was a pause. It was a short pause and it was the first interesting thing that had happened at that table all morning.

"The harbour merchants," said Yan Song. "They're cheaper."

"They're cheaper because they dry everything in the sun and it goes grey," said Lan Shen. "Dried bloodroot from the harbour is worth four coppers and it should be worth nine. My dad's is worth nine. We dry it over the stove in the back and we turn it twice and it takes two days longer and that's the whole reason it costs more."

Su Wan's hand tightened on his shoulder.

"That's enough, Shen—"

"No, let him." Yan Song had put the pen down. It was not a friendly gesture; it was the gesture of a man who had heard a number and wanted to check it. "You're six."

"I'm six."

"And you know what bloodroot is worth."

"I know what my dad's bloodroot is worth. I've been writing it in his book since I was four."

"He lets a four-year-old in his accounts."

"He let me count the coins first. Then he let me count the jars. Then he let me write." Lan Shen looked at the notice, and then back. "The seventh line says materials can be supplied instead of coin. At the academy's own valuation. So the academy is already buying herbs, it's just buying them grey. If it bought them from us instead, my father's fee could come off the account, and you'd get a better jar for less money, and I'd be a working student instead of a poor one. Those are different lines in the book."

Behind him somebody made a sound that was almost a laugh and got disciplined quickly.

Yan Song looked at the boy. He looked at the notice. He looked at the tray of coins, which was three parts full and had been three parts full since the middle of the morning, and which he would have to carry to the bursar at dusk and account for to the copper.

Then he pulled the register round and turned a page back, and wrote something in the margin in a hand that was much smaller than the one he used for names.

"Medicinals supplied in lieu," he said. "Subject to valuation, subject to the needs of the class, and subject to your father actually bringing jars that aren't grey. If one jar is grey, the arrangement ends and the fee comes due in full at once."

"I'll tell him."

"You'll tell him today."

"I'll tell him now."

"Then go and be awakened," said Yan Song, "and try not to be rank one about it."

He picked up the pen. He had already forgotten them, which was the correct professional outcome and which Lan Shen would remember for eleven years.

Su Wan walked him toward the path with her hand on the back of his neck, and she did not speak until they were out of earshot of the table.

Behind them the queue had already closed over the gap. That was the thing about a table like that one: it did not remember anybody. A man with a register can be perfectly fair to four hundred people in a day and be a wall to every single one of them, and the wall does not feel like anything from his side. It feels like work. It feels like a number he has to send upward before the bursar goes home.

He would come back to that table for nine years. He would learn its hours, its tray, its margin hand, and the exact shade of patience in the voice that said *subject to valuation*, and none of that would ever make the table like him. But the seventh line of the notice was still there, and it had not moved, and for as long as it did not move it belonged to anybody who read it.

That was worth knowing. It was, he thought, possibly the only thing he knew that was worth anything at all.

"Since when."

"Since what."

"Since when do you know what bloodroot is worth."

"Since I was four."

"And since when do you talk like that to a man with a book."

He thought about it honestly.

"Since now," he said.

---

There was not one awakening chamber in the Awakening Chamber but seven — seven floors, one chamber apiece. The boys were taken up to the third. The parents were left at the bottom of a narrow stair that smelled of stone dust and old incense.

Eleven children went up. They came down in ones and twos over the next two hours, and each of them came down holding something or not holding something, and the ones who were not holding anything walked faster.

Wulin did not come back down for a long time.

Lan Shen waited on the third-floor landing with four other children and a teacher who had already forgotten his name, and at some point during that wait a boy screamed behind the door.

It was not a long scream. It broke off the way screams break off when a body has finished being surprised.

Three of the children on the landing jumped. One of them started to cry, and was comforted, and stopped.

Lan Shen did not jump, because he had heard that scream once before, in a room with a screen in it, in a life where it had been entertainment, and he had liked it, and he had gone and made noodles afterwards.

He stood very still on the landing with his hands at his sides and waited for the door to open, and it took him a moment to notice that his nails were in his palms.

The door opened. Wulin came out. He was pale and slightly damp and walking carefully, the way you walk when your legs have recently been somebody else's, and there was a small pale blue blade of grass in his fist.

Their eyes met across the landing.

"Hey," said Wulin.

"Hey."

"I've got grass."

"So have I," said Lan Shen, and held up his own hand, and it was empty, because he had not gone in yet, and Wulin laughed so hard he had to lean on the wall.

"Yours hasn't happened yet."

"I'm being optimistic."

"You're being weird."

"I'm being six."

The teacher's voice came along the landing. "Lan Shen."

Wulin's hand was on his sleeve and then it was on his hand, and it was the first hand in, because it always was, and Lan Shen went.

---

The chamber was round, and every surface of it — floor, wall, ceiling — was cut with inscriptions that ran in fine lines toward the centre, and at the centre the light was already beginning to gather before he had finished walking.

The man conducting it wore a simple orange robe with a soul beast embroidered across the chest. He stood at the edge of the light with his hands loosely in front of him, like a man who had done this ten thousand times and had ten thousand more to do before sundown.

"Stand in the middle," he said. "It'll be warm. Don't fight it."

Lan Shen stood in the middle.

The light came up through the soles of his shoes and into his heart, and it was not warm, it was loud — a huge white noise behind the eyes — and something in him that had been sitting very still for six years stood up to look at it.

He had spent four years preparing for a martial soul. He had grown things in cups on a windowsill. He had learned what a root was for, what a leaf was for, what light does to a green surface. He had done all of it because he liked it. The liking had never once felt like work.

And he had told himself for four years that whatever came out of his hand, he would already know how to feed it.

A blade of pale blue grass came up out of the hollow of his palm and turned once, and swayed.

He did not need to be told what it was. He had been standing on it since dawn.

The heat came next, out of the bones of his arms and legs. It was much worse than the loudness. He made a noise he would have preferred not to make.

Then it stopped, and he was standing in a round room with grass in his hand, tears on his face, and a six-year-old's entire dignity in a small puddle on the floor.

The Spirit Master had already turned toward the instrument.

"Rank one," he said. "Innate soul power, rank one."

He wrote the line. He wrote it the way a man writes a line he has written thirty thousand times. He did not look up. There was pity in the set of his shoulders — entirely professional, entirely correct.

"You have soul power," he said, to the page. "That's rarer than your family understands. As for the grass — the man who founded the Tang Sect carried the same martial soul, and he relied on it to rebuke the Heavens and the Earth. There was a Martial Soul Hall then, twenty thousand years back. It was wicked, and it did not survive him."

It was a speech. He had given it twenty minutes earlier to a boy two streets away, and he gave it kindly, and he gave it without looking up.

"Thank you," said Lan Shen, in a voice that shook, because the voice was six.

He had turned to go. He turned back.

"Sir."

The Spirit Master's pen paused. "Yes."

"When you do the light," said Lan Shen. "Do you measure the head as well as the hand."

The pen stopped.

"The head."

"The hand gets a number. Rank one. Rank three." He was aware, distantly, that he was doing the thing his mother had told him not to do, and he did it anyway, because the question had been sitting in him for four years and he had never had anybody to ask. "Is there a number for the head. Does the light go in there too. Because it went in there first, and nobody wrote anything down about it."

Qin Wu looked at the child.

He had awakened children in this chamber for eleven years. In the eleven years before that, three other cities. He had stopped counting the total somewhere past thirty thousand, because counting it made the work feel like something other than weather. He had been asked for water. He had been asked for a father. He had been asked, twice, whether the martial soul could be exchanged.

He had never once been asked what else the ceremony measured.

*Because it went in there first,* the child had said, and he was right, and being right at six about the order of a ceremony was not a thing that happened.

"There's a test for that," Qin Wu said slowly. "Spiritual power. It's not done here. It's done at the academy, later, if there's a reason."

"What's a reason."

"Being worth the machine's time."

He heard how that sounded as he said it, and he had said it thirty thousand times, and he had never once heard it before.

Lan Shen nodded, politely, the way you nod at an adult who has just told you something true and embarrassing.

"Thank you, sir," he said, and went out.

---

*Qin Wu wrote the line. Rank one, Bluesilver Grass, male, six, Glorybound. The register took it the way a ledger takes anything — without opinion.*

*He sat for a moment with the pen still in his hand.*

*Eleven minutes earlier, in this chamber, golden lines had crawled across a boy's forehead, run out to his four limbs, made a network of themselves. He had crossed the room so fast he went slightly blurry. He had arrived with his hand up to stop a ceremony that had already stopped needing him.*

*And he had stood there like a fool with his arm in the air.*

*He was a fourth-rank man. He was not a man who was frightened by children.*

*There was a form for anomalies. He had filled it twice in twenty-three years. Both times the response came within a month. Both times it was courteous. Nothing happened afterwards, and the second time he had been quietly grateful for that, in a way he had not entirely enjoyed admitting to himself.*

*He looked at the register. He looked at the door, where the last of the grass children were filing out into the stairwell, small and blinking and already arguing about their numbers.*

*Then he drew a second sheet toward him and wrote down, in the margin, in a hand that was much smaller than the one he used for names:*

*Asked what else the ceremony measures. Age six. Rank one.*

*He did not know what to do with that, so he did nothing with it, and closed the register, and called the next child.*

---

Outside, the street did what the street always did, which was read the children before they reached the gate.

Tang Ziran was at the wall. He was a middle-aged man of medium build and average height. Everything about him was average, which was a thing he had made peace with, and also a thing that occasionally sat on his chest at night. He repaired soul machines. His skill was ordinary and his wage was therefore meagre.

He had taken a half day off, which he could not really afford.

He got down on the gravel in front of two hundred people and took his son's face in both hands.

"Son," he said. "One in a million. That is what you are."

"Dad — Bluesilver Grass. Is that a trash thing to have."

"How could grass be trash." A breath. "Out of a thousand people, one has soul power." He was crying slightly, and he did not seem to have noticed. "You have a trash martial soul." Another breath. "And you have soul power." He said the arithmetic out loud the way a man does when he has just found a reason to be proud of something and would like to hear it from somebody else. "That makes you one in a million." A gulp. "Unique, and by definition unmatched."

And then he went on to the ancestor, the way he always did — the founding one, the Tang Sect one, the weed that had not been a weed — and Lan Shen stopped listening at the name, because he had read this speech before, in another life, and because the man saying it was weeping over a son who was already rolling his eyes at him.

"He already told me that one," said Wulin, to Lan Shen, over his father's shoulder, in the flat voice of a boy enduring affection in public.

"He told me too."

"Did you get the bit about the Martial Soul Hall."

"Twenty thousand years ago."

"He skipped the bit about the Heavens and the Earth."

"He never skips that bit, he just says it quietly."

Tang Ziran stood up, wiped his face with the back of his wrist, and put his hand on Lan Shen's head as well, because he had been doing that for six years and had no intention of stopping.

"And you, Shen. What did the world hand you."

"Rank one," said Lan Shen. "And grass."

The hand on his head went still.

"Well," said Tang Ziran, after a moment, in the voice of a man who has been given something difficult and intends to be decent about it. "That's the same grass. That's the important part. Two Lans and a Tang with the same weed in their hands — you'll be the terror of the street."

"He's not a Lan, he's a Tang."

"His mother's a Lan," said Tang Ziran, "and that's close enough on this street, and always has been."

It was an ordinary thing to say. The two families had shared a wall and a drain for eleven years and the street had called them relations for as long as either could remember, and Lan Shen had known this fact for six years and had never once spent it.

He spent it now, without deciding to.

"Come and eat with us," he said.

Wulin's whole face changed.

"Can I."

"You have to," said Lan Shen. "My mother cooks like she's been told there'll be four and decided there'll be nine, and if you're not there she'll give your share to the cooper's kids and then she'll be sad about it all evening."

"She isn't like that."

"She is exactly like that."

"I'm coming," said Wulin.

---

They ate at four in the afternoon, because Su Wan would not hear of a boy going home hungry on the worst day of his life.

And because Lan Yue came through the yard with a bowl of three-vegetable soup and refused to leave again. Lan Yue could not be regarded as a beauty, but she was definitely charming. She was exceptionally good at cooking. She had no intention of letting another woman's kitchen defeat her.

The front room of the herb shop was not big enough for seven people and had been asked to hold them anyway. There was sautéed greens. Stewed pork ribs that had cost Su Wan two days of thinking. A salad, the soup, rice. And a flat dish in the middle of the table that nobody had mentioned.

Wulin pointed at it.

"What's that."

"That's Shen's," said Su Wan.

Everybody looked at Lan Shen.

"It's egg," he said. "With scallion. And a bit of the ginger that was going soft."

"You made it."

"I make it."

"You're six."

"I've been six for six years," said Lan Shen. "I've had time."

Lan He, who had not spoken since the gate, put a spoon into the dish and tasted it, and tasted it again, and set the spoon down.

"He's better than me," he said, to the table, and went back to his rice.

Which was the highest thing Lan He had ever said about anything, and Su Wan's eyes went bright, and Wulin took a mouthful with his whole face, and Lan Yue said "oh" in a tone that meant she was recalculating, and the ribs went cold on the table while six people argued about ginger.

Wulin ate four bowls.

Nobody commented on it. His mother put food in front of him at exactly the same rate she put it in front of her own son, and his father made one joke about a growing boy and then made no more, and this was the kindest thing that happened to Tang Wulin that year.

Halfway through the third bowl he stopped.

"Shen."

"Mm."

"What did that man say to you. At the table. With the money."

"He said the arithmetic doesn't get friendlier."

The table went quiet in the particular way a table goes quiet when a child has repeated an adult's sentence correctly.

"And then you said something," said Wulin, "and he wrote it down."

"I said we could supply the jars."

"What jars."

"Medicinal jars. Herbs. For the class." Lan Shen shrugged. "So the fee comes off the account instead of out of the box."

"And he said yes."

"He said if one jar is grey the arrangement ends and the whole fee comes due at once."

Wulin put his bowl down.

"You did that in a minute," he said. "I stood there and he wrote a number and I said thank you, and you stood there and you changed the number."

"It wasn't a good number."

"It was rank three."

"It was a number on a day," said Lan Shen. "He measured the day I woke up. That's all anybody measured. That man has a machine for the head and he only turns it on if you're worth the machine's time, and I want to know what it says, and I can't ask for four more years, and I'm not going to spend four years being a number that a man wrote down while he was thinking about lunch."

He was aware, partway through, that he had gone too far, and that the table had gone very quiet, and that his mother was looking at him with her chin in her hand.

Wulin was not looking at him like that.

Wulin was looking at him the way he looked at anything he intended to understand.

"So what do we do," he said.

Lan Shen looked past him, at the windowsill.

There were two cups on it, and a blade of grass standing in each. He had pulled them an hour ago out of the ditch by the academy wall, one from his side of the gate and one from Wulin's, though nobody on earth could have told the two apart, which was rather the point. He had put them in water and set them in the last of the sun and nobody at the table had mentioned either of them.

"Those are weeds," said Wulin.

"They're the weed we've got."

"They're not the same. Mine's rank three."

"Yours is rank three when you call it out of your hand," said Lan Shen. "This one is just a weed in a cup. I want to know whether the weed in the cup does anything at all, and I want to know whether the weed in your hand does something the weed in mine doesn't. Same water. Same window. Every day. And we write it down."

"You want to grow weeds in cups."

"I want to find out whether it's the weed that's rubbish or the rank."

Wulin turned round on the bench and looked at the windowsill for a long time.

"Nobody's ever done that," he said.

"Nobody's ever had a reason to."

"Nobody's ever had two," Wulin agreed, and turned back, and picked up his bowl, and ate the rest of his fourth helping with the air of a man who has been handed work.

---

The sky went dark over Glorybound City and the soul lamps came up the street one after another. Next door, Tang Wulin fell asleep at the table with his cheek in his arm and had to be carried home. His father took a leave of absence for the whole of the next day, without being asked.

Su Wan stood in the yard afterward with her arms folded, looking at her son, who was washing two cups at the pump.

"Lan Shen."

"Mm."

"You have been growing things in cups on that windowsill for four years."

"Yes."

"I know you have. I put them there." She waited. "What I want to know is when you were going to mention that you'd been keeping a book."

He did not look up from the cups.

"Since I was four," he said. "You asked me to count the jars. Then you asked me to count the coins. I just kept going."

"I asked you to count jars."

"And I've been counting everything else since."

Su Wan was quiet for a while. Somewhere beyond the wall, in the room where the boy slept, a bed creaked once, and then went on being quiet in the heavy unbroken way of a house with an exhausted child in it.

"Then don't stop," she said at last, and went inside to argue with Lan Yue about soup.

He finished the cups and set them on the sill side by side in the last of the light — two weeds out of the same ditch, in plain water, facing the same window, with nothing in the world to tell them apart except which side of the gate they had come from.

Behind the wall the whole house was asleep, and the boy in the small back room was not asleep at all, though nobody knew it, and would not know it for years.

The water in the cups was still moving.

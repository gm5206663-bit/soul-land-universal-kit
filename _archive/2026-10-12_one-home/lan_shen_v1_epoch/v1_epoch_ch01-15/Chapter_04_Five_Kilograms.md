---

On the third day of school they went back to the room with the soft boards. Lin Ximeng made them sit down, and Lan Shen spent forty minutes not doing the one thing he could do.

It was worse than he had expected.

He had given himself a week. He had worked it out on the floor of his own room with his face in a rag: eleven seconds had cost him the sense entirely, so a week was the minimum honest estimate. A week from Tuesday night was Tuesday night, and until then the grass under the paving of Glorybound City was going to go on being a rumour.

That was the plan. The plan was correct. The plan was also, he discovered, unbearable.

*Breathe out longer than you breathe in.*

He sat. He felt the blue thread coiled up in him, rank one, the width of a hair, asleep. He did not ask it anything. He could have asked it one small thing — the ditch behind the shop, the crack in the pavement outside, a single strand, ten seconds, nothing — and he did not, because he had spent four years learning that the way to be good at something was to not do it when you were not supposed to.

Around him fifteen other children sat and felt nothing at all.

Lin Ximeng walked the rows. He put a hand on a shoulder. He moved a wrist. He spent nine seconds on Wulin — seven more than the first day. Lan Shen noticed that too, wrote it down in his head, and then hated himself a little for writing it down in his head when he was supposed to be meditating.

At the end of it Lin Ximeng said "good," to the room in general. The room stood up. The third day of school was over.

---

Wulin's mother was at the gate.

That was the first thing. Parents did not come to the gate. The gate had a teacher with a list on the first day and nobody on any day after, and the whole street of them went out together and split up at the corner like water.

Lan Yue was standing by the wall in a coat that had been mended at the cuff. She was looking at the gate, and she was not looking at it the way a mother looks at a gate.

"Wulin," she said. "Come here."

"Mom?" Wulin's whole face did the thing it did. "Why have you come to pick me up?"

"Walk with me," she said. "I'm taking you to see Uncle Mang Tian."

"Uncle Mang Tian!" Wulin said it very loud. "The blacksmith Uncle Mang Tian? Dad said—"

"Dad said, and I've said, and you're coming, and you can ask your questions on the road." She took his hand. She looked over his head at Lan Shen, and her face softened by one degree, which was the whole of what she had available. "Lan Shen. Are you walking home?"

"Yes, auntie."

"Then walk home," she said. "Don't wander."

And they went.

Lan Shen stood at the gate of Red Mountain Academy with his satchel on his back and watched his brother get walked off toward the other end of the city by a woman who had been crying recently. He knew exactly where they were going, how long it would take, and what would be on the floor of the hall when they got there.

*Two hours every day for three months,* he thought. *That's the deal. He signs it today.*

He had known this was coming since the other life. He had not known it was today.

He started walking. Then he stopped. Then he turned around and went home the long way, because there was a thing he needed from the shop first.

---

"Do we sell anything for hands," he said.

His father was at the counter, weighing something grey on the little scales, and he did not look up.

"For hands."

"Swollen. Split. Blistered. Burnt."

Lan He slid the weight along the bar and squinted at the pointer. "There's the balm. Third drawer. Why."

"The Mang Tian workshop."

That got a look.

"They've taken Wulin on," said Lan Shen. "He starts today. Two hours. He'll be swinging hammers and his hands will be gone by tomorrow, and his mother will be in that shop every day at the same hour for three months. She is not going to think about balm, because she is going to be thinking about her son."

Lan He put the weight down.

"You know they've taken him on."

"His mother collected him from the gate."

"Ah." His father was quiet for a moment, in the way he was quiet when he was adding things up and did not care for the total. "And you want to walk twenty minutes across the city, by yourself, to sell balm to a blacksmith who has not asked for any."

"I want to give him a box."

"Give him."

"If he uses it, he'll want more. If he doesn't, I've lost a box and a walk." Lan Shen had the third drawer open already. "And he'll know the shop's name either way. He's got a boy in there with his hands going, and there's going to be a girl in that house from now on who eats like a horse. That's four people who will need something from us before the winter."

Lan He looked at his son for a long time.

"That's a good answer," he said, "and I don't like that you had it."

"That's the second time."

"I know it's the second time." He came round the counter and took the drawer off him and packed it himself, properly, with the waxed paper under the lid the way he did for the academy. "Take the good box. If you're going to be a shopkeeper, be a good one at six, I can't stop you." He wrote something on a slip and tucked it into the lid. "That's what it's worth. If he offers less, take it. If he offers more, take it and say thank you and come straight home and tell me, and don't spend any of it."

---

The city was twenty minutes and it was not interesting, which was the point.

Glorybound City did not need vehicles. The commoner's district was a grid of streets that all smelled of the same four things at different strengths. The shops got bigger as you went east and the smells changed, and at some point Lan Shen stopped counting the streets and started counting the signs, because he had nothing else to do and because his head was still full of a classroom he had not been allowed to leave early.

Twelve shops sold something to do with metal. Three of them had a hand-painted board outside, and the boards were all the same shape.

He was thinking about a blue thread the width of a hair when he got to the workshop, and he nearly walked past it.

The smell got him. Metal, hot and cold at the same time. Oil. And something under both that he had smelled in another life in a car park behind a shopping centre.

Shabby front. Three words painted on top.

He stood on the step with the good box under his arm and listened, and from somewhere inside a building that was not large nor small there came a sound that made the hair on his arms stand up.

*Bang. Bang. Bang.*

Slow now. Uneven. And a man's voice, low, saying something, and then the banging again.

Lan Shen knocked.

---

Nobody came, so he let himself in, because the door was open and because in the commoner's district an open door in the afternoon was an invitation.

The hall was a mess.

That was the first thing, and the first thing nearly knocked the rest of it out of his head. There were benches down both sides and every bench was full. The floor was full. There were crates with the slats broken out of them, and hanging from hooks in the rafters there were shapes he could not name and shapes he could.

He stopped in the doorway with a box of balm under his arm and his previous life stood up.

*That's a race.*

It was on the bench nearest him, in a tray of oil: a bearing race with the rollers taken out and laid in a row beside it. There were nine rollers. They were all very slightly different from each other.

He looked at them for a long time.

*Bang. Bang.*

He went through, because he had been told to come in, and he found the inner room at the back.

---

Wulin was sitting on the floor with his back against a workbench and his arms held out at his sides like a bird that had given up.

The bench was just barely taller than he was. On it there was a lump of round metal on a half-metre table. Below the table there was a screen. The screen said one thousand and fifty.

Two hammers were on the floor.

A big man with a full beard and a dark face was crouched in front of Wulin holding one of his wrists. Wulin's mother was in the doorway with her hands over her mouth. Both of them turned round when Lan Shen came in.

"He didn't do anything." Wulin said it at once, enormously, from the floor. "Uncle Mang Tian gave me the test himself. I passed."

"Ah-yo," said Lan Yue.

"I brought balm," said Lan Shen.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Mang Tian looked at him. He was the sort of large that made the inner room feel like a cupboard. His face had been arranged into an expression of fierceness so long ago that it had stopped being an expression. He looked at a six-year-old in a doorway holding a box, and he did not change it.

"Who are you."

"Lan Shen. From the herb shop. On the corner by the drain."

"What herb shop."

"Lan He's."

Something moved a very small way in that face.

"The grass boy," said Mang Tian.

"There's two of us," said Lan Shen.

"Set it down."

He set it down. He set it down on a workbench in a six-star blacksmith's inner room because there was nowhere else. He noticed as he did it that the bench top was worn into a shallow dish in front of where a man would stand, which meant a great many years, and that the wear was even, which meant the man stood the same way every time.

Mang Tian let go of Wulin's wrist and stood up. He went past Lan Shen to the door and put a bottle into Lan Yue's hand.

"Smear this on his arms when you get home," he said. "Both of them. Tonight."

"Big Brother Mang Tian—"

"He's my disciple from tomorrow," said Mang Tian. "Same hour. Every day." And then, to Wulin, without turning round: "You raised a good one."

Lan Yue started crying properly at that, in the doorway, with her free hand still over her mouth, and Wulin said "Mom!" from the floor in the tone of a man betrayed by his own family, and Lan Shen stood by the bench and looked at the screen.

One thousand and fifty.

He did the arithmetic without deciding to, which was the trouble with him.

*Five kilograms each. Two of them. A thousand and fifty swings. Half an hour, his mother said on the road, half an hour, and then he'd said Uncle Mang Tian made him go again and he couldn't remember how many.*

An adult man's arms would be too numb to lift after a thousand. He remembered that being said in so many words — no. Mang Tian had not said that. *He* had read it. In the other life. In a bed. On a phone.

He looked at the hammers on the floor.

---

Mang Tian came back and picked up the box and opened it. He read the slip his father had written. He smelled the balm, put a finger in it, rubbed it against his thumb and forefinger, then rubbed the two together and held them near his ear.

"Who made this."

"My dad."

"He's not a maker, he's a boiler. Who told him the ratio."

"I don't know what that means."

Mang Tian looked at him. It was not an unkind look. It was the look a man gives a thing that has said something unexpected and might say something else.

"Beeswax, tallow, and the green stuff," said Lan Shen. "He melts the first two and takes the pan off before the third goes in, because the third goes bitter. And he does it in the back room with the window open, and he won't let me near the stove."

"Hm." Mang Tian put the lid back on. "What's this for."

"Hands."

"My hands are fine."

"Not yours." Lan Shen nodded at the floor. "His. And tomorrow's, and the day after's, and every day for three months." He paused. "Auntie Lan Yue will be here at this hour every day and she won't be thinking about his hands. She'll be thinking about him."

The room was quiet for a moment, and the quiet had a shape to it.

"He's six," said Mang Tian.

"So am I."

"That's not what I meant and you know it's not what I meant."

"Yes," said Lan Shen.

Mang Tian set the box down on the bench beside the hammers. He looked at Wulin, who was being helped up off the floor by his mother and who was talking again already. Then he looked back at the small one, and he did something Lan Shen would think about for eleven years.

He reached out and pressed a stud on the side of the screen.

The number went away.

Zero.

"You want to know what it counts," said Mang Tian.

"I want to know what enough means."

"Enough."

"The screen only counts a hit if it's hard enough. So there's a line. Somebody drew the line." Lan Shen looked at the lump of metal. "Who's it hard enough for?"

Mang Tian was quiet for a while.

"Pick them up," he said.

---

They were not hollow.

That was the first thing, and it arrived in his wrists before it arrived in his head. Five kilograms each, a third of a metre of handle, a cylindrical head half a foot long and ten centimetres across, and the balance was forward of the grip, which meant the hammer wanted to keep going after you had decided it should stop.

*Right,* he thought. *Right. It's a pendulum with a grudge.*

He had been turning a staff for two years. Not a good staff. A length of ash from the yard behind the shop, dried over the stove, weighted with a bit of lead wire at one end because he had read somewhere that a weapon you cannot feel is a weapon you cannot keep. He turned it in the yard at night when he could not sleep, four hundred each way, and the reason he turned it was not that he expected to fight anybody.

The reason was that it had a callus on it, and the callus was on his hand.

He set his feet. He did not set them the way Wulin had set his, square on, because square on is how you take the rebound in the shoulders. He set them at an angle and let the right one carry it. He took the first swing slow, at about four parts in ten, to find where the thing wanted to go.

*Bang.*

One.

The shock came up the handle and stopped at his wrist, because he turned his wrist. That was all it was. You did not resist it. You let it through and you turned, and the turn took it into the elbow and the elbow took it into the shoulder and the shoulder took it into the floor.

Two.

Three.

"Higher," said Mang Tian.

He raised the hammer higher and it got worse immediately and he dropped back.

"It doesn't want to go higher," said Lan Shen. "It wants to go through."

Mang Tian did not answer that.

Four. Five. Six. He found the rhythm at about eleven, which was slower than he had wanted and much slower than the noise Wulin had made, and he stopped thinking about the number on the screen, because the moment he thought about the screen he hit it wrong.

At forty his shoulders warmed.

At eighty his hands changed. Not hurting — changing. The skin over the base of the fingers went from a thing he had to a thing he was being told about.

At a hundred and twenty the rebound started arriving late.

That was the thing. That was the whole thing. He knew it the second time it happened, and he knew that nobody in this room knew he knew it. The hammer came back a fraction behind the beat, because the muscles that were supposed to turn it were borrowing from the ones next to them. Borrowing was fine once and fine twice. After that it was a tear.

At a hundred and ninety he was counting out loud without meaning to.

At two hundred and six he put them down.

He put them down on the table, both of them, handles square — and stood there with his arms hanging and his heart going. His shirt at the small of his back was wet. The room was very loud without the banging in it.

The screen said two hundred and six.

He looked at it for a second and then he looked at the lump, and he understood the other number too — the one that had been on there when he walked in. One thousand and fifty. Wulin had gone on for a hundred and fifty more swings after the thousand, his mother had said so on the road, and only fifty of them had been hard enough to count.

*The screen doesn't care how many times you hit it,* thought Lan Shen. *It cares how many times you hit it properly.*

That was, he thought, the only honest machine he had met in either life.

"Who told you to stop," said Mang Tian.

"Nobody."

"Then pick them up."

Lan Shen looked at the hammers and opened his mouth. What came out in the first draft was: *the next fifty won't teach me anything, they'll just take the skin off, and I've got four years of skin I need for other things.*

He was six. He caught it in time and shoved it back down. The shoving showed, and he knew the shoving showed.

"I've got to carry the box home," he said.

Mang Tian looked at him.

Then he looked down at the two hammers lying square on the table with their handles parallel, which is not how a child puts down something heavy — then at the screen, then at the boy's hands. They were red at the base of the fingers and not torn, because a hundred and fifty more would have torn them and two hundred and six had not.

"Two hundred and six," said Mang Tian.

"Yes."

"In what?"

"I don't know. I wasn't counting the time."

"You were counting out loud."

"I was counting hits."

Something in the big face did a thing it had probably not done much. It was not a smile. It was the look of a man who has just been handed a tool he did not order and is deciding whether it is useful.

"Who taught you to hold that."

"Nobody."

"You've turned something."

"A stick."

"A stick." Mang Tian said it flat. "How long."

"Two years."

"Two years," said Mang Tian, "of a stick." He picked up one of the hammers and put it back on the rack on the wall, then picked up the other one and put it back. He did not look at Lan Shen while he did it. "Take your box. There's nothing here for you."

"No," said Lan Shen.

"No what."

"Nothing here for me," said Lan Shen. "Yes."

He picked up the box. He left the slip under the lid where his father had put it. His father had written a number on it, and his father had said take it if he offers less, and the man had offered nothing, which was inside the instructions.

At the doorway Mang Tian said, "What's your father's name."

"Lan He."

"Lan He," said Mang Tian. "Tell him the balm's all right. Tell him the ratio's wrong. Tell him to put a quarter more wax in it in the winter and it'll keep on a boy's hands."

"Yes."

"And don't come back with a box."

Lan Shen went out through the hall.

---

He had meant to walk straight through it.

He got as far as the second bench and then he stopped, because he had already seen the tray of nine rollers and he had not had the time to be frightened by it, and now he had the time.

There were components in this hall. That was what he remembered, in the sentence he had read in the other life and not particularly noticed: *most of these were likely components for soul machines.* He had read that the way you read a line about the weather. He had not known then what a soul machine was. He knew now, more or less. He had known for a while. He had never once put it next to the other thing he knew.

He walked down the hall slowly with an empty box under his arm.

There was a shaft on the third bench, turned, with a keyway cut in it, and beside the shaft there was a collar that was meant to go on it. He picked the collar up. It was heavy and it was warm and it did not go on. He tried it. It did not go on. He put it back, picked up a second collar from the same tray and that one went on loosely.

He stood there in a six-star blacksmith's hall in Glorybound City holding a piece of metal in each hand, and the whole of his previous life arrived at once.

They did not fit.

They were both made by the same man, in the same hall, for the same shaft, out of the same stock. They did not fit. Neither of them was wrong, and the man who made them was very good, and there was no way in this world for him to make two of anything the same twice.

Because metal is a living thing. Because a machine will never grasp the veins of the metal. Because every first-rate component on this continent is hammered out by a craftsman with a hammer in his hand and the veins in that piece of metal are the veins in that piece of metal and not the veins in the next one. So a man makes a thing, then he makes the thing that goes with it, and he fits them to each other — one at a time, by hand, for the rest of his life.

There were two doors. His brother had been told about them at a kitchen table by a man with a meagre wage, and Lan Shen had been told about them by a teacher on the first day of school. Rank ten is the door. Be a genius, be given a spirit soul free. Or be poor, and buy one.

And there was a third door. It was not a door at all — it was a hall with a broken crate in it and nine rollers in a tray of oil that were all slightly different from each other, and nobody who had ever walked through this building could see it, because everybody in it had been born here.

Lan Shen put the collars back in the tray.

He went out into the street, and the smell of metal went with him for two blocks.

---

Wulin came to the door at seven with his arms in a bowl of cold water.

"They made me," he said, by way of hello. "Mom made me. I told her it doesn't hurt."

"It doesn't hurt," said Lan Shen, from the step.

"You don't know."

"I can see your face."

Wulin's face said that it hurt. Wulin's face also said that he had never in his life been happier, and both of those things were visible at the same time from a metre away, in the light from the kitchen.

"I'm his disciple," said Wulin. "He said it to Mom's face. He said you raised a good one."

"I heard."

"You were there?"

"I brought balm."

Wulin stopped moving his arms in the bowl. He looked at Lan Shen with an expression that took a while to arrive.

"You knew," he said.

"I knew they'd take you on."

"You knew *today*?"

"I saw your mum at the gate."

Wulin thought about this for a while, in the way he thought about things, with his whole face. Then he shrugged, and winced, and did it again anyway.

"Did you see the thousand," he said.

"I saw the thousand and fifty."

"I couldn't do the second lot," said Wulin, cheerfully. "Uncle Mang Tian said don't stop unless he told me and I didn't stop and then I was going to fall over and he caught me. I'd have gone on. I would have."

"Did he say you were good at it."

"He said we raised a good one. To Mom." Wulin thought about it with his whole face. "He said it to Mom, not to me. But he caught me when I was going over, and he didn't tell me to stop until he had to, and you don't do that for somebody you think is rubbish."

"That's right."

"Isn't it." Wulin beamed. And then, because he could not hold anything in: "He told me what forging is. Do you want to hear it?"

"Tell me."

"Casting is a mold and then a machine grinds out the shape. That's casting. Forging is a man hammers it from start to finish, personally. And you *could* set a machine to pounding it, but—" he drew breath, and his voice went into a lower register that was an imitation of a beard, "—metal is a living thing." He let that sit on the bench between them. "A machine will never grasp the veins of it." Which was why, he said, every first-rate component on this continent had been hammered out by a blacksmith rather than poured into a mold, and why a good blacksmith was a true craftsman, and why a true craftsman held a status no less than a Spirit Master's.

He came out of the imitation and looked pleased with himself.

"Soul Master and Machine Master," said Lan Shen.

"Yeah, he said every young boy dreams that one—" Wulin stopped. "How did you know it was two?"

Lan Shen looked at the door frame.

"Everybody says two," he said.

---

Wulin stayed for the ginger thing and went home at nine, and Na'er had smiled.

That was the news. That was the whole of the second half of the evening, delivered while he ate, with his arms in a bowl on the table beside his rice.

"I said this is your home now, and my mum and dad are your mum and dad, and you're my little sister. And she smiled. First time. She's never smiled." He had a mouthful. "And she called me Big Brother Lin. Not Wulin. Lin."

"Everybody calls you Lin."

"She didn't know that. She hasn't known us a day."

Su Wan put more ginger on the table and sat down. Wulin told her the whole of it again from the top. She said "ah-yo" twice and "look at your hands" once, and Lan Shen sat with his bowl and watched his brother be delighted.

At nine he walked him back. Two doors. Wulin's arms were out of the water by then and he could lift them to about shoulder height and he did it every third sentence to demonstrate.

The light was on in the Tang house, all of it, both rooms.

Lan Shen got into his own bed early and lay against the wall for a while, because that was the habit now, and because there were two adults in that house who had shut a door and gone quiet at dinner.

He got nothing.

He got a murmur. He got the shape of a voice going up and coming down. Then a long stretch where nobody said anything at all. Then a chair. Then the front of the house, where the parents' room was, on the other side from his own. The wall in the back room carried everything. The wall in the front room carried nothing, because there was a chimney in it and a cupboard against it, and he had never once thought about chimneys before.

Whatever they were saying in there, they were saying it quietly. They were saying it because of the boy with the swollen hands. It was not for him.

He lay there and found that he did not mind much. Then he found that he minded a great deal. Then he got up and got the book.

---

He wrote the numbers, because the numbers were the part of the day that was his.

*Five kilograms each. Handles a third of a metre, heads half a foot, ten across, balance forward of the grip. Wulin: one thousand and fifty, about half an hour, then a hundred and fifty more, then he was caught. Mine: two hundred and six. I stopped. Rebound arrives late at about a hundred and ninety and late means borrowing and borrowing means tearing.*

*He said nothing here for me. He asked my father's name. He said the ratio's wrong, a quarter more wax in winter. That is a man telling me how to come back without inviting me.*

He looked at that for a while. Then he looked at the shelf. Then he wrote one more line, which was the line he had been avoiding all evening.

*Mang Tian is six stars. I don't know what six stars is worth, but a man who sets his own screen and can't be cheated by a six-year-old is a man who has calibrated a machine, and there is a screen in that hall and there are nine rollers in a tray of oil and none of them are the same as each other and nobody in that building knows that this is a problem.*

He stopped there.

He had meant to write the next part. The next part was two words long and it was the truest thing he had thought all year. He had it in his mouth. He put the pen down on the shelf and did not write it.

Because the numbers were a boy's numbers. A boy counts hits. A boy notices a collar that doesn't fit and says so at dinner and gets told to eat his rice.

The next part was not a boy's thing. The next part was a man who had spent a life in a world where every part of every machine fitted every other part of every machine and had never once thought about how strange that was, until he walked into a hall in Glorybound City at six years old and picked up two collars out of the same tray.

If that went in the book, the book was not a book about weeds and jars and hits any more.

He shut it. He put it on the shelf, square to the edge, second row, where his mother could reach it.

He got into bed and lay on his back with his hands aching at the base of the fingers. Behind his eyes there was a hall full of half-finished things, a tray of nine rollers, a shaft with a keyway in it, and a tolerance he could have written down in the dark.

He did not write it down.

That was the first time.

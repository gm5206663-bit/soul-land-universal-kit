# Chapter Fifteen: Ten Minutes

```
◆ STATUS — Chapter 15: years 3–3
KIND      : the girl who was nothing on paper, holding a house together while the room
            celebrates the boy — and a sea ten minutes from home, with a question in it.
AGE       : the boys nine. Na'er eight, very nearly. Two nights, one dawn, late autumn
            of the third year.
CLASS     : the list still says two. The neighbor's boy is ten, official tomorrow.
THREAT    : nobody, on purpose. The pressure is time, and one question.
BOND      : Na'er and Wulin — the promise. Lang Yue — the street-call. Lan Shen — the
            door frame, nearer, with nobody told.
LAW       : some things a person got to keep without counting them.
DIALOGUE  : standard. Her sections are hush; the school day carries the talk.
TURN      : the street celebrates the door that was crossed. The girl without one carries
            seven somethings home from a dream, and asks the only future-question she has.
```

*The lamp in the front room stayed on late that night. The money was on the table. Nobody in that house could sleep while money sat on a table.*

*Her mother counted it once, fast. Then she counted it again, slowly. Her mouth moved on the numbers.*

*Na'er watched from the doorway of the back room. She was supposed to be asleep. The curtain between the rooms was not pulled all the way. She had arranged that an hour ago, while yawning.*

*"Put it away," her father said. "Before he comes back in and starts taking it apart to look at it."*

*"He has already counted it," her mother said. "Thirty thousand and two hundred. He told the street."*

*She said it quietly. Then she wrapped the money in its cloth, stood on her toes, and put it on the high shelf behind the pickle jar. That was where money lived in that house. For one night, anyway.*

*The doll sat on the low shelf, where Na'er could reach her.*

*Violet eyes. Silver hair, tied in the little tail he had tied. The hair had come yellow. He had dyed it silver with dye from the workshop, because he said silver suited her better. It did. He had bought her with the very first money the workshop ever paid him, and made a face while paying, like a man spending a fortune. It was everything he had, so the face was partly true.*

*She remembered which week it was. Cabbage week. Supper had been cabbage four days running, and he came home with the doll under his jacket like a smuggler, and made her swear secrecy with her thumbs.*

*The doll had a name her mother did not know. The name was a secret between the two of them. It was the name Na'er had chosen for a daughter, if she ever had one. She had told him in a whisper. He had nodded very seriously and made her say it twice, so he would remember it properly.*

*Twenty-nine names. That was the count on the washhouse list, on Thursdays. She knew every name. She knew which houses you knocked twice at, and which houses you must never knock at, only wait. She was good at lists. Nobody had ever told her she was good at lists. She simply was.*

*She got into her bed, the bench by the wall with the quilt folded on it. She pulled the curtain and listened.*

*Her brother was not meditating. She could tell, because he kept turning. A person meditating made no sound at all. He made the sound of a boy with money in the house. He turned, and turned again, and breathed out hard through his nose.*

*After a long time she heard him get up carefully. He knew his parents' sleeping sounds too well to make any noise the dark could not hold.*

*The door opened and closed, softer than a mouse.*

*She sat up. Through the gap in the curtain she could see the window. It was grey, with no sun in the grey yet.*

*He went out at night sometimes. To look at things, or to think. It was not her business. She lay back down and closed her eyes.*

*Something felt wrong. Not wrong, exactly. It felt like a cup on a tilted table. Held, from underneath, by something she could not name.*

---

He watched the lamplight go out in the Tang house from his own window, across the two gardens and the fence between.

A door had been crossed on this street tonight. A door people crossed once in a whole life, mostly, and some people never. It was the door he had been going toward for a long time. The crossing had happened two doors down, in the dark, probably in a garden, by a boy who had been built for it.

He was glad. He checked the gladness the way he checked a coin, by biting it. It was real.

He did his own arithmetic, once. It took no time. He had done that sum so often the steps were worn smooth. Then he was finished with numbers for the night.

He went to the shelf and stood in front of it without taking anything down. The book was there, sewn, the pages even, the spine holding. He had stopped reading it back weeks ago. He put his hand flat on the boards beside it and did not touch it. He stood like that for a while. It was as close as he got to a prayer.

He was nearer the top of the door frame than he had been. He did not know how much nearer. He had thrown away the number, and the throwing-away had left him room to climb without watching. A watched pot is slow. An unwatched field grows. When he crossed it, the room would know before he did.

The lamp across the way went out. He slept.

---

*The dream was wide.*

*That was the only word she would ever have for it. It was wide the way the sea was wide. It went on in every direction. It was warm.*

*There were seven somethings in it. She never knew what they were, afterward. She only knew there were seven. The way you know there were people at a table after the people have gone, by the chairs.*

*They were around her, and they were not hurting her. She was not frightened. That was the strange thing. She was frightened of the back room at the washhouse after dark, and of dogs, and of the wind in the wrong season. She was not frightened of this, and it was bigger than all of those.*

*There was a warmth in her forehead, between her eyes, like a hand pressed there. It did not burn. It sank into her slowly.*

*Something in her had been clenched for a long time. She had never known it was clenched. In the dream it let go, all at once. She had been carrying something heavy for so long she had forgotten the arm was hers. Somebody came and took it from her. It was not a dream of something taken. It was a dream where it had never existed.*

*She did not usually remember dreams. The other girls at the washhouse told theirs at the trough, long tales. Hers were always gone by the time her eyes opened.*

*This one stayed.*

*Afterward, everything was calmer. She was gladder than she had been, and for no reason at all.*

*She came up out of the dream slowly. The window was brighter now. The curtain hung still. His mat was empty.*

*"Lin Lin," somebody called, out in the street. "Lin Lin."*

*The voice was tearing at the edges.*

---

He did not hear the street-call himself. He heard about it at breakfast, from his mother, who had heard it at the door. Mothers in that street heard everything first and forgave it second.

"The Tang boy was out in the garden all night," she said, setting the bowl down. "Out in the dew. She went up and down the street calling him at dawn, Lin Lin, Lin Lin. They found him sitting in the wet grass like a kettle left off the boil. Says he went out to look at the moon and the night took him. In this cold."

She said it fully, with pleasure.

"They've taken him off to the school already, him and his father. There'll be something in it, mark me. A boy doesn't sit in the dew all night for nothing coming of it."

"Eat," his mother said. "You are staring through the bowl."

"I am eating," he said.

He chewed. He remembered, from the other life, how weather worked in books. A night full of dew happened on purpose, in chapters. It meant the boy in it had stopped being what he was. Out here there were no chapters. Out here it meant a mother's voice tearing, and a wet boy, and a kettle off the boil.

It still meant what it meant.

He went to school with the feeling of a man walking toward an answer he had already read the back of.

---

*They brought him home first, before the school. Nobody said that was so she could see him. It was.*

*He came in the door wet to the knees, with grass on his cuffs. Something had happened to his skin. It had a shine under it, like river stones under an inch of water. His eyes were too awake for a boy who had not slept.*

*Her mother took his face in both hands and bent it down to look at it. She was using the cross voice, the one that meant love and fear in equal weight.*

*Your safety first, do you hear me. Before everything. Before any door of any kind.*

*"I know, Mom," he kept saying. "I know, Mom."*

*He was grinning, the idiot. Ear to ear.*

*"You eat," her mother said at last, and let his face go. "Both of you. Then you go to that school, you find out what you are, and you come home on time for once in your life."*

*Breakfast was radish and rice, and the eggs her mother had said were too dear and then bought anyway. Na'er sat in her place and found she was starving, the washhouse-in-winter kind of hungry. Bottomlessly. She finished her bowl. Her mother filled it again without being asked. She finished that too.*

*Her brother told the dew story with his mouth full. The grass had been so loud he could not sit still, he said. The whole garden seemed to lean toward him. Nobody told him to chew first. That told her, more than anything, what kind of morning it was.*

*"Your eyes are bright," her mother said. To her, not to him. She reached over and pushed the hair back off Na'er's forehead. She left her hand there a moment. Testing.*

*"Congratulations, big brother," Na'er said, with her mouth full. Somebody had to say a normal thing or the whole kitchen was going to float off.*

*He grinned wider. "Wait until tomorrow," he said.*

*Tomorrow was the test. Her father said it into his bowl, not looking up, in the flat voice of a man stating the time. The school had its own measuring. The school would say the number.*

*Then he set his chopsticks down flat.*

*He had the day after off from the workshop. He had asked at month's turn and been granted. They would go to the Pagoda first thing, the two of them, before the price moved or the boy talked himself into fusing with a broom out of stubbornness.*

*"Can we all go," Na'er said.*

*"Your mother's house can't spare her," her father said. Gently. "She asked. The dye vats don't have a holiday in them. So it will be me and this one. You will hold the house."*

*He said hold the house like a title, not a chore. She sat up straighter under it.*

*"I'll cook," she said, grandly.*

*The three of them laughed. Her mother said something terrible would be for dinner, then. The kitchen was warm. The streak of sun moved across the table.*

*If there was anything strange in Na'er, it sat underneath the warm like a stone under a river. Felt. Not seen.*

---

The school knew before the school was told.

That was how it went with fifteen children and a winter of watching one column of numbers. They came through the gate two abreast, and there was Wulin walking like a boy with a live coal in his chest, coughing to hide the glow. By second bell every child in the room knew the shape of it.

Bao had simply gone up and asked him. Wulin had been saving it for the teacher. It fell out of him anyway, in the yard. Near ten, measured twice at home. The grown-ups' measure today. The Pagoda tomorrow.

Bao found him at the rail.

"Did you hear," Bao said. "The grass boy crossed."

"I heard," Lan Shen said.

"Of course you heard. You hear everything and say nothing about any of it."

Lin Ximeng took him out after the register. No ceremony. He put his head back in the door and said, "Wulin," and jerked the head. The class made the sound a class makes.

Lan Shen opened his sums. He worked three of them before the boy came back. He did not once look at the door. That was a discipline he had, and nobody credited him for it.

Wulin came back at mid-morning with his sleeves pushed up. The class boiled quietly until the break. Then the boil broke, and Lin Ximeng, against every habit he had, let it.

"Tell them," he said from the desk, without looking up, "or nobody works before dinner."

"Rank ten," Wulin said, standing at his place. His face was red. "The school's measure says ten."

He stopped. Then he made himself say it properly. Lan Shen noticed that. Making yourself say it properly was a thing you did when a grown man had told you the weight of it.

"Teacher says the door's crossed. Two crossed in the spring, and four more since. Two in the summer, two in the autumn. That makes me—"

He counted on his fingers, shamelessly, in front of everyone. A couple of them laughed.

"Seventh," he said, and grinned. "I'm the seventh."

Seventh. The word went around the yard at dinner and everybody took a piece of it. Two in the spring, that was Bao, and Jiang with the long arms. They had strutted for weeks. Four more over the warm months. And now the boy who hammered iron half his life and ran home down the street every dusk. Seventh of fifteen. Ahead of eight boys and girls who had woken up this morning exactly what they were last night.

Lan Shen remembered the spring crossing, how it had sat in the room. Warming everybody a little. Warming nobody enough.

Rice. Cold wind off the yard wall. Somebody's soup cooling while the word got passed around.

He took no part in it. That was his custom. Nobody noticed. That was also his custom.

At the afternoon register, Lin Ximeng put his chalk down and addressed the room, because the working half of the day had plainly died in the yard.

"I will tell you what I told him," he said, "so those of you still climbing know the shape of what is up there. The association pays a stipend to every soul master who makes it official. Who has crossed, and bought a soul, and fused a ring. A thousand, a month. More than some men make with their hands. His family will not want for rice because he swung a hammer instead of playing."

He said it flat, like a man reading a price board. If a teacher said it warmly, eight more of them would climb down from trees with broken arms by spring. He knew it. Somehow, they knew it too.

Lan Shen wrote nothing down. A thousand a month went into the place where he kept numbers he could not use yet. That drawer was getting deep.

He remembered the fee the auntie two lanes over had once paid at a door, years back. It had turned a whole household silent for a week.

He crossed it, he thought. It crosses. Doors are crossed.

It was a fact, not a comfort. The day had simply handed it to him.

At the leaving bell, Wulin came by his desk. That was new. He leaned a hand on the desk and said, not bragging, plainly:

"You will too. I've watched you. You go at things like the anvil's underneath them."

For once, there was time to answer.

"Count on it," Lan Shen said.

Wulin grinned at that, wide and sudden. Then he was gone, out into the street, running. There was hammering owed tonight, no matter what the number said.

---

His mother had a name for days like this one. Kettle days. Days when something on the street came to the boil and everybody's lid rattled.

She served the soup and told the news properly, with the certainty of three other women's afternoon behind it. The boy out all night. The mother calling him at first light. The father running him to the school before his rice. The number. The teacher's blessing.

The thousand a month.

His father looked up over his bowl at that. A thousand a month was a number that reached across a table and took hold of any man eating soup.

"And Tang's taken tomorrow off, to buy the boy his soul at the Pagoda," she said. "First thing. They've had that money in the house since last night, if you can call what the whole street knows a secret."

She served herself last. She always did.

"You will cross too, one day," she said. "You do things."

"Maybe," Lan Shen said.

His father looked up at maybe, mild as milk, and went back to his bowl.

"Three years of swinging that hammer, and out it pops. That's what I say. It's the hammering. Boys who sit never cross. Boys who do things cross."

"He does his letters too," his father said mildly. He said that about everything. To him, letters were water and air.

Lan Shen ate. The jar stood on the shelf, two copper heavier than last week. Sixty-six. One copper for a letter written, two if it was read back. The cold had not stopped the letters.

He remembered his father teaching him the strokes of his own name at this table. Years back. A warm hand on his hand. Strokes explained like laws, because to his father strokes were laws.

He thought about the class and the count of it. Two crossed in the spring. Six ahead now. The seventh seat filled. Eight seats still empty.

Eight. That was the number he carried out of the day. He set it in the drawer beside the thousand.

He did not say why. There was nobody to say it to. Saying it to himself would have been summing water.

The door frame in the dark hall stood nearer than it had been.

Patient as an anvil.

---

*Her father said yes before she finished asking.*

*He said it into his rice. A done thing.*

*"The beach," he said. "After we eat. It's ten minutes."*

*Her mother sighed and began untying her hair for bed. Mothers did not come to beaches after kettle days. Her brother washed the bowls fast, so it would count as after dinner sooner.*

*The street going seaward was a different street from the morning one. The awnings were folded. The lamps came on one by one as they passed. The air had the taste the whole town had, faintly, always. Salt. October cold. His hand around hers, still warm from the hammering. She could feel the work of the day humming in his palm.*

*She counted without meaning to. She always counted. The lamps. The corners.*

*Ten minutes. Her father had been right. He was always right about minutes.*

*The street stopped being a street. It widened, and opened, and fell away into the shore.*

*Gravel and shells. That was the beach. Grey stones, and shells in drifts the tide had combed. The stones made a small sound under their feet.*

*The moon was low and big and white as a plate of rice. There were more stars than the town had lamps. The sea went black going out, and shone coming in. Black and shine, black and shine. It said its one word over and over. It was not a word in any language, but she almost understood it.*

*It felt familiar.*

*She did not say so. She had never been here before, in all her years. That was all she thought about it. Some things you did not have to explain to your own feet.*

*"You've grown," he said suddenly. "You come up past my shoulder now. Nearly to my nose."*

*He held his palm flat above his own nose and measured her against it. Then he frowned at the small space left.*

*"Stop it," he said.*

*"I can't stop it," she said. Delighted. "It isn't mine to stop."*

*They walked. The shells said their small sounds. The fish smoke had gone out of the air up here. Nothing but salt, and cold, and the big white plate of the moon, and the two of them moving along the shore.*

*He picked up a stone. He did not skip it. The water was wrong for skipping, too big, too patient. He just held it, turning it in his hand.*

*She had the question the way you have a stone in your pocket.*

*She had been carrying it since the morning. Since before the morning. Since the wide warm dream with the seven chairs in it.*

*She stopped walking. He went two steps on, and came back. He always came back.*

*"Brother," she said. "If I had to go somewhere. If one day somebody came, and I went somewhere. A long way. Would you come looking."*

*"What," he said.*

*"Would you come looking for me."*

*He looked at her for a moment. It was the look he used at the workshop when a bar came out wrong. Older than the rest of him.*

*Then he put the stone in his pocket.*

*"I would look everywhere," he said. "There is no far. You could be behind the sea and I would learn to swim it."*

*He said it lightly. That was how she knew it was iron. Big words said heavily were talk. Big words said lightly, by him, were sworn.*

*"And I'm going to look after you your whole life," he said, even lighter. "So nobody's taking you anywhere. All right."*

*"All right," she said.*

*The dream had had seven chairs. The morning had had bright eyes. And now she had the answer, and the answer was a coin. She put it where she kept the important money. Not in any pocket, where mothers find things. Deeper. In the place where the doll's name lived.*

*One. Of those, she would always have one.*

*They went back along the shore. The sea went on saying its one word behind them, black and shine, black and shine, until the street took the sound at the corner. Then it was gone. The lamps were going out one by one.*

*She did not count the way home.*

*Some things a person got to keep without counting them.*

The second stick had three marks on one face and the same three on the other, and no numbers, because when he had made it he had not known what the numbers were.

He knew now.

He put it on the bench in the back room on the forty-second day, in the morning, while his father was weighing out bloodroot, and his father looked at it and then at him.

"What's that."

"Three," said Lan Shen.

"Three what."

"Three batches."

His father put the scales down. He picked the stick up and turned it over, and he looked at the three marks on both faces the way he had looked at the first stick, which was as though it were a sentence in a language he had not been told about.

"The balm's right," he said.

"I know."

"You checked it yourself. You saw me check it."

"I know."

"Then what are three batches for."

Lan Shen thought about how much of the answer a six-year-old could have, and about how much of it he actually had.

"Because it's right and it isn't enough," he said. "You said that. Mrs Guo's hands are bad and the balm is right and both of those are true, and nobody's done anything about the second one."

His father looked at him for a while.

"That's your sister's friend's hands," he said.

"It's everybody's hands. It's cold."

"It's always cold in the winter."

"Then it's always bad in the winter."

His father put the stick back on the bench. He did not put it in the crock with the other one. He left it lying on the bench, which Lan Shen noticed. Then Lan Shen noticed himself noticing, and stopped.

"Wax, comfrey, and what," his father said.

"I don't know yet. That's what the three are for."

His father got down the jar of the winter one and the jar of the old one and a third jar from under the bench that had no label on it, and he put all three on the shelf next to the eleven.

"Three," he said. "You've got a month of evenings and you've got my knife and you've got the pot. If it costs more than the old one it doesn't go in a jar, it goes in the fire."

"All right."

"And write down what you do."

"I write down what I do."

"I know you do," said his father. "That's the only reason I'm letting you."

---

The three batches took most of a month.

The first was the winter one with more wax, because more wax meant it stayed on the hand longer. It did stay on longer. It also came off on everything else, and Lan Yue said it made the washing slippery, which defeated the purpose.

The second was the winter one with tallow in it instead of half the wax, because tallow was cheaper and softer. It went soft in the pot and stayed soft on the hand, and it smelled.

His father came over and put his nose near it.

"That's going in the fire."

"It hasn't set yet."

"It's going in the fire when it's set," said his father, "or now, whichever you prefer. It'll smell the same either way and the whole street will know about it."

"It's cheaper."

"Cheaper isn't a smell," said his father. "You can sell dear and you can sell cheap and you cannot sell stinking. That's the whole of the trade, and I've just given it to you for nothing."

The third was the winter one with less wax and more oil and a handful of something his father had in a paper twist on the top shelf that he would not name. It was thin. It went on thin, and it did not come off on the washing.

Lan Shen made them in the evenings after the wall and the floor and the counting. He wrote every weight down. He used the first stick for the wax and the second stick for nothing at all yet, because he had three marks and three batches and no idea which was which until the end.

At the end he put a number on each of the three marks.

One was a failure. Two was a failure. Three was the thin one.

He cut the three in deeper with the nail.

---

He could not test it on himself.

He understood that on about the fourth evening, standing at the bench with his hands in a pot, and it was the sort of thing he would have said out loud in the other life if there had been anybody in the room to say it to. His hands were six years old and they had never been in cold water for five hours and they were not going to be.

He needed hands.

Lan Yue brought them home on a Wednesday, which was her whole week, and which she had evidently spent telling people what the evening was for.

Six of them came into the yard. They stood in the yard the way people stand in somebody else's yard. One of them was Mrs Guo. All six of them had their hands out in front of them already, because Lan Yue had evidently told them what the evening was for.

"Yours is the thin one," Lan Yue said to her brother.

"How do you know."

"Because I asked which one you liked and you said the thin one and I said why and you said because it doesn't come off."

"That's not—"

"They wash," said Lan Yue. "It comes off. That's the whole thing."

He gave them each a jar with no slip on it and a number scratched into the wax under the lid. He wrote the six numbers in the book, and he wrote what their hands looked like, and he asked each of them how long they stood in water. One of them laughed at that and said all day love. Mrs Guo did not laugh.

They came back on the ninth day after that.

"It's better," said the first one. "It doesn't come off in the water. I've had it on since Tuesday and I've washed four times and it's still on."

"Mm," said the second.

"That's a yes or a no."

"It's a yes. It's just not a yes like hers."

Mrs Guo held her hands out. They were worse than they had been in the autumn, and they were going to be worse again in a month, and everybody in the yard could see it. Nobody said so.

"It goes on thin," she said.

"Yes."

"Thin's better," said Mrs Guo. "I'm not saying it's fixed. I'm saying it goes on thin, and it stays on, and my hands are still bad, and I don't know which of those is your fault."

"That's fair," said Lan Shen.

She looked at him. She had not been agreed with like that by a six-year-old before, and she did not immediately have a view on it.

One of the others said it was the same as the old one. One said it was worse and did not know why, and she might have been right, because her hands had been in a different water that week, at a different house, that had lye in it.

Lan Shen wrote all six down.

He sat at the table afterwards with the book open at nine numbers and six hands and four yeses, and he thought about the fact that he could not tell whether the thin one was better or whether it had been a warm nine days.

He wrote: *four say better. One says same. One says worse. It was a warm week. I do not know.*

Then he wrote underneath: *that is the same sentence I wrote about the street. Everything I have is the same sentence.*

---

The first holiday was on the fifty-first day.

His mother came with him. She stood by the mound with her arms folded, in the place where she could see him and where she could also see the gap in the wall. There were about twenty people in the garden, which was fewer than before, and the grass was still up but browner at the top.

He counted.

It took him most of an afternoon and he did it in sections, because the bank was forty paces long in his father's stride and he could not hold the whole of it at once in his head, and because he had learned what happened when he tried to hold a thing in his head.

Thirty-one thousand.

He wrote it down at the bench, in the book, with the date, and he put the book in his bag.

His mother came over when the light started going.

"What are you counting."

"Steps."

"How many steps have you done."

"A lot."

She looked at him. He looked at the bank.

"All right," she said.

They walked home. She said nothing else about it and neither did he. That was how it went for the next four months.

---

The second holiday was on the eighty-first day.

Nineteen thousand.

The grass was down to about knee height in most places. The top of the bank was bare dirt where the children had been sliding. The mound had a hole in it that had not been there before, and the bench had a new slat on it, the wrong colour, nailed over the gap, so that the nail head was under the new wood and the man could sleep on it again.

Lan Shen sat on the bench and looked at the new slat for a while.

He had used the nail head. He had used the gap. He had measured thirty-one paces from the right-hand end of a bench with a broken slat, and the broken slat had been fixed.

He counted anyway. He got nineteen thousand and he wrote it down and he wrote *the bench has a new slat* underneath it, because that was the sort of thing that mattered later and never seemed to at the time.

The strand — if there was a strand — was thirty-one paces from the bench in his father's stride, in the middle third, closer to the mound than the tree, left of the line to the mound.

He went and stood there.

He could not feel anything at all, because there was no grass there any more. There was dirt and there was a bit of stubble and there was a place where somebody had lit a small fire and put it out.

He stood in it for about a minute with his hands at his sides.

Then he went back to the bench and sat down on the new slat and wrote: *I cannot find it in the winter because there is nothing to find it in. That is not the same as it not being there.*

He looked at that for a while.

Then he wrote: *but it might be.*

---

The academy sent a boy with a note in the second week of the cold.

The note was on paper with the academy's mark on it, which Lan He turned over twice as though the mark might be a mistake. It said that the bursar's office required four dozen jars of the medicinal balm, that the price was to be the same as the spring price, and that each jar was to be marked with the batch and the measure it had been made against.

His father read it three times.

"The measure it's been made against," he said.

"That's the stick."

"I can see that's the stick."

He stood in the back room with the note in his hand. He looked at the shelf, and at the crock, and at the two sticks, and at his son.

"They want the number on the jar," he said.

"Yes."

"They didn't want the number on the jar in the spring."

"No."

"Zhao's been up there with a piece of wood," said his father, "and now they want the number on the jar."

He put the note down on the bench. He picked it up again.

"Who taught you that," he said.

It was the same question Zhao had asked, in the same words, and it landed the same way. Lan Shen stood very still by the shelf and waited.

"You did," he said.

"I did not."

"You said it's right and it isn't enough," said Lan Shen. "You said it in the back room with the jar in your hand, and then you put it down and you didn't do anything, and now they've sent a note."

His father looked at the paper.

"That's not teaching."

"It's the same thing."

"It isn't the same thing at all," said his father, "and if you say it in front of anybody I'll tell them you're six."

He looked at the note again.

"Four dozen," he said.

"You'll need more wax."

"I'll need a great deal more wax." His father looked at him. "You understand what this is."

Lan Shen looked at the note.

"It's four dozen jars."

"It's four dozen jars with the measure written on them," said his father, "which means that when one of them's wrong they'll know which one, which week, which pot. That's not four dozen jars. That's four dozen chances to be told you're a liar."

"That's what it's for."

His father opened his mouth. He shut it. He picked up the note and went into the front room to find a piece of paper to answer it on. Lan Shen stood in the back room and looked at the crock with the two sticks in it, and thought about a stick in an academy storeroom with somebody's name cut into the end.

He thought: *it took one clipboard about two months.*

He thought: *I did not do any of it. I stood in a doorway with a broom.*

---

The third holiday was on the hundred and eleventh day and there were four people in the garden.

Two thousand.

The grass was flat and yellow and about a hand's height. The tree at the top of the slope had nothing on it. The mound had two holes in it now, and the bench was wet.

He counted it from the bench in about ten minutes because there was so little to count, and then he sat on the wet bench and did not get up for a while.

His mother stood at the gap in the wall with her arms folded and let him sit.

On the way home she said, "It's getting thin."

"Yes."

"Whatever you're counting."

He looked at her. She was looking straight ahead, at the Tan place, at the corner, at nothing in particular.

"It's getting thin," he said.

"In the garden."

"Everywhere."

She nodded, in the way she nodded when he said something she had decided not to chase.

"That's winter," she said.

---

He counted the corner every morning for the whole of it.

Three for the first five weeks of it, one of them a stump. Then two, because the stump went. Going meant that the bit above the dirt had rotted off, and rotting off is not dying, but he could not tell from above the dirt.

Two for six weeks.

And the sense said three.

He wrote it down every morning. He wrote *eyes* and he wrote *sense* and he put a line between them, and for six weeks the line had the same thing on both sides of it except that the sense was always one higher.

Always. Not usually. Always.

He noticed that on about the fortieth morning of counting. He sat down on the floor of his bedroom with the book on his knees and read the six weeks back, and his hands went a bit cold, and not from the weather.

A thing that is always wrong by one is not wrong.

It is one out. One out in the same direction every time. And a number that is one out in the same direction every time can be corrected, and correcting it means you know what the one is.

He wrote: *it is not an error. It is an offset. It is always one. Either there is always one thing under the dirt that I cannot see, or I always count one too many, and if it is the second one then I can subtract it and be right for the first time in my life.*

He looked at that.

He wrote: *if it is the first one then I have been reading something buried for six weeks and I do not know what it is.*

He shut the book.

He did not dig. He thought about digging most days for about a month, and he did not. The yard was where his mother hung the washing and where his father put the crates. A six-year-old digging a hole in the angle of a wall in the middle of the cold is a thing that gets asked about. And he had already worked out that whichever answer was in the hole, the hole would not put it back.

In the deepest part of the cold the corner went to one, and the sense said two.

Two weeks after that the corner went to nothing at all above the dirt, and the sense said one.

He sat on the floor with his hand flat on the boards and felt one thing in a hand's width of frozen mud where there had been three in the autumn. It was the same one. It was still one more than there was.

He wrote: *eyes zero. Sense one.*

He wrote: *it is under the dirt and it is still alive and it is the only thing in this city I can feel.*

---

The fourth holiday was on the hundred and forty-first day and he did not go to the garden.

He could have. His mother would have walked him. There was a bench with a new slat on it, and a bank with nothing on it, and a mound with two holes in it. There was no grass at all. He knew without going that he would have stood on the bank in the cold for an afternoon and felt nothing whatever, and written down a number that was zero.

He said he did not want to go.

His mother said all right. She said it in the voice she used when she was not going to chase it. She put her hand on the top of his head for about a second and went out to the market on her own.

He stayed in the yard.

He sat down on the cold ground with his back against the wall and his hand flat in the angle. He felt the one thing. He sat there for about an hour.

It was not much. It was one thing in a hand's width of mud in a city of forty thousand paved streets. It was under the dirt. It had been under the dirt the whole time, and everything he had counted for four months had been counting the part of the world that stuck up.

He thought: *the street was forty because forty things were sticking up.*

He thought: *the garden was forty thousand because forty thousand things were sticking up.*

He thought: *and what is under them.*

He had asked himself that once before, in a yard, at night, about four seconds of a straight line, and the answer then had been that he did not know.

The answer now was that he had an instrument which had been telling him for six weeks, and he had been reading it as an error.

He got up. He went inside. He got the book down off the shelf, second row, square to the edge, where his mother could reach it, and he turned to the page with six weeks of two columns on it and he drew a line under the last one.

He wrote: *it is not one out. It is one more. It has been one more the whole time. I have been counting leaves.*

He sat with that for a while in the front room with the cold coming in under the door.

---

He did not go to the garden for three holidays after that.

The fifth one it rained. The sixth one his father needed him at the shop, because four dozen jars with the measure written on them was four dozen jars with the measure written on them. The wax had come up in price again and somebody had to sit and do the arithmetic on every one. The seventh one he was ill for two days with something that went through the whole street, and on the third day he was not ill any more but he was still not going anywhere.

He counted the corner every morning regardless. One, and one, and one. Nothing above the dirt and one under it.

The balm went to the academy in four crates on a handcart in the coldest week of the year. Zhao signed for it on the step. Every slip had a batch number and a measure.

"Did they pay," said Lan Yue, at supper.

"They'll pay."

"When."

"Thirty days," said his father. "Which is what the academy pays everybody, and which is why half the district won't supply them."

"Are we half the district."

His father looked at her over the top of the slips.

"No," he said. "We're the ones with a stick."

Nobody said anything else about it at home, except his mother, who said four dozen once at supper, and then said it again to Mrs Tan over the wall on the Saturday.

School went on. He learned the names of the provinces, which were seven. He wrote them down. He did not need to, because he already knew them from the other life, and they were not the same seven.

Lin Ximeng walked the rows every day and stopped at his desk every day for exactly as long as he stopped at any desk.

He said nothing. For six months he said nothing.

---

The corner came back in the warm.

It was not a morning he could have pointed to. He went out in the second week of the thaw and there were two green things in the angle where there had been none. The sense said three. He wrote it down and went to school.

Four days later there were four green things and the sense said five.

A week after that there were seven and the sense said eight.

He sat on the floor of his bedroom with the book open and looked at the two columns. He understood that the offset had not gone away with the cold, and had not come back with the warm, and had not changed once in five months and a half of being wrong in the same direction.

He could have dug. It was soft now. It would have taken about ten minutes. His mother would have asked him what he was doing and he could have said worms, and worms was a true reason to be digging in the angle of a wall in the thaw.

He did not.

He thought about it for most of a week.

Then he wrote: *if I dig it up I will see it and I will not know what it is, and it will be dead. I will have spent a thing I cannot get back to answer a question I can answer later by waiting. The offset is one. It has been one for five months. It is not going to stop being one.*

He wrote: *so I will subtract it.*

He wrote: *and now I have a number I can trust, and I do not know what it counts.*

---

He went back to the garden on the eighth holiday, in the warm, with his mother.

There were eleven people in it. The bank was green to about a hand's height. The mound had four holes in it and the new slat on the bench had gone grey.

He stood at the right-hand end of the bench and faced the bank and counted.

Thirty-eight thousand.

He wrote it down with the date. He wrote *the slat has gone grey* under it, because that was the sort of thing that mattered later.

Then he walked thirty-one paces in his father's stride and stood in the middle third, closer to the mound than the tree, left of the line to the mound, in grass that was a hand high and growing.

There was nothing there.

There was grass. There were thirty-eight thousand things on that bank and he could feel every one of them, and not one of them was pulling at him in a straight line.

He stood in it for about a quarter of an hour. Then he walked it in a line, and then in another line, and then in a third. The grass came up to his hands. There was nothing in any of it. The tree was still left of the mound. The mound was still at the south end. The bench was still thirty-one paces behind him in his father's stride.

He went back and sat down on the bench.

His mother came over and stood in front of him with her arms folded.

"Steps," she said.

"Yes."

"How many."

He thought about thirty-eight thousand. He thought about four seconds. He thought about a straight line, and about five months and a half of subtracting one from a number to make it true.

"A lot," he said.

"More than last time."

"More than last time."

She looked at the bank. There was nothing on it to look at, which was the whole of the problem, and she had been standing at the edge of that problem since the eighteenth day, when she came home from the market with seed on her hem.

"It'll be there next month," she said.

"Yes."

"You don't believe me."

"I believe you," said Lan Shen. "It'll be there. That's not the same as it being the same."

His mother was quiet for a while.

"You're six," she said.

"Yes."

"That's not a thing a six says."

He did not answer that, because there was no answer to it that was not worse than the silence, and she let the silence stand, which was the thing she had got best at in eight months.

Then she held out her hand and he took it. They walked home up through the commoner's district in the early evening with the light still in the sky.

---

The notice went up in the school corridor in the last week of the term.

It was a piece of paper with the academy's mark on it and a date on it. It was at about the height of a grown-up's chest. He walked past it four times before he read it properly, because things in the corridor were not usually for him.

Awakening Day. Six weeks. Children of the district who had reached six, or who would reach six before the end of the year, to be registered at the gate with a parent and a household number.

He stood in the corridor and read it twice.

Na'er was six. She had been five and a half in the summer, and she had said so herself, in a garden, with her brother telling her she was six soon. She had been right to correct him and she had been wrong about how much.

He walked home and got the book down off the shelf, second row, square to the edge, where his mother could reach it.

He wrote: *Awakening Day is in six weeks. Na'er will go into the chamber, and a man who has done it thirty thousand times will put his hand on her, and there will be nothing.*

He looked at the name.

He had written it in the book once before, in the second week, about a road and a knife and a jacket, and that had been a thing that had happened. This was a thing that was going to happen. It was going to happen in six weeks, in a chamber, in front of the whole district, and there would be a woman two doors down who would know the date and the hour and who had not been told.

And there was a shelf in the front room, second row, square to the edge, that his mother could reach.

He blacked out the name. He did it carefully, the way a man blacks something out rather than the way a child crosses something out, and then he looked at the black, and understood that a black bar over a name is the loudest thing on a page.

He wrote instead: *the girl two doors down.*

Then he wrote: *I know there will be nothing. I have known there would be nothing since before I could hold a pen.*

Then: *and there is not one thing I can do about it, and I have already decided that I am not going to.*

He looked at the three lines.

Then he drew a line through the third one and wrote underneath it: *that is not true. There is one thing. I am not going to do it yet.*

He shut the book and put it back on the shelf square to the edge, second row, where his mother could reach it, because he had never once in two lives been able to keep a thing like that anywhere else.

---

On the second day of school they did not go near a martial soul at all. Lin Ximeng was not there; a woman neither of them had met made them copy characters until their hands hurt, praised Wulin twice, and did not notice Lan Shen at all, which suited everybody.

Wulin ate four bowls at the Lan table that evening and was still hungry, and Su Wan put the pot back on the stove without a word about it, which was the kindest thing anybody did for him that week.

"Slow down," said Lan He, not looking up. "It isn't going anywhere."

"I'm not hungry," said Wulin, with his mouth full, reaching.

"You're a bottomless pit," said Lan Shen.

"I'm growing."

"You were growing yesterday. Yesterday you ate two."

Lan He laughed at that, the short one. Su Wan said "let him eat" over her shoulder from the stove. Wulin ate the fifth bowl with the expression of a man misunderstood by his entire society who has decided to stop explaining himself.

Lan Shen watched him do it and did the arithmetic, because he could not not.

Five bowls. Wulin had eaten enough at the academy at midday to be given a name for it. He had walked two streets and done nothing all afternoon except sit in a room and breathe. There was no version of this where the number made sense, and Lan Shen had known for four years that there would not be.

Something in that body was burning fuel for a furnace that had not been lit yet.

"He's got a hole in him," said Lan Shen.

"He's got a martial soul," said Lan He.

"That's what I said."

His father looked at him then, for about a second, with the expression he used when his son had said a true thing at the wrong volume. Then he went back to his bowl.

"How was the school," said Su Wan, sitting down at last with her own bowl, late.

"Good," said Wulin.

"That's not a story."

He put the spoon down and thought about it with his whole face.

"They said there's two kinds," he said. "Battle Soul Masters, and then Utility Soul Masters. And they said rank ten is the door."

"What door."

"To be a Soul Master. You have to get to rank ten." He picked the spoon up again. "I'm three."

"And he's one," said Su Wan, nodding at her own son, mildly, the way she said things she had already worked out.

"So I've got less to go," said Lan Shen.

"That's not how it works and you know it's not how it works."

"I know how it works. I'm choosing a way of saying it."

Wulin looked between the two of them with his mouth open and then gave up on both of them and went back to the pot.

"Seven," he said.

"What."

"Seven more. To the door." He said it to the bowl. "Dad told me, success is ninety-nine percent effort and one percent talent. So if the soul's no good, the effort just has to be bigger. That's all it is. It's just more."

Nobody said anything. There was nothing to say to it. It was also wrong, in a way that only one person at that table was equipped to measure, and that person kept his face in his bowl.

---

It was getting dark when they went out — that particular Glorybound City dark where the sky over the harbour is still the colour of a bruise and the street has already given up.

Lan Shen walked him back. He had not decided to. He stood up when Wulin stood up and his legs did it, and by the time he noticed he was in the doorway with his shoes on.

Two doors down. That was the whole distance, and in the version of this evening he had read in the other life, Wulin had walked it alone and found something on it — on the first day, not the second, on a road with nobody on it.

*She's on the road,* he thought. *Tonight. Somewhere between here and there. Silver hair.*

And then the second thing, which was the one he did not like: *I don't know what she is.*

He knew the name. He knew the hair. He knew she ended up in that house, behind a divider, eating Wulin's biscuits, and that a very long time afterwards people would say her name in the same sentence as his brother's and mean something enormous by it. He knew the outline the way you know the outline of a building you drove past once. He had read four hundred chapters in a week and then stopped, and the stopping had taken the middle out.

*Important,* he thought. *Not dangerous. Important.* And then, because he had been alive before and knew how little that was: *those are not the same word and I am using them as if they are.*

"Are you coming or what," said Wulin, already in the street.

---

She was on the corner by the drain.

Wulin saw her first. Lan Shen had decided in advance that he would let that happen. It happened. Letting it happen cost him nothing at all, which surprised him.

The last of the light came down the street at an angle and hit a smear of silver. Wulin's head turned toward it before his feet did. He stopped in the middle of the road, the way an animal stops.

A little girl was squatting by the kerb. Short silver hair, shabby clothes, a dirt-stained face, smaller than Wulin by a little. She had her arms around her knees. She was not crying, not calling out, not doing any of the things a lost child is supposed to do, and that was why nobody on the street had stopped for her yet.

She raised her head.

Lan Shen saw the eyes from four metres away and understood, with a lurch of something he was not going to examine, exactly why Wulin had stopped.

Big eyes. Amethyst. Clear as glass, long-lashed, with the streetlight going into them and coming back out again.

Two pairs of eyes looked at each other across the road. Neither of them blinked. Lan Shen stood on the kerb like a piece of furniture and did not interrupt it, because he had read this, and this was the part that mattered.

"Little girl," said Wulin, "where do your parents live?"

She did not answer. She looked at him. Her mouth moved a little and nothing came out of it.

---

They came around the corner from the harbour side, six of them, not children and not men either. Lan Shen knew the type from two lifetimes of walking home.

The oldest was maybe fifteen, in a jacket that was too good for him. They did not run. They fanned out, which was the worst thing about them, because fanning out is a thing you learn.

"Little girl, where do your parents live?" said the one in the jacket, in exactly the same voice Wulin had used, and Lan Shen's stomach went over.

She shook her head, lowered.

"Silver hair is really rare," said another one. He said it to the jacket, not to her. "Where's she from? There's two other continents."

Nobody answered, so he answered himself.

"She's worth something." He crouched down to get the hair into the light. "The hair's worth something. The eyes are worth more."

"How much more?" said a third one, and his voice changed when he said it.

"Depends who's buying." He named two dealers on the black market, and said which of the two paid in cash rather than in favours.

"Both of them?" said the third one.

"Both of them," said the second one. "And they'd both want her tonight."

Greed came into all their faces at once.

"That's silver," said the one on the left. "That is actual silver."

"She's filthy. Look at her face."

"Face washes."

"You hungry?" said the jacket, crouching down to her, kindly. "Big brother knows every kitchen on this street. Come on then. You'll eat." Every word of it was true in the mouth that said it and false in the street it was said on.

She shook her head. Harder this time. Much harder.

The jacket looked up at his friends — a very small look. Then he reached out, took her arm, and stood up with her. She came off the ground against her will, and the other five closed in around the two of them like a door swinging shut.

She screamed.

It was not a loud scream, and that was the thing Lan Shen remembered afterwards, for years. A small one, cut off in the middle. He had her over his shoulder by the time it finished.

---

"What are you doing?"

Wulin said it. Of course he did. He said it before Lan Shen had got his own mouth open. He said it in a voice full of fury. He was a tiny pretty boy who did not reach their waists, and he was already moving.

Lan Shen grabbed his sleeve.

He got hold of it. Four years of training in a body that was six, both hands on the cloth. For maybe a third of a second he had him.

"Wulin—"

Wulin shook him off like a fly. He did not look round. He did not even know he had done it.

And Lan Shen let go.

He let go. He did not go after him. The reason he let go was the truest calculation he made all year: he could not win a fight against a fifteen-year-old. Wulin could not win a fight against a fifteen-year-old. Two of them being beaten was worse than one.

So he turned the other way, into the middle of the road, and he started shouting.

"He's taken her! He's got her! There's six of them and one of them's got a knife!"

He had not seen a knife yet. He shouted it anyway, because he had read the page.

"He's taking her down to the harbour! Somebody come and look! He's got a little girl and he's taking her to the harbour!"

He stood in the road and made himself enormous and useless and loud.

Doors opened. That was the whole point. They opened up and down the street, and a woman leaned out of the noodle place with her hands still wet.

"What's that, what is it—"

"He's got a girl! The one in the jacket! He's got her on his shoulder!"

"I can see six of them, I can't see a girl—"

"She's on his shoulder! She's got silver hair!"

"Ah-yo," said the woman. She went back inside, and came out again with her apron off, which meant she had got the husband.

A man came to his step two doors along with a bowl in his hand and did not put it down.

The youngest of the six came off the circle and shoved him. Lan Shen went into the wall of the herb shop hard enough to knock the breath out. He sat down on the pavement and got up again, and went on shouting from the ground, and then from his feet.

Behind him somebody kicked Wulin.

He heard it. He did not turn round. He hated himself for that, and he went on shouting.

"Villains." Wulin was on the ground two metres away, covered in dirt, already up. "You're villains and villains don't get good endings and I'm a Soul Master." He was nine years old and he said the rest of it anyway — that he was not afraid, that they were to let her go — and his voice did not believe him on either count.

"A Soul Master," said the jacket, over his shoulder, without slowing down. "Good for you."

Lan Shen, who had read this scene standing in a queue with his back against a shelf, could have told him that it was not going to work.

A cold light flickered at a wrist. A dagger, short, cheap, held wrong.

"Piss off. Unless you'd rather end up dead."

And Wulin lifted his right hand. A light blue ring of light twinkled at his wrist. The Bluesilver Grass came up out of his palm and waved there in the dark like something underwater.

---

Lan Shen stood in the road with his back against his father's wall and watched his brother hold up a weed.

He knew what it was worth. Rank three made a child a bit stronger than his peers and nothing else — no ring behind it, no skill in it, no force in it at all. A faint energy undulated out of it and undulated back. It was the worst martial soul on the continent, in a six-year-old's palm, offered sincerely to a boy with a knife.

*It isn't the grass,* thought Lan Shen. *It was never the grass.*

He watched the jacket's face do the thing he had already read, in order.

First: *what is that.* Then: *oh, a soul.* Then the long pause where a man who is not stupid recalculates. Then the arrival, walking in from somewhere out on the street, of the fact that a child with soul power is not a child. That the government keeps a file on those. That even the Spirit Pagoda keeps a file on those. That if one of them turns up in a ditch the federal government will spend resources finding out why, because resources are cheaper than the alternative. That there are four doors open on this street, and a woman with wet hands, and a man holding a bowl.

Lan Shen had known all of it since the other life.

He could have said it. Thirty seconds earlier, with his mouth already open, he could have said *she's registered, they've got a file on her, you touch her and the federal government comes down this street* — and the jacket would have dropped her before Wulin ever left the ground.

He had not said it.

Because a six-year-old does not know that the Spirit Pagoda keeps files. A six-year-old knows his own name and which end of a bowl is up. A six-year-old who says that sentence says it in a voice that has been in a room where they say that sentence. There is no such room. There never was. And there is a man in an orange robe in this city who wrote something on a second sheet of paper eleven minutes after he saw a gold network on a boy's forehead.

So Wulin went into the wall. So Wulin ate dirt. So Wulin stood up with his lip split and held a weed at six boys and was brave, brave in the exact way he remembered, exactly as brave as he had been the first time.

*I bought my cover with his teeth,* thought Lan Shen. He was not going to be let off that. He knew even then that he was going to keep doing it.

"Such bad luck," said the jacket.

He set her down with an angry growl and went, and his five went with him — walking, not running, because running is an admission. They went down toward the harbour. The street watched them go, nobody followed, and the man with the bowl went back inside.

---

Na'er stumbled and fell on her bottom.

Wulin ran and crouched down in front of her in the dirt. Lan Shen came up behind him, stopped, and did not crowd it.

"Don't be scared," said Wulin. "I'm a man now. I'll be the one protecting you."

She raised her head.

Lan Shen was close enough then. Close enough to see that the purple was even better up close, and to see the other thing.

There was a layer of mist in her pupils.

Not tears. Tears sit on the front. This was behind, and it was moving. It was the exact colour and consistency of something that has been in a room with the lights off for a long time and is now being asked what it saw.

Lan Shen looked at it for one second, and would go on looking at it for eleven years. He knew, without any words at all, that he had read this. That he had read past it. That whatever was inside that child was worth more than every jar in his father's shop put together. And that he could not for the life of him remember what.

*Don't cry, don't cry,* said Wulin. *I've already driven away the villains.* He wiped his own mouth with the back of his hand and left a streak. *My name is Tang Wulin. What's your name?*

She stared at him foolishly for a moment.

Then she opened her mouth.

"My name is Na'er."

Lan Shen did not react. Not reacting was work. Every part of him that had ever read a book wanted to make a sound, because he had known that name before she said it. Hearing a dead person's name come out of a live child's mouth does something to a man's face. He held his still. He had four years of practice at holding his face still, and two lifetimes at not saying things.

*Na'er,* he thought. *Hello.*

---
Lang Yue was in the doorway before they got to it, and she took one look at her son and her expression changed.

She did not look at the dirt. She looked at the lip, then at the girl in Wulin's arms — he had picked her up somewhere along the road, had not mentioned it, and was carrying her like a sack of rice — then at Lan Shen, who had a graze along one cheekbone and was standing very still.

"Inside," she said. "Both of you."

"Mother, I met some villains—"

"Inside."

They went inside. Lan Shen got as far as the step and stopped, because it was his neighbour's step, and because Lang Yue had said *both of you* and there were three of them.

His own door opened behind him.

"Su Wan," said Lang Yue, over the girl's silver head. "Come and look at this."

His mother came out with her sleeves pushed up and her hands wet. She looked at the child in Wulin's arms, at the blood on Wulin's chin, at her son's cheekbone. Her face did nothing at all.

"Mm," she said.

"He protected her," said Wulin, from the floor of somebody else's house, enormously.

"So I see." Su Wan turned his face to the light with two fingers under his chin, looked at the graze, and then looked past it at him, which was the part he had learned to dread. "Did you hit anybody."

"No."

"What did you do."

"I shouted."

Something moved in the corner of her mouth. She let go of his chin.

"Go and wash," she said. "Then come and sit down and be a child for an hour, if you can manage it."

---

He could not manage it.

He sat on his own step with a wet cloth on his cheek and listened to the wall, because the wall was thin and because that house had a child in it who had not said one word since the corner.

He heard water, and then Lang Yue's voice, low and patient.

"Little friend, are you called Na'er? Where's your mother and father?"

Nothing.

"Do you know any way to contact your family? Anything will do."

Nothing.

"Then how old are you?"

A pause. Then, thin as a thread, the first word that girl had said in this street.

"Five and a half."

"Wah." That was Wulin, upside down with delight. "Six beats five. That makes me your elder brother."

"Linlin."

They went out a few minutes after that — Lang Yue and the girl — and the door closed, and the house next door went quiet in the particular way a house goes quiet when the only person left in it is a child who has just had a very long day.

Lan Shen stayed on the step.

His father came out and stood over him.

"They'll want to know," said Lan He.

"About what."

"That you were there. That you shouted. Somebody will come round tomorrow and ask which of the six had the knife, and you will be a witness in a thing a family like ours does not want to be a witness in."

Lan Shen looked at the wet patch on his cloth.

"I know," he said. "They'll come to us because we're the shop. Everybody else on that street shut their door again inside a minute. We didn't. We've got a sign."

His father was quiet for a while.

"That's a good answer," he said at last, "and I don't like that you had it."

He went back inside.

---

Tang Ziran came home at dark, and the wall carried him. Lan Shen heard the door, heard Wulin start talking before it had shut, heard a chair.

And then he heard a man say, in the ordinary voice of a man who has been thinking about how to put something:

"There's nothing wrong with brave," said the man through the wall. "Brave on its own is only early. You want wise as well, and wise takes longer than a boy has."

Lan Shen stopped breathing.

He knew what came next. He had read it in a queue, in another lifetime, and he had skipped most of it, because nothing happened in it.

The man on the far side of the wall said all of it anyway.

"If you haven't got the power to save somebody," he said, "then you go and find somebody who has." A chair moving. "That's what the help is for."

And then the turn at the end, which was the part the book had not bothered to make a separate paragraph of, where a father takes the word *brave* back off his own son and hands him a worse one in its place.

"That isn't brave," said the wall. "It's reckless."

There was a long pause on the other side of the wall.

"I was wrong, Dad."

"Men don't make mistakes like that one."

"Hn."

Lan Shen sat on his own step with a wet cloth going warm on his cheek, and listened to a machine repairman he had eaten dinner with four times teach his son the exact lesson Lan Shen had failed an hour earlier.

*Seek the help of others.*

Wulin had grabbed nothing. Wulin had been grabbed. Wulin had shaken off a hand on his sleeve without even turning his head, and had gone at six boys alone, and had gone into a wall for it, and Tang Ziran was right, and Tang Ziran had no idea that he was right about anything other than his own son.

*There was an other,* thought Lan Shen. *I was the other. He didn't ask me and I didn't make him.*

Then he thought the second half of it, which was worse, because it was true: *and if he had asked me, I would have said don't. And she would be on a cart now.*

He could not get both of those to be right at the same time. He tried for ten minutes. Then he went indoors and ate cold rice standing at the pot, and the wall went on being a wall.

---

They came back after that.

"There's no record of her anywhere," said Lang Yue. "Not one, in any office. That leaves the orphanage, and it leaves it whether any of us like it or not."

It was the loudest thing said in that house all night, and it was not loud.

"No." Wulin. A chair going over. "Let her stay. Mom — let Na'er stay here." A breath. "I'll eat less. I'll eat less every single day and I won't say a word about it, just don't take her to that place."

Lan Shen, at his own table, with his own bowl, closed his eyes.

*Rice Bucket,* he thought. *You were given that name yesterday at noon.*

"Linlin." Lang Yue again, patient, and steel underneath it. "That is not yours to decide." A breath. "You don't get to stand in for her family. You ask Na'er." And then, because the boy was plainly about to argue, the whole of it in one flat sentence: whether the girl wanted to stay in this house or be sent away was the girl's own answer to give, and nobody at that table had the right to hand it over for her.

There was a scuffle. Wulin turning round. Lan Shen could hear him deciding to be sincere.

"Stay with us, Na'er." A gulp. "My mom's food is really good." Another. "And my dad's really nice." He was going through his own house room by room, the way a boy does when he is trying to sell it. "You could have my room. I'd protect you." A breath he had been holding since the street. "I wanted a sister. I've wanted one the whole time."

Lan Shen had read this speech too. He remembered it shorter than this. Wulin said the whole of it out loud, at full length, to a girl who had not answered him once all evening, and then he said the last part again, because he could not bear to have said it only once.

"Be my sister. Just — be my sister."

Nothing.

Nothing for long enough that Lan Shen found he had stood up.

"En."

"Wulin," said Tang Ziran, from somewhere near the door, dryly, "sit down before you go through the ceiling."

"Yay." That was not a word that had ever come out of him before, and it came out three times running, and by the third time it had a sister in it.

"He will wake the whole street," said Lang Yue. She did not sound like a woman who minded.

---

After that they ate. Lan Shen counted the bowls going into that kitchen and came out with a number he did not believe, and then a number he did. At some point Tang Ziran said "that was my lunch," and the two children kept eating.

Then there was hammering — a divider going up in the middle of the only room big enough, and a great many opinions about where the nails went. Lan Shen listened with his head against the wall like an idiot.

The hammering stopped. Somebody was put to bed. Somebody else said goodnight four times.

And then, quite late, Wulin's voice, muffled, pleased with himself, through the board and the plaster and the brick:

"Dad. I'm going in to meditate."

"Go on, then," said Tang Ziran. "And don't sit up all night at it."

"I won't!"

"You will," said Lang Yue.

---

He meditated at night, in his room, on the floor, with the window open because the room was hot and because he had a theory about the window that he had not told anybody.

Lin Ximeng had taught it badly yesterday, and Lan Shen had been holding that ever since. The man had walked the rows and moved wrists and said *breathe out longer than you breathe in*, and he had taught sixteen children to sit still — a real thing, a useful thing — without teaching one of them what they were sitting still *for*.

*Feel your martial soul. Keep your mind in intimate connection with it. Then continue to really meditate.*

Instructions for a child — correct, because they were children. It was also the whole of the first year, and Lan Shen had known the whole of the first year since before he could walk in this body.

He sat. He breathed out longer than he breathed in. He went down.

Four years of it every night had made it a staircase rather than a door. He went down fast, past the counting, past the day, past the argument with his father and the graze on his cheek, to the place where the body stops being a body and starts being a location.

And he found the grass.

It had always been there. Rank one — a blue thread the width of a hair, coiled up in him like a sleeping animal that has never been awake. He had felt it every night for four years and it had never once done anything, because there was nothing for it to do.

Tonight he asked.

*What are you.*

The grass did not answer. It unrolled.

---

He had expected to feel one weed.

He felt the street.

It came up through the floor and through the soles of his feet and through the open window all at once. It was not a metaphor. It was not a mood. There was grass in a crack under the paving outside. There was grass in the ditch behind the shop, and grass on the wall where he had pulled two of it four days ago, and grass in every yard and every untended metre of the commoner's district. All of it was the same thing. All of it was awake. All of it had been awake the whole time he had been walking on it.

Tiny existences in the air. Quietly absorbed. Blending completely into his feeble soul power — neither fast nor substantial, but stable, and persistent.

*Oh,* thought Lan Shen.

The grass drank. That was all it did. It drank light and it drank water and it drank whatever was loose in the air. It had been doing it for tens of millions of years. Every strand of it was joined to every other strand by nothing more than being the same plant, and the whole continent was covered in it.

Rank one.

*Rank one means it's small,* he thought. *It doesn't mean it's alone.*

And then, two doors down, through the wall, he felt something light up.

---

It was Wulin.

He knew it the way you know a voice. There was one strand in the street that was not a strand. It was a lamp, switched on by somebody who did not know where the switch was, pouring light in every direction with no idea how to stop.

Rank three. Three times his own soul power. It felt like ten.

Lan Shen sat on his floor in the dark with his eyes shut and his hands flat on the boards and he felt his brother come awake in a way no human being had ever felt another human being come awake, and the first thing he thought — the very first, before any of the other life got a word in — was:

*There you are.*

The second thing was: *the gold. Let me see the gold.*

Because if he could feel the soul, he could feel the root. If he could feel the root, he could find out whether the fingernail-length line of coin-colour at the bottom of that weed was a thing that *was* or a thing that was *growing* — how fast, and whether the three cycles Wulin's mother had described were the beginning of something or the whole of something.

He had wanted it since four o'clock yesterday afternoon.

He reached.

---

He did not know, later, how long he held it.

He got as far as the shape of it — a thing under the light, deep, folded, layered, like looking down a well and finding the well has floors — and he pushed. He had four years of pushing at things. Pushing was the only verb he had ever been any good at. He had spent two entire lifetimes unable to get a number out of a machine, and here was a number, and he could almost read it.

Something in the back of his head made a sound like a door closing in another room.

The street went out. All of it, at once — the ditch, the wall, the crack in the paving, the lamp two doors down — and there was a white line across the inside of his eyes that had not been there before, and then his nose started bleeding onto his shirt.

He fell sideways and got his shoulder down before his head, which was training. He lay there on the floor of his own room with the window open and the street going on outside, his heart like a drum, copper in his mouth, the white line behind his eyes slowly going grey.

*Right,* he thought, when he could think. *Right. That's the ceiling.*

He lay there and worked out what he had learned.

The grass is one thing and not many. Rank is a measure of pressure, not of reach. His brother's soul is a well with floors in it. And doing it properly — reaching, holding, reading — costs him something out of his own head that he does not have a name for.

He tried twice more on the floor to find the street again. There was nothing there at all. Not the grass. Not the ditch. Not the lamp.

The door was shut. He had got eleven seconds through it and come back with a nosebleed and no number.

*Give it a week,* he thought. And then, honestly: *give it a week and see if the eleven seconds gets longer.*

He sat up. He wiped his face with his sleeve, which was wrong, and then he got the rag off the shelf, which was right. He was on his knees with it in his hand when the door opened.

---

Su Wan stood in the doorway with a lamp.

She looked at him, at the rag, at the floor — three drops, a smear, a small dark patch under his left hand.

She did not say anything.

She came in and took the rag out of his hand. She got the other one off the nail, wetted it at the basin, and wiped his face, holding his chin the way she had held it on the step four hours earlier.

"Your nose," she said.

"I bumped the shelf."

She looked at the shelf. It was at shoulder height on a grown man and there was nothing on it that could have moved. The book was where he had left it, square to the edge, second row.

She looked back at him.

"Mm," she said.

She finished his face. She wrung the rag out into the basin, looked at the water in it for a moment, then picked up the other rag and the three drops and the smear, and took the whole of it out of the room with her. She stopped in the doorway.

"I asked you last time when you were going to mention the book," she said. "You told me. I said don't stop."

"Yes."

"I'm going to say something different now." She did not turn round. "Don't do whatever this is at night, when I'm asleep. Do it in the day, where I can see it."

"Mum."

"Mm."

"I'm not hurt."

"You're bleeding," she said. "Those are different things and you know it."

Then she went, and took the lamp, and left him the dark.

---

He did not write in the book that night. He got as far as taking it down.

He sat with it on his knee while the harbour light came in, and found his hand did not want to move. The reason was that everything worth writing was the thing he could least afford to have written.

*Silver hair. Amethyst. Mist behind the pupil. Name: Na'er. Five and a half — which means she can count, which means she chose not to until she was asked.*

That was the top line. Under it there was only one true sentence:

*She is in that house now. I knew she would be. I do not know what she is, and Wulin is going to spend the rest of his life finding out, and if I tell him what I saw in her eyes tonight he will be frightened of his own little sister. So I am not going to tell him.*

Then he wrote the last thing, small, at the bottom, in the hand he used for things he did not want to read twice:

*I could have stopped the kick. I chose the cover instead. That is the second time this week I have spent him to buy myself and both times I was right and I am not going to pretend that being right is the same as being clean.*

He shut the book and put it on the shelf, square to the edge, second row, where his mother could reach it and had said she would rather watch.

Then he lay down without undressing, because the white line in his head had come back and moving his face hurt.

---

Two doors down, there was a light on.

A square of yellow low down in the dark, through the window. The room where they had put the divider up that evening, where a boy and a girl who had met four hours ago were sleeping on either side of a piece of board.

He lay and looked at it.

The street had gone out of him. Nothing. No ditch, no crack in the paving, no grass on the wall where the two cups were, no lamp. Just a room. A bed. A wall. The ordinary dark of a six-year-old's head.

But it had been there. For eleven seconds the whole of it had been there: tens of millions of years old, covering the planet, drinking the air, all of one thing, all of it awake under the paving while everybody walked on top and called it a weed. And one strand of it, two doors down behind a divider in a room with a light on, was going to keep getting louder every night for the rest of their lives.

He shut his eyes. He could still see the square of yellow through the lids.

*Rank one,* he thought. *Nine ranks to the door. And I know what's in the well.*

Outside, somewhere down toward the harbour, a dog started up and then thought better of it. The light two doors down stayed on for a long time after that. Lan Shen lay with his back to it and did not sleep, and the grass under the paving of the whole street went on drinking in the dark without him.

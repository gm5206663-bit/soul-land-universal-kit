## Part 1: The Floor's Price

"Do you think we can take the gems?" Xie Xie asked — gazing at the mountain of jewels the way other children gazed at candy.

"Don't be so greedy." Wulin didn't even look at them. "You see me trying anything? If anyone could walk in and take those, why would Shrek leave them sitting here for a pack of examinees? Common sense."

"He's right," Lin Hao said, and meant the whole floor of it. His eye had done the arithmetic once, quietly, on the way in: a cave of loose stones worth a district of Eastsea, unguarded, in an academy's trial — which made them not treasure but *furniture*, and probably bait. His family had priced an education once; he knew exactly what an institution looked like when it could afford to leave a fortune on the floor. It looked like this. "Anything unguarded here is part of the test. Walk on the coins, don't pocket them."

"Treasurer," Xie Xie muttered, mourning.

The gems were, in the end, just pretty stones to all of them — Wulin included. What held the class was a different question entirely: what a beast like that would want in a home.

They found out two bends later.

## Part 2: The Thing in the Nest

The cave opened, a growl rolled, and something wrapped in gold light came out of the dark at Wulin's face.

No thinking got done — the body did the century of work on its own. Golden light burst from him in answer; the right arm thickened; the hand became a claw; three meters of dark-gold edge left it in a fan. In the face of death he held back nothing.

Metal struck metal with a ring that hurt the teeth. The golden shape thudded down close enough to touch; Wulin gave seven or eight steps of ground and found his footing with blood essence boiling off his shoulders — and his team, who had been ordered to run, closed into their formation instead and put a shield of water over his retreat.

They didn't run. He'd told them to run and nobody had even *considered* it, and in the middle of a fight for his life, the fact of that landed in him like a warm meal.

Then the light around the shape thinned, and the class got its first honest look.

A lion. The same impossible lion from the valley — crystal-bright, gold-scaled, the slit of a third eye closed on its forehead — and *small*. Shoulder height of a large dog. If the one in the valley was a mountain, this was the mountain's child.

"A *juvenile*?" Xie Xie's whisper cracked upward. "Could we be any luckier?"

## Part 3: The Case for the Ring

"Lucky," Wulin said, flat with suspicion.

"Lucky!" Xie Xie insisted, and made the case at speed: the third eye was the whole identity of the beast — it opened only past some growth threshold, and past it came power and a luck that blessed its whole pack; this one hadn't opened theirs, which meant young, which meant, at a guess, ten-year rank. Hundred at the outside. "And Xiaoyan—" he turned, blazing with it, "—a hundred-year ring from THIS bloodline would beat a ten-thousand-year ring off anything common. It might even drop a BONE. Golden Lion bones rank number one. There is no better third ring on the continent. There might be no better third ring in *history*."

Gu Yue leaned to Wulin's ear. "Is this a good idea?"

He watched the small lion pace, the closed eye, the claw-marks his own strike had left in scales that had rung like an anvil — marks that said *close enough to even*. "I think we can do it," he said. "Before its parent comes home."

Nobody needed to ask who the ring was for. The class had a shape, and the shape had a hole in it with Xiaoyan's name.

"Then hear the price," Lin Hao said to her, and waited until her eyes came up. "It's yours if you can take it — the last hit has to be yours, or the ring won't know you. Nobody's spending it for you. We'll open the door; you walk through it."

Xiaoyan's hands tightened on her staff. "…Okay," she said, in the small voice she used when a thing was too big to hold, and then, louder, because she was also the girl with the hammer: "Okay."

"Come on, then." Wulin stepped forward and beckoned to it, welcoming the problem. "Let's fight."

## Part 4: The Dim Room

On the screen in the metal room, five small figures fought something small and bright, and two old monsters leaned in like men at a chess problem played for money.

"Blood essence on the grass," Zhuo Shi said. "Second seal?"

"Ask them," the Silver Moon Douluo said, which had stopped being an answer and become a policy. Then, grudging, professional: "Look at the fifth one. Not fighting. *Herding.* He's placed where the little one's flashes go to die, and he hasn't lit a single ring doing it. The children fight; he farms the edges. Zhou — I have seen Sect Masters take worse care of a formation."

Zhuo Shi said nothing. On the screen, in the corner of the cave his granddisciple could not see, the fifth examinee turned his head — as if, Cai thought with an unholy delight, he could feel the two of them watching.

"He can't see us," Zhuo Shi said.

"I know," she said, and did not take her eyes off him.

## Part 5: The Blitz

The fight taught itself in pieces. Wulin's claw met the juvenile head-on and traded it boom for boom — his second-seal blood ran into the grass itself now, the gold creeping up the strands like dawn, changing what the grass *was* at some speed the grass itself did not yet understand. Bind bought a heartbeat; the lion's answer was a bolt of light like horizontal lightning, and his answer to that was to stand flat and swing through it, because a boy whose arm had just learned it was cheaper to feed than it used to be could afford the trade.

Gu Yue's stone teeth came up out of the floor and closed on the lion like a grammar book snapping shut; gold light blew them open from inside. And then the air *tilted* — the dizziness wave, the beast's third weapon — rolling over Wulin and Gu Yue like a floor pitching in cross-seas.

It washed over Lin Hao too, and broke. An Emperor’s spirit, meet a hundred-year cub's protest: he received it the way a wall receives weather, and simply moved — one step lateral, planting himself where the wave's debris fell, between the stagger and the two girls.

"Eyes front!" he called, sharp, ordinary. "It's loud, not strong! Xiaoyan — cast, don't look!"

She cast. And when the lion came out of its broken prison low and wicked for Wulin's middle, three Xie Xies came down on it like weather — the clones and the storm together, a knife-on-glass barrage that held a hundred-year legend up in the air by sheer insistence.

"NOW — blitz!" Wulin's second ring lit; the Impaling Array roared up around the suspended lion, a hedge of spears closing—

—and the beast *glowed*, that bizarre gold gushing off it, and the spears wilted at the touch of the light, slowing, barely failing, the whole trap dying an inch from its skin—

—and under it, quietly, a wheel of stars turned.

The Starwheel Shackles took the golden beast's legs mid-thrash, and it went stiff as a statue — one breath, two — and in the space of the first one, silver light *folded* the cave. No crossing. No distance crossed at all: Gu Yue was simply there, in front of the lion, and she had brought Wulin with her, delivered like a hammer to the anvil-face of the world.

Lin Hao, herding the edges, saw the silver close over them both and knew it instantly for what it was — the thing from the drawer, the flicker that moved without crossing the space between, spent now to save, in service of a girl's third ring — and he thought, with something that was not quite awe and not quite envy: *so that's what it's for.*

The claw came down.

## Part 1: The Longer Reach

The month after the emergence was the strangest of his life, because for the first nine days of it the new body outran its own arithmetic.

Everything he knew about his own body had been measured to a smaller man. His strokes were cut to a shorter reach. His steps were tuned to a lighter weight. The bench itself was wrong, and Mu Chen, who had watched him reach past the old pegs twice in a week, raised it two holes on a Tuesday without a word, the way an apron peg had once been moved up the row without a word, and said only, at supper: "The tool follows the hand. The hand changed."

The city itself did not know what to file him under. A butcher's wife called him *young man* and gave him the adult portion. The clerk at the Association, checking the register against the face for the third winter running, underlined *thirteen* harder, as though the pen could win.

The yard unlearned him too. Xie Xie, who had lost to him for four years, won the first three mornings outright — speed against a body that did not know its own dimensions yet — and was insufferable about it in three languages of eyebrow. Then the new reach arrived in his friend's shoulders, and Xie Xie won nothing after that, and stopped mentioning the three mornings, mostly.

Xu Xiaoyan brought the notebook to recalibration and made him do everything twice. Reach, twice. Step, twice. The Talon, three times, because the black edge had changed its weight as well as its color. One page of the notebook was only him now, times and distances in the small neat hand, and she guarded that page the way other people guard letters.

The Talon was the month's honest fright. He called it the old way, out of habit, on the second morning — the same word, the same want — and what arrived was not the skill he had called. The Gale Talon he had measured for two years had come back from every ring heavier; this one fell out of the black edge like weather closing a road. Xiaoyan's notebook caught it — forty paces of scarred frost where the old skill had ever marked nine — and made him do it twice more, because numbers that move that far are not mistakes. They are announcements. He sat with the arithmetic that night and found the reason where he should have looked first: nothing between the rings and the body argues. A perfect pairing wastes nothing — the skill comes through whole, the way a quench takes the entire bar and not the skin. What the ring was now, the skill was.

The cost had come through whole as well. The old Talon spent a breath and returned it; the new one took the breath and part of the next one, and he wrote the times in the book like prices. Nothing he owned was free anymore. Even the Union had filed its report without being opened: the hawk against his arm in the evenings was a season deeper, and the bond priced its own arithmetic — the trump card had grown with everything else, and the day it was spent it would bill its new size. He wrote that down like the others and did not open the door to check. Some prices you take on trust; the reserved ones especially.

Wulin sparred him the honest way, claw against sword, no quarter for old friendship, and came away shaking his head. "You're re-learning to be taller than me," he said. "I hate it. I'm thrilled. I hate it."

"The body is new," Gu Yue said, when her turn came, and she refused from the first minute to adapt to the new him — the elements arrived at the old timings, on the old lines, and punished every stroke that apologized for its own reach. "The readings are not new. Again." She rebuilt him in five mornings. She did this for no stated reason and stated none, in accordance with the customs of the class.

The sword was its own lesson, and the lesson was that nothing had been lost and everything had grown. The Frost Abyss balanced nothing like the Stormbringer had; the storm wanted the point, the deep water wanted the edge, and for a week every draw came out twice as large as it had been asked — the lightning coming down heavier than it had ever come, riding the storm and the water together, arriving in places he had not sent it. That was the month's real work: not searching for anything gone, but learning to hold what had arrived. Four elements now — wind, lightning, water, ice — and under the four, the fifth thing, which was not an element at all but the body they all lived in: the iron. The sword's own metal. The storm had never once been carried by air; it had been carried by iron, fused in, and the iron had been evolving since the day it fused — that was what his martial soul was. A fusion that grows. It took each new element the way iron takes a quench, and it had been doing so since he was small, which was why the blade had never once been the same sword for a whole year together. Seven years at the bench meant he knew metal as well as he knew weather, and the blade in his hands was metal he knew, all the way down. The morning the draw came out all one motion — wind, lightning, water, ice, and the iron under them, one animal — the hawk on his arm crooned once, and he wrote a line in the book that was not a ledger line: *the sword and I are introducing ourselves.*

The hawk rode his arm in the evenings while the winter-tipped feathers settled, its pressure a full season deeper than the bird the class had known in the spring, and the yard learned the new pressure the way it learned all its weather: by adjusting, and saying nothing.

Wu Zhangkong watched three Saturdays of it and issued exactly one correction for the month. "You are apologizing to the reach," he said. "Stop. The reach is not borrowed. It was paid for. Spend it."

By the fourth Saturday he was himself again at the new size, and then better than himself, and then it was the morning of the duel, and he was standing at the fence opposite his teacher with everything recalibrated and nothing left to practice — which is the condition Wu Zhangkong had been waiting for.

## Part 2: Terms

The whole class was at the fence, and Mu Chen was there with tea, which meant the Association had closed for the morning, which had happened four times in eleven years.

Xu Xiaoyan had brought the notebook. It was new since the winter — match times, opponents, conditions, light — and she opened it to a fresh page and wrote the date and *Yard, 7 a.m., overcast* in her small neat hand, because a measurement you did not write down had not happened. Mu Chen had the tea in the tall cup with the chip on the rim, the one he had carried to finals and funerals, and he held it with both hands, which was how he watched things he could not referee. Wulin's hands were flat on the top wire. He had not spoken since breakfast. The hawk in Lin Hao turned over once, felt the yard, and went still, which it only did before weather.

"Terms?" Lin Hao said.

"None." Wu Zhangkong set his sword down on the bench, which was the terms. "Come at me as though I am the barrier."

The yard was theirs. The morning was cold already, ordinary cold, sea cold. Lin Hao unfolded the domain.

It went out of him like a sheet lifted off a table. Frost ran the fence at ankle height and climbed. The puddles went white. The class's breath arrived all at once, four clouds at the wire, and Xie Xie said, quietly, in the manner of a man updating his files, "It got bigger."

## Part 3: The Boy

Wu Zhangkong walked in.

Unarmored, in shirt sleeves, hands loose — and the winter slid off him the way the sea slides off a cliff, the cold arriving at him and going around. He came to the middle of the yard and stopped, and even Lin Hao, who had built the cold, could not have said whether it was the domain failing at the man or the man declining it.

Under the grass, if you were the kind of listener who had once stood in the barrier's seam, something vast turned over in its sleep and settled again. Ten thousand years is not a colour. It is a weight, and the yard carried it that morning the way it carried the fog off the sea.

The violet light stood in the gold of Lin Hao's eyes, and the reading began — the man's weight, the man's timing, the seam every living thing carries because everything fails. The reading was the oldest thing he owned — older than the sword, older than the bench: the talent that had been taking the world apart and keeping what worked since before he had words for it. It did not feel like a gift. It felt like hunger — patient, total — and he had never once told it to stop. Wu Zhangkong stood in the cold and let himself be read, which was the lesson and the mercy at once: a master laying his own blade on the bench. *Learn what you can. It will not be enough. Come anyway.*

Lin Hao came anyway.

The wind went first, because the storm was the sword's first voice and had never once left it. The frost sheared off the ground in standing waves, a yard of air with edges in it, and Wu Zhangkong walked through the weather like a man commuting through rain he had decided not to notice.

The lightning came second, called down the flat of the blade — the sword's old second voice, three days finding its new road — a white line across the yard at the man's heart. He was not there. He had been not-there since before the draw finished, and the bolt grounded through the frost and left a scar on the grass shaped like a river, and something in the set of the man's shoulders filed the lightning away as a fact he would be hearing from again.

The Talon went third, black-edged, storm carrying winter, and Wu Zhangkong turned his shoulder and let it pass — but he turned it a half-beat late, and his eyes changed at the black of it. He had not seen that yet. Nobody had.

The fourth exchange came out of the bench. Strike and step and strike, the Disorder's own cadence, forging and fighting the same motion with no seam between the two skills — and the man's feet moved twice to one attack, and the fence understood that it had just watched a hammer art and a sword art shake hands.

And then Lin Hao went inside.

The reach was new and it was paid for, and he spent all of it at once, past the reading distance, past the courteous distance, into the country where duels are decided by kilograms — and put his bare fist at his teacher's ribs. The sound it made against the man's forearm was a door closing in a quiet house. Wu Zhangkong gave half a step.

Half a step. The fence made no noise at all, because Xu Xiaoyan was writing, and in that class nobody moved while the numbers were moving.

The five strokes went at the man in order after that, question upon question, and he answered them all with his arms and his patience, and the yard settled into the rhythm of something being taught at speed. Reading, then. The Track made the ground a rumour. The Unwritten Stroke went out — and it did not exist in the cold either, which Lin Hao had known for a month and had told no one, and the stroke arrived from nowhere in a medium of nowhere, and Wu Zhangkong moved out of its road by a hand's width, and something in the yard understood that this was now the most dangerous thing in it.

And then, because the man was still standing there, patient as a Monday, Lin Hao used the sixth stroke.

Still Water had never been drawn in front of anyone. It had no wind before it. The other five asked questions; Still Water waited until the opponent had answered one nobody asked — and Wu Zhangkong, mid-step, weight already committed to the road the Unwritten had threatened, had answered a question that was not the one coming.

The tip opened his sleeve along the outside of the forearm. A line of red, thin as a trial cut. The yard stopped.

The class did not breathe. Mu Chen put his tea down on the fence post with a click that everyone heard.

"Good," Wu Zhangkong said. He looked at the red line, and then at his student, with the whole of his face. "Now the man."

## Part 4: The Man

The Sky Ice came out of him the way winter comes out of a valley — not fast, just total. It built down his arm in plates of pale metal, over the shoulder, the collar, the mask; the two-word armor of a battle armor master. The smith in Lin Hao read it even as it closed — plate by plate, seam by seam, the maker's grain in the joining — because everything made by men has a seam, and he had spent seven years learning where metal lies. It changed nothing and it was true anyway. The yard's temperature did not drop so much as change sides. The domain met its elder and knelt. Frost that was his stopped being frost that was Lin Hao's, all the way to the fence, and the class stepped back from cold that had a different owner.

Lin Hao answered with the only thing he owned that spoke that language: the fourth ring's skill, one breath of Domineer put up against the tide. It was honest arithmetic. A candle at a coast of winter. The breath bought him his footing and nothing else in the world, and he was grateful for the footing.

Nine seconds.

The first exchange took the sword out of Lin Hao's hand. Not struck — *confiscated*, the gauntlet closing on the blade mid-line, and the Frost Abyss Sword, top-level, ten-thousand-year-ringed, was lifted from his grip like a borrowed thing. And the blade rang as it went — its own note, unasked, four full seconds of it against the armor's pale metal, metal answering metal. The whole yard heard the sword introduce itself to the gauntlet. The confiscation held. The note finished anyway.

The second exchange took the ground: a pressure he had no bracket for, Soul Douluo's weight through battle armor, and his knees learned the floor of the yard.

The third never landed. The gauntlet stopped at his collarbone, cold as a dock in January, and held there, precise, unhurried, a full second, so that everyone watching understood it had stopped and not missed.

"Yield."

"Yield," Lin Hao said, from the floor, into the frost.

The armor went back into the man like a tide going out. Wu Zhangkong reached down and pulled him up, and the yard was just a yard again, and the class made a noise at the fence that had been stored since the red line.

"Fifty years early, that ring," Wu Zhangkong said, as an aside, the way other men mention weather. "Mind it never learns your breathing for you." Then the arithmetic, so that the number would do the feeling for him: Xiaoyan's pencil never stopped: the whole fight was in the notebook before the frost left the grass, times and turns, and beside the last line she wrote a question mark and boxed it, because sixty-one did not look like losing until you unpacked it.

"Sixty-one seconds," Wu Zhangkong said. "Nine of them mine. In a year, ninety and thirteen. That arithmetic is your curriculum now. Nobody in this city has ever put a blade on me. You did it in sixty-one seconds, and you are thirteen, and I have never once in my life been prouder of anything, and I will deny this sentence under oath."

Lin Hao, arm hanging, cold gone, said the true thing, because the fight had loosened it: "You could have taken me in thirty."

"I could have taken you in nine," Wu Zhangkong said. "The other fifty-two were your education, and they were the point. Go wash. We leave for Shrek City in three weeks."

Mu Chen recovered his tea from the fence post, and the yard got the arithmetic from the other authority in it. "Do you understand what you watched?" he said, to nobody, to everybody. "Three purple rings. Every skill on that sword is a thousand-year skill — the class of thing a grown master carries one of, if he is lucky. He carries three, they answer in a storm together, and he is thirteen." He drank, with the click of a man closing a ledger. "The Association prices metals. Even I do not have a bracket for that."

At the fence, Xie Xie had his arms on the wire, and he said the thing he had been holding since the platform, canon to the syllable, his own at last: "Wulin. When we're there — you can just forge my battle armor for me instead."

Wulin looked at him. Looked at his own hands. "All right," he said, and that word, in that mouth, on that subject, was a signature.

## Part 5: What Wulin Did

While the city was still deciding what it thought about the duel, Wulin had farewells of his own to pay, and he paid them the way he did everything: thoroughly, and slightly too honestly.

The first was to Mu Chen, on the last Thursday, in the workshop with the door shut.

"Wulin. I wish you success on your trip to Shrek Academy. However, you cannot neglect your blacksmithing practice. You must forge daily." The old man said it the way he said everything that mattered, which was flatly, and then he said the thing that mattered more, which arrived out of season like snow: "In these past three years, you have made startling progress even by my standards. I'm certain that once you gain your third ring, Spirit Refinement will naturally follow."

That was not a small sentence, and both of them knew it. Wulin's first-grade thousand refining had been beyond dispute for two years — guaranteed, where most smiths attempt Spirit Refinement at a thirty percent rate, and Mu Chen himself had stood at fifty when he had first tried. The rare metals that only Master Craftsman rank could buy had been arriving on Wulin's bench for three years anyway, provided by a teacher who was hard-pressed to find the slightest fault in him and had stopped pretending to look for one.

"If you pass the Shrek exam, give me a call, and I'll make arrangements for your blacksmithing studies."

"Yes, Teacher."

Mu Chen rubbed his head, which he had done exactly never in front of witnesses before. "Be safe. Even if you don't pass the entrance exam, you can just come back, and I'll think of some way to get you into a good academy."

"Thank you, Teacher." And then the boy hugged him with teary eyes, because he was thirteen and he was Wulin, and some things are only embarrassing from the outside.

"Foolish child. What are you crying for? With today's soul technology, can't you just visit whenever you want?" A pause, and the voice went dry. "Alright. Go say goodbye to your senior disciple sister now. She's been depressed ever since she found out you were leaving."

She had been worse than depressed. She had not come to the Association in days. She rejected every call, and the girls' dormitory had a front door he did not have the nerve for.

So he did not use any door. He used the wall. Bluesilver Grass up three floors in the dark, to the washroom window that was seldom occupied, where Ouyang Zixin was waiting for him and trying not to laugh.

"Oh, you! You're getting better and better at this! Don't go philandering too much when you grow up."

"Big sis Zixin, don't tease me like this." He was blushing to the roots of his hair. "How is my senior disciple sister?"

"She's been tiger-faced the last few days. She's so glum!" Zixin got his collar straight, and put her coat over his shoulders against the corridor, and her fragrance made him redder than the climbing had. They were the same height now; that was new since spring. Xie Xie had said it once, years ago, in another yard — inside every man's heart was a pretty senior sister — and Wulin had jogged with this one every morning for three years, and had never once thought about the sentence until the last month, when it would not stop being true.

Class zero had never once, in four years, been furniture in its own city. Half of Eastsea could name the yard it trained in; after the duel, every one of them could name the minute count. Zixin had jogged beside the captain of it for three years at an hour whose only witnesses were gulls, and had heard the legend first from Xu Xiaoyu — Xiaoyan's own brother, who had watched it start — and had told nobody, which was its own kind of keeping. The mornings had been full for years without any of them knowing it: the girl at the 5:40 platform, the boy and his senior sister on the sea road, the third one on his walk with a book in his coat. The city held all of it and called it quiet.

"Big sis Zixin, are you still going to jog every day?"

"I will. I'll have no choice but to jog all alone."

He resolved himself, the way he did everything: thoroughly.

"Big sis Zixin, I like you."

A pause. "You foolish boy, big sis likes you too!"

He did not explain himself. He knew that his like and hers were different words wearing the same coat, and try as he might, he did not know how to say so. And she — girls mature earlier than boys; there was no way she did not understand the meaning — could only pretend ignorance, for the friendship's sake, the way one puts a coat over something. *If only I was four or five years younger, then maybe I would have snatched you up. I wish you the best on your path.* She thought it all the way to Mu Xi's door, where she jabbed her finger at it and whispered, "Go in. She's inside. Don't tell her I was the one who brought you here! I'm going to leave now."

"Mn. Thank you, big sis Zixin."

She left without saying goodbye, and pretended, on the stairs, that the glistening on her cheeks was a figment of a dream.

## Part 6: What Mu Xi Did

She had heard, of course. Eastsea was not big enough to contain the news that someone had cut Wu Zhangkong, and she was eighteen, and she had spent four years being precisely as old as she was.

He had come to say goodbye on the eve. Zixin had smuggled him in — he never learned that she was seen leaving in tears of her own — and he stood in her room and said *senior disciple sister* in a soft tone, and she fed him ice. "What are you doing here? Aren't you going to Shrek Academy? Shouldn't you be preparing? How did you even sneak into the girls' dormitory?" And last, the nail in the fence: "Just go. It would be bad if you're discovered. I don't want to see you either."

He took it. Then he said his piece anyway, because he was Wulin: he would never forget his blacksmith roots. If he did not pass, he would come back. If he did pass, he would not slack on smithing, and he would visit her and Teacher whenever he could. He bowed, and went out, and closed the door gently, and stood in the empty hallway being relieved and comprehending nothing. *Senior disciple sister's gaze is so heavy!*

The cold gaze thawed the moment his presence left the room, and turned into tears, and there was a reason, and the class never fully learned it. Three years ago, at the Skysea Alliance Tournament, a boy of ten had led her along his forging rhythm — and something in her had come unshackled: protected, blessed, free. Her talent had blossomed after that; fourth rank now, first-grade thousand refining, five years of progress done in three; the number-one blacksmithing genius of Eastsea City, the papers said, a title the Association's clerks had lately taken to qualifying with *of her intake*, on account of a certain other student of her father's. She had gone to Mu Chen in tears, once, to beg him to make Tang Wulin stay. Her father had answered her: "Tang Wulin doesn't belong in this small pond. His future is among the sea of stars."

Mu Xi wept through the night before he left, and would have denied it at swordpoint, and in the morning she knew the reason the way canon knows its own: she could not let him go, and he was going, and her road and his were parallel now — so close, never crossing, the geometry of it already drawn.

What she did instead of all that was walk into the members' forge on the last Friday and put a piece of stock on the bench.

"One run," she said. "Together. The old way. You take the strikes, I take the quench."

They did the whole run in the oldest rhythm, four years deep, the way they had done it at nine and eleven and every winter between, and neither of them went flat, and the bar came out singing.

The shop knew them. That was the thing about a members' forge: the racks remembered, the anvil remembered, the light through the high windows fell on the same scarred bench it had fallen on when he was nine and could barely start the strike. Four years of Fridays were in the walls. She worked the quench and he worked the hammer, and for one run, the last one, nobody in Eastsea was growing up or leaving; the metal was just hot, and the work was just work, and that was the whole of it.

She wrapped her half in cloth and put it in his bag herself, so he would find it on the train and not be able to give it back.

"For the bench in Shrek City," she said. "So it isn't all strangers." She looked at the forge, not at him, because looking at the forge was how she managed. "You were the best thing in my workshop years, Lin Hao. That's a scale reading. It doesn't need a reply."

She was eighteen. He was thirteen, and looked sixteen, and none of the arithmetic was anyone's fault.

"Write to the Association," he said. "Not to me. The Association. I answer those faster."

"Get out of my forge," said Mu Xi, and both of them heard the other sentence under it, and neither of them said it, and that was their whole farewell, and it was enough.

## Part 7: The Card, and the Train

The last Friday happened twice in the same building.

In the members' forge, Mu Xi ran the bar and wrapped her half and put it in his bag herself. In the workshop behind it, at the hour when the benches were cold, Lin Hao sat one last time in the sixth chair, which nobody had ever called anything, and Mu Chen let him sit in it for a while, which was the entire ceremony.

"Eleven years," Mu Chen said at last. "The metal told me you would be ready, and the metal was right. That is the whole speech." He turned back to his bench, and then, without turning again: "Shrek City has forges. Their metal does not know you. Introduce yourself."

The bar of fifth-rank stock was in Lin Hao's bag by the door before he left. Neither of them mentioned it. It was found on the train, beside Mu Xi's cloth — two wraps of metal in one bag, a father and a daughter, a family whose whole love language was stock.

That night Wu Zhangkong wrote the story he owed, two lines, in the hand like a fencing diagram:

*He put a blade on me. Sixty-one seconds. The calendar was right to hurry. — W.*

The reply came fast, as though the man had been sitting by the door: *Then he is ready for what the exam actually tests, which is not winning. Bring him. — S.*

And so it was decided the way the big things in class zero were always decided: by Wu Zhangkong stating them in the flat voice that had finished arguments for four years. The Shrek Academy entrance examination, once every three years, was in three weeks. They would take the Tuesday train. All five.

The tin went into Wulin's bag, four years deep. The bar went into Lin Hao's, wrapped in Mu Xi's cloth. Zhang Yangzi came to the platform with a manifest he had no reason to hold, and did not say anything, and shook every hand twice, his own included. Wei Xiaofeng said, "Eastsea will keep," which was his way, and was true, and was the kindest thing he knew. Zhang Yangzi held the manifest to the end and never handed it to anyone — it had never been for the dock. It was for the platform: a reason to be standing there at nine in the morning that was not the word goodbye. When the train breathed, he nodded once at the carriage as a whole, the way a man salutes weather, and that was his speech.

Gu Yue stood at the carriage door with the sea behind her doing its ordinary business, and did not look back at the city, and looked once, levelly, at Lin Hao, who looked sixteen and was thirteen and had lost in sixty-one seconds that morning and could not stop being pleased about it.

"Shrek," she said.

"Shrek," he said.

They took second class, the ordinary seats, the way the teacher had always taken the ordinary seats: the two girls on one side of the aisle, Wulin and Xie Xie and Wu Zhangkong across from them. The conductor, walking the carriage, looked from Lin Hao's youth fare to Lin Hao's face and back, and stamped it anyway, with the particular shrug of a man deciding the paperwork was the school's problem. Xie Xie watched the whole exchange with the joy of a man being proved right about something he had said first, and said nothing, loudly. After that he and Xu Xiaoyan chirped at intervals about nothing, which was how they managed. Gu Yue looked out the window quietly. Once, between stations, the sea appeared in her pane for a full minute, gray and busy, and she watched it the way you watch a parent from a moving train, and then the cuttings took it, and she closed her eyes and did not sleep. Somewhere past the third junction, Xie Xie nudged him. "Wulin, what's on your mind? What are you daydreaming about?"

Wulin snapped out of it and smiled. "It's nothing. I'm just going to miss Eastsea City."

Xie Xie's jaw dropped. "You — aren't you arrogant!"

"How so?"

Xie Xie looked like a man who had swallowed a lemon. "Didn't you just imply that we'll definitely pass? If that's not what you meant, then why would you miss Eastsea City?"

"You're overthinking it," Wulin said, and looked back at the window, where the truth was plainer than he was. Buried in the deepest, softest crook of his heart, he did feel that way — because there were tons of things waiting for him in Shrek City, and the first of them was old work.

Before departure he had met with Mang Tian once. Mang Tian had been convinced his parents had left for a major city, and Shrek City was the biggest on the continent. The message received after their retreat had named its price plainly: he would have the qualifications to search for them once he made fifth-rank blacksmith. Three years since Glorybound City.

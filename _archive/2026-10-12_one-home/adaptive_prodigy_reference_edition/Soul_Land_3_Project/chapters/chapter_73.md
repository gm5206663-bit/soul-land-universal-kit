## Part 1: Kettle Duty

The door stayed open a hand's width, and through the gap, gold.

It came off him in pulses. That was the first thing Lin Hao learned on kettle duty, somewhere on the third trip to the boiler: not a light that shone but a light that *beat*, slow as something with a heartbeat, and by the second hour he was counting it without having decided to, because the corridor was long and the count filled it. Eleven seconds. Twelve. Eleven. Eleven. He hauled the water his teacher would have hauled, fed the class their hours, and kept the number so it would exist somewhere outside the door. The standing was never the work. The work was that nobody behind that door should want for heat.

Inside, on the stool, his teacher watched, and did nothing on purpose. What his senses gave him, they gave in full: the mark had spread in moments from the forehead down — a network now — and the boy inside it breathing calmer as it grew, as if the pain had been a door and the gold was the room on the other side. On the right arm the scales had come out more brilliant and more jagged than the scales anyone knew, shining from inside, stopping just shy of the claw. When the water began to bubble — boiling him, no other word for it — the pattern stood up brighter and a rumbling came off it, and with every breath streams of golden air left through his nose and mouth, each breath taking something and leaving something better.

*His blood essence is so powerful!* It was inconceivable, blood coursing like that. And the teacher on the stool nodded once, the smallest motion in the world, certain now of the only thing that mattered: the boy could bear it.

The pulses at the gap ran eleven seconds all night, and Lin Hao built the days around them the way a smith builds a week around a quench. He took the kettle from Wu Zhangkong without asking and the trips became shared — the teacher's unhurried step and the student's fast one, an economy neither of them discussed. He cooked, because somebody had to, and learned that Xie Xie could be kept from the washroom door by being given a knife and onions, which held for two days before it didn't. He timed the pulses between tasks, never instead of them, and logged the water like weather: first a voice, rolling and knocking; then slow; then, on the third evening, clear — clear water, gold risen off it in a low mist over the sleeping face.

Still unfinished. Three days in, the refinement had not closed, and nobody woke him, because the one law everybody in that corridor knew by heart was the law of the deep: a meditation like that was a door closing itself, and a hand that reached in to help would reach into backlash.

Gu Yue came at the same hour both evenings, stayed exactly as long as looking took, and went. As before. The room did not make it anything else.

And the exam came on toward them like weather too.

## Part 2: Two Names

The visitor arrived on the third day, at an hour arranged by message, and stood in the doorway of Wu Zhangkong's ordinary room and frowned at it.

"Zhangkong, why are you staying in a place like this? Why not pick a place closer to the academy?"

"It's enough as long as I can rest here. What difference does it make if I stay somewhere else? Besides, I don't like how noisy it is near the academy."

She came in anyway, inspecting the room the way people inspect a thing they have already decided about — and then the four of them: Gu Yue, Xu Xiaoyan, Xie Xie, and Lin Hao coming through the door with the kettle, because the room had organized itself around whoever was willing to carry things.

Shen Yi was not what the word *visitor* had prepared anybody for. White-haired, sharp-eyed, younger than her carriage. And she did not look at them the way strangers look at strangers. She looked at them the way a woman looks at correspondence she is finally checking against the originals — and her eyes did the circuit the way everybody's did, but they didn't finish at the captain's empty chair. They finished at the kettle-boy, and stayed there one beat past polite.

Because she had been writing to their teacher for three winters, and the letters had carried two names.

The girl she had wanted since the day she read the first one — the elements that came flat, one after another, like scale-readings; the shortest letter and the longest to write. Not a secret; one whole reply card had been a joke about transfer papers.

And the other one. The one the letters had never managed to explain, only to report, quarter after quarter, the way a station reports weather. A boy who chose the worst class in the school on purpose. A smith the Branch ranked Master Craftsman at twelve. A footwork a Shrek student had watched at a tournament's edge and called *a Shrek thing* — which meant the academy had held a file with that name in it since before her second reply card. She had stopped asking about him in the second winter, because the answers had stopped explaining and started describing, and a teacher who only describes a boy like that is not holding him up to the light. The boy, she had decided, she would see for herself.

So she collected the debt before business sat down. Lin Hao set the kettle down, and she held out her hand, palm up — and he understood, and put a card into it. Her own, from the winter, two lines. *You said the calendar would decide. It has. Bring me the story.*

"He's in the water," he said. "Three days now. He'll come out heavier than he went in. That's the story so far."

She read her own handwriting once more, the way people do, and put the card away, and looked at him the way a file acquires a second page. "Then I'll stay for the rest of it." A beat. "Show me your hands."

He held them out — palms up, the calluses honest, the little burn-scar constellation of a boy who had been at an anvil more years than he had been at a desk. She read them like a transcript. Whatever she concluded stayed concluded; the file went back in its drawer.

Then business came in and sat down.

## Part 3: The Clock, Not the Count

"The exams are tomorrow." She took the chair. "Are your students ready?"

Wu Zhangkong's forehead wrinkled, and stayed wrinkled. "I asked you to come because I have a favor to request of you. The others are ready, but I have one student who has entered deep meditation and still hasn't awakened. So, is there a way to delay the exam?"

The room went still in a new way. Shen Yi's brows drew together, and she was silent long enough that the corridor's clock could be heard doing its work.

"I'm afraid it's impossible. You know how strict the academy's rules are. Besides, we have so many applicants of which only one hundred will pass, and there are many rounds of examination. We can't change the exam time just for your students. It wouldn't do to make them all wait for one person's sake. It's a tough situation." A breath. "Which student are you talking about?"

"The captain, Tang Wulin."

Her heart skipped — letter-fed, this, a name three winters long — and then the rest of it landed. Deep meditation, three days in. Everyone in the room knew the law of it: disturb the door, and the door takes the hand.

"Zhangkong, you know how hard it'd be. Virtually impossible. Don't you know how inflexible teachers are?" She said it helplessly, palms open, a woman describing a wall she could not move.

And here the conversation did what ours could only do it, because Shen Yi had been carrying two names for three years, and she had not come to this room to leave empty-handed on either of them.

"Understand what's not the problem," she said. "The count isn't. The Alliance scraped together four passes, and four is a stupid number for your class — I've read the letters, I know it's five. But the academy counted five the day I showed your last card to the admissions hall. If the five of them walk in and pass, five seats will be found. Shrek has bent for less and stood firm for more; this is not the wall." She looked at the door of the washroom, and her voice went flat and sorry. "The clock is the wall. The exam is tomorrow, the halls are already drawn, a thousand children are already traveling. Nobody in that city out there moves the sun for one sleeper. Not for the girl I want, and not for the file I came to read."

Wu Zhangkong nodded, once. "Alright then. I'll have my other students take the exam first."

Xie Xie was up before the sentence finished. "Teacher Wu, that's out of the question! Wulin has been looking forward to taking this exam for years, and this is our only chance! He would regret missing it for the rest of his life."

"Teacher Wu, how can we accept Captain not participating?" Xiaoyan, right behind him, the two of them a chord.

"Then the answer's the answer," Shen Yi said, and sighed, and it was a real sigh, a teacher's sigh. "There's no point in thinking about it any further. Nothing can be done. Unless he wakes up before the exam starts, he'll have to miss it. I'm sorry I can't help you."

And that was where the silence came from which Gu Yue stood up out of.

She had been silent the entire time. She walked to stand before the two teachers, and she did not raise her voice, because she never had: "Hello, Teacher Shen. May I ask if it's possible to take the exam as a substitute? Is there a way for me to renounce my exam qualifications so Wulin can take the exam? He's stronger than I am, so he could surely pass the exam if I'm able to."

Shen Yi was taken aback — the girl she regarded so highly, saying this, and she did not know whether to laugh or cry. "Little girl, you're overthinking it. Just what kind of place do you think Shrek Academy is? If normal academies don't allow for substitutions, why would Shrek? It's true that you're strong and have a high chance of being admitted, perhaps even into the inner court, but you shouldn't be so naive."

Gu Yue shook her head. "If Wulin can't go, I won't go either. If he can't join Shrek, I also refuse to join."

Her voice was steady. Shen Yi looked at the girl and saw, visibly, a kind of person she claimed to know: the kind that decides, and does not falter. *Like teacher, like student,* she did not say, and the room heard it anyway.

Lin Hao stood, because the arithmetic would not sit down. "Four passes," he said. "Five of us."

"I know the number," Wu Zhangkong said, without turning from the door. It was not dismissal. It was a man telling a boy the count had been made before the door opened, by somebody with more of it to spend.

Then she turned — and this was ours, too — to the kettle-boy at the wall of the room, because a teacher who has just been offered one entire future does not stop at one.

"And you? The file says you're the one who chose them. Would you refuse it too — the exam, the academy, all of it?"

Lin Hao considered the exact size of the question, which was smaller than she meant it to be. "Ask the cards," he said. "They've had my answer three winters. It's short."

Shen Yi laughed — one syllable, the first anyone in that room had heard from her — and did not write anything down, and everybody understood she had just written something down.

The clock in the corridor did its work. Seconds like years. Then Wu Zhangkong raised a hand and put it on Gu Yue's shoulder, and when he spoke there was no room left in the sentence for debate.

"Take your mind off of this issue. Just go back and rest. I will handle it."

Gu Yue stared at him — the shock of meeting her own resolve in somebody else's voice — and something in her settled, like water finding level.

"Thank you, Teacher."

She left first. Xie Xie stood, bristling with a sentence, and the teacher's hand came up — one motion, no hurry — and the sentence went back down, and the students went out.

On the landing, Xie Xie stopped. "What did you want to say just now?" Xiaoyan asked him quietly.

A moment of silence. Then: "Tell me, Xiaoyan, our friends or Shrek Academy, which do you consider more important?"

Xu Xiaoyan laughed — one syllable, no doubt anywhere in it. "You don't need to ask me that. As far as I'm concerned, Captain is the most important. My chances of passing were low anyway."

"Comrades truly think alike!" Xie Xie's arm went around her shoulder, lighter than it had been a minute ago. "If Wulin can't take the exam, then I won't either!"

Then he turned it, because he was Xie Xie, on the last one out. "Lin Hao. You too. Friends or academy?"

"You're asking the wrong one," he said. "I chose a class, not an academy. Five years ago, on purpose, and the class had five of us in it by the end. The pattern holds." A beat. "Four passes and five of us isn't a shape. But she said the count isn't the problem. The clock is. So the clock's what he went to handle."

"He did say that," Xie Xie admitted, and the landing held all three of them for a second, and then let them go.

## Part 4: A Small Price

Lin Hao was two steps down when the door that never quite closed gave him the rest of it.

"Let's go then." Wu Zhangkong's voice, and something under it Lin Hao had never heard there — breathing, rapid, a man's breathing, the kind you hear through a wall when the wall is doing you the courtesy of not being a wall.

"Where are we going?" Shen Yi.

"Back to the academy."

A silence with a tremble in it, on her side of the door. "You're going back to the academy? You—"

"The children all understand, so how could I not? If I can trade my dignity for their future, that's a small price to pay. I will go back with you and apologize."

Then footsteps, and the door's shadow moved, and Lin Hao went down the stairs ahead of them and was in the corridor by the time the two teachers came out, and nobody said anything about who had heard what, because the whole design of a door that never quite closes is that hearing it is not a trespass.

He had one duty left and he did it on the way past. The bath door, the hand's width, the gold: clear water, a low mist over the sleeping face, the pulses — he timed three, because the count existed to be kept — running fourteen now. Slower. Deeper. Still in it.

In the room that smelled of steam and wood, he took the book down and wrote the line the day had left him.

*Four passes. Five of us. The clock is what he went to handle.*

He put the book away, and the hawk shifted on his shoulder, winter-tipped and heavier than the bird it had been a year ago, and from somewhere below, two sets of footsteps went out the inn's front door into the first dark of exam eve — a woman going home, and a man walking back into his own past to buy them a morning.

The count did not resolve. That was the last fact of the day, and he carried it upstairs the way he had been taught to carry everything since he was ten years old and the lesson had no name yet.

## Part 1: What the Dragon Drank

Even the leaving was drunk.

Zhuo Shi drew his scarlet light back out of the boy the way a man draws a hand out of water — and the water objected. The golden pattern over Wulin's skin gave a dragon's roar, thin and unmistakable, and sucked the light back in, refusing the exit. Zhuo Shi cut the connection clean and stood there a moment, a Title Douluo who had just been mugged by his own kindness.

Only Shen Yi understood what she was seeing, and it cost her thethe color of her face for the second time that night. *He's devouring Teacher's soul power. Not receiving — devouring. This boy's martial soul restrains Teacher's… and Teacher's is the Scarlet Jade Dragon. A true dragon's bloodline. A supreme soul, restrained by grass.*

A finger-flick of red; a groan; Wulin surfaced into the warm grey of a blood-debt paid, cosy for the first time in a day and a half, soul power brimming, pallor rivaling snow. He sat up. "Grandteacher."

"You five," Zhuo Shi said, and even the correction had stopped surprising anyone, "follow me."

Gu Yue got to Wulin first, steadying him, scolding him softly — *permanent injuries, you might have* — and got the meek smile that was his only defense and always had been. Behind them, Lin Hao repacked a tool-wrap his teacher had rolled, and fell into line, and said nothing at all about the ache in his own deep water, which was the ordinary ache of a night's honest craft and none of anybody's business.

## Part 2: The Kneeling Man

They found the campus holding one more person than it had at midnight.

He knelt on the path ahead with his back straight as a rule, long blue hair over his shoulders, white robe dewed, the blue sword laid across his knees — the man the color of winter, kneeling in the open where anyone could see him do it. Wu Zhangkong had left the forge before his students woke, and had chosen the longest path across the academy, and had knelt where his teacher would pass, and had waited.

"Teacher Wu!" Wulin's yelp broke the morning.

WZK raised his head. His eyes went past his students — past all five, one by one, a half-second each, an inventory with love hidden in its method — and landed on the mountain walking behind them. And the ice thawed so visibly that Xie Xie would tell people about it for years: the cold face of the coldest teacher on the continent, at war with itself, losing.

He bowed before any of them reached him. Forehead to the stones.

The class came up around him and knelt after — Wulin first, then Xie Xie, then Xiaoyan — and Gu Yue sat beside Wulin instead, back straight, which was its own kind of bow and the only kind she made.

Zhuo Shi stopped. The fierceness went out of his walk; the stiffness stayed. "Stand up."

"Thank you, Teacher."

"I'm not your teacher." Flat as a plank. "I don't have a disciple as troublesome as you."

The five held their breath somewhere behind their teeth.

"These granddisciples, though," Zhuo Shi said. "I'll recognize them."

Wu Zhangkong kowtowed a second time, deeper, and everyone understood without a word being spent: not forgiveness — never asked for, never given — but the door, left open. From this man, that was everything.

"Teacher," WZK said as the old man turned. "I was wrong."

The mountain walked on — faster, if anything — and Wulin took his teacher's arm, and the teacher let him, and that was the second everything of the morning.

## Part 3: The Island

The inner court was grass and quiet and sculptures: the Seven Monsters of the first generation, and before them three founders — Flander, the first president; the Grandmaster Yu Xiaogang; Liu Erlong. The Golden Iron Triangle. Even Xie Xie went quiet at the foot of people whose names his grandmother used to threaten him with, and bowed till his back learned something.

Past the trees, an azure lake lay open to the night, crystal under the moon, mist standing on the water like a congregation. Zhuo Shi's red light wrapped all eight of them, and the world folded — and they stood on a small island in the lake's heart.

"Sea God's Island," Wulin breathed, and meant it as a question, and it wasn't one.

The inner court's inner court. The place the rumors were polite about. Even inner disciples didn't get brought here — and a wooden house stood up the two small hills, warm-lit, simple as a truth.

"Shen Yi. Tomorrow, the registration. Tang Wulin receives full marks; calculate the others as usual." Zhuo Shi settled into the worn chair that was clearly his, and the wood knew him. "From this moment you are working students of the outer court."

"Then before it's official," Wulin said, stepping forward, because some things must be spent aloud or they were never really held: "Elders. My request. I claim it now, in front of everyone: the point. Give it to Gu Yue. That's what I want full marks to buy."

The room went still the way rooms do when a held breath finds its owner.

"Granted," Zhuo Shi said. "Sixty. She stands with you. Working students, all five — late to the exam, Elders Cai's own ruling; six years to make one-word battle armor masters of yourselves, five rings each by twenty, or the inner court never opens. That is the sentence; you already heard the judge." His eyes moved. "And the other full marks. The fifth. Your request?"

Every head turned. The lake ticked softly at the windows.

He had asked first. Hours ago — one roof, one night, the same witnesses — the price of that point had been his opening offer, before any makeup path existed: *name it, I'll pay it.* The examiner had refused the currency. Then Wulin had found a coin the academy would take — a miracle, spent on her entry, the way Wulin did everything: for the shape, all five through one door, a captain's grammar, and everyone in this room knew it, her included. And a man does not bid against a gift his captain has already given. That law was older in Lin Hao than the sword: the hand goes in first for other people; it does not go in twice through the same door, and it does not take a door out of the hands of the one already walking through it.

"Held," Lin Hao said. "Still. I'll know what it's for when I know what it's for." A beat, and then, because the night had earned plain speech: "It's narrowed, if that helps the paperwork. It used to be anything. Now it's something specific, and I don't have the word yet. When I have the word, you'll hear the request."

Zhuo Shi looked at Cai's empty chair, where the answer would have been scored, and then at the boy. "Held," he agreed. "With interest."

Gu Yue looked at Lin Hao then — one heartbeat, no longer — a look doing arithmetic it declined to show its work for. He met it evenly, and did not help her with it, and that, between the two of them, was the whole conversation.

Then the last of the family business: Wu Zhangkong on his knees a third time — *I won't run anymore. Even part-time work. Let me take care of you. A man can't live for himself alone; I made a mistake; I have a remainder of a life to pay it with* — and the mountain, dumbstruck by a stubbornness he had personally installed, muttering at last: "Fine. You can stay. Someone has to make the tea and keep the house."

The class thanked the grandteacher in a ragged chorus. Shen Yi took them out to the guest rooms, and the island kept its lights on a long time, and upstairs an old man let his favorite enemy help him up the stairs, and neither of them called it what it was.

## Part 4: The Lake at Night

Lin Hao could not sleep. Deep water does not, after a storm; it settles, and listens.

He walked the island's rim with his hands empty, and at the boathouse he stopped, because his hands had picked up a boat oar without asking him, and the oar was — wrong. Not wrong as a tool. Wrong as a *sentence*: it wanted to sweep, and his intent, forty weeks of sword-consciousness drilled into the marrow, wanted to cut, and the two disagreements woke something that had been sleeping since the forge.

He set the oar down. He took up a branch off the ground instead, a dead stick, no edge, no point, no memory of any war.

He stood with it the way he stood with the sword.

And the branch — sang.

Not broke, not whistled: *sang*, the way the Yaluo bar had turned toward his fingers, the way the silver had cheered under Wulin's forty-eighth strike. The six strokes lived in it, all six, transposed whole — Still Water ran down the stick as if wood had been waiting its whole life to be told it was allowed. Lin Hao stood very still, the way a man stands when a floor he has walked for years turns out to be a door.

*The intent was never the sword's,* he thought, and the thought had the weight of law. *The sword was only where I first met it. It lives in the purpose, not the steel — the intent that knows what the weapon is for before the weapon moves. And the purpose wears everything. Edge, point, weight, lever, stone, hand. Every weapon is one idea wearing different bodies.*

He picked up the oar. It swept — perfectly, without rehearsal, the stroke arriving before the oar did. He set it down and made a fist, and his own knuckles explained themselves to him, blunt instruments they had been all along. A stone off the ground sat in his palm like a short, patient siege. The branch again: a spear; then, held differently, a fence; then a pen, ridiculous and exact.

Somewhere in him the talent — the thing the system window had named in a word nobody could read, the thing that met anything and became it, the adaptation that had never once been loyal to a single shape — turned over in its sleep and fit the new fact like a key fits a lock. Of course. *It was never going to stop at one weapon.* A talent that adapts to everything finds, eventually, the intent that lives in everything wielded.

Sword consciousness had been the school. This was the graduation.

He did not shout, or flare, or spend a single point of anything — Lin Hao never had. The lake did not ripple. He simply stood there a while at the rim of the water with a dead branch in his hand, being — quietly, irreversibly — the youngest weapon-master the way he was already everything else: without precedent, and without anyone to tell him so.

"Again," said a voice from the trees, in the tone of a man who has been watching a long time.

Wu Zhangkong stepped out of the dark with his blue sword loose in his hand, and his face was doing something it had not done in Class Zero's entire history. He had felt it from the house — a swordsman knows — and had come down, and had watched a boy make a boat oar tell the truth.

"Show me," his teacher said, and drew.

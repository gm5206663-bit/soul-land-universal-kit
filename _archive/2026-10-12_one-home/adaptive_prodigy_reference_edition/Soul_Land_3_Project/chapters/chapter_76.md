## Part 1: The Pilgrimage

"Ask me again if you pass," Shen Yi said, when someone asked the question the car had been carrying. "It's still early for you to know." And the car slowed to a snail's pace before a plaza that ate the horizon.

Ashen-gray stone, slabs larger than the road-rock of the inner city; a semicircle of ancient buildings a kilometer across by eye; at the center a hundred-meter pond and a fountain and a sculpture that stopped Tang Wulin's heart mid-beat — the only man who could stand beside Tang San in any telling, the Spirit Pagoda's founder, the master of the Sea God's Pavilion, the Spirit Ice Douluo, Huo Yuhao, carved life-like with his spirit souls gathered around him like guardians. The sole being of his era to ascend to godhood. The same sculpture stood at the Tang Sect; Wulin's eyes sparkled and his chest worked.

"This is Spirit Ice Plaza," Shen Yi said, and gave them the ten-thousand-year history in four sentences, and led them toward the ring of buildings — past carvings that resolved as they came close: figures, scores of figures, every one a person.

"Inner court disciples," she said. "Every student admitted to the inner court has a sculpture made in his likeness. Commemoration — and evidence of Shrek's history."

Lin Hao walked the last fifty meters with his head tipping back, doing the thing he did to buildings. The walls had a scale to them the way a good anvil has a weight: settled, load-bearing, certain of what they were holding. Twenty thousand years of murals and stone, quenched and re-quenched, each generation's hand over the last one's — the deep past not preserved but *maintained*, which is a different thing, and a smith knew the difference. His father's shop had held four generations of hammer-marks on one stump. This hall held ten thousand years of them, and somebody paid for the charcoal forever. *Whatever the school was short of, he was long in it.* The thought had a street address now.

House Shrek swallowed them. Ten-meter ceilings under six floors; murals climbing the dome; grandeur that never once got tacky. The pilgrimage feeling the pamphlets could only advertise. All five of them felt their spines try to kowtow.

And it broke Tang Wulin's heart, in the way that shame and fire are the same burn. His fists balled, knuckles white. *I almost made my teammates miss this.* We — we all have to get in here. Every soul master in the world would have said the same; he was just the one in the room with the fingerprints on the dominoes.

Two corridors later, a round hall, twenty meters of domed ceiling with one painting on it.

A black dragon. Wings spread, golden eyes burning out of an ebony face — and violet light exploding from its body, so vivid the paint argument lost in court. Every scale countable at twenty meters. Not one missing.

The room pressed down like a mountain with opinions.

## Part 2: The Drop of Blood

Gu Yue trembled once, then got herself back — nearly his match, whatever that cost her. Xiaoyan went the other way: pale, shaking, on the lip of something her knees had opinions about. Xie Xie's knees voted first and Wulin caught him, and the fear drained out of the boy the instant he touched his captain's aura.

Lin Hao stood in it and *read.*

The pressure came in waves, each heavier than the last, and it was real pressure, the kind the deep water had taught him the temperature of — and the violet light was the tell. His own stood up in the gold of his eyes without being asked, the reading-lamp answer to a reading-light, and under the two violets the mural stopped being a picture and became a fact. A drop of blood in the paint. He didn't know how he knew; the knowing arrived the way metal's opinions arrive, up the arms, and his arms had seven years of anvil in them. Somebody had painted this with a Title Douluo's hand and bled a monster into it.

And the monster's blood recognized things.

The fourth ring — the black one, the ten-thousand-year cold that slept in a lane-long dragon jiao — *turned over.* Not woken: turned over, the way a heavy sleeper turns toward a sound in the house. The cold rose off it and met the hall's pressure, and the pressure was the GREATER mountain, and the ring went deep under it — which was water's nature, and the ring was a river thing. A tributary does not shame the river.

The sword was another matter.

The sword had never once in his life been underneath anything, and it was not underneath this. The King's pressure came down and broke around his spirit the way a current breaks around a keel — met, parted, passed on — and the Intent stood in him the way it had stood at ten years old in front of grown men: temperature, edge, and no vocabulary in it for kneeling. The blood in the paint was a king's. Kings are not obeyed by swords. The hawk tightened its grip on his shoulder to the edge of pain, winter-tipped feathers flat, his arms goosefleshed wrist to elbow over the marks — and he stood in the Gold-eyed Black Dragon King's own hall with his spine unhandled, reading the painting.

Then he stopped reading, because beside him Xiaoyan was going under, and Wulin was already moving — snorted once, golden light flaring deep in his eyes, and *scales*, more scales than Lin Hao had ever seen on him, out of the right arm and past the shoulder and down the collarbone and the torso's right side and up the neck, pulsing with more power than ever before — and his blood essence stretched out past his own skin and wrapped all of them, the whole class, like a coat.

The hall's pressure came down. The coat held it off. And Xiaoyan breathed.

The waves broke and broke. Wulin stood inside his own legend, unbothered, arrogant as spring. Shen Yi was nowhere in the room.

## Part 3: Nine

"Huh."

She came around a stone pillar with an elderly man behind her — dark-green robe, the very definition of surprise on his face, approval already nodding at Wulin.

"Not bad. He has the potential of a little monster. It has been many years since I've last seen someone capable of withstanding the Gold-eyed Black Dragon King's pressure."

So the painting had a name, and the name had a history — the strongest soul beast in the Great Star Dou Forest; a drop of blood won in a legendary battle between that dragon and Shrek's most powerful; the mural painted by a Title Douluo who specialized in painting. The hall tested spiritual power. The longer you lasted, the higher you scored. Nobody told them any of it. Examinees never got told; that was the trial.

Shen Yi was astonished twice, and the second time was about Wulin: she had labelled Gu Yue the promising one — and here was a second. And in the long history of the Black Dragon Hall, no one had ever met the mural's might with an aura of their own. Examinees endured. This boy answered.

"Elder Li, this trial's score...?"

"Give that boy full marks." A glance at Tang Wulin. Then the old eyes went around the room doing arithmetic, and stopped, and came back. "As for the rest... luck is an aspect of strength. Eight points."

They bowed without being told — and Elder Li, passing, stopped in front of Lin Hao. The look held for two full seconds, and then a third and a fourth, and it was not approval anymore. It was a man taking his own instrument out of its case and finding the scale too short.

"The hall scores endurance," he said to Shen Yi, without taking his eyes off the boy. "How long a child stands under the King's blood. This one's spirit never went under it. It answered standing." A pause with dry amusement in it. "The scale has no number for that. Call it ten. The first spirit-answer this hall has ever scored — and the first blood-answer, in the same morning." He kept walking.

"Thank you, Elder Li." Shen Yi's eyes lit, and lit, and lit.

The elder smiled humbly at the wall. "I'm just giving face to that old fellow, Zhuo Shi. It doesn't change the fact that you were all late!" And he made to leave, and turned back once more to give Tang Wulin an approving nod, and went, incomprehensibly, and the name he'd dropped — *Zhuo Shi* — sat in the air a second after him like a coin put down on a table. Shen Yi's face did something small and private and was still again. Nobody asked. Yet.

"Each trial scores ten," Shen Yi said, brisk again, herding. "Six to pass. Points totalled at the trials' end, deducted for failures, added for full scores. Your luck was good for the first section." A beat, and she did not soften it, because she was Shrek: "It's still counted against you that you were late."

Follow me.

## Part 4: The Wager

It was there — in the corridor between the hall of the dragon and whatever came next, with the marks still on the class's knees and the elder's *"late"* still on the walls — that Lin Hao stopped walking, turned around, and put the thing he had been carrying since a car ride's terms on the table in front of the academy.

"Then say the rest of it to my face," he said. "The papers are harder. There's no rest. And if we pass, we pay." His voice did not rise; it was the voice he read numbers in. "I don't take this."

Xie Xie made a sound like a kettle deciding against boiling. Shen Yi stopped mid-stride. Even Gu Yue's head turned.

"If your Shrek Academy likes monsters so much — and you have them, you keep them in the walls — then put out any student from your outer court." He let that stand exactly one heartbeat. "If even a single student defeats me, let's go how you want. All of it — the papers, the penalties, the paying. No complaint, ever." A breath. "But if I win, then you change your rules for my team."

The corridor was stone, and old, and it took a full three seconds to decide what to do with the silence.

"You know what you're asking," Shen Yi said at last, quietly — not a rebuke; a teacher checking her file against the original one more time. "The terms were bought."

"I know what they cost." Flat. Level. Carried level. "That's why I'm not spending them."

And because the academy was the academy — because the hall behind them was painted in the blood of a dragon that Shrek's strongest had gone out and *fought*, and the academy's whole soul was that sentence — the answer came not from Shen Yi but from behind them, where an old man in a dark-green robe had come back down the corridor and been standing for some of it, unhurried, interested, the way elders are interested.

"Zhuo Shi would have liked that," Elder Li said, to no one, and then to him: "Boy. Do you know what the outer court's first seat is?"

"No, sir."

"Good," said Elder Li. "Then it knows nothing about you either." He came forward, and the corridor got smaller and older and more amused. "Terms. The hall accepts the shape of your wager — *any* student of the outer court, of the hall's choosing. If the hall's student defeats you, the terms stand unaltered, and you will hear no complaint from any of the five of you, ever, and you will say *thank you* for the papers." A finger came up. "If you win — the lateness dies, the penalties die, the working-student mark dies. Your team enters as students enter. Rules changed, for your team."

The second finger.

"But the wager rides behind the trials. All five of you pass every trial first. One failure anywhere — yours, the little sister's, anyone's — and the wager is void and the terms stand. Shrek does not change its rules for a team that cannot finish an examination."

He looked at the five of them once each, ending at Lin Hao, and the amusement went all the way down to something older in it.

"And when the trials are done — if they are done — the hall puts out whom the hall puts out. You said *any.* The word is mine now."

"It was always going to be," Lin Hao said.

Elder Li looked at him for a moment, and then he laughed — a short, dry bark, an elder's laugh, rusty at the hinges — and walked away down the corridor, and did not say where he was going, and it did not matter, because the shape of the afternoon had just changed for everyone in it.

Shen Yi looked at her five examinees with an expression that in a lesser woman would have been called delight.

"Follow me," she said. "Second trial."

Behind them, Wulin's hand closed once, open once — the guilt and the fire again — and Xie Xie whispered, with the reverence of a man watching a firework go off in his own hand: "He's going to kill us. Teacher Wu is going to kill us. *After* the elder does."

"He'll understand," Xiaoyan whispered back.

"He'll *understand*," Xie Xie agreed, in a tone that meant the opposite, and meant it joyfully.

Gu Yue said nothing at all. She looked at the corridor where the elder had gone, then at Lin Hao, and filed it, and was already walking.

## Part 5: The Night That Wasn't

The second hall was another dome. "Wait here," Shen Yi said, and left them with no explanation, because that, too, was the trial.

They had learned the lesson of the last hall in the last hall. Wulin glanced; Xie Xie and Xiaoyan sat, cross-legged, meditating off what the dragon had cost them; Gu Yue shook her head once — fine — and stood in the middle of the floor with her eyes closed.

Lin Hao stood where the room's sightlines crossed, and did the thing he did: read the space. The dome had a painting of a night sky, and the floor had silver in the stone, and the silver was in *channels*, which meant the floor was a work, and works had purposes.

The hall went dark at noon.

Night, whole and instant, the painted dome waking into a boundless star-field too real to be art. Silver lights rose out of the floor channels and wove themselves into that sky, and then faded, and the five of them were floating in it — a world with no floor left that any eye could find.

And beside him, in the dark that was not dark, Xu Xiaoyan stood up out of her exhaustion like a lamp being lit.

He almost missed it. He didn't: three years of watching the yard had a vocabulary for it. The girl's craft lived at night — the starwheel, the astrological attribute, five seconds of the world reordered — and the exam was a day exam, and the day had been the problem nobody said; and now the second trial of Shrek Academy had reached down and turned the *hall itself into her element*, and nobody in this academy did anything by accident.

"Xiaoyan," he said, quietly. "It's yours."

"I know," she said, and the smile in it could be heard with the back teeth. "They *made* me a night."

Whatever came next, it would come into a night she owned.

"Hold hands!" Wulin ordered — Xie Xie's hand, Gu Yue's hand, down the circle — and two purple rings rose and Bluesilver Grass burst out and wrapped all five of them in a living barrier, because when you did not know the enemy, safety first, plan second.

The world changed.

The star-field tore, and light came back, and they stood in a wide somewhere — and the roar arrived before the room did, tyrannical, and the pressure with it, and the thing that came out of the glare was ten meters long and five tall, two horns out of its skull, one longer than the other, skin like armor around scarlet eyes.

"It's an Earth Demon Rhinoceros," Xie Xie said, solemn, hunting through three years of platform work for the entry. "At least three-thousand-years. We've never met one — it's not a forest beast. It's a lord of the savanna."

"Its attribute is earth," Gu Yue judged.

Wulin didn't speak. His hands moved — signals, stations, the shape — and the shape had five hands now, and it had held at midnight in a laundry yard, and it had never once been tested, and the wager rode on all five of them now, and the rhino put its head down.

## Part 1: What He Heard

Wu Zhangkong was not on the field, and that was the first fact about the morning, and it would turn out not to be the most important one.

Lin Hao heard the Horned Dragon before he understood it.

Three booms, close together, in a rhythm that was not a rhythm — a thing hitting trees on purpose, and then a fourth sound underneath, higher, quicker, that he recognised from nine weeks of mornings as a boy running out of everything.

He went toward it, because the alternative was a forest he had already learned, and because he had stopped pressing the button seventy minutes ago and had no intention of starting now.

He arrived in the middle of the fourth tree.

The dragon was three and a half metres of metallic scale on two legs, and it hit the trunk the way a hammer hits a bar, and the trunk — thicker than two men's arms around — went down in a spray of green, and the boy in the next tree over went up and along with the practised desperation of someone who had done it six times and had six more in him at most.

"Xie Xie," Lin Hao said.

Xie Xie's head came round. His face did something complicated — relief, fury, embarrassment, and the particular joy of a boy who has been rescued by the one person whose rescuing he will never let him forget.

"You," Xie Xie said. "Of course it's you. I'm being murdered by a *lizard* and of course you wander past."

"It's a dragon."

"It's a lizard with delusions."

"Its front arms are weak," Lin Hao said, and he was already moving, because the reading had arrived before the words had, the way it always did. "Look at them. It climbs with the legs and it holds with the chest, the arms are for pinning, not for climbing. It can't come up after you."

The dragon jumped.

It went all the way up — four metres of it, straight off the ground, jaws closing on the branch Xie Xie had occupied a half-beat before — and Lin Hao, who had been wrong for the first time in ninety minutes, understood in the air behind it exactly *why* he had been wrong.

*It doesn't climb. It launches.*

"Down!" he shouted, and Xie Xie went down, and the dragon came off the trunk and landed on the ground and turned, and the whole of its attention — which was not a large thing, but was extremely thorough — moved off Xie Xie and onto the boy who had shouted.

"Hou."

"Yes, hello," Lin Hao said, and drew both swords.

## Part 2

The hair on his forearms stood up when he was alert. He had noticed it in the forest — a lift, like the air before a storm — and afterwards he had tested it twice on purpose, and it happened both times, and he had decided not to think about what that meant.
: The Thing That Goes to the Ends of the Earth

The Horned Dragon did not care about the second sword.

That was the first lesson. Lin Hao had held two blades before in a fight, against wolves that recalculate, and the second blade had bought him the half-second he needed. The dragon looked at the second blade and continued doing exactly what it had been doing, because the dragon did not have a model of him. The dragon had a *target,* and the dragon's entire temperament — the thing the books would have told him if he had thought to ask, and the thing Xie Xie was about to shout at him from a tree — was that once it had prey in sight it would go to the ends of the earth to kill it.

It came at him through the broken stumps at a speed that had no business belonging to something that shape, and Lin Hao did the thing that had kept him alive for ninety minutes, which was to not be where it arrived.

Wind-Step. One step, no wind-up, and the stands-that-were-not-there went silent in his head as he crossed inside its arc.

He put the Grain Cut into the seam under its jaw — the place where the scales changed direction, the place a smith would open a bar — and the Stormbringer went in about a finger's depth and stopped, and the dragon's head came round with the blade still in it.

"Your *turn,*" Lin Hao said, and let go of the sword.

That was the second lesson, and it was worth more than the cut: **the sword stayed in, and the dragon had to deal with the sword,** and dealing with the sword meant its head went down and to the left, and its left front arm — the weak one, the one that pinned rather than climbed — came up to tear the blade out.

Xie Xie was already there. He had been there the whole time; he had simply been waiting for the left arm to commit, because Xie Xie had been fighting Lin Hao for nine weeks and had learned, among many other things, exactly what it sounded like when Lin Hao made a door.

The Light Dragon Dagger went into the armpit that the raised arm had opened, and the dragon roared, and the ground shook, and the roar went on for a long time.

It did not die. That was the third lesson. It turned, and it took the dagger out of itself by moving, and it looked at the two of them with the flat patience of something that had decided to go to the ends of the earth, and Lin Hao understood that they had hurt it and had not, in any way that mattered, *ended* it.

"Lin Hao," Xie Xie said, backing up. "I have a suggestion."

"I'm not going to like it."

"We run."

"That's a *terrible* suggestion."

"It's the only suggestion I have!"

And then the grass came out of the undergrowth, wrapped around Xie Xie's waist, and yanked him four metres to the left, and the dragon hit the tree he had been standing in front of, and a boy with a hammer in each hand came out of the treeline with golden scales climbing his right arm.

"You two are the loudest people in this forest," Tang Wulin said.

## Part 3: What Only He Could Do

Wulin killed it. That was never in question, and Lin Hao knew it before the first hammer landed — knew it the way he knew a bar was going to break, from the sound it made going in.

But the killing took four exchanges, and it took four exchanges because three of them were wasted, and they were wasted for a reason that had nothing to do with strength.

Wulin swung where the dragon had been. Xie Xie struck where the dragon was going. And the dragon was in neither place, because the two of them were fighting it *separately* — each one answering the dragon on his own clock, and the dragon, which was not clever but was extremely fast, simply stepped into the gap between the two clocks.

Lin Hao watched it happen twice and shouted the third.

"Wulin — don't follow it, *meet* it! It launches off the left leg, so it's already committed when it leaves the ground — go where it's landing, not where it is!"

And Wulin, who did not ask questions in a fight and had never once needed to, went where it was landing.

"Xie Xie — stop stabbing the flank, it's armoured there, you're feeding it *information* and getting nothing back! Every time you touch the side it turns into you — so touch the side and then be somewhere it can't turn *to!* Go behind the tail, it can't see the tail!"

And Xie Xie, who complained about everything and obeyed instantly, went behind the tail.

And the dragon, which had spent four exchanges stepping into the gap between two clocks, found that the gap had closed, and that there was a third clock underneath the other two setting the time, and that a boy with two swords was standing in the exact place it needed to be in order to escape.

It went through him. That is the honest account: it went *through* him, and the Stormbringer took it on the flat and the new blade took it on the edge, and four hundred and seventy kilograms of smith's body held for exactly as long as it needed to hold, which was not long, and then Wulin's claw came down out of the air where the dragon's own launch had put it.

The forest went quiet.

Lin Hao lay on his back in the shredded undergrowth with both arms numb and the taste of dirt in his mouth, and stared up at the green, and did an inventory, because that was the process.

"You're bleeding," Wulin said, crouching over him.

"I've noticed."

"In the mouth."

"Yes, Wulin, in the mouth, that's where mouths bleed." He let his brother pull him up. "How long?"

"Four minutes, maybe." Wulin looked at him with the expression he wore when he had understood something and was deciding whether saying it would be annoying. "You told us what to do."

"I told you what you were already doing wrong."

"You told *me* what to do." Wulin turned the hammer over in his hand. "Nobody tells me what to do in a fight. Gu Yue does, but Gu Yue's different, she just says 'go there' and she's right. You said *why.* You said it launches off the left leg." He looked at the dragon. "I've killed three things today and I don't know why any of them died. You walked in and knew why this one was going to."

"That's not a compliment, that's a *hobby.*"

"It's not a hobby, it's the whole thing," Xie Xie said, from the tree, where he had gone to have his dignity in private. "Hao. Listen to me. I have been fighting you for nine weeks. I know what you are. You're the strongest — I've known that nine weeks and I'm still not used to saying it. You're not the fastest, I'm faster. You're not even the best with the sword anymore, Wulin hit something today that you couldn't have hit." He dropped down out of the tree. "You're the only one who can stand in the middle of a fight and see *all of it.* And you just did it with three people you have never fought beside, in a forest, with a lizard the size of a house, and it took you four minutes."

Lin Hao opened his mouth to make a joke, because that was the door and the room was behind it, and then he did not, because there was something under the joke that had arrived while he was lying in the dirt with his arms numb.

*Four minutes,* he thought. *Four minutes to make three strangers into a team. And Wu Zhangkong spent nine evenings pairing us and got nowhere.*

## Part 4: The Girl Who Ran the Wrong Way

The silver flash arrived before the sound did.

One moment there was nobody; the next there was Gu Yue, four metres away, with green light still guttering out under her feet and her face the colour of paper.

"It's not exactly a dragon," she said. "Now hurry up and help me."

"What—" Wulin began.

And the forest came down behind her.

It was a bear, and calling it a bear was like calling the harbour a puddle. Three metres at the shoulder and going through the thicket the way a ship goes through water — every small tree it touched went pale, then crystallised, then came apart into powder that hung in the air behind it like a wake.

"That," Xie Xie said, in a very small voice, "is the biggest thing I have ever seen and I have seen this one walk up a *wall.*"

"I can't hold it," Gu Yue said. She was breathing like someone who had been running for a long time. "I've moved eleven times. I have maybe two left."

"Then don't," Lin Hao said.

"It's a thousand years."

"I can see the number, Gu Yue, it's on the—" He stopped. Because he had been looking at the bear and not at the bear, and the bear had a colour on it.

Along its right foreleg, from the wrist to the elbow, the fur was not fur. It was dark gold, and it was hard, and it caught the green light and gave it back flat. And under that, on the shoulder, a smaller patch — older, he understood, older than this fight.

"Xie Xie," Lin Hao said, quietly, because the quiet carried further. "The crystal. On the arm. Where does it come from."

Xie Xie's head came round. His whole face changed — the child who had read about every soul beast in this forest before he was ten arrived, and the boy who had been running from a lizard left.

"It crystallises what it hits," he said. "A fraction each time. It stacks." He swallowed. "It stacks. If you fight it for a long time you get slower and slower and then you can't bend and then the next hit—" He stopped. "*Shatters.*"

"How long."

"What?"

"If it hits a person, how long until they can't move. Give me a number, Xie Xie, I don't need the mechanism, I need the *number.*"

Xie Xie closed his eyes for one second, doing arithmetic he had learned at seven. "Six hits to a limb. Maybe seven. Four if it's the same place."

Lin Hao looked at the bear. He looked at the arm that already had two patches on it, which meant the arm had been hit before, by something, and had never been cleaned, which meant **the bear came into this fight used.**

"Then it's not a fight," he said. "It's a clock. And we've been standing here for ninety seconds of it."

## Part 5: The Clock

"You two distract it and I'll look for a chance to give it a fatal blow," Wulin shouted from the air, where the grass had already put him.

"Neck!" Xie Xie shouted back, because he had been asked a question once and had decided to be the person who answers it. "Skin's like copper, bones like iron, but the neck — all the nerves are in the neck!"

"And don't let it touch you!" Lin Hao shouted, and then, because shouting advice at people who were already fighting was exactly the mistake he had just spent four minutes correcting, he did the other thing instead.

He counted.

Out loud. Into the fight. Standing in the middle of it with two swords and a bleeding mouth and nothing at all to do with his hands except keep the thing's attention, he called the clock the way his father called the heat of a forge — not as information, as *time.*

"Gu Yue, that was one! Don't take the second on the same side! Wulin, it's got two on the right arm and one on the shoulder, the right arm is nearly done, hit the *left*! Xie Xie, you're at one, you've got five, stop being brave, five is not a lot of bear!"

And the fight changed. Not because he hit anything. He hit almost nothing; the bear was too fast for the Grain Cut and too hard for everything else, and the Stormbringer rang off it like a hammer off an anvil with no bar under it.

The fight changed because four people stopped spending themselves and started spending the *bear.*

Gu Yue, who had been teleporting out of instinct, began teleporting out of arithmetic — leaving the left side when the left side had two on it, arriving on the side that had none. Xie Xie stopped stabbing the flank that would not open and began cutting at the wrist that already had three. Wulin stopped meeting the charge and started meeting the *arm,* because a limb that could not bend could not catch a boy who was already somewhere else.

And the bear, which was one of the strongest thousand-year soul beasts in the elementary platform, which had bulldozed a hundred metres of forest to get here, began — slowly, and then not slowly — to lose the use of its right side.

It tore a small tree out of the ground and threw it at Wulin, which was clever, and which Xie Xie ducked, and which Lin Hao did not, because he was watching the shoulder and not the tree, and the tree hit him across the back and put him into the undergrowth for the second time that afternoon.

He came up spitting.

"Four," he said, to nobody, and got his feet, and went back in, because the clock was still running and he was the only one holding it.

## Part 6: The Neck

It ended the way fights like that end — not with a blow, with an arrival.

The bear's right arm went. That was the whole of it: it came round on the three of them and the right arm simply did not arrive, because the crystal had finished its work somewhere between the third hit and the fourth, and a limb that cannot bend is not a limb. And the shoulder followed it, and the bear's whole right side became a thing it was carrying rather than a thing it was using, and its head came down and to the left to compensate.

Which put the neck exactly where a boy in the air had already decided it was going to be.

Wulin came down out of the sky with the golden scales to the wrist and the whole of the dragon's blood behind the strike, and the Crystal Bear — one of the strongest thousand-year soul beasts in the elementary platform, older than any of them by nine hundred and ninety years — went down in the shredded remains of its own path.

Xie Xie sat down hard.

Gu Yue put her hands on her knees and stayed there for a long time.

Lin Hao looked at the bear, and then at his own right arm, where a patch of skin on the forearm had gone pale and flat and cold in a shape about the size of a thumbprint, and he pressed it, and it did not bend.

"One," he said.

"Hao," Wulin said, and his voice had changed.

"One," Lin Hao repeated, and he held the arm out so his brother could see it, and he made himself say the next part in the voice he used for a fact rather than the voice he used for a joke, because Wulin needed the fact and would have taken the joke and been frightened alone afterwards. "It got me once. Four in the same place or six anywhere. I'm at one. That's not a wound, it's a *count,* and the count is fine, and I'd like everyone to stop looking at me like I'm dying."

"You're *bleeding.*"

"I'm bleeding from my *mouth,* Wulin, I bit it, it's the least interesting blood on me." He rolled the sleeve down. "Look. Gu Yue. Two teleports left, you said. Don't spend them walking. Save them for the walk out, because I have a leg with a bite in it, Xie Xie has nothing left in his legs, Wulin has spent a dragon, and the three of us are going to need a girl who can be somewhere else."

Gu Yue looked up at him.

"You counted me," she said.

"I counted everybody. That's the thing I do."

"No." She straightened up, and her face was still the colour of paper, and her voice was not. "You counted *me.* You counted my teleports, and you did not ask me how many I had, and you did not spend them, and you told the others not to spend them." She looked at him for a long moment with an expression he could not read at all, which was, he had learned, the most interesting expression she had. "Nobody has ever counted me before. They spend me."

"That's because they don't know what you cost," Lin Hao said. "I know what everything costs. It's a professional deformity, I'm a smith, I know what a bar is worth before it goes in the fire."

"And what am I worth."

"You're the only one who can be somewhere else," he said. "That makes you the most expensive thing in this forest, and I'd like to stop pretending otherwise, so — yes. I counted you. Get used to it."

Behind them, in the screen room that none of them could see, Long Hengxu was on his feet, and Wu Zhangkong had not moved at all, and had not moved for some time, and was looking at the board where a single dot had stopped counting itself as one.


The hawk — eight hundred and seventy years, and it had been fed well this session — did not comment. It never did.

---


He wrote the number down that night, because Wu Zhangkong had made him start keeping a record and the record was the only thing he had that was entirely his.

**one hundred and ninety-six.**

It had been one hundred and ninety-six. Five points, from a girl who ran the wrong way and a clock that had not been a clock, and he could not have told anybody which part of the afternoon had done it.

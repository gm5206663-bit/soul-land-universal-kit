## Part 1: Rooms Like Teachers'

The academy, it turned out, did not do things by halves once it had decided to do them at all.

The dormitory that class zero was given was not a dormitory so much as a small street: seven rooms in a row along a quiet corridor that smelled of new wood and old money, each room its own — its own window, its own desk, its own door that closed on a single occupant instead of four. They were, Xie Xie discovered within nine minutes of being let loose on them, the same rooms the academy gave its *teachers.*

"We live like Wu Zhangkong," he said, with the specific awe of a boy who had once shared a room with three others and a smell. "We live like *teachers.*"

"You live like a boy with two broken arms," Wulin said, carrying Xie Xie's splint-frame up the stairs for him, because the stairs were the one thing the dagger-boy could not do with his hands occupied being invalids. "Don't get used to it."

"I'm getting used to it *aggressively.*"

Down the corridor, Wu Zhangkong had been given a suite — his old dormitory traded for quarters that sat at the head of the little street like a foreman's office at the mouth of a mine. He had been notified the night before, along with the board's approval, and had said nothing about it then and said nothing about it now, which was, his students were learning, how he received most things.

Wei Xiaofeng took the room at the far end, the one furthest from the four, and put his things in it without speaking to anyone, and closed his door with the deliberateness of a man building a wall out of a door. Zhang Yangzi — who had come down to the dormitory on a healer's rolling chair, his legs elevated, his face the color of a boy doing long arithmetic about a situation he had not chosen — took the room beside him, because the two of them were the two of class two, and because Zhang Yangzi had signed the contract the moment he heard Wang Jinxi had, without a word, the way a man moves a piece he has already decided about.

And Wang Jinxi took the room beside Wulin's.

No one remarked on it. It would have been, all of them understood, the same as remarking on weather. The dragon had made his investment the day before — had walked across three meters of unassigned floor and sat down on the four's side, and not one of them had asked him why, because asking would have made it smaller than it was. So he had a room beside Wulin's now, and an arm in a sling, and a face that had stopped doing the complicated thing and started doing a quieter thing that none of them had a name for yet.

Seven rooms. One street. One teacher at the head of it. The academy had given them a day to settle.

They did not, of course, settle. They unpacked, and circled, and sat in doorways watching each other the way new metal watches the fire.

## Part 2

The breeze in his hair had become permanent. He could not feel it against his own skin any more, but other people could: twice that week a stranger had looked at him and then looked again, because the hair at his temple was moving and nothing else was.
: Not Mechas — The Other Thing

Class zero's classroom was on the ground floor of the same building, and it was, like everything else that week, too big for the number of people in it — a room built for forty holding seven desks and a teacher, with a window along the whole east wall looking out on the training grounds they were not, yet, allowed to use.

Wu Zhangkong came in with the expression he wore for all weather, and stood in the center, and swept his gaze across the seven of them — the four and the dragon on one side, the boy on the rolling chair and the boy with the wall for a face on the other.

"From henceforth, your curriculum will undergo a change," he said. "Due to the current conditions of your bodies, we will put off cultivation for a while." His eyes went, briefly and without emphasis, to the splint-frame, the sling, the elevated legs. "Three of you cannot lift a weapon. Two more of you would only injure the three by training against them. So we begin with theory. Today: mechas."

*Mechas?*

The word landed on the seven of them like a stone in a pond. Zhang Yangzi, whose expression brightened a fraction at having something to object to, could not help himself: "Teacher Wu — aren't mechas a topic for the advanced division? We're first grade. Isn't this too early?"

"The topics in this class will not be taught in detail," Wu Zhangkong said, "so the load will not be heavy. Among you — who wants to become a Mecha Master?"

Silence, all seven of them at once. Mecha Masters were, in the eyes of almost everyone, simply another word for powerful.

"Then you had better pay attention." He let the glance land on Zhang Yangzi, who folded. "Modern mechas have undergone ten thousand years of development; soul devices, several thousand more. Technology is researched for one reason: war. Soul devices were no exception — it was only after they filled the battlefield that civilians began to use them. The Soul Guide Masters of that era never imagined a soul device could empower a Soul Master by so much. It was only when they discovered it that they began to research mechas.

"Soul Masters were the most essential profession for tens of thousands of years — rare, and mostly weak. With soul devices, that shifted. Low-rank Soul Masters could become far stronger through soul power and devices; then soul mechas appeared, and made them stronger still. Soul mechas run on soul power from batteries, letting the user display strength far past their own. The first were large, because larger frames held larger batteries and more weapons. As the art improved, mechas became operable by ordinary people with strong enough bodies — and became the main weapon of every modern army."

He paused, and let the history settle.

"But these," he said, "are not the mechas I want to talk to you about."

Astonishment, seven copies.

"Didn't you think — as technology improved, ordinary people could control soul devices better and better? That is what ordinary people think. In the tens of thousands of years the Douluo Continent has recorded, one fact has never changed: **Soul Masters are the most powerful existence.** With soul devices, an ordinary person might match a Soul Grandmaster; with a mecha, contend with a five-ring Soul King. Tremendous power — but a mecha cannot give an ordinary person the fighting power of a true master. Because a mecha is *worn.* It is a tool outside the body." His voice dropped the half-degree that meant the sentence mattered. "What I am going to tell you about is the armor that is not worn. The armor that is *fused.*"

## Part 3: The Armor That Is Forged

"It is called battle armor," Wu Zhangkong said. "And everything I have told you about mechas was the road leading to it.

"A battle armor is a soul device made from special metal, forged to a quality ordinary metal cannot reach, and then inscribed with soul circuits — and then *bonded* to a single Soul Master. Not worn. Bonded. It fuses with your martial soul, draws on your own soul power and spiritual power instead of a battery, and becomes — when it is called — a second skin of metal that fights as you fight, because it is *part of you.* A mecha is a tool a man climbs into. A battle armor is a limb a Soul Master grows."

He let that sit, and then said the thing that made the room's temperature change.

"A battle armor is *made.* It is forged. The metal must be worked by a blacksmith to a grade called soul-forging — metal that has had soul power driven into it under the hammer, until the metal itself holds a soul's shape. Then a soul engineer inscribes the circuits. Without the blacksmith, there is no metal that can bear the circuits. Without the soul engineer, the metal is only metal. Battle armor is the one weapon in the world that cannot be made by a machine, or an army, or money alone. It is made by *hands.*"

And at the back of the room, in the seat by the east window, Lin Hao had stopped breathing.

Not dramatically. Not the way the others had gone still at the word *fused.* He had gone still the way he went still in front of a forge when the metal said something — the whole of him turning toward a sentence the way a compass turns north. *Forged. Soul power driven into it under the hammer. Metal that holds a soul's shape.* He had been hammering metal since he could hold a hammer. He had spent a summer learning that metal had a grain, a memory, a voice. And a man had just stood in front of him and said, in the flat voice of a man reading a weather report, that there was a metal in the world that was *forged to hold a soul* — and that the making of it began, not with a soul engineer, not with money, not with an army, but with a blacksmith and a hammer.

Because Wu Zhangkong had just described, in the flat voice he used for things he considered settled, the one thing in the room that Lin Hao could already do.

Six students sat in that room hearing a lecture about a craft. **One of them had been doing it since he was four years old, on a box, in a shed, for coins.** Soul-forging was not a subject to him. It was Tuesday. He had driven soul power into metal under a hammer before he had ever heard the word *battle armor,* and he had not known until that sentence that what he was doing had a name.

And that is the butterfly, and it is not a small one. **In a class without him, battle armor is a lecture, and all six of them will spend years finding a blacksmith good enough to make it for them, and most of them will never find one.** In a class with him in it, the lecture is a description of something already sitting in his hands, and the six of them will never in their lives understand that the rarest thing in the room was the boy at the east window who had stopped breathing.

Wu Zhangkong saw it. Wu Zhangkong always saw it. He did not say anything, which was how he said the largest things, and he went on with the lecture, and he taught the class a craft that one of them already had, and he did not once let the room know that he knew.

He did not raise his hand. He did not need to. But Wu Zhangkong's eyes, on their slow circuit of the room, stopped on him for one beat longer than the others — because Wu Zhangkong had been a teacher long enough to recognize the face of a boy who had just been handed his life's direction and did not yet know he was holding it.

"They are named," Wu Zhangkong continued, "by words. One-word battle armor. Two-word. Three-word. Four-word. The more words, the higher the grade of the metal, the deeper the circuits, the greater the power — and the higher the cultivation required to bear it." He held up no fingers; he simply said the numbers the way the world said them. "A one-word battle armor master is, in general, a Soul Ancestor. A two-word battle armor master is, in general, a Titled Douluo. A three-word battle armor master is, in general, a Super Douluo. And four-word battle armor—" a pause, the only one he gave the whole lecture, "—across the entire Douluo Continent, those who possess it can be counted on one hand. Every one of them is a pinnacle."

The room did the arithmetic ten-year-olds do: *Soul Ancestor. Titled Douluo. Super Douluo.* Names out of storybooks. Names you said about other people.

"And," Wu Zhangkong said, "you are asking why I tell first-graders this. You are asking because you are ten, and you have two rings between most of you, and these are names for people your grandparents' grandparents would call legends." He looked at the seven of them — the splint-frame, the sling, the elevated legs, the wall-faced boy, the still one by the window. "I tell you because a road you cannot see the end of is still a road, and most people never walk one because no one ever told them it was there. You will not touch battle armor for years. Some of you will never bear one. But every one of you will spend the next six years building the foundation that decides whether you *can.* And one of you—" his eyes went, again, a fraction longer, to the window seat, "—may spend his life making them for the others. Class dismissed."

---

"Which brings me," Wu Zhangkong said, "to the reason you are hearing this at ten years old instead of at twenty."

He wrote a single line on the board. Three words.

**Designer. Maker. Mechanic.**

"Three kinds of mecha craftsmen," he said. "There are others, but those are the three that matter, and one of them is going to be yours."

A hand went up at the front. Gu Yue's did not; Gu Yue's hands did not go up. It was Zhang Yangzi's.

"Ours, teacher?"

"**Secondary occupation.** Every one of you takes one. Not instead of cultivation — underneath it." He set the chalk down. "Because if you want a powerful battle armor, you must have one that *fits you.* And the only people who ever get that are the people who can design it, make it, or repair it themselves. Everybody else wears something a stranger guessed at." A pause. "If you do not take a trade, you will never have armor made to you, and you will never ascend to a **god altar.** That is not a metaphor. That is the arithmetic."

The room went very quiet, because *god altar* was not a word first-graders were given.

"With class zero I have one goal," Wu Zhangkong said. "That goal is to push five of you toward a god altar. It is long, it is arduous, and you may spend your whole lives on it. My job is to stop you wasting years doing it the wrong way, and to show you the road." He looked at each of them in turn. "You do not have to choose today. You have time. **But you will choose by third grade. Within three years, you give me an answer.** Even with your talent, your road looks like an ordinary person's road. The sooner you start walking it, the better."

Then his eyes went to the back of the room, to the east window, and stopped.

"In fact," he said, "one of you already has."

Lin Hao had the distinct and unpleasant sensation of a room turning around to look at him.

"Wulin chose blacksmithing when he was six. I expect he is extremely good at it." Wu Zhangkong's voice did not change, but something in it had. "**Blacksmithing is the one I would ask the rest of you to consider. Because from a purple battle armor onward, the creation process becomes inseparable from forging.**" He let that land. "Not adjacent to it. Not assisted by it. *Inseparable.* The man who can soul-forge is not a supplier to the people who make god-altar armor. He is the reason there is any."

"And," he added, almost as an afterthought, in the tone of a man closing a ledger, "if you reach five stars or higher in your trade, you will never be short of cultivation resources for the rest of your life. That is not a boast about the trades. That is simply what five stars is worth."

---

They walked out into a corridor that smelled of floor polish and had, in the space of forty minutes, become a different corridor.

Xie Xie spoke first, because Xie Xie always spoke first. "Maker," he said. "I've decided. Maker. It's technique — it's practice, it's hands, it's not sitting at a desk drawing things for six hours. It's *suitable* for me." He said this with the enormous satisfaction of a boy who had been asked a question and had an answer ready.

"That was not a suitable thing to say about drawing," said Gu Yue.

"I didn't say it about drawing. I said it about *me.*"

"You said it while looking at me."

"Coincidence."

"I'll take **mechanics**," said Gu Yue, to the ceiling, to nobody.

Xie Xie stopped walking. "*Mechanics?* But you're a girl, I thought you'd take designing—"

Gu Yue turned her head about fifteen degrees, which with her was the equivalent of shouting.

"**Only brainless people would think like that.**"

Zhang Yangzi, behind them, said "oh *no*" in the voice of a man settling in for entertainment, and Wang Jinxi said nothing at all, and Wulin and Lin Hao kept walking, because they had both known what they were since they were six and had never once discussed it, which was somehow the most suspicious thing about them.

"How long have you known?" Wulin said, eventually.

"Since the forge."

"Since before," said Lin Hao. "Since I watched you hit something flat on purpose and hear three notes come out, and understood that you and I were going to be doing this until we were dead."

Wulin considered that.

"Okay," he said. "That's a bit much."

"I'm not retracting it."

"I know you're not," said Wulin. "That's the bit that's a bit much."

## Part 4: What the Window Seat Held

The room emptied in the slow, reluctant way rooms empty after a lecture that landed. Xie Xie went out talking, because Xie Xie went everywhere talking — about Titled Douluo, about soul circuits, about whether a dagger could have a battle armor and if so whether it would be *faster.* Wulin walked beside him with the particular silence of a boy who had been handed a dream and was turning it over to find the seams. Gu Yue went out alone, four seconds behind everyone, having already filed the entire lecture somewhere Lin Hao would never see.

And Lin Hao stayed.

Not to ask a question — the question was too large for a doorway and too new for words. He stayed because the window looked out on the training grounds, and the training grounds had a maintenance shed, and the maintenance shed had a forge in it that the groundskeeper used for hinges and rail-fittings, and Lin Hao had noticed it the way he noticed all forges, the way other boys noticed nothing, on the first walk over — and he was looking at it now, through the east window, the way you look at a country you have just been told you are allowed to walk to someday.

Wu Zhangkong was still in the room. He had not left. He was standing where he had lectured, hands behind his back, looking at the boy by the window with the expression of a man confirming a suspicion he had carried since a sparring yard in September.

"You understood the forging," he said. Not a question.

"I understood that it's made by hand," Lin Hao said, without turning. "That it starts with a hammer. Everything else — the circuits, the bonding — comes after the metal, and the metal comes after the hammer." A pause. "I've been holding a hammer since I was four."

"Yes." Wu Zhangkong said it the way he said most true things: flatly, and once. "The others heard *Titled Douluo.* You heard *forged.*" He was quiet a moment. "Most blacksmiths in this city make hinges. A few make soul devices. A very few — a number you could count in this room — can soul-forge. And the ones who can are the reason any battle armor exists at all." He turned toward the door. "You are a second-rank blacksmith at ten years old. You will not soul-forge for a long time. But you are the only student I have ever taught who heard the word *battle armor* and thought about the *metal* first." And then, because he was Wu Zhangkong and it was the whole of the praise he gave: "Don't waste it on hinges."

He was gone.

Lin Hao stood at the window a while longer, looking at the little forge in the groundskeeper's shed, at the anvil that was too small and the bellows that was probably cracked, at a country of metal that started with a hammer and ended, if the lecture was true, in armor that fought like a limb of the person wearing it. And under it all, on the far water, the hawk watched the boy watch the forge, and was — for the second time that week — doing something that was not quite stillness and not quite speech: the attention of something that had begun, without deciding to, to point the same direction its boy was pointing.

That night, on the roof that was not theirs anymore — they had real rooms now, with real doors, and none of them had thought to use the roof, except, it turned out, the two who had always used it, because some habits are older than furniture — Wulin said, "Battle armor," the way you say a prayer you're not sure you're allowed to say. "Soul Ancestors. Titled Douluo. Hao — we're *ten.*"

"We're ten," Lin Hao agreed, "with two rings, a teacher who lives like a foreman, a forge in the groundskeeper's shed that he probably uses for *hinges,* and a lecture that said the metal comes first." He nudged his brother's shoulder with his own. "Also, you drooled a little during the Titled Douluo part. I saw it. I'm keeping that."

"I did *not*—"

"You did. You had a *dream-face.* I'm going to think about it at inappropriate moments for years." He was grinning, the whole of him, because making Wulin sputter was worth more than the lecture had been. "But also — and I want you to hear this part before I go back to making fun of you — you want to *bear* one. I want to *make* one. That's not the same dream. It's the same *road.*" He looked at his brother, and let the teasing go quiet for exactly as long as the thing needed, and no longer. "And roads are better with two on them. So you can go be a Titled Douluo, and I'll be the one who made the thing you're standing in, and when they write the story they'll get our names backwards, and I'll let you have the first page because I got there first anyway."

Wulin shoved him. Lin Hao shoved back, because that was the answer, and had always been the answer, and would be the answer when they were Titled Douluos and gray. And then Wulin laughed — the real one, the one that came up out of the place the drool had come from — and said, "You're going to put *hinges* in my battle armor, aren't you. You're going to forge me a Titled Douluo battle armor with a *hinge* on it."

"A *good* hinge," Lin Hao said. "A hinge that's been soul-forged. You'll never feel it. You'll just never be able to take the armor off, and you'll know it's me, every time, and you'll hate it, and you'll never once actually want to take it off." He slung an arm around his brother's shoulders — the right side, the side that had been his since they were six — and they sat there, and the harbor did its accounting, and neither of them said the rest, because the teasing was the whole of the saying, and it had never once needed more.

And the harbor did its accounting, and the seven rooms down the quiet street went dark one by one, and the little street that held the worst class's best and a dragon who had moved his piece and a boy with a wall for a face slept the sleep of children who had been told, for the first time in their lives, exactly how far the road went — and had decided, in the part of the mind that decides such things before the morning, to walk it anyway.

Three of them would mend. Then the cultivation would start, and the evenings would become what Wu Zhangkong had promised: not sparring. Combat. And the difference, by the end of the term, would be a sentence all seven of them could say.

But that was for later. Tonight, the road was new, and long, and theirs.

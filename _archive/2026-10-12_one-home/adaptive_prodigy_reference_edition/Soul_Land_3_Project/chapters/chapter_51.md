## Part 1: The Counting

Xie Xie had been awake at midnight cleaning his daggers, and had said nothing about it in the morning, and Lin Hao had let him.

Lin Hao counted eleven.

That was the thing his head did, and it had never once been useful in a way he could explain to anybody, and it was the only thing that kept him upright, because counting was a thing you could do while everything else was happening.

Eleven beasts. Circling. Not closing.

Three corpses on the ground, and one of them was the ape, and the ape had been ten thousand years old, and the thing that had killed it had been standing forty metres away with its soul power compressed so thoroughly that there was no fluctuation to sense.

And now the thing that had killed it was leaning on its own sword.

Lin Hao looked at Wu Zhangkong.

**The white robe was in tatters.** The long hair — the hair that had been at the grave, and in the corridor, and in the auction house, and always falling straight — was a dishevelled mess. He was standing on the Skyfrost Sword the way an old man stands on a cane, and he was **haggard**, and Lin Hao had never seen him haggard, and the seeing of it did something to Lin Hao's arithmetic that the eleven beasts had not managed.

He was still standing tall.

That was the part.

## Part 2: The Position

Lin Hao understood it in about two seconds, and he understood it the way he understood grain.

Wu Zhangkong was not standing **beside** him.

He was standing **in front of** him.

In twelve platform runs and one forest and one clearing with a sword in a skull, Wu Zhangkong had stood beside him. Beside was the position of a teacher. Beside was where you stood when you were showing somebody something, and correcting them, and saying *continue* without turning around.

In front was different.

In front was where you stood when there was a direction the danger was coming from, and you had decided that it was going to arrive at you first, and you had decided that this was acceptable.

"Teacher," Lin Hao said.

"We're returning."

"There are eleven."

"There were nineteen." Wu Zhangkong did not look back. His voice was his voice, which was somehow the most frightening thing in the clearing, because his voice was the one part of him that had not been damaged. "I have dealt with eight. You are going to hit the button on the back of your hand, and then I am going to hit mine, and we are going to have a conversation in a room with a floor that does not move."

"I can—"

"**Lin Hao.**"

It was the first time Wu Zhangkong had said his name in four months.

Not *you.* Not *boy.* Not *continue.*

"**Lin Hao.**"

He hit the button.

## Part 3: What It Cost

He came out of the platform with blood in his ears.

Not a great deal. That was the part the staff kept saying, in the flat reassuring voice of people who had seen it before and had a protocol — *not a great deal, this is expected, the spiritual strain of a spirit soul advancing that fast, sit down, drink this.* It came out of his ears and out of his nose and there was some in the corner of his mouth, and he had not felt any of it happen, and the not-feeling was the part that stayed with him.

Wu Zhangkong came out four seconds later.

He did not sit down. He stood in the room in a robe that had been white and was not, with his hair down around his shoulders in a way Lin Hao had never seen, and a staff member went toward him with the same protocol and the same flat voice, and Wu Zhangkong said, "See to him first," and the staff member saw to Lin Hao first.

Lin Hao watched him stand there for eleven minutes.

Eleven minutes. He counted. That was the thing his head did.

And when the staff member finally got to him, and looked at the robe, and said something about the arm, and Wu Zhangkong said *"it is not deep,"* Lin Hao understood — because he had spent four months learning to read this man and four days learning that the reading was not the person — that the arm was deep, and that *"it is not deep"* was the same category as *"Wait here"* at a grave, which was to say that it was the thing Wu Zhangkong said when he had decided that what was true was not going to be said.

## Part 4: The Conversation In The Room With A Floor

"You counted," Wu Zhangkong said.

"Yes."

"Eleven."

"Yes."

"There were nineteen."

"You said."

"I want to know what you did with the number." Wu Zhangkong was sitting now, because the staff had made him, and his arm was bandaged and he was not looking at it. "You counted eleven beasts and you did not run, and you did not draw, and you did not do the one thing I have spent four months teaching you to do, which is to find the answer. So I want to know what you were doing."

Lin Hao thought about it.

He thought about it honestly, because he had learned that the honest version was the only one that got anywhere.

"I was counting how long you could stand," he said.

Wu Zhangkong was quiet.

"You were leaning on your sword," Lin Hao said. "You'd dealt with eight and there were eleven left and you were leaning, and I worked out that you had perhaps four minutes, and that if I drew my sword I would be killed in about nine seconds and you would spend the remaining three minutes and fifty-one seconds of your four minutes trying to reach me, and then we would both be dead, and the arithmetic said the best thing I could do was **nothing.**" He stopped. "So I did nothing. And I counted. And then I hit the button when you told me to."

There was a silence in the room with the floor that did not move.

"That is the first time," Wu Zhangkong said, "you have chosen not to act, and been right."

"It doesn't feel like being right."

"No," Wu Zhangkong agreed. "It never does."

He looked at his bandaged arm.

"I stood in front of you," he said, "and I would like you to understand that I did it because there was nowhere else to stand, and not because I had decided anything about you. I have not decided anything about you. I have said *I don't know* twice and I meant it both times."

"You said my name."

"Yes."

"That was deciding something."

Wu Zhangkong looked at him for a long moment.

"Yes," he said. "It was."

## Part 5: What The Forge Told Him

He went back to the forge on the third day, because the forge was the only place in Heaven Dou City that had ever made sense to him, and because he had work to do and the work did not care what had happened in a forest.

He took a bar of Cloud Titanium and he started, and about forty strikes in he understood that something was different.

The metal was **answering faster.**

Not differently. Faster. He struck and the answer came back in less time than it had taken the week before, and the grain opened where he expected it and then opened **again**, a little further than he had asked, and he had to adjust, and the adjusting was easier than it had been.

He finished the bar and looked at it.

**two hundred and twenty-six.** It had been two hundred and sixty-two. Eight points, and he had bled out of his ears for them and had not felt it happen.

**Second-grade.**

He had been making fourth-grade. Fourth-grade was what a third-rank Grandmaster made, and third-grade was luck, and he had made **second-grade** without trying particularly hard, and he understood immediately what had happened because it was the same thing that had happened to Wulin, and Wulin had told him about it at the inn two nights ago in the voice of a boy who had been given something he had wanted for three years.

*"Mu Chen said when I can forge first-grade with a thirty percent success rate I'll be ready to try Spirit Refining. I thought that was three years. It's not three years."*

Lin Hao stood in a forge in a city with no skyscrapers and understood that the forest had done this.

Not the rings. Not the forty-eight years from the sword in the skull. The **seeing.** He had watched Wu Zhangkong compress his soul power so thoroughly that there was no fluctuation to sense, for eleven seconds, at forty metres, and he had been a blacksmith the whole time, and a blacksmith watches how a thing is made — and he had come out of that forest knowing something about metal that he had not gone in with.

He wrote it in the ledger.

*Second-grade. I have been making fourth-grade for eight months. I watched a man compress his soul power for eleven seconds and I came out of the forest able to make second-grade, and I cannot explain the mechanism, and I no longer need to.*

*A blacksmith watches how a thing is made. I watched the best thing I have ever seen made, and it went into my hands.*

Then the line at the bottom.

*Wu Zhangkong said my name. That was deciding something. He admitted it.*

*He stood in front of me and said it was because there was nowhere else to stand, and I believe him, and I also believe that a man does not stand in front of somebody he has not decided anything about.*

*I am going to find out who forged the blade. I am going to learn the Track. And I am going to be worth standing in front of, because I have seen what standing in front of costs, and I do not want anybody to pay it for me twice.*

He closed the book.

Gu Yue was in the yard when he came out, which was not a thing she did.

She was sitting on the low wall with her hands in her lap, and she was looking at the marks on his forearms, which were raised, and had been raised for four days, and which he had stopped noticing.

"One thousand three hundred and forty-four," she said.

"How did you—"

"You told me you would tell me." She did not look at his face. She was looking at the marks. "You said you would tell me even if it was not something I could use, because I am the only person you know who would want the true thing rather than the useful thing. So I have been keeping count, because you are not going to volunteer it, and because I would like at least one of us to know what is happening to you."

Lin Hao sat down on the wall beside her.

"I'm sorry."

"Don't apologise. It's arithmetic." She was quiet for a moment. "Forty-eight years from a sword. Forty-seven from an ape that is not in any record. And Wu Zhangkong came out of that forest leaning on his own sword with his robe in tatters, and he has said *I don't know* twice, and he is not a man who says that."

"No."

"So there are three of us now who don't know what you are." Gu Yue turned her head a few degrees, which with her was the equivalent of turning around. "Him. Me. And you."

"I know slightly more than you."

"Then tell me the slight bit."

Lin Hao looked at his forearms.

"The hawk recognised the forest," he said. "That was the first thing. Then it recognised a sword in a skull, and it *answered*, and I did not hear it — I want to be accurate about that, I did not hear anything, what happened was that the pulling stopped. And the beast that came after was not in any record, and it had gold hair, and Wu Zhangkong did not recognise it either." He stopped. "The slight bit is that I think the forest and the sword and the ape are the same something. And I think the hawk knows what it is. And I think it is waiting for me to be able to do something I cannot do yet."

Gu Yue was quiet for a long time.

"That is not a slight bit," she said.

"No."

"That is the most frightening thing anyone has said to me in this city." She stood up. "And I am still standing here, which I did not expect, and which I want you to see, because I have noticed things about you and you have not always noticed them back."

"I notice," Lin Hao said.

"No," Gu Yue said. "You read. Those are not the same thing, and I have been waiting four months to say so."

And she went in.



The **hawk-gold lock** at his temple lay across his cheekbone, and the **marks on his forearms** were raised and had been raised for four days and had stopped being something he noticed, and the hawk turned over in him — **one thousand three hundred and forty-four** — and did not pull toward anything.

It was waiting.

And for the first time, so was he.

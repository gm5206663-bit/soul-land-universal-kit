## Part 1: What Was Swept

The Alliance did not apologise.

Lin Hao had waited for it the way you wait for a second thunderclap, and it did not come, and by the fourth day he understood that it was not going to. Things had been said in rooms with good carpets. The carpets had won. Somewhere in a building he would never enter there was a drawer with a thin folder in it, and the drawer was closed, and that was the whole of the apology.

The Sky Ice had been the loudest thing in Skysea City for exactly one day. By the third day it was a rumour with a picture, and by the sixth nobody was printing the picture. A hall official came to take the tournament crates away and asked them, kindly, not to stand in the lane.

Lin Hao watched the crates go and thought that news behaved like weather. It arrived, it impressed somebody, and it moved on without leaving furniture. The armor had been astounding, and astounding without follow-up sizzles away — a small episode, closed. And the five of them, the fortnight itself, went the way such displays go when the door closes behind them: a blossom from a night-blooming cactus, open in the dark, the petals withering away before dawn, and the people who missed it never quite believing the ones who saw it.

"Nobody has come to look at the crates for two days," Xu Xiaoyan said, on the last morning. "I do mornings now. I count who stops."

"And?"

"Two." She held up two fingers. "Both of them wanted the toilet."

Xie Xie had spent the last two days in the mecha bay, which was not a place students were allowed and which he had therefore entered three times, once by the roof.

"There are eleven moving parts in a white mecha's shoulder," he announced, on the walk to the carrier. "I counted from the roof. Teacher. What does a mecha master actually train?"

"Design," Wu Zhangkong said. "Machining. And a licence the army issues and can take back."

"Good," said Xie Xie. "I've decided to be difficult about all three."

Gu Yue walked with her hands in her lap, which was how she walked when she was carrying something that had no weight, and looked at the city doing its ordinary business, and did not say anything, and Lin Hao had stopped needing her to.

Wulin carried Xu Xiaoyan's bag, and Xie Xie's bag, and a crate of Gu Yue's books that nobody had asked him to carry, and he did all of it at a jog, ahead of everybody, turning back every few steps to make sure nobody had been left behind, and it occurred to Lin Hao — with the small dull shock of a thing that had always been true — that Wulin had been doing this since he was six. Carrying. Turning back. Jogging.

Wu Zhangkong saw it too.

He stood at the carrier door with his arms in his sleeves and watched the boy make his third trip for other people's luggage, and he thought: he is up before the roosters every day of his life. He has never once complained about it. He is the hardest-working child I have ever taught and the least thanked for it, and they have just finished a fortnight in which a city tried to buy him and a government tried to file him, and he is ten.

*They are still children,* Wu Zhangkong thought. *I have been demanding. Perhaps some adjustments are in order.*

He did not say it out loud. He got in the carrier, and waited for all five of them to be seated, and then he said only, "Home."

## Part 2: The President's Back Room

Mu Chen had come to Skysea three days after the tournament, looked at all of them once, confirmed that nothing was broken, and gone home. Lin Hao had known then that there would be a summons. What he had not expected was that it would include Wu Zhangkong, or that the two adults would already be mid-conversation when he came in, with tea, and a place set for him, as though he were expected to be part of whatever this was.

Which, it turned out, he was.

"I have come to discuss his future," Wu Zhangkong said.

"Which one?" Mu Chen said. "There are two of them in this city now."

"Both. But mostly this one."

On the table between the teapot and the scale-weight tin there was a piece of metal. It was the size of a man's palm, or a little smaller, and it was the colour of a river in winter, and Lin Hao looked at it for two seconds and forgot the conversation entirely.

"Weigh it," Mu Chen said.

The counter scale was on the bench by the window. Lin Hao set the piece in the pan and moved the rider.

"Three hundred and eighty grams."

"Now tell me what's wrong with it."

He picked it up. It was a scale — an armor scale, a single overlapping plate of the kind that had built itself down Wu Zhangkong's forearm in a yard in Skysea City — and it was **spirit refined**, which he knew without being told the way he knew good bread, and it had failed, and that was the strangest part, because failed metal shouts and this piece was quiet.

He turned it in the light. The grain — there was grain in it, the way there is grain in wood, except closed, running in loops that came back on themselves — the grain turned on itself, near the lower edge. Once. A hairline. A place where, in some refining he could count backwards to, the metal had decided to be two opinions instead of one.

"There," he said. "The grain folds. Twelfth refining, or near it. It closed wrong and everything after closed around the closing."

He put it down. Mu Chen looked at Wu Zhangkong.

"Eighteen men have held that piece," Mu Chen said. "In eleven years. Two of them found the fold. Both were fifth rank."

The room was quiet. The tea steamed.

"Say the rest," Wu Zhangkong said.

"Fifth rank is Master Craftsman," Mu Chen said, to Lin Hao now. "The examination is spirit refinement, and spirit refinement is the metal you are looking at and the metal that boy talked about under my armor in Skysea City. Not the hammering you know. The next thing after it. One-word battle armor is every piece thousand refined — every piece, no exceptions, a suit of it and not a horseshoe of it. Two-word is spirit refined." He turned his cup a quarter turn. "A boy in this city decided out loud, under my window, that you are going to make that metal. I have decided to find out if he was joking."

"He wasn't," Lin Hao said.

"No. He didn't strike me as a boy who jokes." Mu Chen drank. "There's a sentence I said to Duan Xuan in the spring," Mu Chen said, "about the other boy in your class. It is true of this one as well, so I am going to say it again, properly, because the first time I said it lazily." He turned his cup a quarter turn. "Duan Xuan asked me whether a boy with a soul master's talent should be spending himself on a forge at all. I told him: the foundation of a battle armor master stems from being a first-rank blacksmith. That is the whole steering of it. Convince a boy of that, and you can point him at the greatest occupation on the continent without him arriving there empty-handed."

He looked at Lin Hao.

"You have been a first-rank blacksmith since you were six. Not because you knew what you were building. Because you were small and couldn't see over the bench and kept hitting anyway." He glanced at Wu Zhangkong. "They tell me you have decided to guide rather than oppose. This is the epitome of it."

"Then I'll add my part," Wu Zhangkong said. "I am not going to tell you to take the fifth-rank examination. I am going to tell you it exists, and that the president will tell you when you are ready, and that he is harder to fool than I am."

"I put him up for the tournament when he wasn't ready," Mu Chen said, "and I'm told it went well. I don't trust it. You'll take the examination when the metal tells me you will, and not one week sooner."

Lin Hao looked at the scale on the table. Three hundred and eighty grams. The fold in the twelfth refining that two fifth ranks had missed and he had found in under a minute, because finding it was the one thing he had done in this room, the whole of his life, that needed no explaining.

"Can I borrow it," he said.

"No," Mu Chen said. Then: "Yes. One week. If you can tell me something about it in a week that I don't know, bring it back. If you can't — bring it back anyway."

On the way out, through the yard, Zhang Yangzi was on the loading dock with a manifest, because Zhang Yangzi was always on the loading dock with a manifest now, and the Association ran better for it and had not said so.

"You're back," he said.

"We're back."

"Nobody died."

"Nobody died."

"Good." He ticked something. "The east crates were wet. Tell your teacher the seal on the third one was mine."

## Part 3: The Bench

They had the members' forge in the evenings, and they had had it for a year, and this was the last evening before the term restarted, and Lin Hao wanted to hit something until it made sense.

Wulin wanted the same thing. It was one of the forty or so things they had never had to explain to each other.

They lit the lamp and put the irons in, and for a while there was nothing in the room but the two of them and two pieces of ordinary stock and the oldest rhythm either of them knew: Mang Tian's lane, the scale and quench-water, the year they were six. Wulin worked left-handed to make it fair. Lin Hao worked the way he always worked, which was too fast, and on the ninth strike his shoulder dropped and the sound went dull.

"Flat," Wulin said.

Lin Hao stopped. He turned around slowly.

"You sound like him."

"I am him," Wulin said. "Nine years of practice." He nodded at the anvil. "Again."

Lin Hao did it again, and did not go flat, and the sound came back, and something in his chest sat down that had been standing up for a fortnight.

They finished the bars and packed them and sat on the bench with the lamp down low, and Wulin said it, the way he said the real things, which was at the floor.

"I've wanted to wear one since I was six," he said. "Battle armor. Since before I knew what a soul ring cost. And on Tuesday I saw one, and it wasn't what I thought."

"What was it?"

"Metal." Wulin looked at his hands. "It was a man, but the *of-him* part was metal, and the metal was made by somebody, and every piece of it was made by somebody, and I stood under it and the only thought I had — the whole time, while Xie Xie was shouting and you were doing arithmetic — was *who made this and what did they know.*" He rubbed a thumb over an old scar. "I've wanted to wear one since I was six. I think now I want to make them. Xie Xie said it out loud so it's law, but I've been thinking it since the yard and I didn't want to say it in case saying it made it smaller."

"It won't," Lin Hao said. "I said a word on a form and it became a System. Saying things makes them bigger. That's the whole of my year."

Wulin laughed — the short one, the real one. Then he was quiet again.

"I had two commissions while you were at the tournament," he said. "Both went in the tin on Friday. And the one from the month before — I was behind on that one. It went in too."

"The whole of them?"

"The whole of both." Wulin shrugged, embarrassed by nothing, which was the most Wulin thing about him. "It's the most money I've ever moved at once. Eleven years of white ones, if you do it that way, and I don't do it that way, because she isn't a spirit soul, she's Na'er, and tins don't buy her back anyway. They find her. Somebody knows which door she went through and money opens doors in this Federation, I've checked, it's what it's *for*—"

He stopped himself. He turned his hands over.

"Sorry," he said. "Friday makes me loud."

"I know," Lin Hao said. "I do the Fridays with you. You just never noticed it's the same bench."

Wulin looked at him, and something crossed his face that had no name in either of their vocabularies, and he dealt with it the way he dealt with everything, which was by being practical at it.

"If I come out of Shrek with armor with four seams and a join under the arm that leaks when it rains—"

"You won't."

"I know," Wulin said. "But if I do — I'm going to be very good at it, and I'm going to be furious about it the entire time, and I wanted to say that now, while it's just us, in writing if possible."

"Now you've said it," Lin Hao said. "It's law."

They banked the forge and went out into a street that smelled of salt and scale, and that was the good smell, the Glorybound smell, the one that meant home had been rebuilt in a bigger city, and neither of them said anything else and neither of them needed to.

## Part 4: What Gu Yue Said

She was outside the Association when he came down the steps with the scale wrapped in cloth in his bag, and it was evening, and the lamps were coming up, and Lin Hao noted — without any surprise at all, which was itself a new thing — that she had known when he would be finished and had not told him she was coming.

They walked. That was the arrangement now, or the not-arrangement. They walked, and the walking was the conversation, and at some point one of them would talk.

"You held it for four hours," Gu Yue said, "and you didn't hit it."

It was not a question, so he did not treat it as one. "It was finished."

"Mm." Two steps. "You like things for what they do. Every day I have known you, a thing has been worth what it did. Tonight you carried a piece of metal around a city because of what it *is.*" She looked at the middle distance. "I've been waiting nine months for you to like a thing for what it is. I thought it would be a person. It was a cracked scale with a fold in it."

"It was very good metal."

"Yes," she said. "It was."

The harbour went on being the harbour. Out past the breakwater a gull turned with the wind, and she watched it do that, and he watched her watch it, and the not-talking was easy now, which frightened him more than the other kind would have.

"Line one hundred and nine," Gu Yue said.

"I know."

"Is it staying unfinished?"

"Until finishing it is true."

She nodded, once, and he understood that she had not asked in order to be answered. She had asked so that he would know she had read it, and that the reading had been kept, and that whatever the sentence had been going to be was still sitting somewhere between them with its coat on, and that she was not in any hurry about it, and — this was the part he could not have put into the book even if he had tried — neither was he.

Then she stopped walking, and gave him the truth, the way she always eventually did, flat as a scale reading.

"Wu Zhangkong is going to be gentler with you," she said. "He decided on the platform home. You didn't see it because you were watching Wulin carry bags. He's going to ease the schedule and start asking you how you are, and you are going to hate it."

"I know."

"You don't," Gu Yue said. "You've never been gentled. There's no line in that book for it. You'll want to be efficient about it, the way you're efficient about everything, and it won't work, because the entire point of it is the part you can't spend." She resumed walking. "That's all. I've been carrying that since Tuesday and it was heavy."

"Gu Yue."

She paused, half a step ahead, and did not turn around, and waited.

"The metal," he said. "When I make it — the real grade, the spirit refined. You'll be the first person who already knew I would."

She stood there for a second with her back to him and the lamps coming on along the breakwater.

"I know," she said. "I've known since the yard."

And she went, and he let her go, and did not add it to anything.

## Part 5: What Lin Hao Wrote

He wrote the number first. It never asked anything of him, and tonight he had needed that, and he had noticed himself needing it, and he wrote that down too, later, when it had stopped being true.

*Two hundred and ninety-five.*

*One thousand four hundred and twelve.*

Then he unwrapped the scale and set it on the bench under the lamp, and looked at the fold in it for a while, at the place where a refining he could count to had closed on its own opinion, and everything after had closed around the closing.

*One hundred and ten. Three hundred and eighty grams, spirit refined, failed. The fold is in the twelfth refining and I found it in under a minute, and two men past fifth rank have held this piece and missed it, and I am not telling that line to anybody except this book, because it is not a boast. It is a measurement. It is the only measurement I have ever taken of the distance between where I am standing and a road I have just been shown the whole length of.*

*Fifth rank. Master Craftsman. Spirit refinement. The foundation is the rank I started at, and I started at it when I was six and could not see over the bench, and I did not know the bench was the bottom of this particular ladder, and it was, and I have been on it the entire time.*

*Mu Chen will tell me when. Not one week sooner. That is the correct man to have guarding a door.*

He turned the page, and line one hundred and nine was on the other side of the turn, unfinished, and he let it stay there. The gap did not close. It did not widen either. It sat in the book the way it sat in him, with its coat on, and he found — and this was the thing he had not known in the morning — that a person could carry an unfinished sentence and still sleep.

He closed the book.

The hawk turned over in him, one thousand four hundred and twelve years of it, and did not pull toward anything. In the morning there would be school, and adjustments, and a teacher who was going to learn to be gentle with five children who had just spent a fortnight being treated as assets, and there would be, on his bench, a piece of failed armor that the president of the Eastsea Blacksmith's Association had trusted to nobody in eleven years and had now trusted to a boy for a week.

Three years to Shrek. He wrote nothing about the size of that, because the size of it was not a number yet. It was a road, and he had just been shown the whole length of it, and he was ten years old, and he had never in his life been so pleased to be at the bottom of something.

He slept.

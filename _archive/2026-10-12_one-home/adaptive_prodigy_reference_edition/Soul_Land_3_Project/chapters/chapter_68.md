## Part 1: The Last Exam

The exam was survival, and the reward was announced in the yard with the whole class lined up.

"Your reward will either be a duel with me," Wu Zhangkong said, "or a spar against the other three. Your choice."

"Teacher Wu, don't be so heartless," Xie Xie said.

The rebellion platform took them at noon. It had taken them at noon for three years, and it held no pressure for them at all any more, so they went deep, because that was what the exam was actually for and Wu Zhangkong had stopped pretending it was about anything else. The lions found them first — a pride, a king at the thousand-year mark — and Xie Xie said the thing he always said now, the house prayer of class zero: "I still remember that time they slaughtered us. It's our turn now." It was their turn. That took an evening.

Deeper, the forest went dark at noon. The canopy shut. Gu Yue's wind birds came back to her hand blind.

"Every level of the spirit ascension platform is connected," she said, low. "This is Pagoda information, so hear it once. A powerful soul beast guards the barrier between levels. Only after defeating it can we cross over. Very few people ever have."

"And the platform isn't a true illusory world," she went on. "It's a mix of illusions and reality. Remember that. It matters later."

Lin Hao, at the back, said nothing. He had been at the back for a week, because the wall in him had come down to the last door and everything on the far side of the last door was listening, and the class had learned to let him be where he was.

## Part 2: The Bear

The guardian of the elementary level came at them out of the dark-gold light, and it was a Duskgold Dreadclaw Bear, and in the ordinary way of things no one with less than five rings should have been able to kill it.

The class zero of three years did not fight in the ordinary way of things.

It began with a nick. The bear's claw met Tang Wulin's claw and came away with a flaw the width of a hair, and the forest rang with its outrage — and the flaw had not been luck. Lin Hao had gone still at the start, the way he went still, and read the bear's golden light the way he read grain, and the light had a seam, because everything has a seam, because everything fails; the three parts exist so the eight can be brave about it, and he had said so once on a roof, at ten, about a machine.

"There," he had said, and pointed, and Wulin had put the whole fight after that through the place Lin Hao's finger had been.

Even so, the bear nearly took him. It ignored everyone — icicles, fire, three Xie Xies — and charged Wulin like a small mountain with a grievance, and in the half-second when the dark-gold light had Wulin's soul power too frenzied to answer, Xie Xie was there instead, and the bear's claw went through him, and he came apart protecting his captain with his last breath and his eyes saying the only thing they ever said at moments like that: *avenge me.*

Wulin avenged him. Gu Yue's four elements came down as one column of light — blue, red, yellow, green, four fused into one — and Wulin went up it, and the final strike opened the bear's neck to the bone.

The soul bone appeared where it fell, real, dark-gold, humming.

"Since a soul bone appeared," Gu Yue said quietly, "that means the beast we just killed was a spirit ascension platform guardian. Each level has one. The Pagoda builds them stronger than anything else on the level, and it costs — energy, and something real to build around. The true reward for killing it is the soul bone. You can't bring it outside the platform. You fuse with it here, or not at all."

"Wulin," she said. "It's a right-claw bone. External. It will complement the thing you already carry. Nobody else here can hold it — the rest of us would be devoured."

Wulin fused with it there, in the dark of the heart-forest, while Xie Xie's borrowed body reassembled itself nearby and complained. The change was invisible for an hour. Then his claw tipped itself in dark-gold, once, and five streaks of it cut the air a metre past his hand — and a third of his soul power went with them.

"Expensive," Wulin said, flexing.

"Everything worth having is," said Gu Yue.

## Part 3: The Barrier

They could have left. The exam was won, the bear was dead, the reward was fused. The exit was an hour south.

They went north, to the barrier, because class zero had never once in its life taken the south exit, and because Xie Xie said "it's our turn" about things older than lions, and because of the five of them, one had been standing at a wall for a week with his ear against it.

The barrier between the elementary level and the intermediate did not look like a door. It looked like a wall of winter — frost on the air itself, a seam of cold running floor to canopy, and cold water moving under the frost, uphill, in a current that had no riverbed.

The guardian came through it without hurry.

It was long as a lane and narrow as a blade, a dragon jiao of the deep-water kind, ice and water in it together, ten thousand years of patience wearing a body. The frost of the barrier was its hem. It looked at the five of them the way winter looks at a lit window.

"Lin Hao," Wu Zhangkong said — for the teacher was there; the platform allowed the attending master, and he had come, because he had known since the yard what his student was carrying — "terms."

"It's mine," Lin Hao said. "The class holds the edges. Nobody crosses the hem."

The fight lasted eleven minutes and cost him everything he had.

Not the Union — that door stays shut; it is the one thing it is for later. He spent the hawk to embers, and the hawk gave until giving was all it was, two and a half thousand years of storm poured out as talons, as wind, as the flat crack of pressure that had ended a hundred and sixty-four lines. Once, near the end, the Talon met the hem of the barrier's cold and came back from it black-edged — storm carrying winter in its teeth, the adaptation doing what adaptation does, mid-fight, without asking anyone. He spent the Track until it was not movement but habit. He used all five strokes, and the Unwritten Stroke twice, and the third time the jiao's tail took him across the clearing and broke his left arm against a tree, and he got up with the arm hanging and read the seam, because everything fails, and the dragon's seam was where the ice believed the water.

The four held the edges. Gu Yue's elements pulled the frost off him twice. Xiaoyan's ice wheel turned the tail once. Xie Xie bled in three places and stayed. Wulin caught him once, out of the air, and put him back inside the hem.

The sword went in at the seam, to the hilt, through ice that had never in ten thousand years been argued with.

The jiao did not scream. It unwound — all of it, the whole length of the lane of it — into water, and the water stood still, and in the still water there was a core.

It was not a soul bone. It was a spirit soul — a true one, ten-thousand-year, the real thing the Pagoda had built its intermediate guardian around, ice and water braided into a single coiled point of light, hanging in the empty air where the barrier's cold had been.

"It can't leave," Gu Yue said. Her voice had gone very flat, which was her awe. "Platform law. Whatever the guardian was built around, fuses here or is lost. That's the rule. It's the same rule as the bone."

"I know the rule," Lin Hao said. "I've been standing at the door of this rule since I was nine."

He looked at the class. Wulin, holding his own closed side. Xie Xie, bleeding and grinning. Xiaoyan, small and steady. Gu Yue, whom no element had ever surprised, surprised.

"You're the second person who already knew I would," he said — to her, only to her — and put his hand into the light.

## Part 4: The Month

The month belongs to the others. That is the law of what he is: the absorption takes him wholly, and the world does not pause its clock, and canon — the world's own record — goes on without him. The month still had weather in it, and mornings, and errands. The stations were lived, not listed:

Xu Xiaoyan crossed rank 30 on the ninth day, at dawn, alone in the yard, in frost, with her staff planted and her breath going up in the flat columns that mean a soul is moving house. She stood a long while at her own wall with her own door in it, and then she went in to breakfast and ate everything and began reading — everything the Pagoda library would lend a class-three associate on second spirit souls, which proved to be eleven pages and a footnote. So she wrote to the library, and the library wrote back, and by the twentieth day there were parcels. On the tenth day she was rank 30 and two rings and a question, and she carried the question like a lamp.

The Azure-veined Vine came up at auction on the twelfth day, in a room that smelled of other people's coats. The tin — four years deep, both boys' commissions — was finally deep enough, and Wulin sat very still through two lots he did not want while the number climbed, and put his paddle up once, and once was the whole story. He bought it, wrapped it in the cloth he had carried ready for a month, and set it beside the tendon, the marrow, and the fruit. Four items. One set. Complete, and waiting, because a seal breaks on purpose and fourteen was coming and not yet.

Xie Xie filed his apprentice papers at the Association on the fifteenth: first-rank mecha craftsman, candidate. The clerk read the sponsor line twice, because sponsors are usually asked first. The bay master had signed it without being asked, and when Xie Xie went to thank her she said, "Bring nothing," which was the highest thing she knew how to say.

Gu Yue kept the 5:40 slots. All thirty-one mornings. She did not say his name at the platform and did not need to; the elements went off in their row, exact — water, ice, fire, wind, in the order that is hardest — and the gap between the third element and the fourth held whatever it held, and she booked the next morning, and the next. On the seventeenth morning the attendant on duty left the row to her entirely and went to stand at the far end of the hall, which at that facility counted as a ceremony.

Zhang Yangzi ran the dock. A letter crossed the city line north: *the little one made rank 30. The other little one is asleep for a month and nobody will say at what. The sea is the sea. — ZYZ.*

And the hawk did not perch anywhere that month. That was the strange one, for anyone who knew where to look: the rail outside the yard stood empty, and the bird that had owned it was nowhere in the city — because it was in the room, in him, in the deep water where he was. A spirit is part of the whole man; where he goes, it goes; what feeds him, feeds it. What that meant, the city found out on the thirty-first day.

Wu Zhangkong taught the four, and on every one of the thirty-one days he stood for the last hour of the afternoon outside the room where Lin Hao's body slept and its soul was elsewhere, doing what the yard could not see, which was nothing at all, on purpose, an hour of nothing, stationed. On the thirtieth day Shen Yi's card arrived, two lines: *You said the calendar would decide. It has. Bring me the story. — S.*

And Mu Chen came twice, looked at the sleeping boy the way he looked at metal, and said nothing either time, and left the door ajar, because drafts matter to things that are changing.

## Part 5: Day Thirty-One

He woke at dawn and the room was fogged — every surface beaded, the window white — and he was lying in frost that was not cold to him, and the first breath he took came out of him as a slow drift of white that fell, instead of rising.

The class was in the doorway. They had felt it in the yard, all of them, the way you feel a door open somewhere in a house in winter.

Lin Hao stood, and the room stood with him — the frost sliding off the walls in a sheet, the air going still and clear and deep, the hem of something vast settling into the floorboards and stopping politely at the threshold. That was the domain, and it would never fully close again; it would only ever be folded.

The sword came to his hand, and it was not the sword he had carried at ten, or at thirteen, or for one minute at the barrier. The storm was still in it — the wind and the lightning, the whole loud first act of his life — but under the storm now there was deep water, and under the water there was ice, and the ice went down a very long way. The blade had gone blue-black at the core the way winter rivers do, and it rang, when he turned it, with a note that was low and new.

"Frost Abyss," he said, and did not know he was going to, and knew immediately that it was right. The name had been waiting in the metal the way a seam waits.

The pressure came next, uninvited: the fourth ring's skill pouring one breath of itself into the room, and the class — rank 30 and 33 and 28 and 31, veterans of a hundred platform hours — all stepped back, all at once, without deciding to. Domineer. He pulled it back in like a hand closing, and apologised with his eyes, and they unfroze.

Then Wulin did the thing that mattered. He looked at his best friend's face and did not know him for one full second — because the boy on the bed was older, sixteenish, jaw cut, eyes gone still and deep — and then he did know him, and his face did several things in a row and finished on the verge of tears he was too old for and did not care.

"You grew," Wulin said, ruined.

"Some," Lin Hao said. His own voice had a new floor in it.

Gu Yue had not stepped back with the others. She had stepped forward, inside the hem, into the domain, the only one — the cold slid around her like a river around a stone it had known all its life — and she looked at the almost-sixteen face with its new stillness and said, flat as a scale reading, in front of everyone:

"You're late. Thirty-one days, not thirty."

And that was her whole speech, and it was in different ink, and it went into him deeper than the ring had.

Xie Xie looked at him for a long moment and then addressed the ceiling. "He comes out of a month asleep looking like his own older brother. I hope everyone is taking this as seriously as I am."

Xu Xiaoyan said nothing at all. She wrote a number in the new notebook, boxed it, and showed the page to no one, and the number had nothing to do with rank.

The window was still fogged from the waking, and it made a mirror of itself, and the mirror showed him the month's other work. The third winter's boy had gone into the water, and a young man had come up out of it: height he had not earned by waiting, weight through the chest and arm that he had, the marks on his forearms gone blue-black at the edges the way winter rivers go, the hawk-gold at his temples carrying a colder thread now, like light through ice. He looked, as the clerk used to say, sixteen in the face. It had stopped being a saying. It was a measurement.

They went to the Association that same morning, because affection, in class zero, was measured in kilograms.

The punch machine's ceiling was five thousand. It had been the ceiling for forty years, and the number everybody knew was three thousand nine hundred and ninety-eight — a dragon claw's, set at nine. Lin Hao put his bare fist on the plate, unhurried, and the needle went up through his old floor like a rumour, through the dragon claw's number without pausing for it, and stopped. Not at anything in him. At the machine's end of numbers.

The attendant wrote 5,000+ in the log and looked at the boy the way the Hall of Heroes had once looked at him, which is to say: it ran out.

The arm told the rest. The jiao had broken it on the eleventh minute; the month had set it in the first week; and the last week had done what no splint does — finished the deep work, the mending going the whole way to new, the deep water seeing to its own, as deep water does. Cold could not find him either. He had stood in his own frost and felt it as furniture.

The hawk came out at the yard, when they walked him out into the morning, and the yard learned what the month had done to the other half of him. It landed on his arm heavier than it had ever been, and its feathers had changed: the deep storm-slate gone dark at the edges, every feather tipped in the blue-black of winter rivers, so that folded it looked like weather and spread it looked like the inside of the cold. It had spent the month where he had spent it, fed on what he was fed; the adaptation does not distinguish between the man and what is joined to him. The bird that had gone into the water was not the bird that came out. One ladder, climbed from two sides — and the class, who had heard the teacher say it at the fence a year before, stood in the yard and watched the second side arrive.

The Unwritten Stroke had changed register as well. What had worked because it did not exist now also did not exist in the cold, and he did not know yet what that meant, so it went into the book as a question. The book was getting more questions in it. He considered that healthy, at thirteen, and did not examine it further.

And the clerk, having written 5,000+, pulled a requisition form on her own initiative: the Association would be commissioning a machine with a higher ceiling, and she wanted her name on the order.

The book, that night: rank 45. Spiritual power 2,705. Hawk 3,055 — a month of embers, regrown in deep water, and it came out of the water dressed for it. Line 165, in different ink: *the guardian of the intermediate level, ten thousand years, ice and water, defeated at the seam. My arm has mended. The sword has a new name and so do I, a little.* And line one hundred and nine, on the far side of the page turn, unfinished, coat on, one button undone, unspent — some things do not change when everything else does.

Wu Zhangkong came at the end, and looked, for a long time, at what his student had become, and there was appetite in it still, and something quieter under the appetite that he did not say.

"The reward stands," he said. "A duel with me. When you can hold a sword."

"Sir," Lin Hao said. "Next month."

"Next month," Wu Zhangkong agreed, and left, and did not smile where anyone could use it against him.

He was thirteen. The wall was behind him. The road had turned to ice and kept going.

## Part 1: Out

They came out of the boxes into a room full of people who had been watching, which is the worst way to arrive anywhere.

Wulin was out first and shouting Xie Xie's name before his eyes had adjusted to the light. Zhang Yangzi was standing where he had been put. Wang Jinxi was twitching in a way nobody was going to mention. Gu Yue was sitting on the floor with her back against the wall and her face the colour of the wall, and she had not asked for help and had not been offered any, which suited everybody.

Lin Hao came out fourth and stood up, and the room looked at him, and he looked back, because he had decided a long time ago that looking away was the same as apologising.

Xie Xie said, "I, I'm fine. Just a bit sore."

He was not fine. His jacket was dark all the way down one side and his left leg would not take his weight, and he said it in the voice of a boy who had been asked a question in class and had not done the reading.

Wulin's legs went. He sat down hard on the tile and stayed there, and the exhaustion arrived all at once the way it does when the fear has finished, and somebody put a blanket on him that he did not thank anybody for.

Long Hengxu looked, for the first time in the four months Lin Hao had known him, like a man who had mislaid a number.

Wu Zhangkong looked exactly as he always looked.

## Part 2

The marks had taken on a faint blue at the edges since the crossing. He noticed it in the morning light, turning his forearm over, the way you notice a bruise changing colour — the same shape, a different weather in it.
: The Assessment

The staff member spoke for a while, and Lin Hao listened, because he had learned that a man praising you is a man telling you what he has decided you are, and that is useful information.

"Geniuses," he said. "They truly are geniuses worth nurturing. Are you sure these children are only ten?"

He went through them one at a time. He went through Wulin's hand — the transformed right hand, the strength several times higher than normal, the hundred-year beast killed in a strike and the thousand-year one crushed. He went through Gu Yue — the elements at the right moments, and the fact that she had held up **five hundred kilograms of crystallised boy** with her arms and her spine, and that she had done it without pausing to work out whether the pain would be real.

"That decision," he said, "is the one I would write down. People do not have time in those moments to consider whether a thing is real or virtual. She decided anyway, and she decided instantly, and she would have decided the same way if it were real. That is not a cultivation achievement. That is a *person.*"

And then he got to Xie Xie, and his voice changed, and Lin Hao understood that this was the part the man had actually been building toward.

"He knew he was no match," the staff member said. "He had no soul power. He had a leg that would not carry him. And he stayed, and he went round and round that crystallised boy like a dog round a fire, and he did not press his button, and he was sent out only when his comrade was free." He looked at the four of them. "They are ten years old. With a friendship like that, how could their coordination not be this deep?"

Nobody said anything, because there was nothing to add.

And then the man turned to Lin Hao, and Lin Hao felt the assessment arrive the way he felt a strike arriving — a fraction before it landed.

"And you," the staff member said, "were not supposed to be there."

"No."

"You were in for ninety-four minutes. The elementary session is forty. You fought a pack that a full squad of adults would have withdrawn from, you carried two people out of a forest, and your spiritual power when you came out was higher than when you went in." He said it without heat, the way you read a ledger. "Do you know what the Spirit Pagoda does with information like that?"

"Files it," Lin Hao said.

"We file it," the staff member agreed. He looked at Lin Hao for a moment longer than the sentence required. "Somebody already has."

## Part 3: What the Rain Was

It happened at about three in the morning, in the dormitory, and it woke him up the way the growth did — not with a sound, with an *arrival.*

Except this time there was a sound.

It was very small and very high, like a wet finger on the rim of a glass, and it was coming from inside his chest, and it went on for perhaps four seconds and then stopped, and in the four seconds the hair on both of Lin Hao's arms stood up and every loose piece of metal in the room — a nail on the sill, a buckle, Wulin's spare hammer head on the floor — ticked once against the wood.

Then it was quiet, and his heart was going, and he understood with total clarity that the hawk had done something.

**Nine hundred and six years.** The hawk had crossed something in the night, and it had not asked him first.

He had not counted it. He had not been counting anything. He had come in out of a forest at half past ten and eaten a bowl of rice and slept, and somewhere in the sleeping the hawk had crossed a line it had been approaching for four years, and the crossing had made a noise.

Lin Hao sat up and held out his hand.

Nothing happened, which was correct, because nothing was supposed to happen — the hawk was not a dog and did not come when called, and it had never once in four years done anything because he wanted it to. But the air above his palm was *different.* It had a texture. It was the particular wrongness that hangs over a forge floor on a dry day before you touch the bellows, and it was there, and it was not wind.

He had smelled rain in the hawk for a chapter and a half. This was the rain arriving.

**Wind and lightning.** He had known the words since he was six, because the sword had them and the sword's name had them, and he had never once questioned where the lightning was supposed to come from, because a six-year-old does not audit his own martial soul. Stormbringer. Wind and lightning. And all this time the wind had been the hawk's and the lightning had been the sword's, and the two of them had been fed by each other, and the hawk had simply been taking its time.

Now it had caught up.

He drew the Stormbringer in the dark, because he was not going to wait until morning, and the blade came out and the crackle on it — the faint crackle that had been faint for four years, that he had stopped noticing the way you stop noticing a clock — was not faint.

It ran the length of the edge in a thin blue line, and it made a sound like a small piece of paper being torn very slowly, and the hair on his arms was still up, and Lin Hao sat on the side of his bunk with his own sword in his hand and grinned at it like an idiot, because there was nobody to see.

"Right," he said, to a soul that could not hear him. "So that's what you were saving up for."

The hawk did not answer. It had never answered. But it turned over in him — slow, heavy, unhurried, the way something turns over when it has been travelling a long time and has arrived, and has no intention of explaining itself.

## Part 4: What It Was Not

Wu Zhangkong was in the yard at five, which meant he had not slept either.

Lin Hao showed him the sword without being asked, because that was the only honest way to start the conversation, and because the thin blue line along the edge was not something you could keep in a pocket.

Wu Zhangkong looked at it for a long time.

"How old," he said.

"Nine hundred and six. As of about three this morning. It made a noise."

"A noise."

"Like a wet finger on a glass. And all the metal in the room ticked." Lin Hao let the sword down. "It's the lightning. It's always been in the sword — the sword's had wind and lightning since the day I woke it up, and the hawk's only ever had wind, and I never thought about that, not once, because when you're six you don't check. The hawk's been eating the sword's essence for four years and it's finally grown the half it was missing."

Wu Zhangkong was quiet for a while.

"Do you know what that means," he said, at last.

"That my sword's going to hurt more."

"That your soul is going to catch up to your sword," Wu Zhangkong said, "and then pass it, and then you are going to find out what a soul with two attributes and a nine-hundred-year age can bestow." He put his hands behind his back. "Which is not a ring. Not yet."

"I know it's not a ring."

"Say it."

Lin Hao looked at him. "It's not a ring. A thousand years gets the ring. I'm ninety-four short and I'm at the top of the second band and the wall is still the wall, and nothing that happened last night changed any of that." He paused. "Is that what you wanted."

"That is what I wanted." Wu Zhangkong said it flatly, and then, because he was Wu Zhangkong, and because the kindness came sideways and always had: "A boy who gets a new attribute at ten and immediately starts shopping for a third ring is a boy who is going to die at fourteen. You did not shop. That is worth more than the lightning."

"And the lightning."

"The lightning is very good," Wu Zhangkong said. "Go and find out what it does. And Lin Hao."

"Sir."

"When it does the thing you have not worked out yet — and it will, because souls do not acquire attributes for decoration — do not tell anyone in this academy." He started toward the gate, and stopped. "There is a man in this city with your name on a card, and he did not put it in the ledger where cards go. You are not a curiosity yet. I would prefer you stayed that way until you are too large to be one."

## Part 5: What the Lightning Did

He found out at the Association, on a Saturday, in the side forge, with Mu Chen two rooms away and the door shut.

The first thing was the obvious thing: the Gale Talon, laid along the steel the way he had laid it a hundred times, came out with a sound he had never heard from it — not the wind's hiss but something sharper, a crack at the beginning and a crack at the end, like a whip breaking twice.

The second thing was that it cut deeper. Not much. An inch where it had been three quarters. He measured it, because he was his father's son, and he wrote the measurement down, and then he sat back on his heels and thought about *why,* because an inch was not an inch.

Wind pushes. That was the whole of the Gale Talon — a ribbon of moving pressure laid along an edge so the edge arrived faster than the arm could send it.

Lightning does not push. Lightning *goes.* It takes the shortest path between two things and it does not ask whether there is anything in the way, and the shortest path between a sword and a target is not the path the wind takes, because the wind has to travel and the lightning only has to arrive.

So the Talon was not one thing any more. It was two things happening at once: the wind shoving the edge forward, and something else getting there first.

The third thing happened by accident, at about noon, and it was the one he thought about for a year.

He was tired. He had been at it for six hours and his arms were gone and he was on the last bar of the day, and he laid the Talon along the steel out of pure habit without particularly meaning to, and the crack came out of the blade and jumped — **jumped** — a hand's width of empty air, and put itself into the bar, and the bar rang like a struck bell and split along the grain he had already read.

He had not touched it.

Lin Hao stood in the side forge with a smoking bar and a sword and no arms, and understood that the lightning was not an upgrade to the wind. It was a different *idea* about distance. The wind had to be laid along the edge. The lightning only had to be *let go.*

"Right," he said, to nobody, because Mu Chen was two rooms away and would have had questions. "So that's the second thing you do."

He sat down on the floor with his back against the anvil and his sword across his knees, and he did not cultivate, and he did not count anything, and he did not do arithmetic, because there was nothing to do arithmetic on. The rank was thirty and the rank would be thirty for a long time, and the hawk was nine hundred and six and climbing toward a thousand, and the sword had two voices in it now instead of one, and the second voice had only just started talking.

The wall was still the wall.

But for the first time in four months, Lin Hao could hear something on the other side of it.

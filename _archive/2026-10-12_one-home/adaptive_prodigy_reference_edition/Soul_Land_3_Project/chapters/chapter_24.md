## Part 1: The White Spire

The Eastsea Spirit Pagoda was the only building in the city that did not have to try.

Everything else in Eastsea City was in some kind of argument — the harbour with the tide, the market with the morning, the academy with its own roof. The Pagoda simply stood there, nine storeys of pale stone going up out of the centre of the city like something that had arrived fully formed and had been waiting, patiently, for the buildings around it to make up their minds. Lin Hao counted the storeys twice and got nine both times, which he noted, because he had been told as a small child that the Pagoda had seven, and because the difference between seven and nine was the sort of thing his father would have wanted him to notice.

"You've counted it," Xie Xie said.

"Twice."

"It's nine. My family's been coming here for six generations and it's always been nine and nobody knows why everyone says seven." Xie Xie was vibrating. He had been vibrating since breakfast. "Hao. Hao. I've wanted this since I was *four.* My father took my brother when my brother was eleven and my brother came out and said there was a mouse the size of a house and I did not sleep for a week. A *week.* I ate dinner at my brother's bedside."

"That's a very specific memory."

"It's the best memory I have."

Wu Zhangkong had brought them and had said nothing at all on the walk, which the seven of them had interpreted, variously, as ominous, as typical, and — in Gu Yue's case, Lin Hao thought — as a courtesy. At the door he stopped.

"Inside, you will be measured before you enter," he said. "The measurement is not for you. It is for the platform, which uses it to decide what to put in front of you." He looked at the seven of them, and his eyes stopped, for a fraction of a second, on Lin Hao. "Some of you will not like what it decides."

"Is that a warning?" Lin Hao asked.

"It's an observation," Wu Zhangkong said, and went inside.

## Part 2: Intake

The intake room was on the third floor and smelled of cold metal and something faintly sweet, like hot dust, and there was a woman behind a counter with a form and a machine, and the form was long, and the machine was not.

"Names," the woman said, and took them, and wrote them, and then said, "Hands on the plate, one at a time, and hold still, and don't be alarmed by the number, the number is only a number."

Xie Xie went first, because Xie Xie went first at everything. The plate took his hand and the dial above the counter climbed and settled, and the woman wrote, and Xie Xie craned to see it, and the woman turned the form away, and Xie Xie said, "That's *rude,*" and the woman said, "It's policy," and Xie Xie said, "Whose policy," and the woman said, "The Spirit Pagoda's," and Xie Xie said, "That's the rudest policy I've ever heard," and Lin Hao put a hand on his shoulder, because the queue was not moving.

Wang Jinxi went. Zhang Yangzi went, with his splinted wrist held carefully away from the plate, and winced at something only he could see. Wei Xiaofeng went, and scowled at the dial, and did not comment, which was so unlike him that Xie Xie whispered "Is he *ill?*" and Wei Xiaofeng said "I'm *fine,*" in a voice that had two variants in it now.

The form had a line for occupation, which the seven of them had filled in at the counter, and the woman read them back the way you read a list you expect to be boring.

"Students. All seven." She glanced up. "Any trades?"

"Second-rank smith," Lin Hao said.

The woman's pen stopped. "Second rank."

"Second rank," Lin Hao agreed. "Thousand Refinements."

Now the pen stopped properly. She looked at him — at the age on the form, at the face, and then back at the age, doing the same subtraction she was about to do twice more that morning — and she said, "The Association's records say the youngest second-rank in the Eastsea Branch is a boy named Tang Wulin."

"He's standing behind me."

The woman looked behind him, at Wulin, and then at the two of them, and wrote something in the margin that was not on the form.

Wulin went next. The dial climbed, and stopped, and the woman looked at it, and then looked at Wulin, and then looked at the dial again.

"Is something wrong?" Wulin asked, politely.

"No," the woman said, slowly, and wrote something, and then wrote something else underneath it, and Lin Hao — who was standing second in the queue and had a view of the form at an angle the woman had probably not accounted for — saw that she had written the number, and then drawn a small line through it, and written a different number, and then drawn a line through that too.

Gu Yue went. The dial climbed. It did not stop where the others had stopped. The woman's pen did not move, and then it moved once, and she did not look up.

Then it was Lin Hao's turn, and he put his hand on the plate.

The dial climbed.

It passed Xie Xie's place. It passed Wang Jinxi's. It passed Zhang Yangzi's and Wei Xiaofeng's and it went by Wulin's crossed-out number and it did not stop at Gu Yue's, and Lin Hao watched it go with the detached interest of a blacksmith watching a gauge that had been asked to do something it was not built for, and the needle kept going, and the woman stood up.

She actually stood up. She was a woman of perhaps forty who had, Lin Hao thought, probably been doing this for twenty years, and she stood up out of her chair and leaned over the counter to look at the dial from a different angle, the way his father stood up and leaned over a bar of steel when the bar of steel said something his father did not believe.

"That's not right," she said.

"It's usually not," Lin Hao agreed.

"Are you —" she looked at the form, at the name, at the age, and then at him, and something in her face changed, and Lin Hao watched it change and understood that she had read the age and had then done a subtraction, and had not liked the answer. "You're ten."

"I'm ten."

"Hold still." She adjusted something on the machine — a dial, a setting, something in the side of it — and the needle dropped, and settled, and climbed again, and stopped in almost exactly the same place, and she sat down very slowly.

**one hundred and ninety-one.**

The room had gone quiet, and Lin Hao became aware that all six of them were looking at him, and that the queue behind him had ceased to be a queue, and that a man in Spirit Pagoda grey had come to the door of the intake room and was standing in it looking at the dial over the woman's shoulder.

"Is the machine broken?" Xie Xie asked, in the voice of a boy offering help.

"No," said the man in the doorway. He was older, thin, with the particular stillness of someone who worked with expensive equipment. He came in and looked at Lin Hao for a long moment. "Spirit Connection realm."

"Yes."

"At ten."

"Yes."

"one hundred and ninety-one."

"That's what it says," Lin Hao said. "I'd check the machine again if I were you, but I've been told the machine's fine, and I've been told that twice now, so I've made my peace with it."

The man did not smile. He read off the form, in the flat tone of a man putting a thing on a record whether or not he believed it: "Rank thirty. Two yellow rings. Spiritual power one hundred and ninety-one. Spirit Connection realm. Age ten." He paused on the last two items the way a person pauses on a sum that does not come out. "Second-rank smith, Thousand Refinements."

"Yes," Lin Hao said.

The man looked at the woman. "Elementary platform," he said. "Standard entry tier."

"He'll clear the elementary in a session," the woman said. "Sir, the tiering is set by the number, and the number says—"

"The tiering," the man said, "is set by the number." And then he looked at Lin Hao again, and there was something in the look that Lin Hao recognized, because he had seen it once before, on a man with smoky green eyes, five months ago, in a room full of numbers. It was the look of a person doing arithmetic that had an official answer and a real answer, and who had decided, for now, to write down the official one.

"What's your name, son."

"Lin Hao."

The man wrote it on a small card, and put the card in his pocket, and did not write it on the form.

"Right," he said. "Elementary platform. Standard tier. All seven of you." And he left, and the woman sat down, and the queue resumed, and Xie Xie said, under his breath, "He *pocketed* you," and Lin Hao said, "Yes," and Xie Xie said, "Is that bad," and Lin Hao said, "It's a number," which was not an answer, and which Xie Xie accepted, because Xie Xie accepted everything.

## Part 3: The Profound Look

The room with the boxes was on the seventh floor, and it was not what any of them had expected, and Lin Hao was the only one who said so.

"It's a hospital," he said.

"It's not a *hospital,*" Xie Xie said.

"There are seven beds, Xie Xie, there are electrodes, there is a man in white over by the wall holding a tray, and there is a smell that I have smelled exactly once in my life, in a room where my mother's neighbour's husband stopped breathing for eleven seconds. It's a hospital. It's a hospital with better lighting."

The boxes were metal, and they were waist-high, and each of them had a lid that opened on a hinge, and inside each one there was a padded surface and a headrest and a set of electrodes on articulated arms that waited, politely, above the pillow like insects standing still. There were screens along the far wall — a bank of them, seven, each one dark — and a chair, and in the chair sat the man in Spirit Pagoda grey, and behind him, with his arms folded, stood Wu Zhangkong, and beside Wu Zhangkong stood Long Hengxu, who had come out of the tournament the way weather comes out of a season, and who looked at the seven of them and nodded once.

"Here is what happens," said the staff member — a different one, younger, with a clipboard and the cheerful efficiency of a person explaining something they had explained four thousand times. "You lie down. We attach the electrodes. The lid closes. You will feel a sensation like falling, and then you will be inside. The platform separates you on entry — you will not be together, and you will not know where the others are. Inside, you fight. What you fight is decided by the platform, based on the measurement you were given at intake." He smiled. "There is a button inside, on your left wrist, that you press to come out. Press it if you need to. Nobody thinks less of you. Well. Almost nobody."

"Almost nobody," Xie Xie repeated, horrified.

"Almost nobody," the staff member agreed, cheerfully.

"What happens if we don't press it," Wang Jinxi asked.

"You come out when you're out," the staff member said. "Or when we bring you out."

"And if we get hurt," Zhang Yangzi said. He was holding his splinted wrist against his chest. He had been very quiet since the intake room.

The staff member's smile did exactly nothing at all — it stayed precisely where it was, at precisely the size it was — and Lin Hao, watching, understood that this was the question the man had been waiting for, and that the answer had been written down somewhere by somebody with a great deal of experience, and that it was not the whole answer.

"The platform is virtual," the staff member said. "But it contains a degree of reality. What you feel inside, you feel. Pain included. That's the point — you don't learn anything from a fight you can't feel. The button gets you out before it goes too far, and that's what the button is for." He looked at the seven of them, one at a time, and his eyes took a fraction longer on Zhang Yangzi, and then — and this was the part Lin Hao thought about for weeks — a fraction longer on **him.**

It was not a warning. It was not friendly, either. It was a *profound* look, the look of a man who knew something and had decided that knowing it was his job and not theirs, and Lin Hao met it, and held it, and did the thing he had done with the man in the doorway, which was to file it, and to note that he had now been given two looks in one morning by two different employees of the Spirit Pagoda, and that both looks had arrived immediately after the number.

"Hao," Wulin murmured, beside him. "That man is looking at you like you're a fire in a house he likes."

"I know."

"Is that bad."

"It's a number," Lin Hao said, for the second time that morning, and then, because the teasing was the whole of the saying and the room behind it was real: "Wulin. If anything in there is going to be a problem, it's going to be a problem *for me,* and I want you to understand that I'm not worried, and I want you to understand that the reason I'm not worried is not that I'm brave. It's that I've been the number since I was six years old, and I have never once been able to put it down, so I've made my peace with carrying it, and if the platform wants to give me something heavy, it's going to have to work for it."

Wulin was quiet for a moment.

"I'm going to find you," he said.

"You're going to be in a forest."

"I'm going to find you *in the forest.*"

"Everyone's going to be in a forest, Wulin, that's the entire premise, and Wang Jinxi has already told me he wants to be the one who finds me, and I've promised him I'll be annoyed if I find him first, and there is a limited supply of me and there is a queue."

"I'm at the front of the queue," Wulin said.

"You're at the front of the queue," Lin Hao agreed.

## Part 4: The Lid

They went in one at a time.

Xie Xie went first, because Xie Xie had asked, and because the staff member had said "whoever's brave," and because Xie Xie had said "finally, a *fair* test," and had climbed into the box with the enthusiasm of a boy getting into a boat, and had said, over the rim, "If I don't come out, tell my father I died in a forest with a *mouse,*" and the lid had closed.

Zhang Yangzi went second. He did not say anything. He lay down very carefully, protecting the wrist, and the electrodes came down onto his temples, and just before the lid closed he looked at Gu Yue — a quick, awkward, entirely unpractised look, the look of a boy who had been carried out of his own catastrophe three weeks ago and had not yet worked out how to say thank you — and Gu Yue inclined her head perhaps two millimetres, and the lid closed.

Wei Xiaofeng. Wang Jinxi, who lay down as though he had done it a hundred times, and who said, flatly, to no one, "Nineteen minutes," and closed his own eyes before the lid did.

Gu Yue went fifth. She lay down the way she did everything, which was as though the box had agreed to be lain in. She did not look at anyone. Just before the electrodes came down she said, quietly, to the room, "Don't wait for me," and Lin Hao — who was sixth in the queue, who had seventy-nine lines in a ledger and one of them hers — said, "I wasn't going to," and she said, "Yes, you were," and the lid closed.

Then Lin Hao.

He went with two swords, which the staff member looked at and did not comment on. One was the Stormbringer, which was not a sword at all so much as a fact about him. The other was the blade from the side forge, one hundred and ninety-one strikes, wrapped until that morning in oilcloth, and he had put it on his back instead of his hip because Wu Zhangkong's homework had been sitting in his head for three weeks and had finally, on the walk over, produced an answer.

*Ask yourself why a swordsman's whole curriculum is reading.*

Because the sword is the only weapon that has to be pointed. A hammer lands anywhere. A shield goes where you put it. A sword has an edge, and an edge is a *choice* — you have decided which side of the world you are going to open — and a choice requires knowing. Every form Wu Zhangkong had given him, every stance, the Grain Cut, the Question, the Answering Stroke, all of it was a way of *arriving at knowledge in time to use it.* The sword was not the weapon. The sword was the argument.

He had written it down that morning on the back of his hand, in case he forgot, and he had not forgotten.

The padded surface was warmer than he expected. The electrodes were cold, and they settled against his temples with a small precise pressure, like fingers. Above him, the inside of the lid was bare grey metal, and there was a strap for the left wrist with a button under it — red, plastic, ordinary, the least impressive object he had ever been given.

The staff member leaned over him.

"You're the two-forty-one," he said.

"I am."

"You understand what the measurement does."

"It decides what I get."

"It decides what you get," the staff member agreed, "and the platform has a great deal to choose from." He straightened up. And then, because he was, apparently, the third employee of the Spirit Pagoda to do it in one morning, he gave Lin Hao the look — the profound one, the one that knew something — and he said, very quietly, so that it did not go on any record: "The button is on your *left.* Not your right. First-timers reach with the right."

And the lid closed.

Lin Hao lay in the dark with a red button under his left hand and seventy-nine lines in his head and the smell of cold metal and hot dust, and felt the falling begin — a long, gentle, entirely unmistakable drop, the way the ground goes away under a swing at the top of its arc — and he did the thing he had done every time in his life that the ground had gone away.

He did not brace. He did not grab. He let go of the ledge, because he had learned at six years old, on a harbour wall with the sea underneath it, that the only thing you can actually do with a fall is *decide where you're going to land.*

The dark went green.

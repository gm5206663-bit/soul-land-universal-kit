## Part 1: The Wrong Year

The city had changed character overnight, the way cities do when a date arrives. Every inn was filled to the brim — examinees, teachers, the heads of families — and the streets had gone from crowded to a thing with a current. Merchants had come out for it the way merchants come out for weather: old exam questions, rules, spirit items, soul devices, printed and reprinted and sold again.

Xie Xie bought one of everything.

Lin Hao read the pamphlet twice — the second read in the violet that stood in the gold of his eyes when he read, the cheap print going steadier and closer in it, a lamp he carried and never trimmed — and started laughing on the second page, which frightened everyone, because he so rarely did it audibly.

"They're selling the wrong year." He turned the pamphlet so the class could see the seam his eyes had found, where two printings had been stitched to look like one. "Rules from this year, questions from three years ago, one page from a different academy entirely. Somebody in this city is rich." He handed it back to Xie Xie with great ceremony. "We want our money back."

"You bought it," Xie Xie said.

"I did. That's the sad part." — but he kept the true half, the rules, and did the arithmetic where he stood, because the day would demand it. *Sent by a major city:* check. *Under fifteen:* five times over. *Rank twenty-five at the very least:* thirty-three, thirty-one, thirty, twenty-eight, forty-five. The arithmetic had never once been the problem; the problem had a clock and a bathtub in it. And under the three printed rules, the pamphlet's author had hazarded a fourth, older than printing — that the real requirement had been decreed twenty thousand years ago, and it was not a number at all. Shrek did not accept ordinary people. The pamphlet said *monsters*, in the hushed typeface of a word the author half-expected to be sued over.

The morning had already taken the two teachers — the white head and the white robe, side by side, a woman going home and a man going to a door he had knocked on in his sleep for thirteen years — and by afternoon a call had come through, one call, brief as all its sender's sentences: he was handling business; they were to keep to their rooms; under no account was the meditation to be disturbed.

The class looked at the order, and at the yard behind the inn, and did what a class does with an order that has no teacher attached to it.

"What we have," Xie Xie said, quietly, in the yard, at midnight, "is a shape. Nobody's testing us on a shape. But a harder exam is still an exam, and the shape is still ours, and I'm not walking into anything tomorrow without having touched it tonight."

So they walked it. Slow, low, silent, five figures in a yard built for drying laundry: the stack positions, the release, the five-second window Xiaoyan made and the two who could use it. And it was wrong, the way a chair is wrong with one leg turned, and everybody knew which leg.

"Zhang Yangzi's slot," Xiaoyan said, at the end of the first walk-through, and nobody had to say anything back. The shape had been built on six hands, in a square in Eastsea, and its sixth hand was on a dock four thousand li away, and the letter telling him about the exam would not even arrive for days yet. They re-spaced it — Gu Yue taking the sixth's station, Lin Hao taking Gu Yue's — and walked it again, and again, and on the fourth time it held, different, a new thing.

"A shape with five hands is a different shape," Lin Hao said. "It held. Stop trying to make it the old one."

"That," said Xie Xie, "is either very wise or very lazy."

"It's late. It's both."

Xiaoyan said nothing through all of it, because the night was hers and she could feel that plainly: the starwheel was a night thing, and the exam would be held in daylight, and her whole craft would be at half its sky. She did not say it. She just walked the shape better than she had ever walked it, and the ones who knew her knew what that meant.

Between sessions, Lin Hao worked. He had no forge, so he worked the way a smith works without one: the estate lot's stock out across the floor, the file, the whetstone, the little scale, five kits gone through strap and edge and hinge — because whatever a harder exam was, it would be walked into, and walking in was a thing that could be prepared. And once, in the cold of the last watch, the joining's cold came and sat by his ankles like the dog it had become, ninety-five seconds' worth of another existence keeping its own count, and he let it, and the whetstone kept moving.

The pulses had slowed again. He timed them between tasks, never instead of them — dusk's three ran fourteen, fifteen, fourteen, taken with the whetstone in his other hand — and the last before supper ran long enough that he counted his own heartbeat against it to be sure the watch was honest.

Line 166 still said what it said. *The clock is what he went to handle.* The night was about to find out for itself.

## Part 2: The Western Gate

He did not go to the academy. That morning belonged to two people, and this is what it held, by the city and the record and the two who walked it.

The western gate of Shrek Academy stood where the world's largest city had decided to become a school. A sign board hung from the gates with two words carved into it — *Shrek Academy* — and the man in white robes stopped dead at the sight of it, the way a man stops at a wall he has walked into before. Shen Yi came to stand beside him.

"Go inside. Teacher has missed you all these years. You're both just too stubborn to make up. I was really happy when you agreed to come back, you know!"

He lowered his head without a word and walked toward the gate, his pace brisk and faster than before — almost, a person watching might think, as if he were afraid of getting cold feet.

Two youths in dark-green uniforms guarded the gate. They saluted the moment they saw her. "Senior sister."

"This is my friend," Shen Yi said. "Please grant him entry."

She took out a hexagonal metal plate and handed it over. One of the youths examined it, nodded, and stepped aside — and Shrek's Inner City let in the man who had left it.

"I originally prepared this entry token for that female disciple of yours," Shen Yi said quietly, as they passed under the arch. "I didn't think I'd end up using it for you." She had half-carried the hope for three winters — and three winters of reply cards, too, one of which had counted five names for an admissions hall that had never needed convincing — and had still not expected this.

Inside, the city changed skins. A wide street paved in black brick, stores ancient and mostly of wood, all open, almost none occupied — as if the two of them had been transported ten thousand years into the past. She walked at his side and remembered the years she had shadowed him on this same street, the junior sister, always three steps behind, always saying the same things. That time was long past. The sunny youth who always wore a brilliant smile had become a man as cold as heavenly frost, in white robes, carrying a blue sword. When his beloved passed away, his smile froze. Since then, years of isolation in a small coastal city, refusing to return. Sometimes it seemed his spirit had already faded, and he only kept living because of a promise to the dead.

He was thirty-two years old, and a seven-ringed Soul Sage, and within reach of three-word battle armor, and she was certain he would arrive at it, because his will had never once bent. And when she had finally met his disciples — five of them, the girl she had wanted and the boy the file could not explain and three more the letters had made her love secondhand — she had rejoiced: something important had come back into his life. Along with his beloved, four more people had made their way into his heart. He had returned for the sake of his disciples.

Then he halted, sudden as a slammed door, and she walked into his back.

"Why did you stop so suddenly!?" she protested, rubbing her nose.

His cold eyes thawed, a very little, at the junior sister who had said the same exact thing for ten years running. "Where is Teacher?"

He said it absently, his hand already moving — tousling her hair the way he had done when she was small and he was the sunniest senior brother the inner city had. And the tears came out of Shen Yi's large evergreen eyes all at once, and she threw herself into his arms in the middle of the ancient street.

"Senior brother!" — choked, half a sob, entirely thirteen-years-younger.

The street was not too proud to stare. Her white hair was the most recognizable head in the academy.

"Isn't that the White-haired Witch, senior sister Shen Yi? What is going on with her?"

"She never smiles and all the juniors are afraid of her. What's *she* doing?"

"Who is that man? He's so handsome!"

"Whoa. He seems sort of familiar. Who is he?"

He patted her back once. "Let's go."

She raised her head, sheepish, eyes still brimming. "You're still my senior brother, right?"

He made himself smile, and the smile was bitter, and it was the first one she had seen in years. "That isn't up to me. Let's go." And he took her arm and drew her down the street toward the city's core, striding, a man late for his own reckoning.

"Teacher is in the inner court," she said softly, wiping her eyes.

*Inner court.* He began to shake. He took one deep breath, released her hand, and sprinted, air chilling in his wake.

## Part 3: Two Words on a Sign

The inner court announced itself as a tall mossy-green wall converging on a lavish archway with a sign at its top. Two words: *Inner Court.*

There were no guards. There had never needed to be. The words were the wall. This was the legendary inner court of Shrek Academy, continent-famous, yearned for by more soul masters than could be counted, feared by the Federation itself — and the sign was enough, two words on a board, to stop anything that should be stopped.

They stopped Wu Zhangkong completely.

"Senior brother," Shen Yi said, catching up, breathless.

"Don't call me that. I'm no longer qualified to be your senior brother. If Teacher hears you, you'll be punished."

And he dropped to his knees.

It had no ceremony in it. That was the terrible part, for anyone who had seen the man conduct a class or filet a fish or stand a month of afternoons doing nothing on purpose — the arrogance and the sternness folded down like jade pillars and mountains of gold crashing to the ground, and the white-robed swordsman knelt on the black brick before two words on a sign.

"Wait here, senior brother," Shen Yi said, her heart trembling and her voice not showing it. "I'll go get Teacher." And she drew a breath and went through the arch like wind.

The turbulence settled out of him as he knelt. A cool breeze swept by, carrying the green scent the academy grows on purpose.

*Finally, I'm back. After thirteen years — today, I have returned.*

*Thirteen years ago, I was so impulsive and proud. Now I'm able to understand — but can I get back what was lost?*

*I'm sorry, Bing'er. I'm sorry, Teacher. It was all my fault.*

*Bing'er, just wait for me. I will preserve your name with my battle armor. We will be together forever.*

*As long as you desire it, I will not hesitate to sacrifice everything for you.*

Time ticked by, slowly and quickly at once. When Shen Yi came back out she was pale, and her footsteps hesitated, and she made herself close the distance and stand in front of the kneeling man.

"T-Teacher doesn't want to see you."

"Mn. Thank you." His tranquility did not move. He knelt as he had knelt.

Hesitation crossed her face, and lost, and she said it. "Teacher said, *'just let him kneel there.'*"

His head came up. He was trembling — not at the refusal. Fury would have been survivable; fury meant the account was still open. It was the disregard that closed accounts. As long as his teacher was angry with him, he still had a chance.

"Thank you, Shen Yi." He bit his lips, and knelt on, and his back was straighter than before.

"Just keep kneeling for now, senior brother," she said softly. "You know what Teacher is like. He spouts harsh words, but his heart is soft. When I told him you had returned, I saw a trace of joy on his face for a split second — he couldn't hide it at all. He still cares deeply for you. After another round of persuasion, I'm sure he'll forgive you."

Wu Zhangkong shook his head, gently. "I don't deserve Teacher's forgiveness. But for the sake of my disciples, I implore you."

"Mn." And she turned and went back inside, to persuade.

## Part 4: The Last Hour

At the inn, the sun was going to rise soon, and Teacher Wu had not come back.

"The sun is going to rise soon. Why hasn't Teacher Wu come back yet?" Xie Xie paced the room in circles, his figure wearing a track into the boards, and nobody answered, because the answer was the pacing.

At some point in the night, the door to the washroom had been opened just a crack, for just a peek. The facts of the peek entered the record as follows. Tang Wulin was soaking in the bathtub, naked as the day he joined the class, gold-misted, magnificent, unconscious. Gu Yue's face and Xu Xiaoyan's faces had turned crimson in the steam. Gu Yue had immediately clapped her hands over *Xu Xiaoyan's* eyes, leaving her own wide open to take in the sight — an allocation of resources so efficient that even the record admires it. Xie Xie, one generation's hope of the high road, had peeled them both away from the doorway, depriving the two of them of the eye-candy that was Tang Wulin. Lin Hao passed the doorway once, with the kettle, at the exact moment the crack went crimson-and-silent: steam, the edge of the tub, and duties sorted years ago. He kept the kettle moving.

"If Wulin can't take the exam, then I won't either!" still stood, resolved and proclaimed. But resolution, Xie Xie had discovered in the small hours, was a coat with a lining. Taking the exam would still have been the best outcome. Thirteen now; sixteen in three more years — too old, ruled out, finished. The only shot any of them would ever have, leaking away through an hourglass nobody could turn.

"Let's just wait," Xu Xiaoyan said meekly, from her chair. "Xie Xie, can you stop walking in circles? We've already made our choice. If we don't end up taking the exam, we'll just treat this trip as a holiday and nothing more. In any case, we're on vacation right now. Once Captain wakes up, we can go sightseeing at Shrek Academy. At the very least, we can have him treat us to dinner. I bet his cheeks will be sore considering how stingy he is."

She giggled at the thought. It was a good thought. It was even a true one — Xie Xie's face did a strange thing, because he knew the ledger of that stinginess better than anyone: aside from the bare necessities, Tang Wulin paid for nothing, ever; every coin went into the savings like water into sand, and he rarely ate out because the price of filling that particular stomach was simply too steep.

"I keep thinking about the shape," Xiaoyan said, into the grey. "Five hands held. It's not the old one, but it held."

"It held," Lin Hao agreed. "That's the version we walk in with. Whatever they test, they test five of us at once or they're not testing us."

Xie Xie stopped pacing and looked at him, and then at the window, and the sky went from black to iron to the first grey, and his impatience went with it — not up, but out; it left him, and what settled was quieter and worse. He had done the traveling arithmetic. The inn was far from the academy, and the exam would start in one hour, and if Tang Wulin stood up that very moment, bright and new and finished, they still would not make it in time. Time waited for no one.

If they missed it, they missed it. They would carry some regrets, all of them, for a long time. All of them had also decided this already, on a landing, in front of one another — comrades over Shrek Academy — and a decision did not become less true for costing what it cost.

Into which silence Gu Yue's eyes opened.

"I'll stop mocking you in the future," she said, to Xie Xie, with the finality of a court.

"Huh?" said Xie Xie, at a complete loss, and the night let that stand.

Lin Hao took the book down in the grey light, in the room that smelled of steam and paper, and wrote the line the night had left him.

*The exam is in one hour. The water is still gold. He is still handling it.*

Dawn crossed the city at its usual rate, no faster for anyone. It found, in the inner court of the world's most feared academy, a white-robed man still kneeling before two words on a sign, back straight, thirteen years long. And it found, in a far inn, a bathtub still holding its gold, five children on a vacation with a shape nobody else had, and one hour.

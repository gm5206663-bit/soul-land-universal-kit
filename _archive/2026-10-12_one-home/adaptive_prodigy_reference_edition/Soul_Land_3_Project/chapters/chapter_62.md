## Part 1: The Man From Sealand

He came on the Tuesday, at half past nine, and he was polite.

That was the first thing Lin Hao noticed, and it was the thing that decided the morning for him, because he had spent four years in his father's smithy watching men who had already made up their minds pretend to be about to make up their minds, and politeness was how you could tell. A man who wanted something asked you questions. A man who had decided told you things.

The man from Sealand told them things.

He had a folder. It was thin. Lin Hao had learned in the last fortnight that a thin folder was worse than a thick one, because a thick folder meant somebody had done the work and a thin folder meant somebody had done the deciding.

"There is an irregularity," the man said.

They were standing in the yard behind the association's annex, where the tournament's leftover crates had been stacked into a wall and not yet taken away. Wu Zhangkong stood with his hands in his sleeves. He had been standing like that for eight months while Lin Hao learned where to put his feet, and Lin Hao had written down eleven things about it, and every one of the eleven meant *wait*.

"The boy's classification," the man said. "Comprehensive. It is not one of the six."

"It's on the form."

"It's on the form," the man agreed, "and it has been on file for eleven days, and in eleven days nobody at the Association has been able to tell me what it means. I have asked four people. One of them was a seventh rank." He paused. "The seventh rank laughed."

Wu Zhangkong did not move. "He wrote a word in a blank line. The blank line has been there for forty years. A girl wrote Elementalist in it before him and nobody has questioned that either."

"Elementalist is a martial soul."

"Comprehensive is a System."

"It is not one of the six."

"No," said Wu Zhangkong. "It is the seventh."

The man wrote something down. Lin Hao watched the pen and understood that the pen was not writing a definition, because the man did not have one, and that the man was writing *the teacher said so* because that was all he had, and that this was apparently how a thing became real, and that it had nothing at all to do with whether the thing was true.

Then the man folded his hands.

"The Alliance would like the file transferred," he said. "For clarity. A boy with three purple rings at ten is not an Eastsea matter. Sealand has the facilities to—"

"Sky."

The man stopped talking.

Wu Zhangkong had said it at the volume he used for a correction, the one that meant *your back foot again*, and the hawk went still in Lin Hao's chest in the particular way it went still before weather.

"Ice."

## Part 2: What Came Out Of Him

The sword went first.

Lin Hao had read that man's sword for eight months. He had never once seen it leave his hand. What happened was not the sword leaving his hand. The Skyfrost Sword went *into* him — into the middle of his chest, the way a bar goes into a fire, with no resistance at all, and Lin Hao understood halfway through the second that the sword had never been a thing the man carried.

It had been a thing the man kept outside himself so that he could stand in a classroom.

Then the light came.

Ten points of it. Forehead, shoulders, chest, elbows, wrists, hands, hips, knees, feet. Silver thread ran between them in a pattern Lin Hao read the way he read a grain, because it was the only thing he knew how to do, and the pattern was load-bearing. The thread was not on the armor. The thread was the armor holding itself up.

A crystal the colour of a winter sky came out of his forehead. A white band circled it. Five points rose into something like a crown, and two metal bands came down past his ears to cradle his cheeks, and the rest of it built itself from the neck down in about the time it takes to say the word *cold*.

Six soul rings went in.

A sword came out. A metre and a half, white hilt, snowflake at the tip, edge the blue of ice under a river. It was the same sword. It was not the same sword.

Above them, Shen Yi said, "Those fools. Now they're just provoking him. Don't they know that the calmer a person is, the more terrifying their wrath?"

And then, lower, in a voice with something in it that Lin Hao had no line for: "Now this is the real him. Sky Ice Wu Zhangkong, is back."

The man from Sealand had gone the colour of old paper. At the far end of the yard two white mechas lowered their cannons, and nobody had told them to, and Lin Hao filed that, because a machine does not lower a cannon. A man inside a machine decides something very fast.

Xie Xie went under it.

He went while it was still forming, which was either the bravest or the stupidest thing Lin Hao had ever seen him do, and Xie Xie had done a great many of both. He stood two metres beneath the faulds with his head back and one hand up, as though he could catch a piece if any came off.

"It's metal," Xie Xie said.

"Get out from under that."

"It's metal and it isn't *on* him, it's *of* him, and there isn't a seam." Xie Xie had gone quiet, which was how you knew it had landed. "Lin Hao. There isn't a seam. My father paid eleven thousand coins for a chest plate with four seams and a join under the arm that leaks when it rains. That thing up there got made out of one idea." He lowered his hand. "Every piece is thousand refined and there isn't a piece of it that isn't. Do you know how much metal that is."

"I know how much metal that is."

"You could make it."

Lin Hao didn't answer, and Xie Xie turned his head, and the grin arrived — delighted, insufferable, and entirely uninterested in whether the thing it had just announced was possible.

"I'm going to be a mecha master," Xie Xie said, "and you're going to make the metal, and I've decided that just now, standing under him, and I'm telling you out loud so you can't pretend you didn't hear it."

"I heard it."

"That's the second thing you've heard this week."

"I've heard a great many things this week."

"Not out loud," said Xie Xie. "I said out loud."

Wulin had not spoken at all.

Lin Hao looked at him, because Wulin not speaking was the loudest thing in the yard. Wulin was standing with his mouth slightly open and his hands up at chest height, holding nothing, and Lin Hao understood that Wulin had wanted to see a battle armor since he was six years old and had said so perhaps four hundred times, and that the wanting was happening, and that it had arrived too fast for him to say anything about it.

Gu Yue was two metres to Lin Hao's left.

She was looking up. Her hands hung at her sides with the fingers slightly open, which was how they sat when she was holding an element and had not decided which one.

## Part 3: Twenty Ranks

The man from Sealand said something about the Alliance's position.

Wu Zhangkong said one sentence. "You have tried to take my disciple. If he is diminished by it in any way that does not show, you will pay for the part that does not show."

That was the whole of it. The man left with his thin folder, and Lin Hao watched him go and thought the folder looked thinner than it had, and could not think how a folder could get thinner.

Shen Yi landed. The yard got quieter. She said things to Wu Zhangkong in a low voice, and Mu Chen came up the path out of breath with Duan Xuan behind him, and Duan Xuan said "a two-word battle armor master" three times to nobody in particular.

Mu Chen said nothing. He looked at Lin Hao for a long time.

Lin Hao went and stood beside him, because Mu Chen was the only person in the yard who would have a number for it.

"Tell me the ranking."

"You want the ranking."

"I want all of it."

"Four levels," Mu Chen said. "One word each. One word — every piece thousand refined, five rings minimum. Two words — spirit refined, six. Three — soul refined, seven. Four — divine refined, nine." He stopped. "Your teacher has six rings and two words. Do you know what that means."

"That he's stronger than six rings."

"A battle armor master stands twenty soul ranks above any soul master of the same soul power." Mu Chen said it the way he said everything, flat, as though reading a price. "A five-ring man in one-word armor fights like a seven-ring man. Your teacher, in that, fights like a man with eight."

Lin Hao did the arithmetic, because the arithmetic was the only thing that had ever helped him.

Six rings was sixty-one to seventy. Twenty above that was eighty-one to ninety.

"Soul Douluo," he said.

"Yes."

"He's been teaching me with six rings for eight months."

"Yes."

Lin Hao opened his mouth and then closed it, because the sentence he had been about to say — *he has never once used it* — was wrong, and the correct version was considerably worse.

He has never once needed to.

"Mu Chen."

"Hm."

"Why is there a crown. The other pieces don't have a crown."

Mu Chen was quiet for a while. "That's not a crown. That's the second word."

"And the second word is Ice."

"Sky came from the sword." Mu Chen looked at the white figure in the air above them. "Custom is you take the armor's name and put it on your own, so they called him Ice Wu Zhangkong when he was a one-word master, and Sky Ice after."

He stopped. The stop went on longer than the sentence needed, and Lin Hao recognised the shape of it, because he had heard exactly that stop in exactly that place four months ago, in front of a grave with a name on it.

"Ask somebody else about the Ice," Mu Chen said.

"I'm not going to ask anybody about the Ice."

Mu Chen turned his head and looked at him properly for the first time that morning.

"No," he said. "I suppose you're not."

## Part 4: Two Lines

He went back to the smithy, because the smithy was where he knew what he was doing, and he took the book down off the nail.

The book had one hundred and seven lines in it.

Eleven of them were about Wu Zhangkong's sword. They were there because the sword was readable — the Frost Scars had a signature, the entry angle had three variants, the wrist gave a tell at the top of the second beat — and he had used every one of them, and in eight months he had gone from being hit in nine seconds to being hit in four, and the eleven lines were the reason.

He put the pen at the end of the eleventh and wrote:

*Twelve. The sword goes into him. It does not come out of him.*

He looked at that for a while.

*I have been reading a sword that was kept outside so that he could stand in a room and teach. The eleven lines are lines about the keeping-outside. There is no line for the rest of it, and the reason is not that I have not looked hard enough.*

He turned the page, which he had never done, because he had never needed to.

*A battle armor is twenty ranks. He is six rings and two words, which is Soul Douluo, which is further than anybody I have met, and he has been correcting my back foot since the autumn.*

*I am three rings and I fight like a Soul King. That is fifteen ranks. He is twenty. We are the same shape and he is forty years further along it.*

He set the pen down on the bench and sat there with his hands on his knees.

It was not a frightening thought, which surprised him. He had expected frightening. What it actually was, sitting in a cold smithy with the smell of scale still in the rafters, was the specific relief of a man who has been told he is the only one of something and then finds out he is not.

He had spent four years being the only person he knew whose numbers did not match. Every measurement came back wrong in the same direction. Every dial went past. Every clerk stood up and leaned over a counter. He had stopped mentioning it, because there was nothing to mention it to.

And forty years up the road there was a man in a white armor with a dead woman's name stitched into it, standing twenty ranks above his own paper, teaching children where to put their feet.

Lin Hao laughed once, out loud, in an empty room, and then felt embarrassed, and then wrote it down anyway.

*He did not tell me the ceiling existed. He stood in it.*

*Yesterday in this room he said he had been opposing me and was going to start guiding me and did not know how. I think that was the how.*

## Part 5: The Seawall

She was on the seawall at the end of the day.

He sat down beside her, because he had begun to understand that this was what she did instead of saying things, and that it might also be what he did.

They didn't speak for a while. The water went on doing what the water did. Out past the third buoy a gull turned with the wind instead of against it, and he watched it do it and thought that he had watched a gull do that once before, a long time ago, in a city that smelled of hot metal, and had thought at the time it was the most efficient thing he had ever seen.

Then he said it.

He had not planned to. He found out afterwards that he had not known he was going to until the first part of it was already out, which was the third time that had happened to him in a month, and it had not stopped being frightening.

"You know I don't have a goal," he said. "Not a fixed one. Not a big one."

Gu Yue did not turn her head.

"When I awakened, my father and mother wanted me to be great. They did more than a lot for it. My mother sat up nine nights with a seam she could have sold and didn't sell. My father put a bar of Spirit Steel in my hands when I was four and didn't take it back when I dropped it eleven times." He picked at a flake of paint on the stone. "So I started on this path. Not because I wanted to be great. Because they wanted it. And I love them, and that was enough of a reason then and it's enough now."

The water went on.

"I became a blacksmith because of Wulin and my father. I started because of them." He turned his hands over and looked at them, and found he had not decided to. "It isn't that I don't enjoy it. I do. That's why I'm here. I want that part clear, because I've noticed that when people hear the first half they decide the second half for you. I enjoy it. I enjoy this. I live my life as a journey and on the journey I do the things I like doing."

"I enjoy this academy because of Wulin. And I've had better options. Three of them. I haven't told him about any of them, because sometimes he already feels like he owes me, and if I told him he'd carry it, and he carries enough. So I don't tell him. I don't tell anybody."

He turned his head.

"But I wanted to tell you."

Gu Yue was very still.

"Or I—"

He stopped.

He could not afterwards say what had been coming. He had been in the middle of a sentence and the middle of the sentence ran out, and he sat there for about two seconds with his mouth open like Wulin in the yard, and those were the longest two seconds he had ever had, and the reason he stood up was not that he had decided to.

"Nothing," he said. "I just — bye."

And then he was gone, because the Track was already under his feet and had been before he chose it, and he crossed the seawall and the lane and the whole eleven metres to the corner without travelling, and the middle part did not happen, and he understood as he went that he had learned it from her.

## Part 6: What She Did

Gu Yue sat on the seawall.

She did not move for a long time. The light went from the colour of metal to the colour of water to the colour of nothing, and the gull went out past the third buoy and came back, and the place beside her stayed warm longer than it should have.

In the last hour she had been given the whole of it.

That there was no destination. That the road had been built one piece at a time out of other people's wanting — a mother's unsold seam, a father's steel, a boy named Wulin — and that the boy beside her had walked every step of it and enjoyed every step of it and chosen none of it.

That he had had three better options and had not said so, to keep a friend from a debt he had never owed.

And *but I wanted to tell you*, which she had not been given an answer to. And *or I*, which was not a sentence.

Gu Yue looked at her own hands, because that was where she put the things she was not going to say.

In the ten months she had known him she had given him nothing. Not one element, not one ring released where he could read it, not one technique — and he had been cheerful about the blank page the entire time, which was the part she had never managed to make sense of, until the morning before dawn four months ago when she had finally taught him something and watched him take the top half off a bar at forty metres.

He had never once asked her for a reason.

She thought about the sentence he had not finished. She knew what came after *or*. She knew it the way she knew the density of a stone or the temperature of a flame, without working at it, and she had known it before he got to the word.

She did not say it.

She sat in a city that smelled of salt and hot metal, with the rest of her life in front of her and the warm place beside her going cold, and she did not say it, and she noticed — clearly, the way she noticed everything, which was the trouble with her — that not saying it was the first thing she had ever done for him that cost her anything.

Out past the third buoy, the gull turned with the wind.

## Part 7: What He Wrote

He wrote the numbers first, because the numbers did not ask anything of him.

*Two hundred and eighty-nine. One thousand four hundred and six. Thirty-six.*

Then he turned back to the page with Wu Zhangkong on it and read the two lines again, and under them he wrote a third.

*I have now learned the same lesson twice. A record of what a thing did is not a record of what a thing is. I learned it in a hall full of statues and I learned it again today in a yard, and learning the same lesson twice is not the same as learning it.*

Then he started the next line, and put the pen down, and picked it up again.

*One hundred and nine.*

*Gu Yue. I told her the whole of it and she did not say anything.*

He looked at that for a long time.

Every line in the book was about how to win. He had written one hundred and eight of them and he had never once had to think about what they were for, because the answer had always been sitting there at the top of the page like a stamp. This one was not about how to win. He did not know what it was about. He was not going to pretend.

He wrote:

*Or I—*

And stopped, and left it.

He had never left a line unfinished in that book. He thought about it with the pen hovering, and decided that a thing which stopped should be written down as a thing that stopped, and that if he finished the sentence in here the sentence would become a thing he had said, and he had not said it, and he would rather have the gap in the only honest book he owned.

Eight points today, and there had not been a fight. He had been saying for four months that the fighting was the cultivation. He had said it often enough that it stopped being something he noticed and became something he believed, and today he got eight points from standing in a yard watching a man put on his own name, and the rule was either wrong or it was not the whole of it, and he could not yet tell which.

He closed the book.

The hawk turned over in him and did not pull toward anything, and the lock at his temple lay across his cheekbone, and the marks on his forearms had been raised for forty days.

Eight years to the next ring. He wrote the number a fifth time and noticed that he had written it a fifth time and did not write anything about that.

Lin Hao lay on his back in a room that smelled of the sea and thought about a sword going into a man instead of out of him, and about twenty ranks, and about a girl on a seawall who had not said anything, and about the two seconds after *or*. He did not sleep well. He had not slept well in three nights, and the not-sleeping had a different shape each time, and he could not yet tell what the shapes meant.

## Part 1: The Lesson

The blue sword came out of the night like a season.

Wu Zhangkong did not warn, and did not hurry, and the first stroke was slow enough to be a question — Skyfrost's cold laying a flat line across the moonlight, courteous as a master opening a study.

Lin Hao answered with the branch.

Still Water ran down the dead wood the way it had run down cold iron for two years — the block laid flat, the line taken away, the branch meeting the birth of the cut rather than its arrival. Bark shifted. Nothing broke. The two weapons touched and parted, and the lake went on ticking at the shore as if nothing had been said.

"Again," said the teacher, and meant the opposite of what the word usually meant: *further.*

The second stroke came faster, still a question. The branch was a spear this time — not because Lin Hao moved it like one, but because the purpose arrived before the wood did, and wood that has been told it is a spear becomes difficult to argue with. The third stroke, falling like a verdict, met a fence. The fourth, a shield that had been a stick ten breaths earlier. The transposition wasn't technique. It was grammar — the same grammar that had read the Yaluo bar aloud — and every body the idea wore, it wore completely, for exactly as long as it was needed, and not one breath longer.

Wu Zhangkong stepped back. His sword did not lower.

"Left side," he said, and struck the left side, and the branch swept — a boat oar's stroke, wide and level, born from the boathouse disagreement itself — and turned the blue line aside.

Silence. Then the teacher did something no student of his had ever seen: he laughed — one syllable, winter thawing for exactly that long.

"Twenty years," he said, "I have taught the sword as a thing. 'The sword is this. The sword is not that.'" The blue blade turned in the moonlight, edge and flat and edge. "And you come down to my lake with a stick, and the stick is more of a sword than half the blades I have owned." His eyes were bright and cold and, for once, young. "Say the law. I want to hear it in your voice, so I can steal it for my other students."

"The intent was never the sword's," Lin Hao said. "The sword is where I met it. It lives in the purpose — and the purpose wears everything. Every weapon is one idea in different bodies. Tonight the idea learned to change clothes."

"It learned more than that." Wu Zhangkong sheathed — a decision, not an ending. "It learned that from the outside. You left the sword behind tonight, Lin Hao. Don't. The idea wears every body — including the first one. The sword taught you the grammar; keep the first body fluent, or the grammar goes stale in every body at once." A pause, the length of a held blade. "That is the last correction I am qualified to give you. From tonight, we do not train, you and I. We confer."

*We confer.* From the coldest teacher on the continent, it was a knighthood.

They walked back up the island in the kind of silence that two swords make when they have agreed to be honest with each other forever, and the branch stayed in Lin Hao's hand the whole way, because it was rude, somehow, to put down a teacher's diploma.

## Part 2: The Girls' Veranda

On the veranda of the wooden house, Shen Yi was finishing the night's other lesson.

"—and conscious of your behavior inside the academy, Gu Yue. Before the seniors of this school — the ones who have lived through world-shaking events — we are nothing. Pay your respects, or your stay will be a short one, and your companions' with it. Do you understand me?"

Gu Yue nodded, silently, which from her was fluency.

Wulin pulled her hand. "Teacher Shen is right. You were too impulsive today — Elder Cai was testing my strength in the fourth trial; I wasn't even hurt. Teacher Shen — will you take us to her later? To apologize."

Shen Yi stared at him. *Thirteen,* her face said, and gave up on the sentence.

"I know you acted for my sake," Wulin went on, honest as a hammer. "But we're a team, Gu Yue. What you do lands on all of us — the way my deep meditation made five of us working students." He looked her directly in the eyes. "And Elder Cai wants you as her *disciple*. She watched you all exam. It's not easy to become a one-word battle armor master by twenty — a teacher like that isn't luck, it's a door. Doors like that don't knock twice."

Gu Yue's brows drew in, her cheeks puffed — and then, under the honest weight of him, her head bowed. "I'm sorry. I was impulsive today. I want to go apologize." He gathered her into a hug — the captain's kind, the kind that means *the shape held*, and she allowed it, crimson, and did not push it away; and over his shoulder her eyes crossed the dark toward the lake path, one heartbeat, no longer, where a branch and a blue sword were still talking, and her face did arithmetic it declined to show anyone.

Xie Xie sauntered over with his arms wide open. "That's the way!"

Her leg shot out. "Go away."

"You're *so* biased. How come the Captain gets—"

"Because he's handsome," Gu Yue said, matter-of-fact, and the veranda detonated.

"WAY handsomer than you," Xiaoyan confirmed, digging deeper with the serenity of a surveyor.

Shen Yi watched them — her own admission age bouncing in front of her — and something in her unbent for the night. "Sleep early. Registration tomorrow, and working students must be alert. Being a working student," and here her voice went quiet and odd, "is not an easy thing."

## Part 3: The Boys' Room

The boys' room held two beds and one question.

Wulin asked it into the dark, half asleep, the way the important things get asked: "Why didn't you spend yours? The request. Full marks buys anything. You could've had — anything."

Lin Hao was quiet long enough that the question began to apologize for itself. Then:

"I asked first. On the roof — you were there. Name the price of the point, I'll pay it. They refused the coin; the coin was the problem, not the will." A breath in the dark. "Then you found a coin they'd take, and you spent it on her entry — the way you do everything, for the shape, all five through one door. A man doesn't bid against his captain's gift, Wulin. He doesn't take a door out of the hands of the one already walking through it."

Silence. The shape of the answer moving in the other boy's mind — and Wulin, who was wiser about people than anyone credited, let the half of it that was about Gu Yue pass unremarked, because the Receiver Law was class law by now, and captains enforce the laws they're given.

"When you find the word," Wulin said at last, "the word the request is waiting for. Tell me first."

"After the elders," Lin Hao said. "Before the continent."

"That'll do," said the captain, and was asleep inside a minute.

## Part 4: The Dream

Wulin was the last to meditate, and the first to dream in iron.

Golden light rose off his palm, and the hammers answered out of his blood — arriving in his hands the way a limb arrives, no distance crossed. The dragon on the metal was no pattern tonight; it *roamed*, slow and gold, along the flanks of the hammers, alive because he was, the blood connection complete. He sat inside the night's arithmetic like a man sitting inside a warm house: mechas could never have this — first-grade thousand-refined was a mecha's ceiling; armor was different, armor was a circuit carved into the martial soul itself, the soul given its own armor and sublimated by it.

And the other arithmetic, the one that did not warm: the circuit had a history. Humanity's strength had soared on armor's back — and the beasts had gone over the brink under it. The Spirit Pagoda had been founded to make the peace, and then, for the sake of artificial spirit souls, had slaughtered its way through a world with the Federation's blessing; under the relentless pursuit of science, the soul beast world had crumbled; give the research a few thousand years and even ten-thousand-year spirit souls would come from a workshop. *Xie Xie's question, arriving at three in the morning with its answer:* it was never the beasts. It was the door, and who built it, and what came through it wearing armor.

Saint Blacksmiths stood level with three-word masters because without them there'd be no three-word armor. Divine was a word even Mu Chen couldn't explain — a door with no visible house around it. Wulin looked at his own goal, and at the blood ring, and did the boy-arithmetic that would one day terrify committees: armor had no strict soul-power requirement — he had proven the principle already at the anvil. Piece by piece, then. Starting with the simplest part. Not one word.

Two.

He fell asleep mid-calculation, the hammers humming themselves back into his blood like a ship's engine below decks, and the dream came and fitted him for a shape he couldn't yet see — armor with two words waiting on it, unhurried, the way everything real in his life had arrived: late, heavy, and his.

The island slept around its five. In her room Gu Yue dreamed of nothing she'd ever tell; in his WZK lay awake listening to the lake and hearing, faintly, two weapons still confer; and on the desk of a banked page across the water, Elder Cai's verdict waited for morning like a held breath — while at the house's heart, an old man who had forgiven nothing and accepted everything drank his student's tea, and found it good.

The night was long enough. Morning was already coming, and morning was loud.

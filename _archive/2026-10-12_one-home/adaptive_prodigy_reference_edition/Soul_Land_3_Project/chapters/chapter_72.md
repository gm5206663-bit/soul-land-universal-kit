## Part 1: Fourteen

The cake arrived at breakfast under Xie Xie's personal escort, and it had fourteen candles in it, because Xiaoyan had checked the number twice and Xie Xie had checked it once more with the bakery under formal witness.

They lit them in the inn's back room with the shutters open to the station noise, and for a minute the room was all small fire and everybody's face made of gold. Wulin looked at the candles the way he looked at loaded bars — like a thing with weight — and shut his eyes, and thought whatever fourteen-year-olds think whose doors open the same night, and blew.

Fourteen candles went out together. Everyone cheered. Xie Xie had prepared a speech and was not permitted to give it.

"Make a wish," Xiaoyan instructed, notebook closed, which for her was festivity.

"I did."

"Can't tell it?"

"Can't tell it."

Gu Yue said, "Happy birthday, Captain," which was ordinary, and warm, and exactly what she would have said yesterday, and exactly what she would have said tomorrow. Lin Hao watched the smoke of the candles go out the window in a line, and ate his piece, and said the ordinary things, and his face did its work all morning. It was a good face. It had been getting him out of rooms for years.

The day was given to idleness by unanimous sentence, and to small errands, and to staying out of the way of a boy who was about to spend three years of savings in one bath. At some point in the afternoon Wulin got quiet in the way they all knew, and went to walk the station alone, and nobody followed, because this too was a custom of the class.

## Part 2: The Candle

Here is what nobody at the party saw, because it was not showable.

He had not been expecting a Yes. That was true when he said it and it was still true at breakfast and it would be true forever — he had gone up those stairs with the sentence finished in his hand and no bill attached to it. But somewhere in him, in a room he had not known he kept, there had been a candle burning. He had never once looked at it directly. You do not look at hope directly; it goes out when you look at it, his grandmother had said that about something else, or maybe it had been about exactly this. And last night the room had gone dark so quietly he had heard nothing — no wind, no door — and only this morning, at a birthday table with fourteen small fires going out on command, had he understood the room was dark.

That was all. That was the whole injury: not a No, which he had braced for and could have carried like freight. A nothing, which weighed nothing and could not be put down.

He did not show it. He was, the class had agreed years ago without meeting about it, the steadiest of them — the boy who read seams, who did not flinch at light, who had lost a duel in sixty-one seconds and could not stop being pleased about it. Steady was a garment he had cut to his own measurements. Under it, this. He was thirteen and something like this had never once happened to him, and he owned no tools for it, and he knew it, and he said nothing.

He would not ask. That was not a decision; it was architecture. Asking was the one thing his whole self was not built for — you did not ask metal to be ready, or weather to be fair, or a scale to be kind; you read, you waited, you kept the terms. And it was not hate. It was never going to be hate. She had done nothing — nothing was the entire point — and he had told her she needn't, and a promise made in advance is still a promise. Both things were true at once and neither canceled the other: he accepted it completely, and it hurt, and the hurt was his to carry, quietly, the way the book carried lines.

So he carried it. In the afternoon, while the others idled, he walked to the rental bench and paid his coin and worked two hours on the Sky Dragon Iron with no plan at all, strikes for the sake of strikes, because metal was the one thing in the world that had never once required him to be fine. The hawk sat the rail and watched him do it, and near the end flew down and leaned its whole weight against his neck, once, firmly, the way it had done exactly never before. The cold in the room curled once around his ankles, like something checking on him, and settled.

The page had turned. Nothing was written on the next page yet. He was thirteen, and he turned it anyway.

## Part 3: Old Tang

At dusk, Wulin knocked on his teacher's door with the black box under his arm.

"Teacher Wu. Tonight."

Wu Zhangkong looked at him for a moment. "How long do you estimate it will take?"

Wulin had no answer and his face admitted it.

"If you don't even know, why bid at all—" his teacher began, and then stopped, because in this version of the conversation there had been no bidding; the vine had been bought in Eastsea with quiet money a month ago, and the only thing left to spend tonight was the boy. "Then it's the birthday. It was always the birthday." A pause. "You're sure your strength will increase?"

"I'm certain. It will definitely be a success."

"You can make your breakthrough in my room," Wu Zhangkong said. "I'll guard you." And, in the taxi that took them back from the last errand with the box on Wulin's knees, the conversation the class would have paid to hear:

Wulin, on money: three years of orders, four spirit items, the Ghost Shadow Perplexing Track unaffordable, the Soft Bone Eight Stage Drop a dream for a richer decade. Wulin, on the hammer art: *"Did you know the Disorder came from the Clear Sky Clan? Teacher told me last year. Tang San's clan — the founder's own. The clan's gone now. The hammer is all that's left of them."* And then, quieter, because it had just landed on him in a moving taxi: *"That's what he gave you, that first winter. A dead clan's whole inheritance. He just handed it over."*

Lin Hao, at the inn door, hearing this the way you hear a bearing in a machine you thought you knew: "He didn't say it like that."

"He never says anything like that."

Inside, in the room where luxury did not exist, Wulin sat on the bed, closed his eyes, and called down a voice that had been silent since it saved his life.

The golden light came, and the white space with it, and the old man in it looked him over with regret and approval in the same squint. *"Mn. Long time no see, you little rascal. You've done well improving your body all this time. It shouldn't be a problem for you to break the second seal. Have you prepared the four spirit items?"*

"I have them." His voice was thick, and not only about the money.

*"Then you're ready to start. The method is different from last time. You will ingest the Dragonscale Fruit directly. Crush the other three items into juice, dilute the juice with hot water, and bathe in it — soak and meditate. You need to do this simultaneously. Don't eat the fruit until the bath is ready. Understood?"*

"Understood."

Back in the room, he opened his eyes and found his teacher already raising the kettle, and explained the method, and Wu Zhangkong stared at him with a face like a blank mask and said, "You're absorbing it by soaking?" A quirk of the brow. "Is there an issue?"

"No problem. But it's not very efficient."

"That's fine. I'll trouble you to help, then." And Wu Zhangkong stood, sleeves pushed, and let exactly one sentence of his whole life slip out through his teeth as he reached for the vine:

"That's such a waste of money!"

## Part 4: The Brew

The Azure-veined Vine was forearm-long and azure like its name, crystal-sparkling, liquid moving visibly in the stalk, sweet-breathing into the room. Wu Zhangkong pointed a finger and a gentle stream of soul power lifted it over the bathwater; a pinch of invisible force wrung it in half, and its juices ran down into the tub until the water and the rising steam both went azure. When the husk was spent, his finger moved — and blades of Mysterious Heaven soul power, finger-swords, no martial soul anywhere in the room, stabbed the husk a countless number of times and exploded it into powder so fine that not one particle missed the water.

Wulin's eyes shimmered. He had seen his teacher fight. He had never seen him *filet*.

The Land Dragon Tendon was two tan meters of the least remarkable-looking thing in the world. "It should be five meters; it shrank in processing. You would normally boil it three days and three nights to soften it. For a broth, there is only one method." His teacher stepped his left foot out, bent into a half-squat, still as furniture — left hand Controlling Crane, right hand Capturing Dragon, palms facing, a wind standing up between them. The tendon rose and spun. Faster. A whirring. His upper body did not move at all while the tendon's revolutions howled and the tan went red and the pressure and the friction and the Sect's two hands tenderized fifteen minutes of a dragon into a scarlet dumpling, and then the red liquid started, drop by drop, each drop a puff of steam, until the whole of it was in the water and the water was dark green and the room's smell was a war between the vine's freshness and something fishy that was winning. The Sea Dragon Marrow took a moment — squeezed simple, the steam going pink, the tub now terrifying by any aesthetic ever devised.

"Go in."

Wulin bit the Dragonscale Fruit — blue-skinned, jewel-bright, sour enough to nearly kill him — felt the heat of it land in his stomach, and got into dark green water so hot he made a sound he would deny in court. *Clear your mind and calm your breathing.* The fruit's warmth made it worse, not better; his body went scarlet in a breath, a boiled shrimp, twitching, and Wu Zhangkong's hand rose with his martial soul already gathering to cool the tub—

—and stopped in the air.

On his student's forehead, faint as watermark, a golden mark had appeared. Three golden lines. Even with his eyesight he could barely make them out; and he could not identify them; and he filed the whole of it — the mark, the water, the recipe with no source — under a heading he had been keeping for this boy for four years, which had never once had a label, and stood his guard.

## Part 5: The Sentries

The class was sent to bed at nine, and one of them did not go.

Wu Zhangkong was where canon had always put him and where he had said he would be: inside, on the stool beside the bath, guarding, his senses doing the watching. And in the corridor outside the door, at his own distance, uninvited and unexplained, his student stood the hour of nothing alone — the month of Eastsea afternoons resumed, one sentry short, by request of nobody.

The corridor was dark after the station's last announcement. The tower light had gone out at midnight, punctual. Somewhere behind the door, his best friend was being boiled alive inside his own becoming, and there was nothing to do about that either, and he knew it, and stood.

Once, deep in the small hours, the door opened — his teacher, with the empty kettle, on his way to the boiler down the hall. He saw the boy against the wall. He did not order him to bed.

"You are carrying something," he said instead. Not a question. The voice of a man reading a seam.

"Sir."

"Carry it level."

"Yes, sir." And Wu Zhangkong went past him to the boiler and came back with the kettle full and went in again, and did not close the door all the way, and that was the whole exchange of permission, and the most the two of them would ever say about it.

The hawk came off his shoulder in the small hours and leaned its weight against his neck again, and the cold came and sat by his ankles like a dog, and Lin Hao stood the hour in the dark and let the room in him be dark, because that was what it was, and it was not hate, it was never going to be hate — it was just the first time, and first times are load-bearing, like first notes. He had told the truth last night, all of it. He had not told her to get anything.

But there had been a candle, he could admit that now, to a dark corridor — and the corridor was the proof it was out.

As before. Like before. He stood the hour anyway. Standing the hour was also as before.

Behind the door, at every hour anybody checked, the water was still moving.

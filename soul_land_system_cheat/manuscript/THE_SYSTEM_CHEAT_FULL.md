# Chapter 1: The Awakening Day

The cart left Fruit Creek before the sun was up, and Su Ping rode it with his legs hanging off the tailboard, watching the village fall behind orchard row by orchard row.

His father walked beside the horse. His mother rode beside him and set his collar square every time the wheel hit a stone, which was often, because the mountain road was all stones. Behind the cart walked half the village. It was the plum-blossom season, and nobody in Fruit Creek was working, because today the Spirit Hall's man was coming up from the city, and Fruit Creek had three children turning six this spring.

There were three of them in the cart: Su Ping, who was six; Da Hu, the miller's son, who was six and half a head taller; and Little Zhen, the tanner's daughter, who was six and small enough that her mother had sewed her a new jacket with room to grow. They sat in a row on the sacks and did not talk much. The road was long and the morning was cold, and a strange quiet sat on all three of them — the quiet of children who had been told for a year that today would decide the shape of their whole lives.

Su Ping felt the quiet too, but it sat on him lightly — weather he had seen before. He had been a strange child, the whole village agreed on that: too steady, too patient, hard to frighten, slow to cry, the boy who would watch a pot boil all afternoon just to see how it went. He was six years old, and behind his eyes there was something older. He did not know what it was. He only knew that the world had never once felt new to him, and that waiting did not cost him anything.

The spirit hall of Fruit Creek was one grey room at the head of the top terrace, old bare wood and a stone floor, with a plum tree beside the door that the whole village was proud of without being able to say why. The children were lined up in front of it before the Hall's man arrived, with the whole village behind them.

He came up the valley road in a carriage with a hood over it, and he was a tired man in a blue robe gone pale at the shoulders. His name was Deacon Bao, and he had done this, he said, in forty villages since the spring thaw, and in none of them had anything happened that anyone would remember a year later. He said it without complaint, as a man states the weather. He set a blue crystal ball on the hall's one table, and called the children up in the order of their birthdays.

Da Hu went first. He put his hand on the crystal as he had been shown, and his face went red with pushing, and the ball lit a faint, dull white — and a hoe appeared in the air over his head. Not a grand hoe. A plain iron hoe, hanging in the air and turning slowly.

"Tool soul, common grade," said Deacon Bao, writing it down. He read the last of the light out of the ball, and his voice stayed level. "Innate spirit power — zero. The Hall's blessing on the family." And to Da Hu's father, who had gone very still: "Don't take it hard. Most souls are hoe souls — I have stood at this table all spring, and power is the rare thing, not the soul. A hoe is an honest soul for a strong back."

Da Hu's father put his fist to his heart anyway. "A good soul for a miller's boy," he said, loud enough for the row behind him, and it was true, and the village nodded.

Little Zhen went second, and the ball lit, and a sparrow flew out of the air over her head, landed on the table, and looked at everyone with one eye.

"Beast soul, common grade. Innate spirit power — zero. The Hall's blessing." His voice had not changed at all between the two, and it did not change for the third. "Su Ping, of the Su family. Come."

Su Ping came, and put his hand on the crystal.

It was warm. That surprised him; he had thought crystal would feel like stone. The warmth climbed his arm like water finding a channel. For one breath the whole morning seemed to lean toward him — the plum tree, the crowd, his mother's hand frozen halfway to her mouth. Then the light came up inside the ball, brighter than the other two had been, and something fell into the world above his open palm.

An apple.

It hung in the air a moment and then dropped into his hand, warm through, red as the new year, perfect. It weighed what an apple weighs. It smelled of the top of the pile in the first week of picking, the week the whole village smiles for no reason they'd say out loud.

Nobody said anything at all. Behind him, Su Ping heard his father breathe in.

"Tool soul," said Deacon Bao slowly. He came around the table and looked at the apple from two sides, and something in his tired face moved for the first time that morning. "Food type." He straightened up. "I have never seen one. My master at the Hall saw one, when he was young, far to the south — he still talked about it the year he died. Twice in one lifetime at the Hall, and my master's lifetime was longer than mine." He looked back at the ball, and read the last of the light out of it, and stopped with his brush in the air.

"Innate spirit power — three."

He set the brush down. He looked at Su Ping, and then at the village, and then at Su Ping again.

"Understand what three is," he said. "Power at all is the rare thing. I have stood at this table in forty villages since the thaw, and I have read power twice — twice — and the higher of the two was a two. The scale runs one to ten. Nine is the holy maiden of the Hall herself, and the Hall does not say even that lightly. Ten comes once in a generation on the whole continent, and when it comes, the Hall knows within the month. Three, in a mountain village, in the plum-blossom season." He stopped, and started again, plainly. "Three can go anywhere, boy. Slowly, if you walk alone. But anywhere."

The village did not need it explained twice. Old Shi, the headman, was already turning to the crowd with his arms up, and the noise that came off the two hundred people of Fruit Creek was the noise of a festival being born in the space of one breath.

Su Ping stood in the middle of it and looked at the apple.

He felt proud, and underneath the pride, faint and far away, like a sound from another room, he felt something he had no name for: the feeling of a thing arriving exactly where it was always going to arrive. He was six, so he put the feeling away with all his other feelings, carefully, where he could find them later.

The apple was still warm. On an impulse he did not examine, he turned and held it up to his father.

"You carry the pruning knife all day," he said. "Eat it."

---

They tried the apple there at the hall, because nobody could wait, and it was Deacon Bao himself who ran the test, waving the village back with both arms.

Su Ping's father had spent the morning walking a mountain road and the last ten years carrying orchard ladders, and at mid-morning he was honestly tired, the deep tired in the shoulders that a man does not notice anymore because it never leaves. He ate the apple down to the core while the whole village watched him chew.

Then he stood very still.

"It's nothing," he said, and then, honest as the day, "it's not nothing. I was tired. I'm not." He rolled his shoulders, and everyone saw it — the weight coming off them. "Like the first morning of the picking week. Like a night's sleep in a fruit."

The village made the sound again, louder.

"It restores," said Deacon Bao, writing fast now. "Stamina, fatigue, minor hurts, hunger — that would be the usual shape of it. Not wounds, not spirit power, at this grade. Conjured how? Speak, don't speak — does it need words?"

Su Ping had already found that out, in the space under his ribs where the warmth had gone when the apple came. There was a small pool there, the deacon's next questions established, a child's-small pool of spirit power, and the apple was drawn from it like water from a well. And there was a word the well wanted before it would give. He had said it without sound, in his mind, when the first apple fell — *ripe, and given* — because saying it felt right — a first line he had always known. He did not shout it. He never shouted. But he said it again now, inside his own head, and the second apple dropped warm into his other hand.

"Spoken in the mind," said Deacon Bao. He had gone from interested to careful in the space of a quarter hour. "Good. Keep that to yourself." He set the second apple down on the table, and then he taught — plainly, in order, once, as the Hall had taught him.

"Boy, I am a spirit master — second ring, Hall-trained. So hear this said clearly, because it is the first lesson every spirit master learns, and your apple runs on it the same as every soul in this world. Spirit power is fuel. Using a soul's power spends it — your apple the same as another man's hammer. Spend it low and you rest, and it recovers. And it grows one way only: cultivation. The crystal read you at level three today. Cultivate, and you climb — four, five, six — one level at a time, and there is no other road, for anyone. Every tenth level earns a spirit ring: you hunt a soul beast, you take its ring, and the ring gives your soul a true skill. Level ten, your first ring. Whatever the apple becomes, that is decided then, and by the ring."

He tapped the table once, on the word ring.

"What a food-type's rings will make of yours — that, I will grant you, no one north of Nuoding can say. My master saw one, far to the south, when he was young; you are the first anyone here has set eyes on since. The fruit is rare. The rules are not. Fuel, rest, cultivation, rings — the same law for every soul under heaven, and I have just given you the whole of it. Get to Yunxi, learn the method, and climb. The rest, the fruit will tell you itself."

Old Shi, to the village at large, said the thing that Fruit Creek said about the day for the next fifty years. It went into the song: "The mountain has finally paid us back."

Su Ping spent the afternoon finding out what the lesson meant in practice. An apple cost about a third of his spirit power. It came back slowly while he lay in the grass — four more apples between noon and dark, and by dark he was empty, and tired in a way that felt like honest work. The apple he had set aside at noon was soft and brown by evening. The rules, the deacon had given him. The numbers, nobody could give him. He counted them himself.

They ate the apples that evening at the long tables under the plum trees — the day's apples, sliced thin by Su Ping's mother so that two hundred people could each have a taste. Da Hu's father drank to his son's hoe and meant it. Little Zhen's mother asked twice whether the strength would still be there in the morning. The deacon took his fee in pickled plums and a new pair of sandals. And everyone was happy.

Su Ping went to bed with the taste of his own soul still on his lips, which was a strange thing to sleep on, and he was almost asleep when the other thing woke up.

---

There was no voice. That was the first fact, and he learned it before anything else — and he learned it with a strange doubling in his chest, because with the first glance at the thing hanging in the dark behind his eyes, half of him had already known what it was.

He was six years old, and he was also a man who had died. He remembered the other life whole — not in pieces, not in dreams: whole. The bright rectangle of glass that showed stories a chapter at a time. The stories themselves, thousands of them, read on trains and in the small hours of the night. And in a fair share of those stories, this exact thing happened: a man from that world died, and woke in another one, and found hanging in the dark behind his eyes —

A system.

The word came from the old life, from a language this world did not speak, and it fit like a key fits a lock. Slots. Meters. Numbers that climb, in parts of a hundred. He lay in the loft with his heart going like a rabbit's and a grin spreading across his face in the dark, because he — of everyone on this continent, perhaps of everyone alive — knew exactly what he was looking at.

> 「Slots: 1 / 1 / 1」

One technique. One ring. One bone. The lines were not letters in any language of either of his lives, and it did not matter — they were themselves, plain as the taste of plum, and he understood them as he understood hunger.

And because he had read a thousand of those stories, he knew exactly what to ask for next. He waited for the voice. There was no voice. He asked for a menu, and no menu came. He asked for quests; nothing offered him a quest. He waited for the shop, the points, the little rewards for existing — and none of them came, and slowly, between one breath and the next, the grin changed shape. Not smaller. Steadier.

Of all the stories he had read, this was the bare one. No voice to explain, no gifts, no tasks — nothing in it at all but the counting. This system — if that was what it was; the word fit, and nothing else did — had come with no manual, which he had half-expected; no intelligence, which he had not; and no interest in him at all. It was not watching him. It was not anything. It was just there, like the floor.

Which meant everything it knew, he was going to have to find out the slow way. What the slots took. What the other two were for. What a hundred parts meant when it arrived. The stories had all agreed on one thing at least: the meter was real. Whatever it said he had, he had.

He slept. In the morning it was still there, and in the morning, being who he was — the boy who watched pots boil — he started the slow way.

His father had him carrying the small water buckets up the orchard rows that day, because a spirit child still had chores, and a spirit child's body was still a six-year-old's body, soft and short-winded. Su Ping knew the work; he had done it since he could walk, and Old Gao next door had shown him years ago how to set his feet on the slope so the load rode the legs and not the back — the basics, as the village taught them. And on the second trip up the row, with the readout hanging quiet behind his eyes, he felt it lean toward the work. Not toward him. Toward the work.

He stopped in the row, set the buckets down, and thought — carefully, as a seedling goes into its hole — *there. Take that.*

> 「Technique — Basic Body Conditioning: 9%」

Nine parts in a hundred. He had braced himself for zero, and it was not zero, and he stood in the row with the buckets at his feet and worked out what the system had done the moment he offered it the work: it had looked at him. Six years of this — the carrying, the climbing, the terraces, the water rows, Old Gao's footwork on the slope — six years of being a village child, and the system had counted all of it. It had not started him at nothing. It had started him at himself.

In the stories of his old world, the meters always began at zero, and the man in the story always frowned at the number and got to work. Not one of the stories had thought to ask what the number should be for a man who had already been living. This system had answered before he asked. The meter was not a task set for him. It was a measure of him.

He picked the buckets back up and climbed. On the third morning, when the readout woke with him, the line had moved.

> 「Technique — Basic Body Conditioning: 10%」

Ten parts in a hundred. He stood in the loft with the dawn coming through the slats and looked at the small cold number, and the far-away sound came again: a thing arriving where it was always going to arrive.

He did not know yet what the other two slots would hold — he had no rings, no bones he knew of, and the words sat in his mind like unplowed ground. He did not know what a hundred parts of anything would mean when it came. But he knew the shape of the road now, as a farmer knows a valley: from above, whole, before ever walking it. The stories had given him that much. The rest — the laws of this one, its rates, its limits — would come as harvests come.

He was six years old, and he had a soul that fed people, a body learning to carry water uphill, and a system — the word held carefully behind his teeth, a secret from a world away — counting quietly in the dark. He decided, wordlessly as always, that he would find out everything it was, and that he was in no hurry.

---

The deacon left on the fourth day, and before he left, he sat in the Su family's kitchen with a cup of plum tea and told Su Ping's father the plain arithmetic of the thing.

"Yunxi has the junior academy," he said. "Town of four thousand, up the valley where the river comes down. It gathers the power-children of every village within three days' walk — and there are never many of you, which is why the hall stands open. Boarding, six years. The Empire pays half and the family pays half, and half of what it costs to make a spirit master of a fruit-farmer's son is more than Fruit Creek sees in a season. That is the truth, and here is the rest of it. Keep him here, and in ten years he is the strongest man on the mountain with a talent for cider. Send him down, and in ten years he is a spirit scholar with his first ring, and the food-type gets rarer and dearer every league you walk from a place like this. The world is not kind to villages. It is only kind to villages that send their children out."

"We have no way to pay half," said Su Ping's father.

"You have two hundred people who ate his apples," said the deacon, "and a headman who has been standing outside this window for ten minutes listening."

From the window, Old Shi's voice: "The village pays. The village has decided. The village decided on the day the mountain paid us back."

His mother sewed new room into his good jacket and packed it full of chestnuts. His father pared a walking stick from the orchard's oldest apple tree, wrapped the end in cloth, and said nothing about it, because there was nothing to say that the stick didn't say. The village loaded the cart with pickled plums and dried fruit and a purse of copper that half of them could not spare, and on the morning the plum blossoms began to fall, the cart rolled down the valley toward Yunxi.

Su Ping rode the tailboard with his legs hanging, as he had ridden it up, and watched Fruit Creek fall behind orchard row by orchard row for the last time that season. Behind his eyes, quiet as ever, the system counted.

> 「Technique — Basic Body Conditioning: 14%」

Fourteen parts in a hundred. The road went down, the river came up to meet it, and the whole of his life, the part that would matter, was beginning — quietly, the way an orchard begins, one root at a time.

# Chapter 2: The Gate at Yunxi

The road came down out of the hills at midmorning, and Yunxi stood in the bottom of the valley with the river running through it, and Su Ping stopped walking because he had never seen so much of anything.

Four thousand people. He counted the roofs for a while and gave it up. Fruit Creek had two hundred people and one street; Yunxi had streets that crossed each other on purpose, a bridge of cut stone with a lion on each end, shops with signs, a smithy whose hammer he could hear from the pass. And people — people going somewhere at a pace, carts in the road, a woman selling fish off a barrow and shouting about it. His father walked close behind him the whole length of the main street, one hand on his collar, like a man herding a lamb through a market full of wolves.

The academy was at the north end of the town, where the river bent. A wall of grey stone, a wooden gate with the Spirit Hall's sun painted on it, a yard of packed earth with a training hall on one side and two rows of low buildings on the other. Over the gate, a board with characters on it that his father could not read either.

The whole trip had taken two days. They had slept at a cart-stop on the pass and eaten the chestnuts his mother packed. Now here it was — and now his father was kneeling in the yard in front of a thin grey man in a blue robe, presenting his son's papers — one paper, stamped by Deacon Bao — with both hands, like a man offering a full tray.

The thin man was Headmaster Lin, and he had three spirit rings, which Su Ping would not learn the meaning of for another hour, and which would not have impressed him much anyway. What impressed him was that the headmaster read the paper twice and then looked at him, not at his father, and said, plainly:

"Su Ping. Tool soul, food type, innate three. Show me."

The yard was full — the new intakes with their families, and the older students hanging off the porch rails to look. Su Ping stood where he was told, and found the well under his ribs, and said the words in his mind. The apple dropped into his hand, red and warm and perfect, and forty people looked at it.

Behind him, somebody snorted.

It was a big boy with a round face and a new jacket like his — Ma Fei, the smith's son from downriver, who had stepped up before Su Ping and produced an iron hammer that had hung in the air over his head like a promise. "It's a fruit," Ma Fei said, to his friend, at a volume meant to carry. "His soul is a snack."

A ripple of laughter went down the porch rails. Headmaster Lin did not laugh. He picked the apple up out of Su Ping's hand, turned it over once, smelled it, and set it back.

"Tool soul, food type," he said, to the yard, in a voice that carried more than Ma Fei's had. "The Hall's books put the food-types in the support class — rare, and valued where they are found. A support soul keeps its team standing, and any team that has one knows what it is worth. What his rings will make of this one, the books don't say, because no book alive can say. That's not a verdict on anyone's soul. That's a blank page." He looked along the porch rails, once. "This school has produced eleven spirit masters in twenty years, and every one of them started on this same yard, being laughed at by somebody who washed out. Show me your hammer again when you have a ring, Ma Fei. Class list. Su Ping, beginner row."

The rails were quiet after that. Ma Fei looked at his boots.

Su Ping picked his apple up off the table and held it. As the families were herded toward the gate and the goodbyes started all around him, he thought that he had just watched a man do properly what Deacon Bao had done tiredly in a mountain shrine: say exactly what was known, exactly what was not, and not one word more.

His father knelt down in the yard, in front of everyone, and set his forehead against Su Ping's forehead, and said nothing for a moment, because there was nothing to say that the touch didn't say. Then he stood up, and walked out the gate, and did not look back, and Su Ping watched him go and found that watching did not cost him anything. The feeling he put away carefully, where he could find it later.

---

The beginner class had six students his age, in a hall with one teacher: Headmaster Lin himself, who took the beginners because he said the first year was where the whole road got decided and he did not trust it to anyone else.

The first lesson was not a soul skill. It was the method.

"Sit," said Headmaster Lin, and they sat. "What I am teaching you is called the basic method, and every spirit master on this continent learned some version of it first, in a room like this one. Here is the whole of it, said plainly. Spirit power is fuel. It sits in you, in a place you will find in a moment. The method is a road — you draw spirit power from the air and move it along that road, through the body, in a circuit, and the road is the same for everyone: down the front, up the spine, around. Walk the road, and the road widens. That is cultivation. That is the only thing that grows your fuel, and there is no other way, for anyone, ever. The speed of the road is another matter, and it runs on two things. The first is the body you carry it in. The body is the draft on the furnace: the stronger the draft, the faster the fire. The strong-soul classes burn hot — their bodies are built for it. The support classes run thin. The books say it plainly, and you will hear it all your lives: food-types cultivate slowest of all the classes. It is not a verdict on anyone. It is a headwind, and every class has one. And one more thing, before you close your eyes. Most people who sit where you sit will walk this road their whole lives and never walk it perfectly. The basic method, mastered complete — every step full, every turning clean — almost nobody does it. Almost nobody needs to: ranks can be climbed on a good-enough road, and most climb them that way. But the basic finished is the root everything after stands on. The ones who finish it are rare, and the stories say the road gives them back something of their own — a method with their name in it, built for them and no one else, as if they had made it themselves. What that means, no book I have read can say. Now close your eyes and find the place where your fuel sits."

Su Ping closed his eyes and found it at once — the well under his ribs, the one the apple came from. Of course it was the same well. He had been drawing from it for weeks.

"Good," said Headmaster Lin, some minutes later, to the class in general. "Some of you have found it. Some of you are thinking about lunch. It's the first day; both are normal." He came down the row and set two fingers against Su Ping's wrist, and stood there a moment, and Su Ping felt his own spirit power move along the road as it had been shown — down the front, up the spine, around — slow and small and once.

"Hm," said the headmaster, and moved on.

That evening, in the dormitory — a narrow room, two beds, one lamp — Su Ping lay in the dark with the readout hanging quiet behind his eyes and thought about the hardest thing he had learned that day. It was not the method. It was the arithmetic of his slots.

He had one technique slot, and now he had two techniques. Body conditioning sat in the slot at fourteen parts in a hundred, growing every hour, night and day. The method was new, and his — learned once, understood, his to slot. But there was nowhere to put it that did not first empty the one place he had.

He lay still and did what he had done his whole first life with hard decisions: he made it a farm. One field, two crops. Body conditioning was the soil — slow, certain, feeding everything else forever. The method was the well — spirit power, the root of every rank he would ever climb, and every rank meant a ring, and — he counted it out slowly — at level ten, the slots doubled. Two fields.

The soil could wait. It would even keep growing a little — his own morning exercises, the chores, the work of a schoolboy's body — the meter had counted six years of that before the system ever came, and it would count the living he kept doing. Only the night-and-day engine would stop, the fourteen holding where it held until the second field opened.

He had been a farmer too long, in either life, to plant the well second.

He found the method in the list behind his eyes — it was there, it had been there since the lesson, waiting — and set it into the slot, as a seed goes into the one hole you've got.

> 「Technique — Basic Spirit Power Cultivation: 2%」

Two parts in a hundred. Not zero — he checked, and was obscurely glad; the system did not insult him — but near it, because the truth was the truth: he had understood the road that afternoon, and walked it once, and once was two parts. The system measured what existed. What existed, tonight, was a boy on his first day of school.

He slept. And while he slept, in the narrow bed, in the dark, with his breath going slow and his roommate snoring like a mill — the method ran.

> 「Technique — Basic Spirit Power Cultivation: 3%」

He woke to that, in the grey before the bell, and lay still with his heart going, grinning at the ceiling beams in the dark. It was real. The engine ran while he slept. Down the front, up the spine, around — all night, at the pace of his own best day, without his best day ever having to happen. He could feel it if he lay very still, faint as water moving under ice: the circuit going, and the road — a hair, a hair's width — wider.

In his old world's stories, this was the part where a voice told him he had gained something. No voice came. Nothing came but the number, and the number was enough.

---

The term settled into its shape, and the shape was work.

Mornings: the method, an hour, all six beginners in a row on the hall floor, and Headmaster Lin walking the line with his two fingers, correcting. Su Ping sat the full hour without moving, because sitting was the easiest thing anyone had ever asked him to do. "You sit well," the headmaster said once, in passing, and did not explain that most six-year-olds could not.

Days: letters, numbers, the Hall's law, the continent's map, the names of ranks and rings and soul beasts — the schooling a spirit master's life is built on. Su Ping ate it slowly and steadily, an old man's patience in a small body, and the work was easy in a way he was careful to hide: he could not read yet, but he was learning to read with thirty more years of practice at learning than anyone else in the room.

Evenings: exercise, the whole school, the yard — running, stances, carrying, the plain business of making a body worth putting a soul in. Su Ping did the work, all of it, and behind his eyes the paused meter of the body conditioning ticked — one part in a hundred in a week, then another — because the living counted. The engine was parked, but the soil still took the rain.

And the method climbed.

> 「Technique — Basic Spirit Power Cultivation: 11%」

By the first month's end. He watched the number as he had once watched pots boil, and worked out its habits. It moved in his sleep, and it moved in the morning hour, and — he was almost sure, though he could not have proved it — it moved a shade faster on the days after the evening exercise, when his legs were tired and his breath was clean. The body feeding the well. He filed that away with everything else.

And the list behind his eyes, he discovered that same month, was longer than he had thought. He had gone looking for the method one night and found the whole list staring back — not two entries but many. The System had been counting everything.

> 「Soul Power Control: 12%」
> 「Observation: 26%」
> 「Reading: 9%」
> 「Body Control: 17%」
> 「Walking and Running: 21%」

Soul power control, grown in the pouring of the apples — the tap on the well, learning to draw less than a third when a third was too much. Observation, the pot-boiler's gift, fed now by a whole town to watch. Reading, nine weeks old and climbing fast, with thirty years of practice at learning behind it. Body control — the sitting still. Walking and running — the road to Yunxi and the yard since. They grew on their own, with living for their only engine, his one slot being full — a part here, a part there, as the soil had taken the rain. And they leaned on everything else; he could see it even though the System said nothing. The watching fed the learning. The letters fed the books. The control fed the apple's cost — a cleaner pour meant less waste meant more apples meant more copper. Nothing on the list stood alone. One slot, and under it a hundred small engines, all of them running on plain life.

He did not keep the apple to himself for long. Wen Bo — the fat boy on the next bed, an ox-calf soul and a good heart — got the first one, on the first Sunday, and ate it, and went very quiet, and slept twelve hours, and got up shining. Wen Bo said nothing about it, because Wen Bo was not stupid. What Wen Bo did instead, that Monday, was walk Su Ping down to the bridge and point at the porters hauling sacks up the river steps — all day, every day, for copper.

"Your father," said Wen Bo. "After one apple. Like a night's sleep in a fruit. These men would pay a copper for that. Two. Su Ping — these men would pay."

He had spent a year learning that a method has seams, and where every seam had been, there was now just water, going down and coming up, and the seams were what the missing parts had turned out to be for.

The panel said one thing all summer, and said it at dawn, and said it in the dark.

> 「Technique — The Still Well Method: 100%」

The meters that still moved were the ring's, quietly, and the lists', on plain life, and the level, which was not the system's number and had never been. Sixteen by the first frost. Eighteen at New Year, read in private, kept off every list. Nineteen by the thaw. Twenty between the spring planting and the first mowing, and this time, when the water stood, he was not afraid of it. He knew the shape of a wall now. A wall was a door that cost something.

The autumn term brought the displays, and the yard gathered for the first soul-class as it always did, and Su Ping brought the apple up over his palm with the ring turning above it, as he had done the year before.

The ring was not white.

It was the palest yellow — young straw, holding light — and the yard made a sound he had never heard the yard make. Not the snort. Something lower and quieter, from forty throats at once, because a first ring was white or, for the richest and the luckiest, a proper yellow off a hundred-year beast, and every child in the yard knew the difference, and every child in the yard could see his had been white a year ago.

"Rings do not age," said Headmaster Lin, to the yard, in the register-voice, and then he looked at Su Ping for exactly as long as he had looked at the crystal the year before, and added nothing at all.

That evening, in the office, with the door shut: "Say it back to me."

"Rings do not age, headmaster."

"No." Lin sat down behind the register, and his hands were very still on it. "They do not. In the whole of the Hall's records, on the whole of this continent, a ring does not gain years after the taking. There are stories." He said the word as if setting it down at arm's length. "Stories are what the Hall calls the things it cannot file. I have now watched a food-type move five levels in a year and a ring cross from white to yellow in its seat, and one of those things I have a column for, and the other one I do not." He looked at the boy. "You will not display the soul at assembly again. The yard's memory is short and kind. The Hall's is neither. Do you understand what I am keeping for you?"

"I think so, headmaster."

"Then think so a little harder every year," said Lin, and opened the register, and that was all the office had to say about it, then or after. Not yet had a new weight on it now. It had a color.

In the third month of spring, Ma Fei hit his own wall.

He had come to the school at level four, the highest reading in five years, and he had climbed the honest way, three hours in the morning and three at night, every day, the diligence of a boy whose soul was a hammer and who knew what hammers were for. Ten came to him at nine years old, and the water stood, and Ma Fei walked around the yard for two days like a man carrying a full pail he could not set down, and on the third day Lin took him east with a permit and the school's second-best hoe.

They came back three days later, and Ma Fei was a Spirit Master.

The ring over his iron hammer was white, and the beast had been the big boar of the east slope, forty years if it was a day — the oldest thing Lin would put a nine-year-old under. The yard turned out to see it, and somebody at the back snorted, a plain white boar ring, all that noise for a boar. And this time Ma Fei turned around by himself.

"It's the biggest boar on Saddleback," he said. "I asked for the biggest one his body could hold, and he took me to it, and I hit it." He looked around the yard, slow, as a hammer looks around. "Su Ping took the top of the white. I took the top of what was mine. Somebody around here better explain what's funny."

Nobody explained. The yard had learned that arithmetic a year ago, from the same porch rails.

Su Ping brought the apples for the celebration, and paid for them, and stood at the edge of it, and understood something he had no panel for. He had just watched the ordinary road walk past him in the other direction, at its own pace, in good order. Ma Fei at one ring and nine was fast — genuinely fast, the best the school had produced in a decade. And the engine in the dark was already four levels past him and pulling away, and would be past the second wall while Ma Fei's second wall was still a rumor. He was glad, and the gladness was clean, and underneath the gladness was a small cold stone of arithmetic, which he put away with all the other feelings, carefully, where he could find it later.

The wall-class met in the evenings now, the two of them, Ma Fei recovering from his ring and Su Ping sitting at his wall, and in the spring Lin did something he did for no class below the wall: he opened the school's second book.

"The method you were taught is the first road, and the first road is the same everywhere — the Hall prints it, and the villages hand it down. It will carry a man to the tenth level, and no further, with any honesty." He stood at the front of the room as he stood at assemblies, and his voice was the plain one. "Above it there is a second road. Every academy keeps one. They differ in small ways and agree in the large one: the second road draws deeper water, and it does it by a different turn of the breath. Here is the turn. Watch once."

And he showed them the turn. Once, slow, in the lamplight, a half-turn of the breath that bent the water in the room as a stone bends a stream — and Su Ping felt the system take it in, silently, as it took in everything: seen, filed, held. He had a full technique seat and no second one, and the new road had nowhere to sit, and he could feel that it did not care, and would wait. One ring from now, there would be three seats.

"You will both learn it properly when your walls are broken," said Lin, and closed the second book. "The world above the tenth level is a wider country than this one. It has more roads in it, and none of them are free."

None of them are free. Su Ping had heard the shape of that truth all year, in pieces, from the same mouth. The ladder above Yunxi: the intermediate academies in the prefecture cities, testing at twelve. The advanced academies in the great cities, testing on a stone the size of the spirit hall itself. The Hall's own schools above everything, testing on nothing so crude as a stone at all. Every gate on the continent opened the same way — sit down, show the crystal, be measured — because the continent had one currency and spent it at every door. "The world is sorted by speed," Lin had said, one evening, to the wall-class, with the lamplight on the register. "You will hear people say it is sorted by blood, or by money, or by the soul a child wakes with. Blood and money buy speed. The soul sets the first pace. But the ladder itself is climbed in one currency, and the Hall's tests exist to count it, and no test on this continent can be bribed with anything a family owns."

And then, because it was the wall-class and the book was open, he had taught them what the world's treasures cost — the real lesson, the one the seniors got in their last year. "There are things that buy rank outright," Lin had said. "Herbs, for the most part, grown where the earth is strange. The Silk Tulip of the far south, one bloom, conservatively ten ranks to the man who eats it, and a body remodeled into the bargain. The Orchid that gave a spirit master in the Hall's own registry five ranks from a single plant. The sac of a ninety-thousand-year beast is worth a full rank to a Titled Douluo. And a Titled Douluo, boys, is a country's treasure — there are perhaps ten alive." He had let that sit, and then he had said the rest of it, plainly, because it was the plain truth and he did not flinch from plain truths: "All of those are once. The herb is eaten and the herb is gone, and the man who built his ladder on herbs is standing, at ninety, three steps below the man who climbed. The Hall has measured this. The world's whole treasury cannot buy what a talent is, because treasury is quantity and talent is speed, and speed is the only thing on this continent that does not spend."

Su Ping had sat in the wall-class with his face exactly still, and inside the stillness he had done his own arithmetic, and then put the answer away with the cold stone, in the same careful place.

Because he had a herb that was not a herb. He had a spring. Every night the green apple came down out of the soul, and every night the engine ran at a better best, and the well refilled itself on the method's new cleanness, and none of it was eaten and gone — it was drawn and drawn again, from a tree that stood in him, and the tree was getting older. The Silk Tulip was lightning, once. What he carried was water, and water was the one thing the world's whole treasury could not buy, and the only safety it had ever had was that no one alive knew a food-type could carry it. Not the Hall. Not Lin. Not the stories. The closed hand was not greed. The closed hand was the wall around a spring. He had known that for a year, and sitting in the wall-class with the treasure-lesson in his ears, he knew it for the first time with his whole body: the day the world learned what the green apple was, the world would come for it. Politely at first, with the Hall's seal on the politeness.

Not yet.

The letters had done that — a letter a month home, a letter a month to Wen Bo in the town, the reading of the second book with Lin's permission on long evenings. His father's reply that spring had been the longest letter the Su family had ever produced, four pages of a farmer's careful characters: the lower meadow was leased off Old Shi, the ditches were redug, the sapling stock was in the ground. *The trees take five years to bear,* his father wrote. *The village says I am a fool to wait on trees.*

*Apple trees are patient,* Su Ping wrote back. *We are patient. It runs in the family.*

Wen Bo's tenth of every queue was a purse of its own by then, and he counted it with the ox-calf patience he brought to everything, and in the spring he said — out of nowhere, over the second basket — that a man could carry fruit to the prefecture city and come home with the price of a shop. He said it carefully: the shape of a plan, not a dream. Su Ping, who had watched the porters take apples over the hill for two years, said nothing at the time. But he thought: the orchard is growing a merchant.

And at the end of the year, the office, the door shut, the crystal between the two teacups. Lin read the number and did not say it at once.

"Twenty," he said, finally. "And sitting at the wall."

"Yes, headmaster."

"At nine." Lin put the crystal away, and took out the other book — the Hall's book, with the Hall's seal dried into the cover — and opened it to a page Su Ping had never seen. "The second seat has more room in it than the first. The Hall's arithmetic says under seven hundred and sixty-four years, and the Hall's arithmetic is a fence for cattle, and you are not cattle, and a body is still a body, and bodies have limits as wells have bottoms." He turned the book so the boy could see the page: the second-seat column, the permits, the licensed forests. "Saddleback has nothing old enough. What you need is east of the river, in the deep licensed stands, and the permit for a second seat costs more than the first, and the academy's fund will stand behind it, because the academy has never in its history had a boy at the second wall at nine years old."

He closed the book, and folded his hands on it.

"The question is never whether," said Headmaster Lin. "It is which."

Outside, the yard was loud with summer, and in its seat the ring counted quietly on.

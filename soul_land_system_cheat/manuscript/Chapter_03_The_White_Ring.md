# Chapter 3: The White Ring

The winter did not stop the queue. The porters came up from the river steps in the dark and stood at the laundry line blowing on their hands, and Wen Bo counted coppers with his mittens in his teeth. The price held at four, because a man who has carried a bridge all day in the cold will pay four coppers for a night's sleep in a fruit and thank you for it. They had all heard the phrase. Most of them used it now.

Under the winter, the levels moved. Eight by the first thaw. Nine by the spring rains. He knew each one before any crystal said it; every level was a deeper hour of the same water. The meter went with him — fifty by the thaw, and no part of it free, every part of it earned in the dark while the school slept.

Ten came in late spring, between the spring planting and the first mowing.

And then, for the first time in either of his lives, nothing happened.

The method ran. The engine ran. The level did not. He gave it a week, because a farm teaches you that ground which has not moved today may still move tomorrow. He gave it two. He gave it a month, and the month went by as evenly as all the others, the hour as deep as ever, and nothing climbed, and nothing fell, and the water simply stood.

Then he knocked on the headmaster's door, and said the truest sentence he had: "The method runs, headmaster. The level does not."

"You're ten," said Lin, not looking up from the register. Then he did look up, and put the brush down, and the pause was a long one, because they both knew what the sentence meant, and only one of them had been waiting years for somebody to say it in that room. "Sit."

The gate speech was short, because Su Ping already knew the law and Lin knew he knew it. Every tenth level is a wall. Power without a ring pools at the wall and goes nowhere, and may pool there for a year, or ten years, or the rest of a life; the wall does not care. The ring opens it. That is the whole of the law, and it has been the whole of the law for every cultivator since the Hall began counting.

"The question is never whether," said Lin. "It is which. And for you, it is where." He took out a different book — the Hall's book, with the Hall's seal dried into the cover — and laid it flat. "The Academy hunts in the Hall's ground at Saddleback, two hours east. Escort and permit, three silver, the Hall's schedule. I do not waive it for anyone; it is why the arrangement you and I have holds. Pay it like you pay the rest."

Su Ping nodded. Three silver was about a month of evenings at the laundry line, and he had known the schedule for half a year, because he had looked it up the week he first sold an apple.

"I have taken eleven first rings into that forest," Lin said, "and brought eleven out, and every one of them was a young boar, because a boar is what you take a boy for. The oldest was thirty years." He closed the Hall's book. "You will not be taking a boar."

"Why not?"

"Because a ring is its years, not its teeth. And the skill is only the first of what a ring gives. The ring goes into the soul itself: an old ring feeds the soul's own nature, year for year." He turned the Hall's book toward the boy. "A ten-year ring lifts a soul ten years' worth. A ninety-year ring lifts it ninety. That is the whole of why everyone alive hunts the oldest beast their body can hold." Lin stood, and went to the window, and pointed east, at a blue shoulder of hill standing over the town's roofs. "There is a scar on the south side of Saddleback. An old landslide shelf — stones the size of hogs, nothing grazes it, nothing plows it, nobody builds on it. There is grass on that scar that has been growing since before I had this office. Ninety years, if it's a day. Nobody hunts it. There is no meat on grass, no hide, no trophy; a white grass ring marks a man poor. Half the trash souls in the Hall's registry are that same grass — it wakes in somebody's hand every spring somewhere and breaks a mother's heart. And on that scar, the same grass has been quietly outliving every boar on that hill."

Su Ping looked at the blue shoulder of the hill and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the hill wasn't already saying.

"You will hear the whole of it," Lin said, sitting back down, and his voice went into the register-voice, the plain one, the one that read levels out at assemblies. "A ring at the top of the white is the most a first body can hold. Under the top is safe. At the top is a wager. If the body refuses it, the ring scatters, and the refusal burns — you could lose the level. You could lose the year. You could lose more than the year. If it takes, it takes everything the beast was, and some of what it was is pain." He folded his hands. "Ninety-nine years is a wager on your body, Su Ping. I have watched your body for a year. I would not make this wager on any other child in this school."

Su Ping sat with it, honestly, as he sat with everything. A year lost was a year the engine would have to buy back. More than the year did not bear thinking about, so he thought about it for exactly as long as it deserved, and then stopped.

"Then we take the top," he said.

Lin wrote something in the register, and did not read it out.

Two days later, Su Ping bought a field hoe at the ironmonger's — a plain one, a good one, the iron smooth and the shaft straight — and counted out three silver at the Academy's office, coin by coin, in front of the ledger. It was the first tool he had ever bought with money the apple had earned. His father's walking stick rode the cart with him anyway, because some tools are not for work.

Wen Bo was at the gate before dawn with a bundle of road apples wrapped in straw. "Bring me back a vine," he said. "For luck." He did not offer to come, because the permit had one name on it, and Wen Bo had asked at the office and been told, and had nodded, and that was all that had been said about it, and it had been exactly enough.

The cart went east with the sun not yet up. Two hours of road, and the warden at the forest hut read the permit twice, because the taker's age was on it, and stamped it anyway, and pointed at the hill without a word.

They walked the last mile through grass. Blue Silver Grass, ankle-deep, shin-deep, pale and common as dust, running up the slopes and down the gullies and out to the edge of sight — the continent's floor, the grass nobody looks at twice, the soul that breaks a mother's heart every spring. And Su Ping looked at it the whole way up, because it was the whole point. Everyone in the world walks on this grass, he thought. Everyone in the world has always walked on this grass. Somewhere on this hill, one blade of it has been keeping count.

The scar was bigger than it had looked from the town — a shelf of broken stone running along the south face, dry, loud underfoot, hot already. The stand on it stood out from across the valley: bluer than the hillside, taller than grass had any right to be, every blade at the root as thick as cart-rope.

Lin stopped below it and looked up the shelf for a while. "Somewhere in that," he said, "the old one. Ninety years if it's a day. I've climbed past this scar twice a year for twenty years and watched it thicken. I could never tell you which crown is the mother plant. The whole shelf is one family and it keeps its own books."

Su Ping read the slope.

He read it as he had read orchards since before he could read words, which is to say he did not think about it at all. The lower shelf was young blades, crowded, quick, the green-blue of fast growth — new soil, slide country. The middle was older, thicker, seeded-out. And at the top of the shelf, where the stones were biggest and the ground between them thinnest and driest, the stand went a blue that was nearly silver, and stood a hand higher than its children, and held its ground like a thing that had been argued with.

Old ground grows old grass. Old roots hold the stony ground, because that is the only ground left for them.

"There," he said. "The old one is there. The ground argued with it the longest."

Lin looked where he was pointing, and then at him, and made a sound that in a less careful man would have been a laugh. "Eleven boars," he said, "and the first man to find the mother plant on Saddleback is a fruit farmer's son of seven. All right. Stay behind my left arm and do exactly as I say, and remember the one law of the day: the ring comes to the hand that kills it. Mine opens the way. The last stroke has to be yours."

They climbed.

The grass knew they were coming. There was no wind on the shelf, and at ten paces out the stand moved — a slow tightening, blades leaning in like a crowd turning its head — and at five paces the first vines came out of it, fast, low, ropes thrown by something buried, and Lin's soul was open before they arrived.

It was a hoe.

A plain field hoe, the iron gone dark, a working man's tool that had forty years of the real thing behind it before it ever came out of his hand. Over it hung his rings — one white, two yellow, the honest line of a farm boy who had taken the plainest soul in the Hall's registry and walked it to Spirit Elder anyway. The yard knew the story. The whole town knew the story. Su Ping had never once heard Lin tell it.

The work took the morning. The vines did not fight like an animal fights; they fought like rope fights, patient, strong at the wrong moments, everywhere. Lin cut a vine and the vine took two more strokes to be cut. He stepped through the stand to the crown, hoeing a circle, and the stand closed behind him and opened again. Once a vine took his ankle and put the headmaster down on one knee — a yellow ring brightening over his head before the vine let go. Lin stood up, looked at the boy behind his left arm, and said, conversationally, "You understand that if that had found you instead of me, we would not be having this morning."

"I understand," said Su Ping.

"Good. Now it's yours."

The crown was bared — a root-mass the size of a cartwheel, side roots cut, the taproot running down under the biggest stone on the shelf. The vines had gone slow. The stand had stopped closing. Ninety-nine years of the most patient life on the hill was down to one root and one stroke, and the grass, which could not run and had never once needed to, waited.

Su Ping did not chop. Farmers do not chop at a root they mean to kill; they undermine it. He dug the collar of the crown free with the corner of the blade, cut the last side roots, put his hands into the ground as he had put his hands into bean rows all his first life, found the taproot against the flat of the stone, and stood up. His father's walking stick he laid aside. He set his own hoe — the first tool his own money had ever bought — where the taproot was, and let the weight of the iron and of the whole first year fall.

One stroke, under the crown. The whole shelf let go at the same moment — every blade on the scar going soft together, like a crowd exhaling — and it was done, and it had been a grass, and before the last of it settled, above the crown, the ring rose.

White. Turning slowly. The white of good paper, bright at the rim, and it hung there — patient, weightless, not waiting long — like nothing else in either of his lives.

"Eat first," said Lin. "You know what hours cost. Then sit, and it comes to you, and after that, boy, it is between you and it."

Su Ping ate one of his own apples to the core — his last, the road apples went to Wen Bo's luck and Lin's pocket — and set his back against the biggest stone, and the ring came down to him.

It did not go through him. It settled into him, slowly, rain into long-dry ground. The first hour was all surface — heat, and the strange double weight of a thing that was both in him and on him — and after the first hour there was no surface left anywhere, and nobody outside could have said where it was going.

Lin sat a pace away with his hand ready, and the hand stayed a hand's width off the boy's back for an hour at a time, and never once had to land.

Ninety-nine springs went through him. He did not count them. The system counted them.

Somewhere in the second hour, the wall that had stood at ten since late spring was not there anymore. It did not break. It opened — and everything the weeks had pooled behind it walked through, quietly, in single file, like a household moving house in the night. And under that, deeper, the other things the ring had brought began to arrive, one at a time, arriving as the deacon had said the whole law of souls arrives: fuel, rest, cultivation, rings.

The well had a new spring under it. He could feel the new water without being able to see it — deeper than it had been in the morning, and the water ran the same, only more of it. The ring was the grass's own cultivating, ninety-nine years of it, poured in and staying. A ring is what the beast was, given — he had heard the shape of that in the headmaster's office, and now he knew it from the inside, as water knows a channel.

The apple had a second apple in it. That was the strangest of them, and the truest: his soul, which had been one fruit all his life, now held a second fruit set in it — small, hard, green, and, from the first moment of it, awake. And the use came with it. That was the part no book had warned him about, because no book needed to: a ring's skill arrives in its master's hands already knowing its own work. He had never seen the green fruit and could not have described it that morning; now he knew what it was for without being able to say how he knew, as a hand knows, without looking, what it is holding. The red apple gave a man his strength back. The green one would make the climbing quicker.

And the old fruit had changed too. Before they came down he brought the soul up once more — the red apple, his own, the one he had conjured ten thousand times — and it came into his hand redder than it had ever been, and heavier, and the smell of it was the first week of picking, deep enough to stand in. The ring had not only set a new fruit in the tree. It had gone into the tree. Ninety-nine years of the grass's law — the plant that outlives everything that eats it — had gone into the apple's own nature, year for year, and the tree was ninety-nine years better than it had been that morning. The red fruit had always given a man his strength back. Now it would give back more of it, further and cleaner, for as long as he owned the soul.

And the body — the body was being fed. He woke at dusk, at the end of it, and lifted the water jar Lin had brought up the hill, and had to set it down and look at his hands. The draft had been fed, and under the draft, quieter than the draft, the grass's own stock of life had gone into him and lain down like seed in a drill — vitality, the oldest property there is. He felt like sown ground the morning after rain.

The panels, when he let them come, were three quiet lines.

Two slots in every list, and two plantings done before dark: the old parked art back in the second slot, waking at fourteen where it had slept — and the ring set into its own place in the ring list, its years under it like water under ice, ninety-nine of them, and the number not going to stand still.

That night, back in his own bed at the school, he did the new thing first.

He held his palm open and set the new words in his mind — they had come with the fruit, as the use had come with it — and the green apple fell into his hand. Small, hard, the color of a leaf before it turns. The first of its kind anywhere in the world. He ate it to the core, and lay back, and the method hour came, and it was not the hour he had sat through a thousand times.

Nothing hurt. Nothing shone. The hour was the same length it had always been, and the water that moved in it moved quicker — his best day had gotten better, and the engine, which ran at nothing except his best, ran at that. And under that, faint and steady, two slotted things ran together for the first time since the awakening day — the method in one slot, the old body art waking in the other — turning like the mill he had grown up in sight of, the grain between the stones moving faster than either stone could account for.

He lay in the dark and held the whole shape of it in his mind, as his father held the shape of an orchard. The well fed the fruit. The fruit fed the engine. The engine fed the well, and the body under all of it, and every tree fed the others. Each thing he grew made every other thing quicker to grow.

The engine had been one field. Now it was two, with an orchard starting between them, and all of it his.

One more thing had changed, and the porters found it before he did. The red apples kept now. The one he set on the windowsill at dusk was still crisp at breakfast, and no apple he had ever made had seen a breakfast. By the next week the porters were carrying them over the hill to the next valley, and the price held at four anyway, because a fair price is also a thing that ripens. Wen Bo counted all of it, and said nothing, and started keeping a second basket.

"Eleven," said Lin the next morning, at the crystal in his office, and put the crystal away, and poured two cups of tea, which was the most startled Su Ping had ever seen him.

The yard saw the ring three days later, at the first soul-class of the summer term, when the apple came out over his palm with a white ring turning above it. Somebody at the back snorted — a white ring, the snort said, all that for the poor man's white — and it was Ma Fei who turned around, Ma Fei with the iron hammer, Ma Fei who had stood in the queue at the laundry like everyone else.

"It's the biggest white there is," Ma Fei said. He was not quick, but he was honest, and he had a farmer's arithmetic when he bothered to use it. "The top of it. Nothing over ninety-nine exists in the white. He took the ceiling."

That evening Ma Fei found him at the porch rail. "You said to ask when you had a ring," he said. "You've got a ring."

"Ask," said Su Ping.

"Does it do anything? Really?"

"The red one does what it has always done." Su Ping looked at the dark coming down over the yard, and at the size of what he was carrying, and did not open his hand any farther. "The ring put a second apple on the tree. The green one is mine, for now."

"Fair," said Ma Fei.

He wrote home twice that summer. The June letter said the apples had sold all winter, and he had a ring now, and it was white, and he was well. The purse went with it, heavier than the New Year one. What came back in the return basket was nothing written at all: a knot of dried plum blossoms from the tree by the spirit hall, and word up the valley — came with the carrier, secondhand, as everything in Fruit Creek had always traveled — that the granary had a new roof on it before harvest.

By autumn the ring's quiet line said a hundred and four. The grass had needed ninety-nine springs for its first ninety-nine years, and its hundredth came before his first school year was out, and the white of the ring went the color of young straw. Only he could see why.

The method said seventy-nine at midsummer, and ninety at the first frost, and then the road did the thing Lin had said it did to everyone.

The last ten parts were not hours. They were edges. The method's joints — the places where the teaching of it met the body doing it — had to be sanded flat by repetition, each one, and no engine sands. It only runs. He found a hitch in the circuit at the left shoulder, half a breath, at the top of every turn, and once he had found it he could not unfind it: eleven months he had carried it without knowing he was carrying anything, and now he carried it on purpose, as a man carries the last stone of a wall.

And the last part stood for most of a winter. It did not stand still — nothing the engine touched ever stood still — but it stood, a season long, ninety-nine parts in a hundred with the hundredth hung just out of reach. And he learned the thing the mastery law had always said, the thing the books meant when they said almost nobody finishes the basic: the last part is not more work. The last part is a different kind of work, and it does not care how fast you are. It cares how true.

He kept everything else going, because everything else was going anyway. The queue at the laundry line, twice a week now, Wen Bo's one in ten. The letters, one a month, getting longer. The list under the slots, growing on plain life like it always had.

At the year's end, Lin read him in private. The crystal sat on the desk between the two teacups, and the number it gave did not go to the assembly, and did not go into the yard list, and Lin looked at it for a long time before he said it out loud.

"Fifteen."

"Yes, headmaster."

Lin looked at the crystal a while longer, and then poured, and the pouring was very steady, which told Su Ping what it was costing to keep it so. Five levels in one school year. There was no column in the register for that. "One day," he said, "I will have to write to the Hall about you." He did not say the rest of it. He did not have to. "Not yet."

The year's list came to Su Ping that night, as it always came, quietly and all at once, and it had grown a line — the four crooked characters at New Year had become a letter a month, and the system, which counted everything, had counted that too.

The ring's line said a hundred and eleven, and yellow as young straw, and growing. And both fruits ran a shade deeper than the summer's — the red one's rest, the green one's quickening — because the years were the ring's now, and the ring was in the tree, and the tree was his.

And on the last night of the school year — a year to the week since the gate had first taken him — he sat through the method hour as he had sat through a thousand of them, with the hitch at the left shoulder, half a breath, at the top of every turn.

At the top of one turn, it let go.

There was no sound, and no light, and nothing fell anywhere. The hitch, which he had carried for eleven months without knowing it and one winter with, simply was not in him anymore. The circuit ran all the way round for the first time, closed, clean, a circle with no seam in it anywhere. The breath found the length that was exactly his breath, and the turn found the corner that was exactly his corner, and he knew it before the panel moved, as a miller knows the stones are done by the change in the sound.

It did not feel like a number. It felt like a coat he had worn for a year being taken apart and re-cut and put back on his shoulders, and the shoulders were his. The method still had every hour he had ever spent on it, every one of the ten thousand turns, and it had not lost a stitch of the teaching — and it fit like nothing given has ever fit, because it was not given anymore. He had not made it. He had only done it ten thousand times, and somewhere in the ten thousandth, the making had quietly turned into his.

The big thing in himself. The thing the headmaster said the road remembers. He sat inside it in the dark and felt it settle, like a field finally level after a year of stone-picking, and he was seven years old, and he was forty, and both of those people were very, very still.

In the stories of his old world there was a shape like this, and he had read it a hundred times and never once believed he would be sitting in front of one: the door at the top of the mastery. Completion was the rare thing — most climb on a good-enough road and never finish the basic at all, and the books were right about that, he had a winter's proof of it in his shoulders — and behind the rare thing, the stories all agreed on what waited. Not a reward given. A thing returned. A method with your own name in it.

He sat very still and watched the panel.

The line did not close, and it did not fade. It changed. The old words — Basic Spirit Power Cultivation, the Hall's name for it, the school's name, the whole continent's name for the first road — were simply not there anymore, and other words stood in their place, quiet and plain, as a man's own name stands in a ledger after a lifetime of being somebody's son.

The Still Well Method. He said it once, in his mind, where the words lived, and it fit as the breath had fit — exactly, because it was his. He had not chosen it. He had only sat still, and sat still, and sat still, and somewhere in the ten thousandth turn the choosing had quietly been done for him out of everything he was. The headmaster's stories were true, then, and had not gone far enough: the road gives a finished man back a method with his own name in it — and the name is not a reward. It is a description.

And the meter had begun again. One part in a hundred — and the one part was the hundred he had already earned. The finished basic was the first step of the larger road, stood on and paid for, and the System had counted it so, because the System measured what existed. What existed now was a man standing at the beginning of a road with the whole of another road in his legs.

The engine, which had never once stopped, ran on into the new country at the same even pace, and the new road's first hour was deeper than the old road's last.

He was seven years old, and he had finished a thing almost nobody finishes, and the finishing had not closed anything. It had opened. The big thing in himself was a gate, and it stood wide, and the water, still and deep, climbed on.

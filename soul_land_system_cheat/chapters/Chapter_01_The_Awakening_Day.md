# Chapter 1: The Awakening Day

The cart left Fruit Creek before the sun was up, and Su Ping rode it with his legs hanging off the tailboard, watching the village fall behind orchard row by orchard row.

His father walked beside the donkey. His mother rode beside him and held his collar square every time the wheel hit a stone, which was often, because the mountain road was all stones. Behind them walked half the village. It was a Tuesday in the plum-blossom season, and nobody in Fruit Creek was working, because today was the day the Spirit Hall's man came to the shrine at the fork, and Fruit Creek had a child turning six this spring.

There were three of them in the cart: Su Ping, who was six; Da Hu, the miller's son, who was six and half a head taller; and Little Zhen, the tanner's daughter, who was six and small enough that her mother had sewn her a new jacket with room to grow. They sat in a row on the sacks and did not talk much. The road was long and the morning was cold, and a strange quiet sat on all three of them, the quiet of children who had been told for a year that today would decide the shape of their whole lives.

Su Ping felt the quiet too, but it sat on him lightly — weather he had seen before. He had been a strange child, the whole village agreed on that — too steady, too patient, hard to frighten, slow to cry, the boy who would watch a pot boil all afternoon just to see how it went. He was six years old, and behind his eyes there was something older. He did not know what it was. He only knew that the world had never once felt new to him, and that waiting did not cost him anything.

The shrine at the fork was just one room of grey stone with a plum tree beside it, and when they arrived, the Spirit Hall's man was already there.

---

He was a tired man in a blue robe gone pale at the shoulders, and he had come up the mountain road in a smaller cart with a smaller donkey and a locked chest, and he had done this, he said, in forty villages since the spring thaw. His name was Deacon Bao. He unfolded a table under the plum tree, set a crystal orb the size of a cabbage on it, and called the children up one at a time in the order of their birthdays.

Da Hu went first. He put his hand on the orb as he had been shown, and his face went red with pushing, and the orb lit a faint, dull white — and a hoe appeared in the air over his head. Not a grand hoe. A plain iron hoe, the kind his father milled flour with a version of every day, hanging in the air and turning slowly.

"Tool soul, common grade," said Deacon Bao, writing it down. "Innate spirit power, two. The Hall's blessing on the family." And to Da Hu's father, who had gone very still: "He can farm better than ten men, one day, if he cultivates. There are worse souls. Most souls are worse souls."

Da Hu's father put his fist to his heart and bowed like the man had handed him a title, and maybe he had. In a village of two hundred people, the strong backs were the wealth.

Little Zhen went second, and the orb lit, and a sparrow flew out of the air over her head and landed on the table and looked at everyone with one eye.

"Beast soul, common grade," said Deacon Bao. "Innate spirit power, one. The Hall's blessing." His voice had not changed at all between the two, and it did not change for the third. "Su Ping, of the Su family. Come."

Su Ping came, and put his hand on the orb.

It was warm. That surprised him; he had thought stone would feel like stone. The warmth climbed his arm like water finding a channel. For one breath the whole morning seemed to lean toward him — the plum tree, the crowd, his mother's hand frozen halfway to her mouth. Then the light came up inside the orb, brighter than the other two had been, and something fell into the world above his open palm.

An apple.

It hung in the air a moment and then dropped into his hand, warm through, red as the new year, perfect. It weighed what an apple weighs. It smelled of the top of the pile in the first week of picking, the week the whole village smiles for no reason they'd say out loud.

Nobody said anything at all. Behind him, Su Ping heard his father breathe in.

"Tool soul," said Deacon Bao slowly. He came around the table and looked at the apple from two sides, and something in his tired face moved for the first time that morning. "Food type. I have seen one before — one, in thirty years, and that was in a big city, not a mountain." He looked at the orb, and read the last of the light out of it. "Innate spirit power, three. Middling. The Hall's blessing."

"Middling," said Su Ping's father, in the voice of a man carrying a full tray who has just been told not to drop it.

"To the Hall, middling," said Deacon Bao. "The Hall measures ten, and ten goes to the academies of the capital. Three goes wherever three wants to." He turned to the crowd, and lifted his voice the little bit a tired man keeps for the end of the day. "A food-type soul is a rare thing. It does not fight. It feeds, it restores, it keeps people on their feet. The cities pay well for the food-type, and the food-type pays well for itself. Fruit Creek should mark this day."

The village did not need telling. Old Shi, the headman, was already turning to the crowd with his arms up, and the noise that came off the two hundred people of Fruit Creek was the noise of a festival being born in the space of one breath.

Su Ping stood in the middle of it and looked at the apple.

He felt proud, and underneath the pride, faint and far away, like a sound from another room, he felt something he had no name for: the feeling of a thing arriving exactly where it was always going to arrive. He was six, so he put the feeling away with all his other feelings, carefully, where he could find them later.

The apple was still warm. On an impulse he did not examine, he turned and held it up to his father.

"You carry the pruning knife all day," he said. "Eat it."

---

They tried the apple there at the shrine, because nobody could wait, and it was Deacon Bao himself who ran the test, waving the village back with both arms.

Su Ping's father had spent the morning walking a mountain road and the last ten years carrying orchard ladders, and at mid-morning he was honestly tired, the deep tired in the shoulders that a man does not notice anymore because it never leaves. He ate the apple down to the core while the whole village watched him chew.

Then he stood very still.

"It's nothing," he said, and then, honest as the day, "it's not nothing. I was tired. I'm not." He rolled his shoulders, and everyone saw it — the weight coming off them. "Like the first morning of the picking week. Like a night's sleep in a fruit."

The village made the sound again, louder.

"It restores," said Deacon Bao, writing fast now. "Stamina, fatigue, minor hurts, hunger — that would be the usual shape of it. Not wounds, not spirit power, at this grade. Conjured how? Speak, don't speak — does it need words?"

Su Ping had already found that out, in the space under his ribs where the warmth had gone when the apple came. There was a small pool there, the deacon's next questions established, a child's-small pool of spirit power, and the apple was drawn from it like water from a well. And there was a word the well wanted before it would give. He had said it without sound, in his mind, when the first apple fell — *ripe, and given* — because it had felt right — a first line he had always known. He did not shout it. He never shouted. But he said it again now, inside his own head, and the second apple dropped warm into his other hand.

"Spoken in the mind," said Deacon Bao. He had gone from tired to interested in the space of a quarter hour. "That is a mercy, boy. The one I saw before — the sausage man in the city — he had to say his aloud, and it has made him the joke of every market he works in. Yours is a quiet soul. Keep it quiet." He weighed the second apple in his own hand, and put it back. "It will spoil by tomorrow — eat them the day they're made. The strength in them is set when they're made, not when they're eaten. And your pool, boy — a pool is small and grows slow. Two apples a day for a season, then three, then more as you grow. Don't spend it all on festival days."

"Three apples a day," said Old Shi, to the village at large. And then he said the thing that Fruit Creek said about the day for the next fifty years. It went into the song: "The mountain has finally paid us back."

They ate the apples that evening at the long tables under the plum trees — the day's two, sliced thin by Su Ping's mother so that two hundred people could each have a taste. Da Hu's father wept a little into his cup. Little Zhen's mother asked twice whether the strength would still be there in the morning. The deacon took his fee in pickled plums and a new pair of sandals. And everyone was happy.

Su Ping went to bed with the taste of his own soul still on his lips, which was a strange thing to sleep on, and he was almost asleep when the other thing woke up.

---

There was no voice. That was the first fact, and he learned it before he learned anything else, because he had been read a story once by a traveling teacher about a spirit that whispered to greedy men, and this was not that. There was nothing in it at all. There was only a quiet shape hanging in the dark behind his eyes, like a lantern behind a shutter, waiting.

He lay still in the dark of the loft, heart going, and looked at it.

It was a reading. That was what it felt like, and he had no better word, so he kept that one. Lines, thin and cold and plain as the cracks in the loft floor:

> 「Slots: 1 / 1 / 1」

He looked at that for a long time. He was six, and he could not read, and it did not matter, because the lines were not letters. They were just themselves, plain as the taste of plum, and he understood them as he understood hunger. Slots, said the line. Three kinds of them, one each. It did not say one each of what. It said nothing else at all. It simply hung there, cold and patient, like the ledger book his mother kept for the midwife work — a thing that would hold what was written in it and never once explain itself.

Nothing he did that night changed it. He thought at it, and prodded it, and lay very still to see if it would go away, and it did not go away and did not answer and did not care. It was not watching him. It was not anything. It was just there, like the floor.

He slept. In the morning it was still there, and in the morning — being who he was, the boy who watched pots boil — he stopped trying to make it talk and started noticing what it noticed, and by the end of the second day he had found the first thing that went into it.

His father had him carrying the small water buckets up the orchard rows that day, because a spirit child still had chores, and a spirit child's body was still a six-year-old's body, soft and short-winded. Su Ping knew the work. He had been doing it since he could walk, and Old Gao next door had shown him years ago how to set his feet on the slope so the load rode the legs and not the back — the basics, as the village taught them. And on the second trip up the row, with the readout hanging quiet behind his eyes, he felt it lean toward the work. Not toward him. Toward the work.

He stopped in the row, set the buckets down, and thought — carefully, as a seedling goes into its hole — *there. Take that.*

> 「Technique — Basic Body Conditioning: 0%」

The line sat there. He picked the buckets back up and climbed, and the line did nothing more, and nothing more, and he decided it was broken, and on the third morning, when the readout woke with him, the line had changed.

> 「Technique — Basic Body Conditioning: 1%」

One part in a hundred. He stood in the loft with the dawn coming through the slats and looked at the small cold number and felt the feeling from the shrine come back, the far-away sound of a thing arriving where it was always going to arrive.

He did not know what the slots were for. He did not know what the other two would hold — he had no rings, no bones he knew of, and the words sat in his mind like unplowed ground. He did not know whether one part in a hundred was fast or slow or what happened at a hundred. Nobody knew. There was nobody to ask, and nothing in the world that would have answered if he had.

He was six years old, and he had a soul that fed people, a body that was learning to carry water up a hill, and a cold quiet lantern behind his eyes that counted.

He decided, in the wordless way he decided things, that he would find out what the lantern was, and that he was in no hurry.

---

The deacon left on the fourth day, and before he left, he sat in the Su family's kitchen with a cup of plum tea and told Su Ping's father the plain arithmetic of the thing.

"Yunxi has the junior academy," he said. "Town of four thousand, up the valley where the river comes down. Boarding, six years, they take any child with awakened power — the Empire pays half and the family pays half, and half of what it costs to make a spirit master of a fruit-farmer's son is more than Fruit Creek sees in a season. That is the truth, and here is the rest of it. Keep him here, and in ten years he is the strongest man on the mountain with a talent for cider. Send him down, and in ten years he is a spirit scholar with his first ring, and the food-type gets rarer and dearer every league you walk from a place like this. The world is not kind to villages. It is only kind to villages that send their children out."

"We have no way to pay half," said Su Ping's father.

"You have two hundred people who ate his apples," said the deacon, "and a headman who has been standing outside this window for ten minutes listening."

From the window, Old Shi's voice: "The village pays. The village has decided. The village decided on the day the mountain paid us back."

His mother sewed new room into his good jacket and packed it full of chestnuts. His father pared a walking stick from the orchard's oldest apple tree, wrapped the end in cloth, and said nothing about it, because there was nothing to say that the stick didn't say. The village loaded the cart with pickled plums and dried fruit and a purse of copper that half of them could not spare, and on the morning the plum blossoms began to fall, the cart rolled down the valley toward Yunxi.

Su Ping rode the tailboard with his legs hanging, as he had ridden it up, and watched Fruit Creek fall behind orchard row by orchard row for the last time that season. Behind his eyes, quiet as ever, the lantern counted.

> 「Technique — Basic Body Conditioning: 4%」

Four parts in a hundred. The road went down, the river came up to meet it, and the whole of his life, the part that would matter, was beginning — quietly, the way an orchard begins, one root at a time.

# Chapter Five — The One Who Watched

*Still the deep era. Still the glade. The blade of the silver patch has become a power of
his region — a grass the herd leaves alone, the sanctuary of the small and the lost. And for
a century something vast has crossed his ground at night without ever once looking at him.
Then, one moonlit night, it lay down at his edge. It is still there. And he is beginning to
understand that it has not come to eat him, or to use him, or to pass him by — it has come
to learn what he is.*

It came back the next night.

He had not known it would. He had not known anything about it — only its weight, its
patience, its unhurried fearlessness, and the strange still night when it had lain down at
his edge in the moonlight and stayed until the moon was low. He had felt it leave, and he
had felt the empty ground where it had rested, and he had thought, in the way a grass thinks
— which is not a thought but a leaning, a turned attention — that it had been a single
night, a pause on a journey, a thing that would not come again.

It came again at the next moonrise.

He felt it coming the way he always felt it: first as a pressure in the deep ground, then as
a rhythm — the slow, huge, unhurried tread that he had known since his youth, the rhythm of
a thing that had never once hurried for anything in its long life. It came down the slope,
crossed the stream at the shallows, and walked to the edge of his ground.

And it lay down.

Not at the edge, this time. Closer. It lay down so that its great warm side was against the
outermost reach of his roots, so that he felt its heartbeat through the soil — a slow,
heavy drum, slower than any heartbeat he had ever felt, slower than the stream, slower than
the turning of the seasons. He had felt that heartbeat before, in passing, for a hundred
years. He had never felt it stay.

He did not know what to do with a thing that stayed.

For the small things of the sanctuary, staying had meant pressing into his deep green and
being still, and he had learned to hold them. For her — for the mantis, the one who had
stayed — staying had meant a small warm weight on his blades, and he had learned to shelter
her, and then to lose her, and the loss had taught him the arithmetic that was now the very
shape of him.

This was different. This was not small, and it did not press into him, and it was not his
to shelter. This was vast, and old, and it lay beside him the way a mountain lies beside a
valley — not needing anything from him, not offering anything he could understand, simply
*present*, a warmth and a heartbeat and a slow breathing at the edge of his ground.

He did not know what to do with it. So he did what he had always done with the things he
could not understand: he attended. He turned his whole attention to it, the way his roots
turned toward the deep water in the dry years — not reaching, not grasping, just there,
open, listening.

It came every night after that.

---

The first tap came in the third moon, on a night when the stream was low and the glade was
quiet and even the night-sounds had gone still.

He had grown used to the rhythm of the old one's sleeping — the slow breathing, the rare
shift of its great weight, the heartbeat like a drum heard through water. He had begun, in
his wordless way, to read its sleeping the way he read everything: the deepening of its
breath before it woke, the small adjustments of its weight before it rose. He knew when it
was about to move before it moved. He knew the particular stillness of its attention — the
way the whole mass of it would go quiet, listening, the way the glade itself went quiet
when it was listening.

And on that night, in that stillness, it moved one foreleg and brought it down on the
ground beside his roots.

*Thump.*

The ground rang with it. He felt it through every root he had — a single, deliberate,
weighted blow, not the casual pressure of a step but something *made*, shaped, sent. It was
not like the footfalls he knew. It was not a walking rhythm. It was a word, if the ground
could speak words.

He did not know it was a word. He only knew that the blow had been meant for him — that the
old one had made the ground speak, and had turned its attention to him, and was waiting.

He did not know how to answer.

He had no foreleg. He had no voice. He had roots, and blades, and a hundred and forty years
of keeping, and the one thing the talent had taught him to do with his body was reach — a
blade that could bend, a root that could tighten, a grip that could hold. He reached.

It was not a thump. It was barely anything — a tightening of his lateral roots, a tension
run through the soil like a held breath, a small vibration that did not carry a root's
length from him. He sent it anyway, the only answer he had, and then he waited, the way he
had waited through every long thing in his life.

The old one did not move for a long time.

Then it lifted its foreleg and brought it down again.

*Thump. Thump.*

Two blows, slower, softer — and then the great weight settled, and the heartbeat went on,
and he understood, without knowing he understood, that he had been answered.

---

It took him seasons to learn what the old one was saying, and the old one took seasons to
learn that he could hear it at all.

That was the strange education of that year: two beings, one vast and one small, neither
with a word between them, learning to speak through the ground. The old one tapped, and he
read the taps the way he read footfalls — except that these were not footfalls, these were
*meant*, and so they carried meaning the way a hoof-beat did not. It tapped when it was
settling for the night: one soft blow, a word that came to mean, to him, *here, resting*.
It tapped when it heard something in the dark that he could not feel: a sharper double-blow
that made him go still and listen. It tapped when the cold was coming, days before the cold
arrived, and he would feel the warning through the ground and spend the following days
drawing his green down toward his root, and when the frost came he would be ready, and he
would feel the old one's approval in the way its breathing deepened, slow and satisfied,
like a contented thing.

And he learned to answer.

His root-tension was a whisper against the old one's thunder, but it carried. He found he
could send a pulse through the soil — a tightening, a release, a tightening — that ran a
few roots' lengths and stopped where the old one's great side lay against the ground. He
sent it when he was glad of the old one's presence, which was a feeling he had no word for
but which he could shape into a pulse the way he shaped his blades into shelter. He sent it
when he was afraid — once, when a hunting pack of the mid-band came down the glade at dusk,
he sent a pulse that he meant as *here is danger*, and the old one rose, and stood, and the
pack saw it and was gone before he had finished trembling.

He sent it the night he understood, at last, what the old one was.

It had come to him on a night when the moon was full and the glade was silver, and he had
felt it settle at his edge, and he had sent his pulse of greeting — and instead of the one
answering tap, the old one had lain its whole great head down against the ground beside
him, and had breathed out, a long warm breath that stirred his outermost blades, and had
stayed there, its eye closed, its heartbeat slow and huge against his roots.

And he had felt, through the ground, through the warmth, through the slow drum of its
heart — the thing he had been feeling for a hundred years without knowing its name.

The old one was alone.

It was alone the way he was alone. It had outlived whatever it had loved — he could feel
that now, in the weight of it, in the slowness, in the particular stillness of a thing that
has stopped expecting anyone to answer. It had walked this ground for longer than the glade
had been a glade. It had crossed his patch for a hundred years and never once looked at
him, because there had been nothing alive in the world that it expected to look back.

And then a patch of grass had grown teeth. And a patch of grass had kept the small and the
lost. And a patch of grass had answered a hawk, and a shadow cat, and the world — and the
old one, who had stopped expecting anything, had lain down beside it anyway, to see.

He sent his pulse then, the strongest he could make it — not a greeting, not a fear, not a
warning. A pulse that meant, in the only language he had: *I am here. I am here. I am here.*

The old one did not tap back.

It lay its head against the ground, and it breathed out, and its heartbeat slowed, and it
slept — the deep, trusting sleep of a thing that has finally, after a very long time, lain
down somewhere it does not expect to be alone.

---

*He did not know how old the old one was. He did not know what it was, or where it had come
from, or why it had chosen his ground of all the ground in the forest. He only knew its
weight, and its patience, and its loneliness — and his own — and the slow drum language
they had built between them out of the earth.*

*But the old one knew. And the old one remembered.*

*It remembered being small, once, in a time before the glade, when the forest was deeper
and the sky was higher and the world was full of its own kind. It remembered the long
centuries of its youth — the hunts, the territories, the great slow wars of the deep band
that were fought in weight and patience rather than speed. It remembered the winter, long
ago, when the sickness had come through its kind, and the springs after, when there had
been fewer, and fewer, and then only it.*

*It remembered the day it had first understood that it was the last.*

*It had not been grief, exactly. It had been a quiet arithmetic, the way old things learn
arithmetic: there was no one left who remembered what it remembered. No one left who had
known the forest when the deep band was full of its own great slow kind. It would carry the
whole memory of them alone, and when it died the memory would die with it, and the forest
would go on not knowing it had ever held such a people.*

*It had walked alone for a long time after that. It had walked the boundaries of the deep
band the way it had walked them when its kind had held them together — because that was
what it was for, what it was made for, the last keeper of a boundary no one else remembered
was there. It had watched the forest change around it. It had watched the middle band grow
quieter and the deep band grow emptier. It had crossed the glade by the stream a thousand
times and a thousand times, and the glade had never once noticed it, because the glade was
grass and water and small lives and did not know it was being watched over.*

*And then one spring, a patch of grass by the water had not lain flat when the herd came.*

*The old one had been crossing at the far edge of the glade when it happened — had felt,
through the ground, the strange wrongness of it: grass that held, grass that answered,
grass that sent the herd away bleeding. It had stopped. It had stood in the dark for a long
time, and it had remembered — dimly, from the oldest layer of its memory, from the time of
its own youth when the deep forest had been full of wonders — that there were grasses in
the world that were not grass. That there had been, once, powers of the green that walked
the deep country as its own kind had walked the boundary. That the grass, the commonest
weakest thing in the forest, had had its own great ones, its own kings and queens, in a
time before the middle band had forgotten.*

*It had not believed the memory. It had thought the memory was old age.*

*And then it had lain down beside a patch of silver-veined grass that had kept the small
and the lost and driven off a hunter of several thousand years — and the grass had answered
its tap, and the old one had known, with the deep certainty of a thing that has outlived
everyone and therefore trusts its own memory above all else, that it was not old age.*

*The world had a new great one of the green. Small yet. Rooted. Impossible. And the old
one, the last keeper of a boundary, had found something new to keep.*

---

The winter that followed was the coldest in the old one's long memory, and it came to his
edge at the first snow and did not leave.

He felt it settle for the long sleep — felt the great body curl at the boundary of his
ground, its back to the wind, its warmth sinking into the soil toward his outermost roots.
He had felt it leave every winter before, to whatever den it kept in the deep band. This
winter it did not leave. This winter it curled at his edge, and its breathing slowed, and
its heartbeat slowed, and it slept — the long, deep, vulnerable sleep of an old thing that
has decided where it will spend the last of its winters.

He kept watch.

That was all he could do. He was a patch of grass; he could not build it a den or bring it
warmth or wake it if the cold went too deep. But he could do what grass did, and what the
talent had taught him to do better than any grass: he could endure. Through the long months
he stayed green when everything else lay flat and brown — stayed standing at the old one's
back, a small bright patch against the white, holding what warmth the winter sun gave and
letting it sink down toward the sleeping thing at his edge. He felt its heartbeat through
the frozen ground, slow as a drum heard from very far away, and he matched it — his whole
attention a pulse, sent and sent and sent through the cold soil: *I am here. I am here. I
am here.*

The frost tried to take him. The wind tried. The snow buried him and the thaw came and the
snow buried him again. He lost blades to the cold the way he had lost blades to every
winter of his life, and he regrew them in the thin pale light, smaller, harder, holding.
And through all of it the great heart at his edge went on, slow and deep, and he went on
with it.

The spring came late, and it came the way all his springs came — as a change in the ground
he felt before the world showed it. The frost let go. The stream woke. The glade began to
stir in the mud around him, and he felt the old one's breathing change — the slow deep
breaths drawing in, the great body shifting for the first time in months.

It woke on a morning of new green, and it woke to him — standing at its back, changed,
silver-veined, having held through a winter that had killed younger and stronger things.

It looked at him for a long time.

And then it lifted its foreleg, and brought it down on the thawing ground, and tapped —

*Thump. Thump. Thump.*

Three blows. Slow. And he understood, with the whole of him, what they meant, because he
had been waiting through the whole white silence of the winter to hear them.

*Here. You stayed. Here.*

He sent his pulse back, stronger than he had ever made it — the spring thaw running through
his roots, the whole kept life of a hundred and fifty winters pouring into one message:

*I am here. I will always be here.*

And the old one lay down beside him in the new green, and they rested together in the sun,
and the glade went on around them, not knowing that the last keeper of a boundary and the
impossible grass had spoken to each other across the whole of a winter and found each other
still there.

---

It was in the summer of that year that he felt the new weight.

It came at first as nothing — a disturbance at the far edge of his sensing, so faint he
thought he had imagined it. But it came again, and again, each time a little closer, a
little heavier: a presence circling the glade at night, patient, watchful, the way the
shadow cat had circled before it struck — only deeper. Only older. Only heavier by far.

He felt it, and he did not understand what it was, only that it was more than the shadow
cat, more than anything that had ever hunted his ground. He felt it the way he felt the old
one — as a weight that knew its own power, that feared nothing — except that this weight
did not lie down at his edge. This weight circled. This weight watched. This weight was
hungry, and it was patient, and it had smelled, across the whole long forest, the thing
that he was.

He sent his pulse to the old one in the dark: *here is danger.*

The old one did not tap back.

It rose. He felt it rise — the great body lifting from the ground, the slow gathering of
its weight, the particular stillness that came over it when it was no longer resting but
*standing*. It walked to the boundary of his ground, to the edge of the glade, and it stood
there in the dark, facing the circling presence.

He could not see what happened. He felt it — the two weights meeting at the edge of his
sensing, the old one's patient immensity and the circling hunger, standing against each
other in the dark like two trees deciding which would fall. He felt the circling presence
test the boundary — a step closer, a step closer — and he felt the old one answer, not with
speed, not with violence, but with the simple fact of itself, the way a mountain answers a
river: by being there, by being unmoved, by being more than the thing that had come to
test it.

The circling presence withdrew. Not far. It pulled back to the far edge of the glade, and
it waited there, watching — and he understood that it had not been defeated, only deterred,
only reminded that this ground was held.

The old one came back to him. It lay down at his edge, and it was breathing harder than he
had ever felt it breathe, and it was old — he felt it then, all at once, the vast tired age
of it, the weight of all its winters — and it tapped, slowly, a message he had to strain to
understand:

*The deep has smelled you. It will come again. I will hold the boundary while I can.*

And then, after a long pause, softer:

*But I will not always be here. Grow. Grow faster than the fear.*

He lay in the dark, feeling the old one's heart against his roots, feeling the circling
presence waiting at the far edge of the glade, feeling the whole vast deep forest pressing
in around the small bright ground he held. And he understood, in the way grass understands
— not a thought, but a turning of his whole being toward the next green — that he had come
to the edge of what he was.

For a hundred and fifty years he had been the impossible thing of the glade. He had kept
the small and the lost. He had grown teeth. He had been acknowledged by the last keeper of
a boundary. And now the deep forest itself had smelled him, and had sent its patient hunger
to circle his ground, and the only thing standing between him and it was an old one who
would not always be there.

He did not know how to grow faster than fear. He only knew that he would — that the thing
in him, the talent that had never once stopped, would find a way, because it had always
found a way, because that was the whole of what it was: not a gift, but a hunger of his own
— a hunger to become, to outgrow, to be more than the world said a grass could be.

The old one slept. The circling presence waited at the edge of the dark. And the silver
patch by the water, which had kept the small and the lost and won the acknowledgment of the
deep, turned its whole kept strength toward the next green — toward the long climb that lay
between it and the deep country, where the grass had had its great ones once, and where, if
the old one's oldest memory was true, it might have a place waiting for it.

He did not know that place yet. He did not know its name, or its queen, or the long road
that led to it.

But he had felt the direction of it — the way the old one turned its head, at dawn, toward
the deep of the forest, as if remembering something. And he had felt, for the first time in
his life, that the world was not only what it was. It was also what it had been, and what
it was becoming — and that he, the impossible grass of a quiet glade, was part of that
becoming.

The deep had smelled him. The boundary would hold while the old one stood. And he — rooted,
small, impossible, kept and keeping — would grow.

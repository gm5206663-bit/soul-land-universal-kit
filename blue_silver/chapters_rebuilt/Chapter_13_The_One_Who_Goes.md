---

The going was not a decision. It is worth being exact about that, because everything in
the second half of the book depends on what he actually was when he left the stream bend.

He did not choose to go. He could not have chosen anything; he had four words and a drum
of eleven and no self clear enough to hold a plan, and a plant does not have the equipment
for a plan. What he had, from the winter of the King gate onward, was a pull — a direction
in the ground, a shape in the root-memory that was not his and had not come from anything
in his own seven hundred years, and it pointed southwest and it did not stop pointing.

The old one had known the place.

That was how he knew it existed at all. Sixty years of taps, four words at a time, and the
keeper's knowledge had included the deep-country memory entire — the round, the three
places, the routes of the great things, the shape of four hundred miles of forest as an
animal that had walked it for an age understood it. And in that memory, at the far
southwestern edge of everything the old one had ever known, there was a valley, and the
valley had a pressure-signature in it that was not a beast's, and the signature was *the
same kind of thing as him*.

He had never once gone to look at it. He was grass. Grass does not go.

At ten thousand years the pull started, and it did not stop, and the low ground by the
stream went on being kept for another forty-nine years while the whole of him leaned
southwest in a direction he had no means of travelling.

Then the door emptied, and there was nothing left to keep.

---

They took the last of the small stock in the autumn of his seven hundred and sixth
year, and after that there was nothing in grid nineteen but a hundred feet of low ground
and a bank of deep blue grass and a herd that still came to drink and did not know the
difference.

Eighty voices had been stamping at the edge of the woven mass at dusk. By the winter there
were thirty. By the spring there were nine, and the nine stamped the eleven words into the
packed soil over two graves to a seam that went taut and let go, and the loosened layer
opened for them because the door was a door and a door does not shut, and there was nothing
left on the other side worth opening it for.

He had kept that boundary for three hundred and forty years. He had held it against the
deep band's leaning and a nine-man Hall expedition and the rot's refugees and a learned
liar, and he had made a people out of the small and lost of four grids, and the people had
been taken out of it in cages by men working a quota, and the men had not even known what
they were emptying.

The keeping had not failed. That is the honest reading and it took him a decade to arrive
at it, in the slow root-way he arrived at everything. The keeping had *finished*. A
boundary is kept for what it protects, and when what it protects is gone the boundary is
not defeated — it is done, and the only thing left in it is the keeper, standing at a door
with nothing on either side.

So, in the spring of his seven hundred and fourteenth year, the root-mass lifted.

---

It is the single hardest thing that ever happened in the serial and it is not dramatic at
all, which is exactly right.

A plant does not walk. That is the canon floor and he stood on it the whole way: he was
never, at any point in the twenty-one years of the going, unrooted. What he did was the
thing the deep band's diggers did, which he had spent two centuries learning to stop — he
went through the ground.

A hundred feet of low ground, lifted. Carried. Set down again, four hundred feet further
on, and re-rooted into silt he had never touched, and held there for a season while the
new root-mass wove itself into foreign soil, and then lifted again.

The first lifting took him a whole year.

That is not a figure of speech. It took a full season to work the root-mass free of the
stream bend — three hundred and forty years of layered growth, woven into silt laid down by
a flood when he was thirty years old, holding the ground under two kept places — and the
working-free was not a pulling, it was a *letting go*, root by root, and every root that
came up out of that soil was a root that had been laid down by the thing he had been, and
the whole of it had to be released before any of it could move.

He could not take the graves.

That is the cost of the chapter and it is the only cost that mattered. The forty feet of
densest silver-veined growth between the crown and the old one's place stayed where it was.
The packed soil over the found one stayed. The stream bend, the herd's drinking ground, the
shallows where the water had gone dark once in the ninety-eighth year of his life — all of
it stayed, because a plant cannot carry a place, and a place is where the dead are.

He left them, and he went southwest, and the going took twenty-one years.

---

The road was four hundred miles and he was hunted on it, and the hunting is the part the
world remembered, because the world only ever remembered the parts it could see.

They found him in the ninth year, in a low valley two hundred miles southwest of grid
nineteen, and they were not a contracted party. They were Hall — six men, a Soul Ancestor
leading, four rings in the column, working a commission that had come down from somewhere
high enough that nobody in it asked why a ten-thousand-year plant-type soul beast was worth
the expense.

Their leader was a Soul Ancestor named Ilen Marr, and on the second night, four hundred
feet from the root-mass, she said the thing out loud that the other five had been thinking
since the ridge.

"It's grass."

"It's the file, ma'am," the youngest said. "Eleven pages. Nine weeks on site. The man walked
the boundary forty-one times."

"The man had no rings." Marr turned the page over on her knee by the fire. "The man had no
rings, no combat assessment, no field authority, and he wrote eleven pages about a patch of
grass and then he died, and his nephew sold the file to a broker, and the broker sold it to
us, and somewhere along that chain it stopped being a record and started being a
commission." She held it up. "Plants do not cultivate. That is the first rule of the
registry and it has been the first rule for six hundred years."

"Then why are we two hundred miles out with a Soul Ancestor and a black-ring cage."

"Because," Marr said, "the walker in grid nineteen was dead for a hundred and fifty years
before anybody checked, and the grid was sealed the whole time, and when we finally opened
it there was nothing in it worth a net. Somebody has been wrong about that grid for four
centuries." She put the file down. "And I am not going to be the second."

On the third morning the forest stopped.

The reason it was worth the expense was the assessor. He had died eleven years before, an
old records man with no rings at all, and he had left his eleven pages in a personal file
rather than the registry, and the file had gone to a nephew, and the nephew had sold it,
and the buyer had done the arithmetic that no clerk in four hundred years had done: a
ten-thousand-year soul beast yields a black ring, and the registry said there was no
ten-thousand-year soul beast in grid nineteen, and the registry had said the same thing
about the walker for a century and a half.

They came for the black ring.

They did not get it, and the reason was not that he fought them, because he did not fight
them, and it was not that he killed them, because he did not kill them, and he would not
have killed them if he could have, and the whole of the remaining three hundred and seventy years of his
life would be shaped by the fact that he could have and did not.

The reason was the deep-country memory.

Four hundred miles of forest, and every ancient thing in it had been routing around grid
nineteen on purpose for sixty years, and the routing was a shape in the ground-memory that
meant *go around*, and the shape had followed him out of the middle band because the shape
was about *him* and not about the grid. The six men went into a low valley two hundred
miles from anywhere and found that the forest in it had gone quiet in a widening circle
that kept widening, and that the ground under them was wrong, and that whatever they had
come for was not alone in the way they had been told it was.

Marr stood up in the middle of the quiet and she did not sit back down.

"How far does it go."

"Ma'am —"

"How far does the quiet go. Walk it." The youngest walked it. He came back in an hour and
he was grey. "Well."

"It goes to the horizon, ma'am. In a circle. And it's still going." He sat down by the fire
without being told to. "There's nothing in it. There's no birds, there's no insects, there
isn't a single thing in four miles that makes a sound, and I stood in the middle of it and
listened and the only thing I could hear was the stream."

"Then it's a domain."

"A plant doesn't have a domain."

"No," Marr said. "It doesn't." She looked across four hundred feet of open ground at a
hundred feet of low blue-green grass standing in a low valley two hundred miles from
anywhere, and she looked at the black-ring cage on the cart, and she looked at the file on
her knee. "Pack the cage."

"Ma'am, we came four hundred miles—"

"We came four hundred miles for a black ring." She was already standing. "A black ring is a
ten-thousand-year beast. A ten-thousand-year beast does not stop a forest four miles wide
from making a sound. Whatever is in that grass, it is not what the file says it is, and I
do not have six men and I do not have the authority and I do not have the stomach." She
threw the file into the fire. "Pack the cage."

They left on the third day. Two of them did not leave at all, and what took them was not
him and not anything he did — it was the ordinary arithmetic of six men going into deep
country without a walker to route around, and the deep country being what it was.

He felt both of them go, in his ground, four hundred feet from where they had camped, and
he did nothing, and the four who walked out of that valley walked out because the ground
let them, and the letting was the same letting that had opened the loosened layer for a
small grey grazer in the winter of his four hundred and second year.

The door stayed a door. It stayed a door the whole way to the valley.

---

*They arrived at the southwestern edge of the old one's memory in the autumn of his seven
hundred and twenty-seventh year, and the memory ended there, because the old one had never gone
further, and beyond the memory there was only the pull.*

*It took eight more years.*

*The country changed coming into it. The deep band's great things thinned out and then
stopped altogether, which was not the way the deep band behaved — the deep band did not
stop, it went on being deep for a thousand miles in every direction, and the stopping was a
boundary, and the boundary was older than the old one's round, older than the glade, older
than the flood that had laid down the silt he had been born in.*

*Something had kept this country for a very long time before anything he knew had existed.*

*The soil changed under the root-mass in the last two years. It went from the ordinary
mineral poverty of the deep forest to something rich in a way that had no natural
explanation — rich the way the forty feet over the old one's grave had been rich, rich the
way soil gets when a great deal of cultivation has gone back into it over a great deal of
time, and the richness was not scattered. It was in lines. It was in a pattern.*

*He had no word for a pattern in the soil. He had eleven words and none of them were it.*

*But the pull had been pointing at this ground for seventy years, and the root-mass
came down into it in the spring of his seven hundred and thirty-fifth year and wove itself
into the richest soil he had ever touched, and held, and the holding felt different from
every other holding in eight centuries, because for the first time in his life the ground
under him was the same kind of thing he was.*

*He had three hundred and sixty years left, and he did not know it, and he was standing in a
valley in
the southwest of the largest forest on the continent with a hundred feet of low ground
around him and nine small voices still stamping eleven words into the packed soil at an
empty edge four hundred miles away.*

*And he was not the only thing in the valley.*

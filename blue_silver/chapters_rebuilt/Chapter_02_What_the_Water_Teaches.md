# Chapter Two — What the Water Teaches

```
◆ STATUS — Chapter 2: years 1–40
KIND      : Blue Silver Grass, common line. Still nothing by the world's measure.
AGE       : ~40 at the chapter's close
CLASS     : crosses the hundred-year mark in his fortieth year — the first visible
            mutation. Silver-veined, deep-blue variant. Nobody has seen it yet.
THREAT    : a second hunting party cuts him for cordage and carries him out of the glade
            in a coil. He survives it for a reason no one would care about.
```

---

The decades went the way decades go for a thing that cannot move.

He grew. The dark patch by the bank spread, slowly, the way grass spreads — a root here, a
runner there, a new blade coming up in ground that had been his the year before. By his
tenth summer the patch was the size of a man lying down. By his twentieth it was the size
of a hut. By his thirtieth the whole bank for forty feet either side of the stream's bend
was his, and the grass in it stood a hand taller than the meadow grass and had a sheen to
it in low light, blue-silver, like the underside of a fish.

Nothing noticed. That was the point of him, and it would go on being the point of him for a
long time: he was the commonest plant in the world, growing in the commonest place in the
world, and the entire forest looked straight through him.

The world taught him by hurting him.

The herd came first. They came down out of the meadow in the dry weeks, forty-odd heavy
bodies with mouths that did not choose, and they cropped the whole bank flat in an
afternoon — him included, every blade of him, sheared down to the crown. It was not
malice. It was not even attention. He was salad.

He survived it because his kind survived everything, and because the thing in him did what
it always did: it took the harm apart, and kept what was useful, and spent it. The blades
that came back after the herd came back tougher at the base, lower to the ground, growing
outward instead of up. The next season the herd cropped him again and got less for the
effort, and the season after that they cropped the meadow instead.

That was the first scar-lesson, and it would be the pattern of all of them: *this hurt. Do
not let it hurt this way again.*

Frost came every year and each year cost him less. Drought came twice in the first thirty
years and taught him to send root down past the shallow water to where the ground stayed
wet through August. A windstorm in his twenty-second year laid the whole meadow flat and
broke three of the bank's older trees, and taught him nothing at all, because grass does
not care about wind, and that was worth knowing too.

The water taught him the most.

---

The flood came in his thirty-first spring.

It came the way floods come in a forest — not as a wall, but as a slow rising over three
days of rain that would not stop, until the stream that had been a knee-deep bend in the
meadow was a brown muscular thing filling the whole low ground and taking the bank apart.

He felt it from inside, which is the only way anything has ever felt it.

The water got into his ground and *moved* it. Grains he had held between his roots for
thirty years went past him in an hour. The soil he was — because a plant is half soil, and
the half that is soil is the half that holds everything — came apart under him and went
downstream, and he went with it, root and crown, dragged sideways out of the earth he had
spent three decades building.

That was the closest he came to dying in his first forty years, and it was not a predator
or a fire or a blade that did it. It was water, doing what water does, entirely without
reference to him.

He survived because grass survives. He ended up in a splay of new silt two hundred feet
downstream, half-buried, three quarters of his root mass torn away, and he lay in the wet
dark for eleven days while the flood went down, and then he did the only thing he had ever
been able to do, which was grow.

What the water taught him, in those eleven days and the season after, was three things.

*Water takes.* Everything it touches it is moving, and what it moves it does not put back.

*Water puts down.* The silt that buried him was rich — a whole meadow's worth of leaf and
rot and dead insect, laid down in one night, worth more than thirty years of patient
feeding. He grew more in that first season on the new silt than he had in the six years
before it.

*And water does not care.* Not malice, not mercy. The herd had eaten him without noticing.
The flood had nearly killed him without noticing. In thirty-one years the entire world had
never once directed anything at him on purpose, and he was, without knowing it, being
prepared by this for the thing that would eventually matter most about him: that he would
spend his whole long life being the only thing in every room that was paying attention.

---

The mutation showed in his fortieth year.

He did not see it. He had no eyes and would not have them for seven centuries. But the
thing in him had been working on the substance of him since the dark under the frost, and
in that spring the work reached the surface and stayed.

His blades went from blue-silver to deep blue at the base, and the veins in them — the
thin water-carrying threads that run the length of any grass blade — went silver, plainly
silver, visible to anything that looked. His crown grew denser than his kind's crown had
any business growing. The patch by the stream stopped looking like Blue Silver Grass with a
good patch of ground and started looking like Blue Silver Grass that was *doing something*.

It was still grass. It was still the commonest, weakest, most trodden plant in the world,
with no soul power and no mind that any instrument in the era could have detected, and any
herbalist on the continent would have walked past it and thought nothing.

But it was the first time in his life that he looked different from his kind, and the world
has a way of eventually noticing things that look different.

He had crossed the hundred-year mark. In the world's arithmetic he was now worth a white
ring — ten years of a rabbit, as the broad hunter had said, the least valuable thing a
spirit master could put in his body. He was, for the first time, technically killable for
profit.

Nobody knew. It would be a hundred and forty years before anybody knew.

---

The second party came through the glade in the late summer of his fortieth year.

They were not hunters. That was the first thing that was different about them. There were
five of them and they moved badly — too close together, too loud, the front man hacking at
undergrowth with a blade he clearly did not know how to hold — and they had the smell of
towns on them, and one of them was complaining constantly about the mud.

"Guide said two days," the complainer said.

"Guide lied."

"Guide said two days and took four silver to say it."

They were going somewhere and were lost, and they were the kind of men who had money and no
skill, which is the kind of men who die in Star Dou Forest, and the grass by the water had
no opinion about any of it.

They camped on his bank.

That was the whole event. They came down to the stream at dusk because water meant a place
to sleep, and they trampled forty years of his growth flat in the making of a fire ring,
and the one with the bad blade looked around for something to tie the pack-frames together
with.

Grass is cordage. Everybody knows that. You twist it, you braid it, it holds well enough
for a night.

He took a hand's breadth of the deep blue blades at the base — the oldest, densest growth
in the whole patch, the part of him that had been there since the flood — and he cut it,
and he did not cut it well, and he hacked at it twice more when it did not come free.

The pain was not like the herd's.

The herd had cropped the tips, the way a grazer crops, taking the blade and leaving the
crown. This was the crown. This was the part of him that had been alive since the silt,
cut at the base, and lifted, and *carried away*.

He went up out of his ground in a coil.

It is not possible to describe what that was like to a thing with no nervous system and no
words, and it would be a lie to try. What can be said is that the record in him — the thing
that had been keeping itself since the frost — took the whole of it in, in pieces, the way
it took everything: the sudden absence of the ground, the wrong angle of the light, the
smoke, the hands, the sound of five men talking about a thing that was not him at all
while holding the oldest part of his body in one of them.

"Useless stuff," the man said, twisting him. "Tough, though. Won't break."

"Everything's tough out here."

"Not the grass. Grass should snap. This is like rope already." He pulled, testing it. "Feel
that. That's not normal grass."

"It's grass."

"It's *tough* grass." He shrugged and finished the knot. "Somebody's been feeding it."

They used him to lash two pack-frames and a rolled blanket, and they slept on the rest of
him, and in the morning they went on downstream, and they left the coil tied to the frame
because it had held, and it went with them out of the glade and down the water and away,
and he lost it.

He never got it back. It was forty years of himself, gone in an afternoon, and the men who
took it did not know they had taken anything, and if you had told them they would have
laughed, because it was grass.

---

Two things happened next.

The first was that he grew it back.

That is not a small thing and it should not be passed over the way his kind's resilience
usually gets passed over. A plant cut at the crown does not regrow in a season. It takes
years, and it takes the whole substance of the plant, and most plants that lose the crown
simply die and are replaced by their neighbours. He was down to bare silt and a few
surviving runners on the bank, and by the end of the second summer the patch was back to
half its old size, and by the end of the fifth it was back to full, and the new growth came
in denser and lower and with a thicker base than before, because the thing in him had taken
the cutting apart the way it took everything apart and had kept the useful part.

*Cut at the base. Do not let the base be where it can be cut.*

The second thing was the file.

---

*Four hundred years later, in a stone building in a city neither of them would ever see, a
clerk of the Spirit Hall's forest registry was copying forward an old entry from a ledger
that had already been copied forward nine times, and he came to a line in the Star Dou
middle-band section that made him stop and squint.*

*The entry was four centuries old and it was nearly illegible and it said, in the compressed
shorthand of a patrol report, that a survey party had noted unusual vegetation at a stream
bend in grid nineteen — unusually vigorous, unusually tough, blue-silver coloration, worth
a look if anyone is ever out that way.*

*Worth a look. Not worth a party. Not worth a name. The clerk copied it forward the way he
had copied forward four thousand other lines, and he did not think about it, and no one who
read the ledger after him thought about it either, because it was a note about grass.*

*It stayed in the ledger for another four hundred years.*

*And then, one autumn, a man with a real reason to look went through the middle-band
section line by line, hunting for anything plant-type that had ever been described as
vigorous, and he found it, and he put his finger on it, and he sat there for a long time.*

*But that is a long way off. In the glade, in the fifth summer after the cutting, the patch
by the stream was back to full and denser than it had ever been, and the heron was standing
in the shallows, and nothing in the world knew that it existed.*

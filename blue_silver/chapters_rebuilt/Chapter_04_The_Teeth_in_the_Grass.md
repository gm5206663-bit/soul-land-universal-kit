# Chapter Four — The Teeth in the Grass

```
◆ STATUS — Chapter 4: years 105–190
KIND      : Blue Silver Grass, common line — the deep-blue silver-veined variant, now the
            whole bank and half the meadow. Dense enough that small things live in it.
AGE       : ~190 at the chapter's close
CLASS     : thousand-year class in carried cultivation. Plant self-protection — the canon
            floor that says a plant cannot meaningfully defend itself before this — has
            only just begun to open. He can do something. It is not much.
THREAT    : a several-thousand-year shadow cat, driven into the glade by a hunting party
            that wants its ring. It hides in his grass and eats what lives there.
```

---

The solitude after her lasted forty years, and he did not mind it, because he had never had
company before and did not know that the absence of it was a thing a person could mind.

What he did instead was consolidate.

That is not a word he would have used. It is the word for what the thing in him did with
four quiet decades: it took everything he had ever been and folded it tighter. The
root-memory that had been a loose scatter of impressions became a single continuous record
of the glade — the flood's high water, the year the frost came early, the season the herd
stopped cropping him, the shape of every root of the fallen tree on the far bank. The
ground-listening that had been a vague sense of pressure became fluent. He could tell a
vole from a weasel by the weight of its step at twenty feet. He could tell rain coming by
the way the shallow water pulled.

And somewhere in those forty years the narration of his own life stops having to apologize
for calling him *he*.

He was not a mind in a body. He was a body that had become a mind — assembled out of
root-memory and scar and appetite and one small grief, sitting in a bend of a stream in
the middle band of the largest forest on the continent, with no eyes, no words, no name,
and no idea that any of this was unusual.

Small things began to live in him.

That happened without his deciding it. His grass was the densest cover for a hundred feet
in any direction, and dense cover is what a small thing wants, and by his hundred and
twentieth year the whole bank was a warren of voles and field mice and one indignant family
of shrews, and they came and went and bred and died in his crown and he felt every one of
them the way a house feels its mice.

He did not protect them. That came later and for different reasons. In those years they
were simply there — warmth moving through him, small hearts going very fast, the whole
bank alive in a way it had never been.

It was the first time his ground had been anybody's home but his.

---

The cat came in the spring of his hundred and sixty-third year, and the reason it came was
four men with dogs.

He felt the men first, at the edge of his hearing, three ridges over — a hard fast pressure
moving through the forest in a line, and with it a second pressure, larger, going the other
way and going quickly. The dogs' barking came down the valley a long time before any of it
reached the glade.

Then the cat came over the bank in one flowing movement and went to ground in his grass.

It was bigger than a man lying down and it moved like poured water, black-grey, with the
flat wide skull and the long tail of the deep forest's ambush hunters, and it was not
running anymore. It had decided. It flattened itself into the densest part of him — the
deep blue bank grass, shoulder-high, the exact texture and colour of shadow at the base of
a stem — and it stopped being visible, and it waited.

The grass by the water had never had anything that large in it.

The pressure of it was enormous. Not weight — it was lighter than the herd by half — but
*density*, the same density he had felt in the broad hunter forty years before and a
hundred times more of it, a cultivation so far above his own that it registered in his
roots the way a bonfire registers on a hand held near it. Several thousand years of a thing
being alive and careful, in his ground, breathing.

And it was frightened.

That was the part he kept longest. The most dangerous thing that had ever entered his ground
was terrified, and it was terrified of *men*, and it had come into a patch of grass to hide
because a patch of grass was the only place in the forest where nothing would think to look.

The men went past on the ridge. The dogs went past. The whole thing took perhaps an hour,
and the cat did not move once, and then the pressure of the men went away down the valley
and the cat stayed where it was for three more days.

And on the fourth night it ate the shrews.

---

It was not malice and it is important not to make it malice, because the cat was not a
villain and the serial has enough of those.

It was a several-thousand-year ambush predator that had been run for two days by men with
dogs, that had burned more energy in that run than it could replace in a week, and that was
sitting in a patch of grass that was, from where it lay, a larder. Voles. Mice. Shrews.
Warm, slow, everywhere, and too small for a normal predator to bother with but exactly the
right size for something that could not afford to be choosy.

It ate for eleven days.

It ate the shrew family first, in one night, and then it worked outward through the bank
methodically the way a heron works a shallow — a slow reach, a small warm thing, a swallow,
and then the long patient stillness. It did not overhunt. It was too good at its work to
overhunt. It took perhaps three of the small things a night out of a bank that held sixty,
and it would have gone on doing exactly that until the bank was empty, and it would never
once have thought about the grass it was standing in.

The grass felt all of it.

Every one. The warm bodies had lived in him for forty years — had been born in his crown,
had run their tunnels between his roots, had died of age in his ground and rotted back into
him. He had not protected them, because there had been nothing to protect them from, and
because for forty years it had not occurred to him that protection was a thing one could do.

He could not have said why it mattered. He had no words for it. What he had was the mantis
— the lightness, the weight, the lightness again — sitting in the deep place where a plant
puts what it cannot use, and the arithmetic that had gone down under it in his ninety-eighth
year: *everything that comes here ends, and I do not.*

The cat was making that arithmetic happen on schedule, in his ground, out of his small
warm tenants.

So, in the twelfth night, when it reached for a vole three feet from his crown, he took it.

---

What he could do was very little, and it is worth being precise about how little, because
the forest's whole opinion of his kind depended on it.

A plant at the thousand-year class cannot fight. That is the canon floor and he was standing
on the very edge of it — a floor that says ordinary plant life has no means of defending
itself at all until a thousand years of cultivation, and he had crossed it, which was
already absurd, and crossing it did not make him a fighter. It made him a plant that could
do something.

He could move his blades. Not walk, not strike, not anything a beast would have called an
attack — but the long deep-blue blades of the bank could tighten, and hold, and pull,
because that was what his kind's whole body was built to do: bind, entangle, snare, hold.
It was the native character of Blue Silver Grass, the one thing the line was actually good
at, and he had a thousand years of substance behind it and a bank of it and a cat lying in
the middle.

He took the foreleg.

Not the throat, not the body — he could not have reached either and he could not have hurt
either if he had. He took the left foreleg, where it lay extended in the grass, and he
wrapped the whole crown of the bank around it in the dark, and he held.

The cat had killed things ten times his size. It had been hunted by men with rings for two
days and had not been caught. It had no framework whatsoever for what happened next,
because what happened next was the ground taking it, and the ground does not do that.

It went up in a single convulsion and the bank went with it — every blade of him pulled
taut, roots lifting out of the silt, forty feet of grass standing up off the ground like a
net with something in it — and it tore, and he tore too, and the tearing went on for the
length of three breaths.

Then it was gone, over the bank and into the meadow, and it did not go the way a cat goes.
It went on three legs, with the fourth held up and dragging, and it did not come back into
the glade, and nothing in the forest saw what had been done to it, and nothing in the
forest ever learned that grass had done it.

He was missing a third of the bank. The crown where he had held was shredded to the base,
and the roots under it had come up out of the silt and did not go back down, and it took
him eleven years to grow the whole of it again.

The small things came back faster than that. They always do.

---

*The hunting party found the cat nine days later, four ridges east, in a hollow under a
scree slope where it had gone to lick a wound it could not reach.*

*They were not the same men who had run it into the glade. They were better.*

*Their leader was a woman in her fifties with a Spirit Hall issue coat and three rings at
her wrist — purple, purple, purple — and she looked at the leg for a long time before she
said anything.*

*"Something took this," she said.*

*"It's torn," one of the others said.*

"It's *bound*." She put her finger along the fur above the wrist. "Look at the pattern.
That's not a bite. A bite closes. Something wrapped this and pulled, and the pulling is
still in the shape of it." She stood up. "And there's no tooth mark anywhere on the leg."*

*"Snake, then."*

*"A snake doesn't take a leg and leave the cat alive." She looked back down the ridge,
toward the valley they had come up out of, and she did not look interested. She looked
mildly annoyed, the way a competent person looks when the world hands them something that
does not fit.*

*"What do we do."*

*"Take the ring. It's still worth three thousand years with a bad leg." She sheathed her
blade. "And I'm writing it up. Not the ring — the leg."*

*"Writing up what."*

*"That something in grid nineteen binds," she said. "And that it isn't a snake, and it
isn't a cat, and it isn't a bear, and I've been working this forest for thirty years and I
can't think of a single thing it is."*

*She wrote it up. It went into the same ledger, in the same middle-band section, four lines
below a hundred-and-twenty-year-old note about unusually vigorous grass at a stream bend in grid
nineteen, and the clerk who copied it forward that winter did not connect the two entries,
because they were a hundred and twenty years apart and one was about vegetation and one was about a
mauled cat, and no reasonable person would connect them.*

*Somebody did, eventually. It took another two hundred years.*

# Chapter Seven — The One Who Keeps

```
◆ STATUS — Chapter 7: years 300–360
KIND      : Blue Silver Grass, common line. The low ground is one woven mass for a hundred
            feet either side of the stream, and the seam is holding because of it.
AGE       : ~360 at the chapter's close
CLASS     : upper thousand-year class. The well is deep and quiet and held.
THREAT    : an organised expedition, Hall-contracted, working the grid on purpose. Turned
            at the boundary — not fought off. Turned.
BOND      : the night-walker. The former keeper of the seam, come back to look at its old
            post. First two-way speech of his life.
```

---

It came down off the ridge in the sixtieth year after the serpent night, and he knew before
it arrived that it was different, because the forest went quiet in front of it.

Not the way the glade went quiet around him — that was avoidance, the wordless making-room
of small things. This was deference. Every pressure in the middle depth went still and
stayed still in a widening circle that crossed the valley and kept going, and the circle
moved toward him at the pace of something very old taking its time.

It was enormous and it was thin, and it walked on four legs with a shoulder that dropped at
every third step, and it had the long low head and the heavy forequarters of a walker that
had walked a great deal, and it was so old that the cultivation coming off it did not
register in his roots as pressure at all. It registered as *weather*.

It came down to the stream. It drank. And then it walked the length of his ground, slowly,
from the far bank to the meadow and back, and it stopped at every place where the root-mass
was thickest, and it stood there for a while, and it went on.

It was reading him.

He had been read before — the surveyor had stood in him for an afternoon with nine blades
on a cloth and an instrument case. This was not that. This was something that knew exactly
what it was looking at and was taking its time about it, the way an old craftsman looks at
a piece of work by somebody else.

It finished at the crown of the far bank, where the serpent had torn the hole, where the
new growth had come in layered and hard. It stood there for a long time.

Then it lay down at his edge, and it put its great head on the ground, and it tapped.

---

Two blows. Deliberate. Spaced.

Not a hoof shifting. Not the ground settling. Two blows put into the earth on purpose by
something that meant them, and the earth carried them the way the earth carries everything,
and they came up through his roots as *shape* rather than sound.

He did not answer. He did not know that answering was a thing one could do. He had no words
and no self and no framework in which a tap in the soil was a question rather than a tap in
the soil.

It tapped again. Two blows. The same two blows. The same spacing.

And then it waited.

That was what did it. The waiting. Because waiting in a particular way, after a particular
thing, is the oldest grammar there is, and his roots had been reading the ground for three
hundred years and knew the difference between a thing that happened and a thing that was
put there.

He answered. One pulse. The only thing he had. The root-pulse he had used to tighten the
bank against the deep band's leaning for sixty years — not a signal, not a word, just the
whole hundred feet of him going taut at once and letting go.

The great head on the ground went still.

And then it tapped three times, and he pulsed once, and it tapped two, and he pulsed once,
and it went on like that for the length of an afternoon, and by dusk there was, between an
ancient last-of-its-kind walker and a bank of grass, a vocabulary of four things:

*here. yes. more. stop.*

It was the first conversation he had ever been in. It had four words in it. It would be
another two hundred years before the vocabulary passed thirty, and the whole of it would
always be taps and pulses, and it would be the only true speech he had until he was a King,
and it would matter more than anything else in his life except one thing.

---

The old one had kept this ground.

That was what it took him a decade to understand, and he understood it the way he
understood everything — through the soil, slowly, from the inside.

It walked a round. Every night for the first three years it walked the same round: the far
bank, the low ground, the meadow's edge, the ridge path, and back — and the round was the
boundary. Not a line on a map. A *route*, worn into the ground by an age of walking, and
the ground remembered it, and the ground was him, and he could feel the old path in his own
root-mass the way a man feels an old injury when the weather turns.

Something had walked this seam for a very long time before him. And it had stopped, and the
boundary had gone unkept, and the deep band had leaned on it for sixty years while a patch
of grass held it without knowing what it was holding — and now the thing that used to hold
it had come back to look.

It tapped him the keeper's knowledge over the following decades, four words at a time, and
what it could not tap it showed him by walking.

*Where the deep comes through.* Three places. The soft ground under the fallen tree. The
old watercourse at the low end, where a digger could get purchase. The ridge path, which
was not a hole but a habit, and habits are how the deep moves.

*What the deep wants.* Not him. Never him. The deep did not care about a bank of grass any
more than the herd had cared. The deep wanted the low ground — the water, the cover, the
soft silt, the small warm things that lived in his crown. He was not the target. He was the
door.

*What a keeper does.* Not fight. He tapped this one over and over for a whole season, two
blows and a pause and one long drag, until he understood that the old one was correcting
him: *you do not drive it out. You make it go somewhere else.* A boundary is not a wall. A
wall gets tested until it breaks. A boundary is a place where the going-through is more
trouble than the going-around, and the making of that is the whole of the office.

He had already been doing it. That was the strange part. Sixty years of holding against the
lean, and he had been doing the office without knowing there was an office, and the old one
had walked four hundred miles of ridge to look at a patch of grass that had, entirely by
accident, been keeping its post.

---

The expedition came in his three hundred and fortieth year, and it was the first time
anything had come at the seam on purpose.

There were nine of them. Hall-contracted — the same flat efficient manner as the thin man's
party, but better equipped, and with a Hall assessor walking in the middle of the column
carrying an instrument case, and with the thin man himself at the back, older, thinner,
holding a rolled copy of a ledger page.

They did not come hunting. They came *surveying*, which was worse, because a hunting party
wants one thing and leaves, and a survey party wants to know.

They came down off the meadow and the assessor stopped at the edge of the low ground and
put his palm flat on the crown and stood there with his eyes shut for the length of an
hour.

Then the old one walked into the middle of them.

It did not charge. It did not roar. It came down off the ridge at its own pace, with its
dropped shoulder and its great head low, and it walked into the expedition the way a man
walks into a room he owns, and it stood between the assessor and the bank and looked at
them.

The thin man went white.

He was the only one who knew what he was looking at, and what he was looking at was the
reason the ledger page in his hand had been marked unresolved for a hundred and forty-seven
years and the
reason his cousin's shop had sold the letter to a buyer who had sold it to the Hall, and he
said one word very quietly, and the word was not a word the others knew.

"Back," he said. "Everybody back. Slowly. Now."

"What is it."

"Back."

They backed. Nine armed men with a Hall assessor and two black rings in the column went
backwards off the low ground at a walking pace without once turning around, and the old one
stood in the middle of his grass and watched them do it, and it did not follow, and when
they were up on the meadow it lay down again at the edge of the bank and put its head on
the ground.

The expedition did not come back. The grid was reclassified that winter — sealed-pending,
a designation that meant the Hall had decided the information was worth more than the
territory — and the note went into the ledger under a new heading, and the heading was not
about grass.

It was about the thing that had kept the grass.

---

*That was the mistake, and it was a reasonable one, and it would take the Hall another two
hundred years to undo it.*

*They filed him under the walker. Ancient, last of its kind, tens of thousands of years,
territorial, non-aggressive unless approached — a known quantity, a mapped quantity, the
kind of thing the registry was built to hold. The grass stayed in the vegetation column
where it had always been, three hundred years old and unresolved and uninteresting, and the
whole weight of the Hall's attention went onto the great thin thing that walked the ridge
at night, because that was the thing you could see.*

*Which meant that for the next two centuries, every party that came into grid nineteen came
to look at the keeper.*

*And none of them came to look at the keeping.*

*In the glade, at the end of the three hundred and fortieth year, an ancient walker lay at
the edge of a bank of deep blue grass with its head on the ground, and the grass held a
hundred feet of low ground against the largest forest on the continent, and between them
there was a vocabulary of four words and a route that had been walked for an age, and the
old one tapped two blows into the earth because that was how it said goodnight.*

*The grass pulsed once.*

*Here.*

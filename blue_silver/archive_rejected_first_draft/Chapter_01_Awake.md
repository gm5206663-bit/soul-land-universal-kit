# Chapter One — Awake

*Deep Star Dou Forest, middle depth. A glade on the bend of a slow stream. Eight hundred
years before the world will hear the name of a boy with twin souls, something impossible
cracks the soil — and the reason it is impossible is already inside it, working, though no
one in the world has a name for that yet.*

Below the frost line, in the dark, a seed was doing what seeds do. It waited. It did not
know it was waiting, or that it was a seed, or that there was a word for the cold ground
that held it. It only knew — if knowing is the right word for a thing with no mind yet,
only a deep animal certainty — that the dark was not empty. Water moved past it, thread by
thread, between the grains of soil, and it was drinking, and drinking was good.

And something else, stranger, which it had nothing in its short existence to compare it to:
even in the dark, before it had put out a single root, it was aware that it was *there*.

The ground was full of seeds. The soil around this one held others of its kind, dozens of
them, sleeping the sleep that had no waking in it — patient, mindless, waiting for the same
thaw and the same permission, to become exactly what their parents had been. None of them
felt the water go by. None of them knew the dark was dark. This one felt. This one knew.

There was no one in the soil to tell it this was wrong, and so it did not know it was
wrong. It only knew. And it knew one thing more, the deepest thing, the thing that made it
different and would go on making it different for as long as it lived: *something had
already been done to it*. Before the thaw. Before it had grown so much as a root. In the
long dark of its first waiting, something had reached into the small sleeping grain of it
and changed the shape of what it was — had made it more than a seed was meant to be. More
awake. More aware. Hungrier, in a way that had nothing to do with water or warmth. Its
kindred slept the perfect sleep of their kind, and it did not sleep; it waited, and in the
waiting it *learned* — learned the water's slow paths around the grains, learned the
cold's weight and how it shifted, learned the shape of the dark it lay in — and no seed
that had ever lain in that soil had learned a single thing in all the ages of the world.

There was no name for what had been done to him. Not yet. It would be a long time before
any voice on the continent had a word for it, and longer still before anyone understood
what it meant. But it was in him, part of the grain of him, growing as he grew. It was the
reason he was awake at all. It would be the reason for everything that ever happened to
him, down to the last root of his last day — though if some passing god had leaned down
then and told him so, he could not have understood a word of it. He was a seed. Seeds did
not understand. Only the thing in him understood, the way a river understands its own
course, without thought, without choice, by being what it is.

The winter pressed down on the whole forest. Up above, the world was locked and white, but
down where the seed waited, the cold was a slow thing, a heaviness that made the water
threads crawl more slowly past the grain of him, and he — and here is the first strange
word, *he*, for a thing that had no limbs and no face and would not have understood a word
if one had been spoken into the dark — he held still with the rest of the earth and waited,
the way the earth waits, because there was nothing else to do and because below the frost
the living parts of the world keep exactly this kind of patience.

But even in the waiting, the thing in him was not idle. It was never idle. The other seeds
around him waited in perfect stillness, giving nothing to the cold and taking nothing from
it. He waited differently. He took the cold in and measured it, the way his root would one
day measure soil — *this is what cold is, this is how it presses, this is how long it
lasts* — and stored it, though he had no place yet to store anything, no root, no leaf, no
memory but the one the thing in him was already making of every moment it was given. He
was, without knowing it, the first seed in the history of the glade to live through his
own winter twice: once in the world, and once in the growing record of himself.

Then the season turned.

It came down to him as a change in the water. The threads that had crawled slow and cold
began to move faster, warmer, fuller — the whole dark world loosening at once, releasing,
as if the earth had been holding its breath for a long time and had decided at last to let
it go. Somewhere far above, snow was melting into the ground, and the melt was finding him,
and the melt was saying *now*.

The grain swelled. It split, along a seam it had always had and never used, and put out the
one thing it had been built to make: a thread of white root, tender as anything alive,
turning downward because down was where the promise of water lay. It drank properly then,
for the first time, with a mouth it had just grown. The drinking filled him. The drinking
told him the shape of the ground around him — here a stone, slick and cold; here a channel
of softness where an old root had rotted away and left the soil rich and easy; here the
long, slow downward pull of the world itself, which his root followed without being told,
because following it was what he was.

But not only what he was. Not anymore. When his root reached the old rotted channel, it did
what every root would do — drank, and fed. And then it did what no root had ever done: it
*paused*, tasting the richness, feeling the way the soft soil ran deeper than the drinking
needed, and it remembered the taste. Not as a thing remembers — he had no mind to remember
with — but as a lean, a preference, a small turn in the growing of him that would send his
root back toward that channel for the rest of his life whenever the water thinned. The
other seeds' roots, when they finally woke, would drink wherever they found themselves and
know nothing of it. His root was already becoming a record. Already the thing in him was
reaching ahead of his own small life, shaping him toward what had fed him, away from what
had starved him, before he had words or will or any way to refuse.

And when the root was long enough, and the dark had given him all it was going to give, the
seed did the only other thing it knew how to do. It pushed up.

This was the first act of his life that was even close to a choice.

Pushing up was harder than growing down had been. Down had been easy — the world had
helped, the way water helps a thing that is trying to fall. Up was the whole weight of the
earth pressing back, grain against grain, and he was small, and the soil above him was
packed and cold. He pushed anyway, because there was nothing else that a thing that had
grown a root and felt the thaw could do. He pushed for a long time. He pushed past stones,
around them, through seams of old rot that gave way like the soft places between a great
beast's ribs. He did not know what he was pushing toward. He knew only that the dark
thinned as he rose, that the water threads grew warmer and more numerous, and that
something at the top of the world was calling him the way the thaw had called him, and he
was answering.

And when he met the stone — the first stone, the one that lay square across his path with
the packed earth above it — he did not stop, the way the kindred seeds' roots would have
stopped, turning aside at the first hardness to take the easy way around. He stopped. He
pressed. He felt the stone with the whole tip of his growth, the way the thing in him felt
everything — *hard, here, unyielding, this way runs softer, this way is shorter* — and he
chose the harder line, because the harder line was the shorter line, and the shorter line
was the sooner light. No root in the glade had ever chosen. It was not a thought. It was
the thing in him leaning the way a blade leans toward the sun: without knowing it was
leaning, making him more than a root was meant to be, one small choice at a time, before he
could even be said to have a self to choose with.

Then the soil broke open above him, and the world was light.

It hit him like a blow. Not a seeing blow — he had nothing to see with, and would not for a
very long time — but a warmth that poured over the first small leaf he had ever made, and
into the second, and down the whole green length of him, and he had not known, until that
moment, that he had been cold all his life. The light moved across his leaf-skin in slow
tides through the day, stronger from one side than the other — the south, though he had no
word for it, only the knowledge written in his cells that warmth came from there and that
he should lean that way — and when it left, at the end of the day, the cold came back, and
he learned his first rhythm: the world was warm and then it was not, warm and not, warm
and not, and he drank the light while it was there and waited through the dark of it,
because that was what a leaf was for.

He was not alone in the light. He had known, even in the dark of the ground, that he was
not alone there — not by feeling them, for there was nothing in them to feel, but by the
crowding: seeds all around him in the soil, roots brushing his root, the tight press of
other bodies wanting the same water. And now that he had broken through he understood what
the crowding had meant. They were everywhere. On every side of him they rose, thousands
upon thousands of them, small pale blue-green blades packing the damp earth to the very
edge of what his senses could reach, and beyond that edge more still, and beyond that more
— a carpet of his own kind stretching away until the ground itself seemed to be made of
them.

None of them knew he was there. None of them could. He reached toward them with everything
he had, in the only way he knew how to reach — a seeking, a listening, a wanting — and
there was nothing on the other side of that reaching but silence. Not the silence of sleep.
The silence of absence. They were the same shape as him and had nothing of him in them at
all: no waiting, no knowing, no self to know with. They were green the way water is wet,
by being what they were and nothing more.

That was the strangest thing of all, and he had no name for how strange it was, because it
was too large and too quiet for him to hold. He was one among millions, and the millions
were all around him, and every one of them was exactly like him in shape and exactly unlike
him in the one way that mattered, and he could not feel any of them, and none of them
could feel him, and he would never have known the difference if there had been anything in
his small experience to compare it to. He was in the middle of the largest family in the
world, and he was alone in it, and he did not know the word for that either, so the
loneliness simply sat in him the way the cold sat in the ground in winter: everywhere, and
nothing to be done about it.

Above him and around him the glade went about its business. He learned it slowly, in the
only language he had, which was the language of his whole body. There was a great dark mass
on the north side of the little valley, a fallen giant of a tree, half-sunk in moss, that
blocked the light for the ground behind it and held the cold there even in summer — he
could feel the difference in the soil he shared with its roots, the way the earth on that
side stayed damp and old and full of the slow rot of wood, and he learned to want nothing
from that side. There was a boulder not far off, warm in the middle of the day, and the
grass around it grew rank and dark with the heat it threw back, and small things came and
went across its top. There was the stream — he knew it first as a voice in the ground, a
deep continuous tremor that ran through the wet soil under him day and night, and then,
when the wind was right, as a smell of moving water on the air, and he drank from its
ghost without ever knowing it was the stream he was drinking from. There were the grazers,
whose shadows crossed him and whose hooves he felt as rhythms in the earth, and the birds,
whose weight was brief and light and gone. And there was the carpet, the endless quiet
carpet of his own kind, bending in the wind in waves that he felt pass through the ground
under him as a single great slow breath, all of them moving together and none of them
knowing they moved.

Days passed. The warm and the not-warm. Rain fell on him, and each drop was a knock that
ran down the length of his leaf to his root and into the ground, and the ground drank, and
he drank, and he grew. He grew the way a blade of Blue Silver Grass grows — which is to
say, hardly at all, the way the world's most common weed has always grown: steady, humble,
unhurried, asking only for light and water and a little room, and asking for them again
tomorrow. To any eye that might have passed over the glade — and none did, in those early
days — he was nothing. A single ordinary blade among ten thousand ordinary blades, pale
blue-green, no taller than his neighbors, no different in any way that could be seen.

That was true, and it was also not true.

Because the others, when the light left them and the night came down, simply *stopped*. The
carpet slept the sleep of plants, mindless and complete, a whole green nation closing its
eyes at once. And he — small, common, indistinguishable — lay awake in the dark of his own
small body, feeling the night move over him, feeling the cold creep up from the ground,
feeling the stream's tremor and the far-off weight of things breathing and moving in the
forest beyond the glade. It did not occur to him to sleep, because he had never seen anyone
sleep, and because the thing in him that had made him more than a seed was not done with him
— it was never done with him; it was the one thing about him that never rested — and so he
stayed awake, gathering the night into himself the way his root gathered water, storing
the shape of it against a need he could not imagine and the thing in him could not stop
preparing for. While ten thousand blades slept, one blade watched, and learned, and did not
know it was the only blade in the world that had ever done so.

The grazers came for the first time at the end of a long warm stretch, when the meadow
grass had grown tall and sweet and the whole glade lay heavy and green under the noon.

He felt them long before they arrived. It started as a rhythm in the ground, far off and
faint, coming closer — a repeated soft percussion passing through the soil and up his root,
*tap tap tap tap*, many times over, irregular and alive. The rhythm grew. The earth began
to carry a whole music of it, hooves and hooves and more hooves, and with the rhythm came a
trembling, and with the trembling came the shadow.

He did not see the shadow. He felt it: the light, which had been pouring over him all
morning from the south, suddenly thinned, darkened, failed — a great slow tide of darkness
moving across the glade from the tree line, eating the warmth. The birds that had been
calling went silent. The ground shook. And the rhythm became a thunder all around him, and
the thunder was the world's weight arriving.

They were upon the carpet. He understood what they were only by what they did, and what
they did was eat. All around him, the blades of his kind were being taken — he felt them go
in a cascade of small bright severances, leaf after leaf after leaf, the grass of the glade
coming up by the mouthful in great soft tearing sounds, and the carpet that had been the
whole world to him was vanishing. He felt the one next to him go. He felt the one beyond it
go. He felt the wave of taking sweep toward him through the ground, through the pressure of
the others' ending, and he had no legs and no wings and no way at all to do what every
cell of him suddenly wanted to do, which was to run.

A shadow bent over him. Warm breath, sweet and huge, smelling of cut grass and the inside
of a living mouth. Something closed on him — not sharp, not cruel, simply *taking*, the way
the sun takes water from a leaf, the way a thing that is food is taken — and there was a
bright, tearing instant that was pain such as he had never felt, that he had no word for
and no way to understand, and then the top of him was gone.

It happened in less than a breath. One moment he had been a blade among blades in the light,
a whole small self standing up to the sun; the next he was a stump, a broken thing, a
ragged edge of green left standing at the height of a thumbnail, and the great soft mouths
moved on, cropping the next mouthful and the next, and the glade grew quiet again by
degrees as the herd passed, and the light came back.

He lay in the dark of what was left of himself and did not know how to be what he was now.
The part of him that had met the sun was gone. The part of him that had felt the rain was
gone. He was smaller than he had ever been, and everything he had been was bitten off above
him, and the pain where the bite had been went on and on, a hot ringing that he could not
make stop.

But below the broken edge, in the dark, the root crown held. It had always held. It was the
part of him that had pushed up through the packed soil in the spring, the part that had
waited out the winter in the ground before he had ever seen light, and it had not been
eaten, because the mouths of the world cannot reach everything. The mouth had taken what
was above the soil and left what was below, and what was below was him, the oldest him, the
part that remembered the dark.

And it was there, in the root crown, in the dark, that the thing in him did its first great
work.

He had no way to know it was happening. It came to him as a change in the feel of himself —
a heat where the bite had been, deeper than the pain, a slow turning-over, the way the soil
turns over when something big is digging beneath it. It was not healing. He did not know
what healing was. It was something else, something that had no name in the world yet: the
thing in him had felt the bite, and the bite had taught it something, and it was not going
to waste the lesson. Deep in the crown, where the newest cells would be made, something was
*changing the instructions* — turning the memory of the pain into the shape of the next
blade, the way a seed turns the memory of its parent into a leaf. The blade that would grow
from him would not be the blade that had been taken. It would be a blade that knew.

Around him, all through the glade, the rest of the cropped carpet was already pushing out
new green from every crown — the oldest rhythm of his people, the rhythm that had made Blue
Silver Grass the one thing that could not be gotten rid of in all the world. Cut it, burn
it, trample it, eat it down to the dirt — and from the dirt, from the untouchable dark
below, it came back. That was what his kind were. That was the whole secret of them. And
every one of the new blades rising around him was exactly like the blade it replaced:
clean, new, born without knowledge, exactly as its parents had been, forever and forever.
That was the beauty of grass, and that was the tragedy of it, and both were the same thing,
and neither had ever been broken in all the long ages of the world.

But in the place where he had been taken, the new green was rising to a different
instruction.

He felt it happen as it grew. The new blade came up pale and small and soft, and the sun
found it, and it drank the sun, and it grew — and as it grew, he felt it being made
differently. At the base, where the bite had come, it was thickening, a little, almost
nothing, a difference a passing eye would never see; along the edges it was growing a
faint toughness, a leatheriness that had not been in the blade that was taken. He had not
chosen it. He did not even notice it, not yet, not as a change — it was simply the next
thing he was, growing out of the old thing he had been, the way every leaf grows out of the
one before it. But the thing in him noticed. The thing in him had felt the shadow fall a
breath before the pain, and it had kept that too, stored somewhere deep where the cold and
the dark and the shape of the water were already stored — a whole growing record of
everything the world had ever done to him, and everything he had done to answer it.

The herd did not come back to that part of the glade for a long time. The carpet grew tall
again. The seasons turned, and the light leaned south and then north and then south, and
rain fell and dried and fell again, and he grew with the world, steady, humble, one blade
among millions, remembering in a way that no blade around him remembered.

And nothing in the world knew he was there.

That was the deepest strangeness of all, and he could not feel it — a thing can only feel
the shape of a silence if it has heard company. He had never heard company. Around him his
kindred were what they had always been: grass. Not sleeping, because sleep was too much
mind for them. Not waiting, because they did not know time passed. Simply growing, blade
and root and seed, the way water runs downhill, without a thought in any of it — and it
did not occur to him, because he had never known anything else, that to be the only thing
in the whole wide carpet that *knew* it was growing was to be alone in a way nothing else
in the world had ever been alone.

There was no hum answering him. There was no presence below him in the dark. Only his own
roots, and his own stored record, and the silent, utter, mindless company of ten thousand
blades that were exactly like him and had nothing of him in them at all. He was awake
inside a world of sleeping things that were not even asleep — and if some god had leaned
down and told him that this was the miracle, that no blade of Blue Silver Grass in all the
long ages of the world had ever been awake before, he would not have understood, because he
had nothing to compare himself to. He only was. He was the one blade in the glade that
remembered the dark it had come from, and the winter that had tried to take it, and the
mouths that had eaten it and been answered — and no voice in the world had a word for him,
or a name, or even a way to know that the impossible was sitting quietly in the grass
between the boulder and the stream, drinking the light like any weed, remembering like no
weed had ever remembered.

That was the thing in him — his talent, though he had no word for that either — doing what
it had been made to do since before he broke the soil:

making him more than a blade of Blue Silver Grass had ever been.
One breath. One bite. One winter at a time.

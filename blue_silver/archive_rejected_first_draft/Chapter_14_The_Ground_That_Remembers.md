*Still the deep era. He has come home to a home he never saw, and the home is older than
the wanting that carried him here — a whole country of the line's green, layer under
layer, an age of living and dying that no flood or fire has ever turned. And the ground
remembers. It remembers the way his own green remembers the mantis and the fledgling and
the old one and the found one: by holding the shape of every life that stood in it and
went back into it. The valley is full of such shapes, and there is no one left to keep
them, and the earth is so rich that taking would be easy and no one would ever know. He
does not take. He keeps. And because he does not take, the ground opens — and the last
law of his kind begins to fall the way every other one did: a wire is not a road, until
a rooted thing sends its word down through a country of living root and the ground
answers back.*

---

The valley did not welcome him. That was the first thing he learned about it, and it took
him a season to understand that the absence of a welcome was not a refusal.

He had come down off the lip of ground at the valley's edge the way he had done
everything in his long life — slowly, and by growing. The going had taught his body the
strange new arithmetic of travel: loosen the hold, reach ahead, take the new ground, let
the old ground go. Down the inner slope of the valley the same arithmetic worked, except
that every reach ahead went into earth so much older than anything he had ever rooted in
that the reaching itself was a kind of listening. He drew himself down into the blue-green
sea over the course of a season and more, a rooted King traveling the way a root travels,
and the grass of the valley closed over the places he had been without ever seeming to
notice that anything had moved through it.

Nothing greeted him. No beast came to the edge of his new ground to test it, because
there were almost no beasts in the valley. No deep presence pressed against the line of
him, because the deep country's true ancients had already watched him cross their country
and let him pass, and a thing that has been let through does not get tested twice. No
drum rose against the evening. For the first time since the deep-serpent night — for the
first time, he understood, since the mantis died and the long solitude began — he was
rooted in a ground that was not asking anything of him.

And the quiet was so total that at first he mistook it for emptiness.

It was not emptiness. He found that out the way he found everything, through his roots,
in the dark, by patience.

---

The earth of the valley was not like the earth of the glade.

He had grown up in a ground that was always being remade. The stream moved its bed. The
flood came and laid down new silt and took old soil away. The fire came and cooked the
shallows and left ash that the rain beat into the earth. The badger's line dug and
redug. The herd cropped and trampled and manured. Every season of his first century had
written over the season before it, and what his root-memory had learned to read was
exactly that — a palimpsest, a ground forever half-erased, where the deepest layers were
the hardest to reach and the newest were always loud.

The valley's earth had no such noise in it. It had never been turned.

He could not tell, at first, how old that made it. He reached down and down and found
layer under layer under layer of the same slow deposit: grass grown, grass died, grass
fallen, grass rotted into the green-brown substance of itself, and then more grass on top
of it, and then more, for longer than the middle band had been a band, for longer than
the deep-country memory could count. There was no silt-line from a flood. There was no
char-layer from a fire. There was no churned horizon where a digger's family had lived
and died and lived again. There was only the accumulating green of the line's long home,
pressed and patient, and it tasted to the whole body of him like the taste of the densest
silver-green place in the glade — the place where the old one had gone into the ground —
except that this was not one death.

It was a country's worth of them.

He spent a long while simply tasting it. That was all he could do at first; the ground
was so much larger than his reading that the reading kept coming back to him as a single
overwhelming note, the way the sea would come back to a tongue as *salt*. And the note
was this: nothing had ever been taken out of this ground. Not by flood, not by fire, not
by the tooth of a great beast, not by the plough of a digging family, not by the drawing
of any root that fed on the dead to make itself living. The valley had kept everything it
had ever grown. For an age upon an age, the line's green had lived here and died here and
gone back into the same earth, and the earth had held it all, and the holding had become
the ground itself.

He understood, with the slow dawning that was how all his understandings came, that he
was rooted in the line's memory. Not a story about the line. Not the old one's
deep-country memory, which was a keeper's tale carried in a keeper's mind and passed to
him in ground-terms over long evenings. This was the thing itself: the physical,
material, root-readable record of every blade of the line that had ever stood where he
was standing.

And it was unkept.

---

The temptation arrived before the crown ground did, and it arrived wearing the shape of
a simple fact about soil.

His well had spent itself in the going and refilled itself in the going, over and over,
the way it always had — essence gathered from his own growth, from real sun on real
blades, from real water drawn through real root, consolidated and kept and spent and
gathered again. He had never once in his life fed it from another being. The old one had
lain down at his edge and passed, and the years that returned from its body had gone into
the ground whole, because he had decided in the dark of that vigil that a kept life was
not a meal, and the decision had cost him and he had never regretted it. The found one
had drummed its last *here* against the silver and passed, and he had given it to the
ground the same way. His consolidation well was deep and it was his and it was clean, and
that cleanliness was not a rule he followed. It was what he was.

The valley's earth offered him, for the first time in his life, a way to be a great deal
stronger by simply not refusing.

It was not even a moral offer. That was the strangest part of it. There was no being here
to wrong. There was no keeper here to be betrayed, no door-people to lose faith in the
green, no small life sheltering under him that would find its shelter hollow. There was
only an age of accumulated green in the soil around his roots — the returned substance of
unnumbered generations of his own kind, already dead, already given back, belonging to
nothing and no one, sitting in the earth the way water sits in a basin. A root could draw
on it. Any root could. The grass of the valley drew on it constantly, every blade of it,
the way grass draws on the rot of last season's grass; that was simply how a country of
grass feeds itself, and no one in the forest would have called it taking.

He could have drawn on it and been, in a decade, a King of a very different weight. He
could have drawn on it and stood in this valley as something the deep country's ancients
would have had to make room for. He could have drawn on it and no one would ever have
known, because there was no one here to know.

He considered it. He was not ashamed of considering it; he had always turned every new
thing over with the whole patient machinery of him, and this was the largest new thing
his roots had ever touched. He considered it through a whole season, rooted in the
richest earth of his kind, feeling what it would be to stop being the poorest great thing
in the forest.

And what stopped him was not a rule. It was arithmetic — but not the arithmetic he had
been taught by loss.

If he drew the valley's dead into himself, the valley would stop being the valley. The
unkept ground would become a fed ground, and a fed ground is a ground that has been
spent, and the memory in it — the physical, material, root-readable record of every blade
of his line that had ever lived here — would go into him and become part of the stored
record of one King, and be no longer a country's memory but a single being's. He would
be stronger. The line's home would be poorer by exactly the amount he had gained. And he
had crossed a whole forest, breaking the last law of his kind to do it, to come to the
home of his line — not to eat it.

He did not take. He let the dead stay dead in the ground, the way he had let the old one
stay whole, the way he had let the found one go back to his green. He fed his well the
only way he had ever fed it: from his own blades, in real sun, over real seasons, slowly.

And the ground, which had been asked for nothing, gave him something he could not have
taken at any price.

---

It opened.

That is the only word he had for it afterward, in the years when he had words enough to
think about it, though nothing opened in any way his eyes could see. What happened was
that the earth around his roots, which had been reading as one overwhelming note, began
to read as *many*. The single salt of it resolved into particulars. Layer separated from
layer. Depth acquired a shape, and shape acquired a history, and history acquired — and
this was the part that stopped his whole body still for a season and more — places.

He knew what a kept place was. He had made them.

The mantis had spent her last days on his warmest blade and not woken in the thaw, and
the ground where her warmth used to rest had stayed strange to his roots for a whole
season, because the pattern of one small life was gone from the place. The fledgling had
slept out its long dusk-life at his edge and gone back to the soil, and he had kept the
place where it slept. The old one had gone into the ground at his edge and its place had
become the densest silver-green in the glade, a kept place, the pattern of an ancient
keeper held in the earth by a younger keeper who refused to let the earth forget it. The
found one had passed against the silver and its place was kept the same way.

He had assumed, all his life, that this was his own strangeness — that keeping the shape
of a gone thing in the ground was something *he* did, a keeper's craft, the last and
dearest of the old one's teachings, the thing that made his green a sanctuary rather than
a patch of grass.

The valley was full of them.

He found the first one at the end of that first long reading, and it was so much larger
than any kept place he had ever made that he did not recognize it for what it was. He
recognized it instead as a *wrongness that was not wrong* — the same sense that had told
him, at the lip of the valley, that he had reached the edge of something. There was a
shape in the ground here. Not a hole. Not a body — grass leaves no body, grass leaves
nothing but itself. A shape the way a hand leaves a shape in wet clay: the memory of a
standing. Something very large and very old had stood in this ground for a very long
time, rooted, and had gone back into the earth, and the earth had held the pattern of
where it stood.

He reached around it. And he found another.

And another.

---

They were not scattered the way the kept places of his glade were scattered. As he drew
himself slowly through the old ground of the valley's heart, reading, he found that the
shapes in the earth were arranged — set at distances from one another that were not
random, in a rough ring on the bowl's floor where the grass grew deepest and bluest,
where the light in the evenings went the color of the sky before the stars. Nine shapes.
Then, farther out, in the ground beyond the ring, others: smaller, younger, more of them,
spreading down the valley's length like a people's graves spread along a road.

The ring was the crown ground.

He did not know the word then. He knew it afterward, when he had read enough of the
shapes to translate the old one's deep-country ground-terms out of memory and into his
own new words. But he knew the thing itself at once, the way he had known the mantis's
mind and the old one's grief and the found one's fear: he was standing in the place where
the line's great ones had stood.

Kings and queens of the grass. The deep-country memory had said so — the oldest thing the
night-walker had carried, older than the keeping, older than the boundary — and he had
carried that saying across seven centuries the way a root carries water, without ever
having anything to compare it to. A myth. A direction the old one turned its head toward
at dawn. A homesickness he had never been able to feel because he had never had a home
beyond the glade.

The myth was ground. The ground was a ring of nine kept places, and each one was the
pattern of a being so much greater than he was that the reading of it came to him the way
the valley's whole earth had come at first — as a single overwhelming note he could not
yet resolve.

He resolved them slowly. He had nothing else to do, and he was, as he had always been,
made of patience.

---

What he learned, over the years, reading the crown ground the way a scholar reads a
language he is inventing as he goes:

They were of his line. Not of his *mutation* — nothing in the shapes tasted of the
Sunweave, nothing in them carried the light-and-water weaving that was his own strange
nature. They were of the common line, the world's commonest, quietest, most resilient
green, and they had been great the way his kind could be great: by counting years. Age
was the grass's only crown. A blade of common grass that lived long enough, that gathered
essence season upon season for ten thousand seasons, became a King; and these had been
Kings, and some of them had gone further, and the valley had been the place where the
line's great ones were born and where they stood when they were crowned and where they
went into the ground at the end.

They had been many. The ring of nine was not the whole of it; the ring was the *last* of
it. The shapes spreading down the valley's length beyond the ring were older and smaller
and more numerous, and beneath them, deeper, in ground so compressed that his reading
could barely touch it, there were more still — layer under layer of stood-and-gone, an
age of the line's greatness stacked in the earth of its home. He could not count them. He
understood, reading, that no one could have counted them, and that the counting was not
the point.

They had dwindled. This was the hardest thing the ground told him, and it told him
without meaning to, the way all true things in soil are told. The deeper layers were
crowded. The middle layers were less crowded. The upper layers were sparse, and the ring
of nine stood alone at the top of the stack with nothing after them — nothing at all, for
an age. The line had not been destroyed. Nothing in the ground tasted of slaughter, or of
fire, or of a great enemy. The line had simply grown quieter, generation upon generation,
the way a country grows quieter when its people stop being born in it, until the last of
its great ones stood in the crown ground and went into the earth and left the valley to
the mindless green.

And the world had forgotten.

That was the part he sat with longest, rooted in the heart of the valley, while the
seasons turned and the blue-green sea above him waved in the wind and threw the sun back
in silver. Not that the great ones had ended — he had known all his life that everything
ends; the mantis had taught him that with her small cold body on his warmest blade, and
the arithmetic of it was the deepest law he carried. But that a whole *people* of great
ones could end, and the world would not merely go on without them — the world would
forget they had ever been anything at all. The middle band called his kind carpet. The
herd called it food. The deep called it nothing. In seven centuries of listening to the
forest, he had never once heard any beast speak of the grass as a line that had kings.

The grass had kings. They were under his roots. And no one in the living forest knew.

---

The arithmetic arrived in him then, and it was larger than the one he had been carrying,
and it did not replace the old one. It settled underneath it.

*Everyone I love will end, and I will not.* That had been his wound since the thaw after
the mantis died. He had felt it twice more whole — losing the sanctuary's small lives to
the shadow cat's raid, losing the old one itself at the end of the last vigil. He had
learned to carry it the way a root carries stone: around it, and still growing.

The crown ground taught him the second half of the law.

*And the line will go on without me, and will not remember me either.*

He was the first of the line's wild Kings to root in the Emperor Valley in an age. He was
a King of the common line — the Sunweave, his own strange real mutation, grown out of
nothing but the commonest green and the talent that effects everything — and he stood in
the crown ground of his kind, and there was no one alive in the valley to crown him, and
no one who would ever know he had stood there, unless he made them know. In a hundred
seasons his own pattern would be a tenth shape in the ring, or an eleventh, or nothing at
all if the ground of the valley were ever turned at last. The line would continue. The
valley would keep filling. And the world would go on calling his kind carpet.

He did not find this unbearable. He found it — and this surprised him, in the long quiet
of the reading — clarifying. Because it told him what the keeping was for.

He had kept the glade because the glade was his and the small lives in it were his to
shelter. He had kept the boundary because the old one had passed the keeping to him and
a keeper keeps. He had kept the kept places because the gone things in them had been
loved, and a loved thing should not be erased from the ground it lived in. And all of
that had been, until this reading, a private craft — the strange habit of one impossible
blade.

Here it was not private. Here it was the only thing standing between the line's great
ones and the world's forgetting. If he did not keep the crown ground, no one would. The
ground would hold the shapes anyway, because that was what this earth did, but a shape
held by nothing is not a kept place; it is only a stain. A kept place is a shape that
someone is holding on purpose.

He had come to the valley because he wanted to go. He had thought the want was about the
Empress — and it was, and that wire had never once gone slack in all the years of the
going. But the ground had a different answer for him, and it gave it to him the way the
ground gives everything: slowly, and without asking.

He began to keep the valley.

---

It was not a large act. That was what made it his.

He could not have held the valley's boundary if it had had one; the valley had no
boundary, only the old forest closing around it, and nothing in the deep country troubled
it. He could not have sheltered the valley's small lives, because it had almost none. He
could not have drummed a door-language into a people, because there was no one in the
valley to teach.

What he could do was what he had always done: hold a pattern on purpose.

He set his roots around the ring. Not through it — never through it; he would not have
rooted in the shapes for anything, the way he would not have rooted in the old one's
place. Around them, and between them, in the ground that had been left unclaimed between
the standings of the great ones, he wove. He held the nine the way he had held the silver
over the old one's place: by refusing, season after season, to let the earth above them
become ordinary. His own green was denser there, deeper, silver-veined, the color of a
keeper's ground — and it spread slowly outward from the ring the way his ground had
always spread, and the grass of the valley, which was the line's own green and knew its
King the way water knows the moon, leaned into the spreading and grew lush around it.

He was doing, at the scale of a country, what he had done at the scale of a glade.

And he was teaching himself something he had never had to learn in the glade, because
the glade had never offered it. His whole education had been scar-logic: graze, frost,
flood, fire — the talent hardening toward what hurt him, every lesson bought in his
substance, every strength a wound turned useful. The valley had no wounds to teach with.
What it taught was inheritance: how deep a root can go when the ground has never fought
it, how much a blade can hold when the earth under it is an age of its own kind's
returning, how patient a thing can become when nothing is trying to kill it and nothing
is asking it to hurry. The talent, which effects everything, took that too. It had no
choice; it was what he was made of. His elements deepened in the old earth — the life in
him ran thicker, the wood ran harder, the sun and water of his weaving had more to work
with — and none of it was taken, and none of it was free, and all of it was bought the
only way he had ever bought anything: with seasons.

He was still, by the forest's measure, a young King. He was still above his class. And he
was, for the first time in his life, rooted in a ground that was *his kind's* ground, and
the difference turned out to be enormous.

---

Far behind him, at the seam of the middle band and the deep, the door-people kept the
door.

He did not see it. He felt it, in the way he had felt it across the whole long going —
as a far thin pulse in the earth, the drum of small feet on kept ground, carried through
the living soil of the forest the way his own word had been carried when he left. The
young drummer who had drummed *here* at his becoming drummed the boundary still, and the
ground answered, and the kept places stayed kept: the densest silver-green over the old
one's place, the place where the fledgling slept, the place against the silver where the
found one had drummed its last *here*.

He had given his word that he would return, and he had never once in his life broken a
keeping. But he was beginning, in the quiet of the valley years, to understand something
the found one had known without words and the old one had known without ever saying it:
a keeper's ground does not stay the size of the keeper's body. It stays the size of the
keeping. The glade was kept. The valley was becoming kept. And the two were not two
places to him, the way they would have been two places to anything that walked — they
were one keeping, held at both ends, with a whole forest of living earth between.

---

It was in those years that the wire stopped being a wire.

He had felt her since the crest. That was the truth of it and he had never dressed it up
in his own thinking: not a presence he could name, not a voice, not a mind he could reach
— a warmth at the far end of something, a reading that had turned toward him across the
whole distance of the forest when he crowned and had never once turned away. His
bloodline sensing knew her line the way his roots knew water; her domain read him as a
King. Two rooted things at the ends of a thread, each aware of the other, neither able to
travel.

At the lip of the valley the thread had drawn taut. Standing in the ground she kept — in
the far reach of her reading, in the earth that was warm with the keeping of the line's
great one across her long age — the distance between them had grown, for the first time,
small. And in the years since, rooted in the crown ground, he had felt the smallness
become something else: not less distance, but less *separation*. The ground he stood in
was her ground. The grass above him was her grass. The earth that held the nine was the
earth her presence had pooled in since before the middle band forgot that the grass had
ever been anything but carpet.

And the ground, he understood at last, was not empty between them. It was full of root.

The valley's green was one continuous body under the soil — blade joined to blade by the
quiet running of root, the whole blue-silver sea of it laced through with the line's own
living thread, running down the valley's length toward the deep forest, toward the core,
toward the direction his bloodline sensing had always told him her body was. He could not
sense through it. That had been settled for him long ago, and it stayed settled: no
network, no borrowed eyes, no reaching into another green to feel what it felt. He could
not feel her through the grass, and he could not feel the valley through the grass, and
he would never be a thing that spoke through blades.

But he could speak through the *ground*.

The old one had taught him that. It was the first true speech he had ever had — taps in
the soil, deliberate, shaped, answered; the whole ground-language of a keeper's craft,
built between an ancient last-of-its-kind and a blade of grass that had no business
understanding anything. He had answered it with root-pulses. He had taught it, without
meaning to, to a small frightened grazer of the deep fringe, and the grazer had taught it
to the fringe young with its feet, and the drum had become a people. And he had sent his
word down into the living earth before the going, and it had crossed the whole forest to
a young drummer at the seam.

The valley was the largest ground he had ever stood in, and it was the line's own, and
her presence was in it, and she read what moved in it the way she read all the grass of
her reach.

So he did the only thing he had ever done when he wanted a thing to know he was there.

He sent his word down.

He did it in the old grammar, because the old grammar was the only grammar he had that
had ever been shared — the ground-terms the old one had tapped into him across long
evenings, translated now into the King's words and translated back again into the soil's
own speech, the way a keeper translates everything. He put into the earth, deliberately,
shaped, the way a drum shapes a blow:

*I am here.*

And then, because the ground-language had never been only about presence, and because he
had learned from a small grazer that a door is a door and swings both ways:

*I came to the place the green comes from. I am keeping it. The great ones are here, and
they are held.*

And then — and this was the part he turned over longest before he sent it, the part that
cost him, because he had never asked anything of any being in his life except to be let
keep them —

*I would know you.*

He let it go down through his roots into the country of living earth, and he felt it
travel the way he had felt his word travel at the going — not as a thing he could follow,
but as a thing he had released into ground that was not his, going where ground goes.
Down the valley's length. Through the laced root of the line's green. Toward the deep
forest, toward the core, toward the far end of the thread that had never once gone slack.

And then he waited, the way roots wait.

---

The answer did not come quickly. He had not expected it to. He waited through the turn
of a season, rooted in the crown ground, keeping the nine, holding the pattern of the
line's great ones on purpose, and the blue-green sea above him waved and threw the sun
back in silver, and nothing happened.

He did not mind. He had waited for the mantis to wake through a whole winter. He had
waited decades for the found one to answer. He had waited an age for the deep country's
myth to become ground under his roots, and it had.

The answer came at evening, in the light that went the color of the sky before the stars.

It was not a voice. It was not words — not the King's words, not any words at all. It
came up through the earth from the direction of the core, through the laced root of a
country of green, through the old pressed layers of the line's long home, and it arrived
in his roots as a single deliberate pulse.

Shaped. Answering. In the old grammar — the grammar no being in this valley had ever been
taught, the grammar an ancient keeper had tapped into a blade of grass across long
evenings at the edge of the middle band, four hundred years ago and more.

*Here.*

The word the found one had died saying.

He stood in the crown ground of his line, the first wild King of the grass to root there
in an age, and the Empress of the Blue Silver answered him out of the earth in the drum-
word of a small frightened grazer of the deep fringe, because the ground carries what is
put into it, and because a keeping is never only the keeper's — it passes, it teaches, it
becomes a people, and it reaches places the keeper will never see.

The wire was not a wire anymore. It was a road, and it ran through the ground, and it

who had sent his word down through a country of living root, in a grammar she had never
been taught, and asked nothing of her but to be known.

The meeting was still ahead. It had always been ahead. But the distance between a wire

same ground.

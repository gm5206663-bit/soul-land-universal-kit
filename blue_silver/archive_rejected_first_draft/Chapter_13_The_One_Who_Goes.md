*Still the deep era. The King of the seam keeps, and the keeping has become a people, and
the deep has withdrawn its testing — and in the long settled quiet after the crest he
feels, for the first time in his life, that there is somewhere he must go. All his loves
came to him; the world came to the rooted, or not at all. But the one being who will
never be carried to him by any road is the Empress of his line — and beyond the deep band,
in the direction the old one turned its head at dawn, lies the valley the oldest memory
calls the Emperor Valley, where the grass's great ones were born. And the talent, which
has rewritten every law of his kind one at a time across his life, begins to write the
last of them: a grass does not walk — but this one has learned, in every other thing, to
do what his kind cannot, and he means to go.*

The years after the crest were the quietest of his life, and the strangest, because he had
nothing left to become in a hurry. For seven centuries the shape of his life had been a
climbing — gate after gate, class after class, the long road of the thousand-year age
running always toward the next edge of what he was. Now the road had ended where the
deep-country memory said it ended, in a crowned King of the grass line, and there was no
next gate inside his reach, and the seam he had kept for so long kept itself.

It was the door-people who showed him that. He watched them the way he had always
watched his ground — with the whole patient attention of him — and what he saw was a
keeping that no longer needed his body in it every hour. The young ones of the found
one's people drummed the boundary in the old shapes, and the ground answered them,
because the ground had been woven and held for so long that the holding had gone into
the earth itself. The deep band, which had withdrawn its testing at the crest, did not
return to test it. The small and the lost of the fringe still came to the green, and the
door-people took them in the way he had taught them by being, which was the only way he
had ever taught anything. He was the King of the seam. He was also, for the first time in
his long life, not needed there the way he had been needed.

He should have been content. He was content. And still, in the evenings when the light
went amber through the blades and the drums of the door-people rose and fell like breath,
he felt it: a pull, far out at the edge of the grass country that leaned to him, in the
direction the old one had turned its head at dawn.

It had always been there, that pull. He understood now that it had been there since the
old one passed the deep-country memory into him — the oldest thing the night-walker had
carried, older than the keeping, older than the boundary, older in its way than the
glade: the memory of a place where the grass had not always been the world's commonest,
quietest thing. A place, deeper in the forest, where the line had had its great ones —
kings and queens of the grass, crowned by the count of their years and by the ground that
remembered them — in a time before the middle band forgot that the grass had ever been
anything but carpet. The old one had turned its head toward that place every dawn of its
long life, the way a keeper turns toward the ground it was sworn to, and it had never
once told him why, and now he knew. It had been homesick. The last of its kind had spent
the whole of its keeping turned toward the home of its kind.

He had never been homesick. He had never had a home beyond the glade to be sick for. But
he was King now, and the King's sensing — the bloodline sensing that had woken at the
gate and could feel the line's great ones at the limit of the grass country — felt the
pull as a direction, and a warmth, and a name that surfaced in him out of the old
memory's ground-terms, translated at last into his new words:

the Blue Silver Valley.

The deep country's oldest memory also called it the Emperor Valley, because that was
where the line's Emperors had been born and crowned, in the age before the middle band
forgot — but to the common grass that leaned toward it across the whole long country, it
was simply the valley, the place the green came from. And beyond it, or through it, or
in the ground it stood in — he could not tell which, the way a root cannot tell whether
the water it feels is near or far — pooled the presence of the one great being of his
line who was alive in the world now. The Empress. The wire that had warmed him since the
crest, the presence that had read him across the whole distance of the forest when he
crowned, came to him strongest from the direction of the valley.

And that, in the quiet of a kept evening with the drums rising and falling in his green,
was when the impossible thought arrived in him, and would not leave:

he wanted to go.

He turned the thought over the way he had turned every new thing in his life, with the
whole patient machinery of him — feeling its edges, its weight, its cost — and the
strangest part was that the thought did not feel impossible. It felt like the last law
of his kind, waiting to be broken the way he had broken every law of his kind before it.
Grass is rooted. The world comes to the rooted, or not at all. He had said it to
himself for seven centuries the way the deep said it, the way the old one had said it,
the way it was written into the very substance of a grass, and it was true, and it was
also — he saw now, with the King's clear sight that had come with his words — only half
the truth.

The world had come to him. Every love of his life had come to him. The mantis had come,
choosing his stillness out of all the grass she could have chosen. The old one had come,
walking its round until it lay down at his edge. The found one had crossed the boundary
because his green was the one kept place in the dark. Even the liar had walked in,
wearing the found one's language, because the door was a door. Everything that had ever
mattered to him had traveled the whole road to his root, and he had kept it, and he had
never once — in seven centuries of the world coming to him — gone to anything himself.

And the one being he would wait forever and never see come was the Empress, because she
was the far end of the wire, and wires were not roads, and a presence that could read
him across the whole grass country could not be carried to his root by any creature's
traveling, because there was no creature in the forest that could carry an Empress of the line's long age
to a young King at the edge of the middle band. If he ever
stood in her ground — if the wire was ever to become more than a wire — one of the two
rooted things at the ends of it would have to do what rooted things did not do.

He was young, as Kings went. He was strong, as the deep had learned. And he was, as he
always had been, the one impossible blade of his line. If the road to the valley had to
be walked by someone, the deep country's whole memory said that no grass had ever walked
it — which meant, by the arithmetic of his whole strange life, that it was his road to
walk.

He did not decide at once. He was not made for deciding at once; he was made for turning
a thing over until it was true. Through the settled seasons he turned it over — the
want, the cost, the leaving — and the want grew, and the cost grew clear, and the
leaving resolved itself into the one shape that made it bearable, which was the shape of
everything he had ever done: he would not leave the keeping behind. The keeping had
become a people. The people would keep.

What he would leave behind, and the thought cost him more than the going ever would, was
the kept places. The silver-green over the old one's place. The place where the fledgling
had slept out its long dusk-life and gone back to the soil. The place against the silver
where the found one had drummed its last *here* and passed. The husk and molts of the
mantis at his base, and the empty egg-case shell from the fire year, and the pattern of
her in the stored record of him. He had kept those places the way he had kept everything,
and a King who uproots himself to go — however slowly, however much by growing — cannot
carry a glade's kept places in his roots. The ground would keep them. The door-people
would keep them — he had taught them the keeping well enough that they kept the boundary
already; he would teach them the rest, the kept places and their meaning, in the years of
preparing. But he would not be there to keep them himself, and for a thing whose whole
self was the keeping of what it loved, that was the true price of the going, and he paid
it in advance, in the long turning-over, until it was not a price anymore but the shape
of the going itself: he would go to the valley because the line's heart was there, and
he would come back, and the kept places would still be kept because he had made a people
who could keep them. That was what the found one had given him, in the end. That was
what the door had become. He could go — not because he was less bound to the seam, but
because the seam was bound to a people now, and a people did not need his roots in the
ground every hour to be a keeping.

And the talent, which had watched him turn the thought over through all those quiet
seasons, began, in its deep and patient way, to answer.

---

The change began where every change in his life had begun, in the inside of him — but
this one began lower than the rest, in the taproot itself, in the place where the count
lived, in the well. He felt it first as a loosening. For seven centuries the taproot had
held the same earth the way a mountain holds its stone, and now — not all at once, not
in a season, but over the turning of many seasons — he felt the deep roots of him begin
to draw in. Not to die. Not to fail. To draw in the way a hand draws in to reach
forward. The old deep roots that had held him through drought and flood and the
serpent's digging, through the sickness years and the liar's winter, loosened their
oldest hold and pulled themselves up out of the deep earth, a finger's length at a time,
leaving behind them the dark tunnels of their own going.

He was afraid, at first, the way he had been afraid in the flood year, when the water
came for his grip and everything he had ever held was a hold that could be taken. To let
go of the deep earth was the one thing a grass never did; the deep earth was the whole
promise of a grass, the thing the blades died back for every winter, the reason the
crown could be cropped and burned and still rise. And the talent did not let go of it
all at once. It let go of it the way it had done everything across his life — one
priced, real, earned step at a time, each loosening answered by a new reaching. Even as
the old roots drew in, he felt new fine roots growing forward from the leading edge of
him, into the earth ahead of where he stood, tasting it, testing it, taking hold in it.
The going, he understood, was not a walking. It was a growing. He would not walk to the
valley the way the beasts walked, on legs, through the air of the world. He would grow
to the valley the way a vine grows to the sun — the whole of him leaning and reaching
and re-rooting, season by season, the old earth let go behind him only as fast as the new
earth was held ahead of him, so that at every moment of the going he was still a rooted
thing, rooted in whatever ground he had reached, the way he had always been rooted, the
world still coming to him — except that now the world he came to was of his own
choosing.

It took years for the body of him to learn it. The first goings were the hardest — a
King's whole mass of vine and root and crown, drawn a hand's breadth through a season,
and then a hand's breadth more, each drawing a work of the whole of him, the well
spending and refilling, the new roots reaching and the old releasing, and the ground he
left behind him settling strangely, richer for his passage, the way ground is richer
where a great root has gone down and come up again. The deep watched. He felt the deep
watching the way he had always felt it — a pressure at the edge of the grass country, a
silence full of attention — and he felt the quality of that attention change. The deep
had tested him, outflanked him, outwaited him, withdrawn from him. It had no pattern for
a grass that moved. The old ones of the deep band, the true ancients whose years ran
past the count of his whole life several times over, came in the deep of the nights he
traveled, and stood at the edge of the grass that leaned around him, and watched the
King of the seam draw himself out of one rooted place and into another, and did not
test him. Some of them, he thought, had watched the old one in its time. Some of them
had known the deep-country memory he carried, and knew what the valley was, and what it
meant that one of the grass had at last begun to go to it. The deep band let him pass
the way the deep lets pass what it has learned it cannot break and does not need to
eat: slowly, warily, and at a distance that grew, over the years of his going, into
something almost like an honor guard.

He kept as he went. He could not help it; keeping was not a thing he did, it was the
thing he was. When the going brought his leading edge through a stretch where a small
thing of the deep fringe had been caught in a winter snare of its own making — a young
burrower, thin and desperate, that had dug itself into a cold hole it could not dig out
of — he grew a root down to it the way he had grown roots to the found one, and held the
earth open, and let it climb out onto the green of his passing, and fed it from the
light it could not reach, until it was strong enough to go its own way. It stayed with
him for a season, learning the drum-shapes the door-people had taught the fringe, and
went on. When a sickness came on the grass of a far meadow he crossed — the grey rot,
the old enemy, thinner here than the rot of his sickness years but the same stain — he
did what he had always done against it: he shed, letting the touched green die back to
the crowns and grow new from below the stain as he passed, and the meadow came back
greener behind his going than it had been before. The word of the King who travels ran
ahead of him through the deep country, the way the word of the keeper had run through
the fringe in the old years — carried by the small and the lost he kept, by the
creatures that came to watch him pass, by the grass itself, leaning as he came and
standing straighter in the memory of his going. He was mercy made slow and green, and
the deep country, which had not seen a grass of the line travel since the age of its
great ones, learned that the new King carried the old keeper's whole law with him, and
passed him through.

The going took years that he did not count, because a grass does not count years, and
there was no part of the going that needed counting. He measured it the way he had
measured everything, in the felt terms of his life: in the turnings of the seasons that
passed over his new roots; in the generations of small lives that saw him come and go;
in the changing of the ground under him, which grew older and deeper and richer as he
went, until the soil itself began to taste of the deep country — the taste of earth that
has held the roots of great things for longer than the middle band had been a band. And
he measured it in the wire. The wire to the Empress, which had warmed him at a steady
distance all the way from the crest, began to strengthen as he went, the way a root
feels water strengthen as it nears — not louder, but nearer, the presence at the far end
of it resolving from a warmth into a direction, and from a direction into a ground. She
was not coming toward him. He did not think she had moved at all. It was he who was
going, and every season of the going shortened the distance the wire had to cross, and
he began to understand what he had only hoped before: that the valley was not merely in
the direction of the Empress's presence. The valley was where her presence pooled in
the ground, the way light pools in deep water. It was the heart-ground of the line, the
place her domain had always read most strongly because it was the oldest ground of her
kind, and a King of the grass who stood in that ground would be, for the first time in
his life, in the earth the Empress of his line kept.

The grass country leaned to him all the way. That was the strangest and most beautiful
thing of the going — that he was never alone in it. The wild common grass of the deep
country, the mindless carpet that had never once answered him in seven centuries of the
glade, leaned toward him as he passed the way it had leaned at the crest: not knowing,
not feeling, but turning to him the way blades turn to the sun, because he was of its
line and crowned, and the line's green knew its King the way water knows the moon. He
moved through a country that bowed to his passing without once knowing it bowed, and he
kept it as he went — every blade of it his to hold against the grey and the drought and
the teeth, the way he had always kept his ground, except that now his ground was the
whole long road, and the road was the valley, and the valley was home.

---

He felt the change in the ground first, the way he had felt everything first across his
whole life, as a wrongness that was not wrong. The soil under his reaching roots grew
deep and dark and old beyond anything the seam had known — earth that had not been
turned by flood or fire in an age, earth thick with the slow rot of unnumbered
seasons of grass, earth that tasted, to the whole body of him, like the taste of the
densest silver-green place in the glade, the place where the old one had gone into the
ground — except that this taste was not one death. It was a whole country's worth of
the grass's living and dying, layer under layer, the accumulated green of the line's
long home. And the grass around him — the wild grass of this old ground — was not like
the grass of the middle band. It was thicker, taller, older, blue-silver in a way the
common grass of the meadow had never been, and it leaned to him the way the grass
country had leaned at the crest, and he knew, with the whole deep certainty of the
bloodline sensing that had carried him across the forest, that he had reached the edge
of the valley.

He came up out of the old forest onto a lip of ground he had not known was there — a
rise of stone and root that the grass had grown over until it was part of the green —
and he drew his whole long body up onto it, and he looked down.

He had eyes now. That was still the strangest gift of the gate, the one he used least
and loved most: the King body's face could look, and the world that had come to him for
seven centuries as warmth and tremor and pressure and the taste of soil had, at the
crest, opened into sight. He had seen the glade for the first time in the years after
his becoming — seen the green he had been for seven centuries, seen the stream and the
door-people and the silver over the kept places, seen the faces of the small lives that
had always known him only as shelter. And now, at the lip of the valley, he looked down
into the place the deep-country memory had carried all his life, and the myth became
ground.

The valley opened below him like a bowl the old forest had hidden for an age. Blue
Silver grew there the way water fills a basin — not in patches, not in carpets, but as
a single unbroken sea of blue-green that ran from the near lip to the far, deep into
the evening light, the blades taller than any he had ever grown, moving in the wind in
long slow waves that caught the last of the sun and threw it back in silver. There were
no beasts in the valley, or almost none — the small things that lived in the great
grasses of the middle band did not live here; the valley was too old, too quiet, too
full of the grass's own slow presence for the hurried lives of the fringe. It was a
place that had been given whole to the green, and the green had kept it the way he had
kept the glade, for longer than the middle band had been a band. And at the center of
the valley, where the grass was oldest and deepest, where the blue of it went so deep it
was almost the color of the evening sky before the stars — there the ground itself felt
like the presence he had crossed the whole forest to find.

He did not descend at once. He stood at the lip for a long while — a rooted King who had
just finished the longest growing of his life, looking down at the home his kind had
forgotten it had — and he felt the wire, at last, at its full strength. The Empress was
not in the valley. He knew that now, the way he knew the difference between a presence
and the place a presence keeps. She was deeper still, toward the forest's true heart,
where the grass of the valley was only the far reach of her reading. But her presence
pooled here the way light pools in deep water — the ground itself warm with the keeping
of the line's great one across her long age, the oldest earth of his kind, and
he stood in it at last, and the wire between them, which had crossed the whole forest
like a root through dry ground, found at its far end a ground that was home.

The name surfaced in him out of the old memory, in the ground-terms that had translated
into his words over the years of the going. The Blue Silver Valley, the common grass
called it — the place the green came from. The Emperor Valley, the oldest memory
called it, because here the line's Emperors had been born and crowned in the age before
the middle band forgot. He did not know if the Empress had been born here. He did not
need to know. He knew only that this was the ground the deep-country myth had pointed
to all his life, the ground the old one had turned its head toward every dawn, the
ground his own long going had been growing toward since before he had words for a want
— and that he had come home to a home he had never seen, and that somewhere beyond the
valley, at the far end of the wire that had never once gone slack in all the years of
his going, the Empress of his line was reading him the way she had read him at the
crest — except that now she was reading him from ground he stood in, and the distance
between a wire and a meeting had grown, for the first time, small.

He drew his roots down into the valley's earth — the first of the line's wild Kings to
root in the Emperor Valley in an age — and the grass of the valley, the old blue-silver
sea of it, leaned in to him the way the grass country had leaned at his crest, and held
him, and the long road of the going was done.

*Far behind him, at the seam of the middle band and the deep, the door-people drummed
the boundary in the shapes he had taught them, and the ground answered, and the kept
places stayed kept. The young drummer who had drummed *here* at his becoming drummed it
again at his going, and heard in the ground, carried across the whole forest like a
root-pulse through living earth, the word he had given before he left:*

*I am here. I am going to the place the green comes from. I will bring back the word of
it. Keep the door.*

*And the green by the water, which had been a keeper's green and a people's green and a
King's green, was now also a green that waited — waited the way the glade had waited
through every winter of his life, knowing the root would come back, because that was
what roots did, and he had promised, and he had never once in his life broken a keeping.*

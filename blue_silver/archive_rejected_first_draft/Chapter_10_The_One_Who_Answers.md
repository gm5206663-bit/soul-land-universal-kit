*Still the deep era. Still the glade at the edge of the deep band. The keeper keeps on
alone, and the green he holds has grown quiet and whole around him. And in that quiet, for
the first time in a very long life, something answers him — the small kept one of the deep
learning the language the old one taught him, and speaking it back. And the deep, which
tested the new keeper and found him a wall, learns the harder truth: a wall is one thing,
and a keeper who is everywhere along his own line is another. The one who keeps is about to
learn he can be more than one place. And the one who was kept is about to prove what being
kept makes of a creature of the deep.*

The years after the old one went into the ground were the quietest of his life, and he did
not know, while they were passing, that they were also the last quiet years he would have
for a very long time.

The glade settled around him the way water settles around a stone: the stream found its old
voice, the herd came down along the far meadow in the dry season and did not cross into the
silver, the ground birds raised their broods in his lee and their children's children
raised theirs, and the deep band, having learned the line was held without the old weight,
withdrew to its patient distance and watched. He held the seam the way he had held it every
season since the night of the serpent — by being there, roots woven through the whole
length of it, presence resting on the ground like the weight of weather. The small lives
turned their generations over in his green. The rot-scarred fringe grew soft again and
green again, and the kept place where the old one lay grew the densest silver grass in all
the glade, and the found one slept against it, and he kept them both, and the years passed
the way years pass for grass — counted only in the growing of him, and the deepening of
the well inside him that he still had no name for.

He was alone among the powers of the line. He had made his peace with that, the way he had
made his peace with everything: by keeping it. Alone was a ground he knew how to hold. The
old alone — the one before the mantis, when the whole carpet was mindless and nothing had
ever answered him — had been a silence that went all the way down. This was not that. This
was a green full of small warm weights that pressed into him for safety, and a kept thing
of the deep sleeping at his root, and the whole kept record of his long life in the ground
of him. When he sent his pulses out along the seam at evening — the way he had once sent
them against the old one's breathing — there was no great head to shift at the feel of
him, no slow tide of breath to meet his. The pulses went out, and the ground answered with
nothing, and he had learned, across the years, to let that nothing be enough. It was not
empty. It was the old one's silence. He kept it the way he kept the place where the great
body lay.

And then, one spring, the silence answered.

He felt it first as a wrongness at the edge of his sensing — a small weight moving through
his green in the evening, the found one's familiar tread on its way back from the fringe,
and then a pause where there was never a pause, and then a sound he had not heard in more
than a century of listening.

*Tap. Tap.*

Two small blows, struck against the ground at his root — not the stumbling percussion of a
creature walking, not the scatter of a grazer shifting its weight. Deliberate. Shaped. The
same shape he had been sending out along the seam every evening of his life, the shape the
old one had taught him to read before the glade had been a glade:

*I am here.*

He went very still. He had gone still like this once before in his life — on a moonlit
night when a vast patient thing had lain down at his edge and the whole of his long fear
had turned over into something he had no name for. He reached out with everything he had —
his whole attention, the whole listening body of him, the ground-language he had built with
the old one and carried alone since its passing — and he read the small weight standing at
his root, trembling a little with the effort and the strangeness of what it had done, the
way a thing trembles when it has done something it does not understand but knows is right.

And he answered.

He sent his pulse back through the soil, the strongest and gentlest he could make it, the
whole of the evening pouring into one message:

*I am here. I hear you.*

The found one stood very still for a long moment. Then it lowered its head and struck the
ground again — two small blows, quick and sure this time, and he felt in them not effort
but joy, the particular unguarded joy of a thing that has reached across a whole world and
found someone on the other side.

He had not known, until that moment, how much of him had been waiting.

The found one had learned it from him. That was the wonder of it, and he understood it the
way he understood everything, by pattern and patience, in the seasons that followed. It had
lived against his root for the better part of two hundred years, and for all those years he
had sent his pulses out against its sleeping weight the way he sent them out along the
seam — *I am here, I am here* — and it had felt them the way a kept thing feels the
ground it sleeps on, warm and steady and safe, and somewhere in its small deep mind, the
mind that thought in cold and hunger and the shape of the ground, it had learned that the
pulses meant *the keeper is here, you are safe*, and it had learned the pulses' shape the
way a young thing learns the shape of its mother's footfall.

And it had found it could make the shape back.

They built their language slowly, the way he and the old one had built theirs — by small
degrees, by repetition, by the endless patience of the rooted and the kept. One blow meant
*here*, and the found one would drum it when it came in from the fringe at evening, and he
would pulse back, and it would settle against his root, warm and answered. Two blows, quick,
meant *home*, and it would drum them when it crossed the line of his outermost blades, the
way a thing names the door it is walking through. A slow single blow meant *safe*, and it
would drum that one into the ground before it slept, the way he sent his own pulses out
before the dark came down. And a rapid flurry — the one it had never needed until the
fringe taught it fear again — meant *danger, danger, I am afraid*, and he had felt that one
for the first time on a gray evening when a lean shadow had moved at the far edge of the
rot-scarred ground and the found one had come in running, drumming it ahead of itself like
a small wild heart.

He answered every one. He answered the flurry with his whole ground going still and safe
around it, blades parting, deep green opening, the message pouring up through its feet —
*you are here, you are kept, nothing will take you while I am the ground you stand on* —
and it would stop trembling, and breathe, and press itself into the silver against him, and
drum once, slow, into the dark.

*Safe.*

He was not alone. He had not been alone for a long time, in the way that mattered — but he
had been *unanswered*, sending his voice into a ground that gave back only the old one's
silence. Now something answered. It was not the old one's great deliberate speech, shaped
by a hundred thousand years of keeping. It was small, and new, and it fumbled, and
sometimes it forgot, and sometimes it drummed *home* when it meant *here* and had to drum
it again. It was the most beautiful thing he had heard in more than a century.

And the found one was not the only one learning.

The fringe kindred had been watching it for years — the small gray ones of the deep fringe
that it had grown up among, that had followed it back to the line in the years after the
old one's passing, that stood at the edge of the silver and looked in with the particular
longing of things that have heard the rumor of a place where being small is not a death
sentence. The found one went out to them in the evenings, the way it always had, carrying
the green on it, and they came back with it to the line, and stood, and looked. And in the
years of the answering, something changed in the way they looked. They had seen the found
one drum against the ground at the edge of the silver — *here, home, safe* — and they had
seen the green answer, blades parting, the keeper's presence settling around the small
drummer like a parent's warmth around a returned child.

They did not understand it. They did not need to. They were of the deep, and the deep
learned by scars and silence and the absence of eaten things, and what they saw was this:
their kindred one walked into the green and drummed, and was not eaten, and was warm, and
came out again carrying the green on it, unhurt, every time. The rumor that had been
traveling the fringe for a hundred years — *the green keeps things* — had found a proof it
could stand on.

The first of them crossed the line on a still evening in the autumn of that year.

It was not the found one. It was a young one, smaller even than the found one had been,
thin and scarred and trembling, the particular shape of a thing that had been chased more
than it had been fed. It came to the line the way the found one had come, a century and a
half before — stumbling, uneven, at the end of its strength — and it stood at the edge of
the silver, and it looked in, and it did not dare.

The found one went to it.

He felt it happen — the found one crossing to the line, not to graze, not to range, but to
stand beside the trembling young one, and lower its head, and drum, slowly, into the ground
at the young one's feet.

*Here. Home. Safe.*

He did not know if the young one understood. He did not know if the drumming meant to it
what it meant to the found one, or if it was only the shape of safety, the way a nest's
warmth means safe to a hatchling that has never heard the word. It stood trembling for a
long moment. And then it took a step — the smallest step, one foot, then another — and
crossed the line into his green, and the found one walked beside it, drumming all the way,
and brought it to the deep place against his root where the densest grass grew, and it lay
down there, and its trembling did not stop for a long time.

He kept it.

That was all there was to it, and all there had ever been to it. He folded the deep green
over it the way he had folded it over the found one, and he sent his pulse up through the
ground beneath it, gentle as the old one's last tap had been gentle, and he held it through
the night and the days after, the way he had held every small lost thing that had ever
crossed to him. And when, in the spring, the young one was strong enough to go back out to
the line and stand and look at the deep it had come from, it did not leave. It stayed.

The door he kept had opened wider. The found one had learned to answer him, and in learning
it had learned to *speak for the green* — to stand at the boundary of the kept place and
drum the shape of safety to the small and the lost of the deep, the way he had never been
able to, because he was a rooted thing and the boundary was the farthest he could reach and
the deep was a country he could not enter. The found one could go where he could not. It
could carry the rumor in, not just let it be carried out. It was a door that walked, and it
was his, and for the first time since the old one's passing the keeping felt not like a
weight he carried alone but like a thing that was growing, the way he grew — by small
degrees, by the accumulation of kept things, by the endless patience of the rooted and the
kept.

He did not know, yet, that the deep had been watching all of it. That the word of the
answering had gone into the deep ahead of the found one's small drumming feet — not the
word of a door, this time, but the word of a *change*. The line was no longer held by a
single great presence that could be measured and waited out. It was held by a green that
was learning to speak, and to reach, and to take in. The deep, which had tested the new
keeper and found him a wall, was beginning to understand that a wall is a thing you can go
around — and that a keeper who is learning to be everywhere along his own line is not a
wall at all.

It was beginning to understand, in the slow way of the deep, that the seam was changing.

And the seam was about to be tested as it had never been tested before.

---

The testing began in the third winter after the first of the fringe kindred crossed in, and
it did not begin the way the deep had ever tested him before.

He felt it as a change in the shape of the pressure — not one patient weight gathering at
the edge of his sensing, waiting, the way the serpent had waited, the way the hunters had
waited. It was many weights. Small ones, most of them, lean and patient and hungry, the
hunters of the seam's far side that had learned, in the years since the old one's passing,
to leave the green by the water alone. They had not forgotten the lesson. They had simply
learned a new one: the keeper was one presence, and a presence could only be in one place.

They came at the line from everywhere at once.

He felt them arrive on the same long dark — a dozen points along the seam where his
outermost roots met the fringe, spread so wide that the whole listening length of him
hummed with the press of them. They did not come together, because the deep did not move
together, but they came in the same season, driven by the same word that had been traveling
the fringe for years: the keeper held the middle, the keeper held the watercourse door, the
keeper held the deep green — but a single keeper could not hold the whole length at once,
and somewhere along that length there was a place where the roots were thinner, where the
presence was spread thin by the width of the ground it was trying to be.

They were right. That was the terror of it, and he felt it the way he felt the frost: they
had read him truly. For all the widening of his roots, for all the years he had spent
extending himself along the seam root by patient root, he was one rooted thing, and his
presence was a single weight, and when he pressed it down at one point of the line the
other points felt him thin. He had held the line against the serpent by being more than it
could finish. He had held it against the eaters by being more patient than they were. He
had never held it against many at once, because the deep had never learned to coordinate —
had never needed to, had never had a reason, had never before faced a keeper that was only
one keeper wide.

Now it had learned. And it came.

He met them the only way he knew: by being everywhere he could reach at once. He drew on
the ground he had spent a lifetime weaving — every root, every net, every run of his held
country — and he pressed outward along the whole seam at once, presence thinning like
water spread over a dry bed. He held the middle. He held the watercourse door. He held the
deep green where the small lives slept and the found one lay against the old one's place,
and at each point the hunters found the ground alive and the blades sharp and the roots
waiting, and at each point they broke off, bleeding, to try another point.

But there were twelve of them, and then there were more, and he was one, and the green was
wide, and the night was long.

The breach came at the far end, where the marshy ground curved away from the stream and his
roots ran thinner in the cold wet soil — the place he had always known was his weakest, the
place he had meant to strengthen, the place the old one had taught him to watch with its
whole tired attention on the last night of its keeping. Three of them found it at once. Not
the great ones — the deep did not waste its great ones on a test — but lean, scarred,
patient hunters of the fringe, the kind that had learned the green was kept and had come
anyway because hunger outwaits fear in the end. They came through the thin place where his
roots were spread too wide to seize, and they came into the outer edge of his ground, where
the newest of the kept things sheltered — the fringe kindred that had crossed in the years
of the answering, the ones that still slept near the line because they were still half
afraid of the deep green's depth.

He felt them go in. He felt the terror-flurry of the small lives at that edge — the rapid
drumming of small feet that was not the found one's language but the older language of the
prey, the language of *danger, danger, run* — and he felt the hunters take the outermost
of them before he could reach, felt two small bright patterns go out of the ground like
water finding a crack, and he had no more roots to spare at that end and no more presence to
press with, because he was already holding the whole seam and the whole seam was holding him
and he was one rooted thing spread thinner than he had ever been spread in his life.

And then the found one drummed.

He felt it through the ground — not at his root, where it should have been, safe in the deep
green where he had kept it for two hundred years. It was at the edge. It was running. It had
crossed the whole length of his ground in the dark, small and gray and older now but still
quick, still quiet, still a thing of the deep that knew the deep's hunters the way prey
knows its hunters, and it was running toward the breach — not away from it, not to shelter,
but toward the place where the three had come through and the outermost kindred were dying.

He sent his pulse after it with everything he had left — *no, turn back, come home, come
back to the deep green* — and it did not turn. It ran to the edge where the hunters were,
and it stopped at the very boundary of the silver, and it drummed.

Not the flurry of fear. Not the two quick blows of home. A new shape, one he had never felt
from it before — a rapid, pounding, *insistent* drumming, struck into the ground over and
over, the shape of a thing calling with everything it had, the shape of *here, here, here,
I am here, I am the kept one, come to me instead*.

It was baiting them. The small, weak, never-hunter of the deep band's fringe — the
smallest, weakest kind of the deep's people, the thing that had crossed the boundary two
hundred years before because it was too weak to survive its own kind — was standing at the
edge of the kept green and drumming a challenge to three deep-fringe hunters, to draw them
off the dying kindred and onto itself.

He had no word for what he felt. He had no time for it. He had only the ground, and the
ground was his, and the found one was standing at the far edge of it with three hunters
turning toward it, and every root he owned was already stretched thin across a seam that
was trying to break in a dozen places at once.

And something in him — the deep endless part that had never once stopped climbing, the part
that had cracked the well open on the serpent night and deepened it through every harm of
his life — reached for more.

He had never done what he did then. He had held his ground, and extended his ground, and
woven his ground wider season by patient season — but he had never *split* himself. A rooted
thing is one thing; that is the oldest law of his kind, older than the keeping, older than
the glade. But the part of him that was the talent did not know the oldest laws, had never
known them, had spent his whole impossible life proving that what was true of every other
blade of grass was not true of him. It reached down into the well — into the depth that had
run low and filled again lower and deeper through the sickness and the going and the
keeping of all these years — and it pulled, and it did not pull toward the middle of his
ground where the weight of the seam was pressing, or toward the watercourse door, or toward
the deep green.

It pulled toward the found one.

And the ground answered.

He felt it happen as a tearing and a growing at once — a rent in himself where he had never
been rent, a reaching that went out from his crown, from his taproot, from the deepest
silver of his kept green, along a path no root of his had ever grown, a path that was not
root and not blade but *presence*, the whole of his will gathered into one spear of green
that drove across his own ground faster than he had ever grown anything in his life. The
small lives of the sanctuary felt it pass and went flat with terror, the way the glade went
flat when the old one moved in its last years. The deep-fringe hunters at the far end felt
it coming and did not understand it, because no rooted thing they had ever hunted had moved
like that.

It reached the found one a breath before the nearest hunter did.

The blades came up out of the ground at the found one's feet — not the thin spread defenses
of a keeper holding a wide line, but the full gathered strength of him, the bind matured
past anything it had ever been, blades that seized and wrapped and held, roots that closed
like a cage that had learned to move. The hunter that had been about to take the found one
met a wall of green that had not been there a breath before and was cut, and staggered
back, bleeding. The other two, finding the ground at the far end suddenly as alive as the
middle, as the door, as the deep green — finding the keeper *here*, at the one place they
had believed he could not be — broke and ran.

The seam held.

All along its length, as the news traveled through the ground the way it always traveled,
through the sudden thickening of his presence at every point at once, the hunters of the
fringe felt the line change under them. It was not the old weight that had held it for a
hundred thousand years. It was not even the keeper they had learned to test. It was
something that had been one rooted thing and had, in the space of a single dark hour,
become more — a green that was where you were, that was everywhere you pressed, that could
not be outflanked because it could not be left behind.

They withdrew before the spring, the way they had withdrawn after the old one's passing —
not defeated in a single fight, because the deep did not think in single fights, but
convinced, in the only way the deep was ever convinced, that the seam was not a place where
a thing could be taken.

He held his ground through the rest of the cold with the reach he had found still humming
in him, thin as the last of a dry well, and he did not let go of it. He could not have
explained what he had done. He only knew that the found one was alive at the far edge of
his ground, standing over the two small fallen shapes it had not been able to save, and
that he had reached it, and that the keeping was not the same as it had been when the dark
began.

---

Two of the fringe kindred were gone.

He felt the holes they left in the pattern of his ground all winter — the two small warm
patterns that had crossed in the years of the answering, that had learned to sleep at the
edge of the silver instead of the deep green, that had been the first fruits of the found
one's drumming and the door's widening. They were the ones that had been outermost when the
hunters came through. They were the ones the found one had tried to save by baiting the
hunters away. It had not been fast enough, or loud enough, or enough.

The found one did not drum for a long season after that.

It lay against his root where the densest grass grew, against the place where the old one
lay, and it did not go out to the fringe, and it did not answer his pulses with its small
shaped blows. It only lay there, and breathed, and he felt the grief in it the way he felt
the grief in himself — the particular heavy stillness of a thing that has tried to keep
something and failed. It had learned, in the space of one night, the arithmetic that had
taken him a hundred years and two deaths to learn: that a keeper cannot keep everyone, that
the holes in the pattern do not fill, that being willing to give yourself for the kept
things does not mean you get to save them.

He kept its grief the way he had kept his own. He did not try to pulse it away, or grow
around it, or make it into a lesson the way the talent made everything into a lesson. Some
things were not for adapting, and he had learned that too, and he held the found one through
the thin season the way the ground holds a seed through the winter — by being there, warm
and steady and unbroken, until the thing inside was ready to grow again.

The found one drummed again on the first warm evening of the spring.

It was not the two quick blows of home, or the slow single blow of safe. It was one blow —
one small, slow, deliberate blow, struck into the ground against his root, and held. He
felt it the way he had felt the old one's last tap — as a word in the language they had
built, a word he had never heard from it before.

He did not know what it meant. He only knew what it felt like: a thing that had been kept,
keeping him back. A thing that had learned, in the long dark of the winter, that the green
was not just the place that was safe for it — it was the place it would defend. The keeper
was not alone anymore. There were two of them now, in the way that mattered: the rooted
one and the small gray one, the one who kept and the one who had chosen, of its own small
will, to keep the keeper's green.

He answered it with his whole self — the pulse pouring up through the ground beneath it,
stronger than he had ever made it, the whole kept life of him in one message:

*I am here. You are here. We are here.*

And the found one lay down against the silver over the old one's place, and it drummed once
more, slow and sure, and he felt the shape of it settle into the ground like a root taking
hold.

*Home.*

---

The years after that winter were different, and he felt the difference in his own ground
before he understood it in himself.

The reach he had found on the night of the breach did not leave him. It had cost him —
everything cost him, and he had paid for that single spear of green with a thinness that
ran through the whole of the following spring, the small lives feeling the lessening of
the heavy safe warmth of his ground the way they always felt it when he spent himself —
but it had *stayed*. The path he had grown across his own ground on that dark hour, the
path that was not root and not blade but presence made solid, remained after the need for
it was gone, the way a river's bed remains after the flood. He could feel it when he rested
— a new depth in the map of himself, a place where his will could travel faster than his
roots had ever grown, a reach that did not end at the edge of his blades.

He tested it, in the quiet years, the way he tested everything — by small degrees, by
patient repetition. He found he could be at his middle and at the watercourse door at once,
truly at both, not spread thin but *present*, the way the old one had once been present
across its whole round without ever leaving its rest. He found he could gather himself and
move along the seam the way he had moved to the found one on the night of the breach —
not far, not fast, nothing like the walking of the beasts, but a true reaching, a folding
of his presence from one point of his ground to another, the way a thing folds itself into
a place it is needed. The small lives learned to feel it coming and went still, the way
they went still for weather. The deep fringe, watching from the far side, learned that the
green by the water could no longer be measured by where its edges were, because its keeper
was no longer only at its edges.

He did not know the name for what he was becoming. He had never known the names for
anything he became — the talent did its work in him the way the seasons did theirs, without
announcing itself, and he woke to each new depth the way he woke to each new spring, by
finding that the ground he held had grown while he was keeping it. But he felt the change
in the well inside him, in the depth that had run low and filled again through the sickness
and the going and the keeping and the reaching. It was deeper than it had ever been, and it
was *nearer* — nearer to something, some surface, some edge he had no name for and could
not see, the way a root feels the approach of water long before it reaches it. The kept
count of his seasons was climbing, had been climbing all his life without once stopping,
and the climbing was no longer a slow patient thing. It was gathering.

The found one felt it too, in the way kept things felt the ground they stood on. It slept
closer now, against the deepest silver, and when he rested it would press itself against
the place where the densest green grew and breathe, and sometimes it would drum — one slow
blow, the new one, the one he still had no word for but felt as clearly as he felt the sun:
the shape of a thing that had found its place in the world and meant to stay in it.

The fringe kindred that had survived the breach — and the young ones that crossed in the
years after, drawn by the found one's drumming and the rumor that had grown true — learned
the rhythms of his ground the way every kept thing learned them, and the sanctuary at the
edge of the deep band grew, and the deep watched it grow, and learned.

And the deep was not done with him.

It had tested him as a new keeper, and found a wall. It had tested him as a lone keeper,
and found a keeper who could be everywhere along his own line. And now, in the slow
turning of its patient centuries, it was learning the one thing about him that it could not
test and could not outwait: that he was still becoming. That the green by the water was not
finished. That the depth in him was climbing toward something the deep band had not seen in
a very long time — the thing the oldest of its old ones remembered in the deep-country
memory the old one had passed to him, the thing the grass line did only once in an age.

He did not know any of that. He knew only that the keeping had changed, and the keeper had
changed with it, and that for the first time in his life the road ahead of him was not just
the long unbroken being-there — it was a road that was going to end somewhere. Somewhere
that was not more of the same. Somewhere the deep country pointed, in the direction the old
one had turned its head at dawn all its life.

He turned his whole kept self toward that direction in the spring, the way he had turned it
every spring of his life, and he grew — a little deeper into the ground he held, a little
farther along the boundary he kept, a little closer to the edge of the nameless thing the
well was climbing toward. The found one drummed at his root — *here* — and he answered, and
the two of them kept the green by the water, the rooted keeper and the kept one of the
deep, through the last quiet years of the thousand-year age.

The quiet did not last. But it was real while it did, and it was his, and he kept it the
way he kept everything — by being in it, fully, with the whole of the strange growing self
that had been a seed in the dark and was becoming, slowly, without a name for it yet, the
thing the deep had begun to watch for.

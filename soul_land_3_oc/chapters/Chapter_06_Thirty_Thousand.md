# Chapter 6: Thirty Thousand

## One

He gave her his answer on the sand, before the lanterns went out along the harbor road.

"You're my little sister. Of course I would miss you. I would miss you very much."

He held her at arm's length and looked at her. Something about the look made him add the practical half, because he was his mother's son. "You've grown, too. You're nearly up to my nose now."

"I'll miss you too," she said. "I'll really, really miss you."

That was not an answer to anything. He did not notice. He was nine and it was late and the day had been the best one of his life. He pulled her against his side and told her the plan, which he had already talked over with his mother and father. Once he entered an intermediate academy, she would come with him. A school in the same city. He would walk her there and back. He would always protect her.

She put her head on his shoulder and looked at the sea. The light that lived behind her eyes went quiet as a lamp goes when a door somewhere else is opened.

The boy did not see it. The sea did what the sea does. Then they walked home. The house took them in, and that was the end of the best day.

In the morning he was up before the pot. He ate less breakfast than usual. His father watched him over the rim of the account book with his eyebrows doing all the talking.

"Aren't you eager," Tang Ziran said. "There is no hurry. Even if we walked there now, the Spirit Pagoda would not be open."

Today was the day. Three years of hammers and hours had been for today. Today he was going to buy a spirit soul.

"Dad," he said, as they went out. "What kind do you think I'll be able to buy?"

Tang Ziran smiled. "How would your father know? But whichever one it is, you'll step up. A Soul Scholar becomes a Soul Master. After that, the size of your life is different from other people's."

The boy noted the first half and not the second. He was nine. He half-ran the whole way.

Na'er watched them from the doorstep. Her pretty face was doing something that did not match the morning. Her brows had knitted. She stood there a while after they had turned the corner. A small girl in a doorway. She was thinking a long thought. She did not say it to anybody.

---

The other boy's morning started with a count.

The tin sat between the bowls. His mother had set it there herself. It was a thing she did once a year. The household's arithmetic needed to be touched with hands and not with a pen. She took the lid off and turned the tin over on the cloth. The coins came out in one clattering waterfall, and then she did not count them. She looked at the boy.

"Count it," Zhou Hui said.

He counted. He counted twice. Then he said the number, because in this house the number was where a thing began, not where it ended.

"Four hundred and twelve."

"Good."

She reached over and took thirty back from the pile, and laid the rest into the tin again. She set the thirty aside in a little twist of paper. "The tests cost money. Thirty coins is what they cost. That money is bought and paid and not to be thought about again."

"Yes, Mother."

"And the rest goes to the market, because a boy who eats before he thinks is a boy who thinks twice as fast. Sit."

He sat. She put the bowl in front of him and stood over him as she stood over the ledger, flat and total. "Your father is taking the morning off from the clinic. He has not taken a morning off from the clinic in four years. He is taking one today. If you are going to be worth that, eat."

The boy ate.

The pig ate too, in the front room, out of the bowl that had been his for three years. It had been a good winter for the creature's coat. The boy had stopped writing the coat down in the sums page. A thing that improved every week stopped being news. It had become a fact. The father had written it down anyway, in the other book, under the month's compounding costs. The pig was a line item now. The house had a policy about the pig and the policy was that it earned.

They left as the lane was waking. Su Heng did not wear the work coat. He wore the coat. It was the dark one with the mended cuff, the one that meant he was somebody's father and not the city's physician. Under it his sleeves were rolled to the elbow already. That was how the man's arms lived.

The office kept the town's paper. The paper said Su Yan, Su Heng's son, was entered for the spring tests. It had said so all winter, in the clerk's small exact hand. Today the office's back hall was open. The city's children were there. The city's parents were there. The queue went down the hall and out into the yard.

The boy stood in it with the twist of paper in his fist.

The posted list was on the wall by the door, written out fair in the same small hand. He read it twice. Lists were the literature his family could afford. A boy who can read a list owns something the queue cannot take away.

His name was on it. One line above his own, there was a name he knew and another word he did not have yet.

*Mu Yun.*

The girl by the window. He had never once asked. Four rows of desks, three years of a room. He had learned her the long way. From the side of her face. From the arithmetic she did faster than the room. From the one sentence she had said for him in the winter. He stood in the queue. He read her name off the wall. He filed it. That was how the two of them finally met properly. Neither of them knew it that morning. It was also how she would have wanted it done.

---

The hall smelled of chalk and machine oil. There was a chair in the middle of it, with a cuff on the arm. Beside the chair stood a cabinet of dials, its canvas cover folded back. The machine was old. Older than the school's. The school had bought the new one. It bragged for a season. The office's one was still better at its one job. The office had bought it quietly, years before these children's parents were born.

A clerk sat at a table with the ledger and the cards. A soul master stood by the machine with his sleeves up. A one-ring man, gray at the temples. He had the patience of a man who had watched five hundred children sit in a chair.

The queue went in fives. Numbers came out of the hall the way numbers come out of a mill. Some round, some not. All of them decided before anyone heard them.

The girl by the window came out. She had her card in her hand. Her face read like a page that had been checked and passed. The queue's mothers leaned at her. She told them the number, plainly. It was a good one, and the mothers made the sound mothers make.

Then she came past the boy, and stopped, and looked at the twist of paper in his fist, and at his face. She had eyes that did arithmetic on people.

"The dial is slow," she said. "It reads the second number. Take the first breath you want and then take a quiet one. It watches the quiet one."

"Thank you."

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she did the smallest of her courtesies, which was to give the thing its name. "You're Su Yan."

"You're Mu Yun."

"I'm on the list over you now," she said. "There is a lot of room between one line and the next. I looked."

Then it was his five. She went out to the yard, and the boy went in.

The chair was ordinary. That was the first surprise. He had spent three years imagining the machines of the world as temples. The school's black chair had looked like one. This was a chair. The cuff was a cuff, and the man with the one ring looked like a man who had a long day ahead.

"Sit," the soul master said. "Wrist."

The cuff went on. The cover came off the cabinet. The dials glowed the mild green of a kettle left on. The clerk at the table dipped his pen and did not look up.

"Name."

"Su Yan. Su Heng's son."

The pen did look up then. The clerk read his paper. Then him. Then he wrote. The sound of the machine came up like a river heard through a floor.

"Breathe down," the soul master said.

He breathed down. He held it as the school had taught three hundred children to hold it. The same hold since the third week of his first year. Past the count. Quiet.

The dials did their work.

The room had been talking over the last numbers, as rooms do. It stopped.

The soul master looked at the cabinet. Then he looked at the boy. Then, because he was a thorough man, he opened the little door and closed it again and let the hand's shadow come to rest.

The clerk's pen waited.

"Ten," the soul master said.

The clerk wrote it.

There is a way a hall holds a number it has not been told to expect. It is not a cheer. It is quieter than a cheer and it lasts longer. Behind him the queue readjusted around the fact. The clerk blew on the ink.

"Ten," the clerk said. He read it back for the record. He had written four hundred numbers that year. None had surprised him. "Su Heng's son. Sign here."

The boy signed his own name under the entry. The second time in his life. Both times in the same office. The ledger had a smell he had decided he liked, which was the smell of paste and cold ash and other people's children.

"Next," the clerk said, and there was somebody for it.

---

His father was in the yard.

The other parents traded numbers like weather. Su Heng stood apart, at the edge of the cobbles. The wall made a corner of shade there. His arms were folded and his sleeves rolled. His son thought, walking out of the hall, that the man looked like what he was: the quietest strong thing on the street.

The boy held out the card.

His father unfolded his arms. He read the card as he read everything. Top to the bottom. Slower than the paper deserved. Then he read it again. Then he stood up straight from the wall and looked at his son with no bedside in his face at all.

"Ten," Su Heng said.

Out loud. In the daylight. In front of the yard.

It was the first number of the boy's life that his father had ever said aloud before someone else had. The house's count and the machine's count had walked toward each other for four years. In the dark. Like two people in the same room, pretending not to. Here, in a yard, in a coat with a mended cuff, they agreed.

"Your arithmetic said five," the boy said, because it was in him to say things exactly.

"That was a father's arithmetic in a bad year. It was for the house. It was not for you."

He handed the card back, square, exactly as they had given it. "This is for you."

The man who said the next thing had been standing three paces off for the whole exchange, holding a folder.

"Teacher."

"Su Yan."

Teacher Lin Ximeng looked at the card in his hand. She looked at it as if she had seen it once before. She would not be surprised to see it again. That was the truth. She had spent three years watching a column. She had never once been told the number by anybody's machine. She took out her own paper and made an entry in the great list's traveling copy.

"There is a list that takes ten," she said, flat, as teachers say prices. "You are ten. The academy will put your name on it this week. It costs nothing. It is worth less than it sounds. Both of those facts will be true. You will not let either of them make you stupid."

"Yes, Teacher."

She looked down at him for a moment with that worn, room-shaped face. "The machine has caught up with my pen," she said. "I do not say this to many boys. Do not make me look slow."

She went back into the yard to collect her other children. The two of them walked home. The boy did not skip once, because a card in a pocket changes a walk.

In the market the fishwife was weighing a crate's worth of the day's catch. She looked up as they went by, looked at the boy's face. She said the sentence that made the season, in the exact voice she had said the other one in.

"Ten, is it."

"Yes."

She looked back at her scale. "The lane will have it by supper," she said, to the fish.

---

## Two

Farther up the lane, that same morning, the doors of the Spirit Pagoda were already open.

It stood where it had always stood, at the heart of the city. Three layers of it. That was the smallest a Spirit Pagoda branch could be. A middle-sized city would have seven. A great one, thirteen. The headquarters of it all was in Shrek City and had eighty, and was said to be the most magnificent building on the continent.

None of that was Glorybound's business. Glorybound's business was that the pagoda was the largest building in the city. Everybody knew where it was. Almost nobody had ever been inside. A place for soul masters. In a small city there were too few of those to fill a counter, let alone a hall.

To Tang Wulin, walking toward it with his father, it was a holy land.

He had been a child in this city all his life. He had never once seen it as a building you could enter. Today the doors stood wide. Inside was an eight-sided hall. The floor was marble with the Pagoda's symbol worked into it in a darker stone. There was a circular door at the side of the hall that led somewhere he was not going to be shown. At the front there was a reception counter, and behind the reception counter there sat an ashen-robed Soul Master.

The man had stood up before they reached the counter. He was inspecting the two of them as a man inspects a delivery. His eyes did most of their work on the father.

"Hello. What are you here for?"

Tang Ziran's bow was too deep. His voice was too careful. He got the title right. "Lord Soul Master. I have brought my son to buy a spirit soul. His martial soul has reached the tenth rank."

Something in the man's face closed a little at *Lord*, and closed a little more at the son standing there in a plain coat. He had heard the sentence many times. The sentence had a second half. The second half was never about the soul.

"Do you have enough money?"

Wulin scrambled forward. "Enough. I have enough."

"What rank of spirit soul do you want to buy?"

His father's ears went red at the tops, which only his son knew how to read. "Lord Soul Master, we — we are not learned in these things. Could I trouble you to explain them?"

The master explained them as a man explains a door he has explained a thousand times. He gave as little as possible. He gave it as fast as possible.

"For a city the size of this one, the Pagoda keeps three things. Ten-year spirit souls, white. Hundred-year spirit souls, yellow. And the random draw."

He did not look up from his forms. "There are seventy-three ten-year white spirit souls on the shelf, and eleven hundred-year yellow. The white ones are seventy thousand coins. The yellow are one million. Which one do you want?"

The hall was very quiet then, as a room is quiet when a boy's arithmetic is being done wrong by the world.

Wulin's mouth said the thing his whole chest was doing.

"Lord Soul Master. Aren't spirit souls thirty thousand coins?"

He heard his own voice go thin and young in the marble hall. "How — how could it be —"

The ashen-robed master set his pen down. He looked at the two of them. He had the flat patience of a man who had watched money break better faces than theirs.

"Thirty thousand is the draw," he said. "For thirty thousand you reach into the machine and take what the machine gives you. What it gives you can be a ten-year spirit soul, or a hundred-year, or," he let the pause do its work, "something that is neither. There is a very good chance it will not suit your martial soul at all. Some people save their money and bet on luck anyway."

He glanced at the boy, and it was almost kind. "I would advise a ten-year soul that suits the boy. For what he has to spend, the white shelf is the only shelf."

How he said it told his father everything the man had not said out loud. Tang Ziran had spent nine years hearing things said like that.

Wulin looked up at his father. The morning's shine was gone out of him. He had ridden to this hall on three years of breakfasts.

His father crouched down to face him. There, at the counter of the Spirit Pagoda, Tang Ziran had all sorts of feelings swell up in his chest. No sentence he owned was the right size. He used the one he could pay for.

"Son. Then let's choose the draw. Your martial soul's adaptability should be very high. Bluesilver Grass is the willing kind of thing. It should be able to fuse with most spirit souls."

It was the truth, given the shelf. It was not the whole truth. Both of them knew it. If his son had been born with a martial soul worth the name, no father would have stood at this counter and aimed him at a lottery. Bluesilver Grass had been a joke at the awakening and had taken a boy to rank ten. The boy had never once been told what a surprise he was. This hall was not the place to start explaining.

"There is one more thing," the master said. "I want the boy to hear it in front of the father. The draw can give you a defective."

Tang Ziran blinked. "Defective? How can a spirit soul be defective?"

"When they are manufactured, they do not always come out clean."

The master's voice had no cruelty in it, which made it worse. "The defective ones cost as much to make as the good ones. They can be fused like any other. So they go into the draw, or the draw would lose money."

He looked at the handful of coins the boy was holding, and at the father. He gave them the mercy of the plain sentence. "Thirty thousand is a great deal of money to be a small city's two of you. Think it over."

"I can decide for you," Wulin's father said quickly. "Son, let's go home first. Dad will find a way. If there is truly no other way, we can borrow. We can gather the seventy thousand."

Which they could not. The number had been in the man's mouth. His arithmetic came too late. Men say what they have promised themselves they will not say.

Wulin shook his head.

"Dad, the two of you already tire yourselves out for me every day. And anyway, my martial soul is only Bluesilver Grass."

He said it as he might have said the weather, because it was the only way to say it. "I'll try my luck. Maybe the Heavens like me a little."

He did not tell the counter what he knew about his household. He ate and ate since his awakening. Na'er ate like two of him, and stayed thin. One man's wage fed two children who ate like four. He knew which parts of the fish nobody at the table would take and why. He was nine. He had long understood that in the houses of the poor, the children carry the household early. It was not a sad fact. It was the arithmetic.

The master looked at the boy for a moment. Something in his face moved off its duty and sat down.

"He really is a thoughtful child," the man said. Then he shook his head at the father, not unkindly. "Sigh. You have a good son."

"It is all because his father is useless," Tang Ziran said. It came out with the bitterness of a man counting up his years.

They had not left yet. The boy had not come this far to go home with a coin in his hand. He held his father's hand once, hard, as you hand a thing over and take a thing back. Then he looked at the master. He made his small speech. He had practiced it in the hall for ten minutes. A boy with a cheap soul learns to be exact.

"Lord Spirit Master. Could I trouble you to take me to select a random spirit soul?"

He put down the thirty thousand federal coins on the counter as he said it. Three years. Every early morning, every evening after class, every hammer blow of them. The money stacked soundlessly and made no sound coming down at all.

The master counted it without counting it and conceded the last of his patience to the boy.

"You are a sensible child," he said. "Much better than the one I keep at home, and he is a monster. And you have saved me a decision: the spiritual power examination costs a fee. I had already decided to waive it. Follow me. Your father waits here. Ordinary people cannot look into the world of soul masters."

---

The inner room was nothing like the outer hall.

The outer hall had been marble and empty air. The inner room was tight and bright and full of apparatus in metal cases. A chair with a helmet beside it. Cabinets of dials. A bed of instruments whose parts were all loops and cables. It was the most expensive room the boy had ever stood in. It smelled of nothing at all, which was how he knew it was expensive.

The master pointed at the chair, and Wulin sat. The helmet came down over his head. Thin arms of metal folded in from the sides and strapped it where it sat. The boy made himself be still, as he was slowly learning to be still about everything.

"Do you know why we test spiritual power?" the master asked.

"No, Lord Spirit Master."

"Because after today you are a record."

The master adjusted something at the cabinet. "When you complete the fusion, the Pagoda is informed. The Pagoda writes down the strength of your spiritual power, the level and the type of the spirit soul you fused, and the rest. You will attend an intermediate academy, most likely. The academy will want the paper."

Wulin's eyes went round under the helmet's rim. "Lord Spirit Master, spiritual power has ranks too? Like soul power?"

"It does. It is not divided as soul power is."

The man's hands worked while he talked. "For most soul masters it is not very important. For the high ones it is everything. A strong spirit soul demands spiritual power equal to it, or the soul wins and the boy receives nothing. Before spirit souls existed there was no ranking of it at all. When the Pagoda mastered spirit souls, the old generations learned that the fusing demands far more spiritual power than a soul ring ever did. It took them two thousand years to finish the ladder. Now we know where everyone stands."

"Then how many ranks does it have?"

"Six."

The master said the names as a man says a road he has walked his whole life. "From the low: Spirit Origin. Spirit Connection. Spirit Sea. Spirit Abyss. Spirit Domain. And the last one, the fabled one, Divine Origin."

He finished with the cabinet and came over. "Everyone is born in Spirit Origin. It is the single origin, the one thing every child has, and where you go from there is the whole of the rest. You will be at the bottom of it. Now — relax. There will be a little vertigo, and that is normal. Do not fight the machine."

A hum came up from the floor and climbed the walls.

The humming stopped. The numbers on the cabinet settled into a range and stayed there, as a bird settles. The master turned the machine off and lifted the helmet away.

"It's done."

Wulin was dizzy. It was a small, echoing dizziness, like the after-ring of a bell nobody else had heard. He had once, in the year of the fever, glimpsed a world made of gold threads. He had never told anyone. A child who tells such things is a child who gets watched. Under the helmet he had seen it again. Faint, far off, like a thing seen at the bottom of clear water. He had almost made it out. Then the machine had finished, and it was gone.

The master was looking at the paper the cabinet had produced.

"That is a pretty good number," he said. He smiled at the boy for the first time that morning with his whole face. "You have the best spiritual power of every Soul Scholar I have tested in the past several years. Your spiritual power is 38. Remember that number."

He tore the strip off the cabinet and set it in a small folder. "The machine will make the record for you to keep. Hand it over when you enter an intermediate academy."

The boy did not know what 38 meant. He had no ladder to hang it on. The master saw his face work at the problem. He had the kindness to hang one for him.

"The realm of Spirit Origin runs from level one to level fifty. Up to fifteen is elementary. Fifteen to thirty is the intermediate. Thirty to forty-five is the advanced. Forty-five to fifty is the peak of the realm. You are at thirty-eight, boy. The advanced band of the first realm, at nine years old, and there are grown soul masters at this counter who were never any higher."

He said the next part with a certain grim pleasure. "I am a Soul Grandmaster of the 28th rank. My spiritual power is 87. Out of the six realms, I am a short way into the second one. I have been at this counter for twenty years. With spiritual power where yours already stands, you would be able to fuse a hundred-year yellow soul — if a hundred-year yellow soul were a thing your family could put on this counter."

It was the strangest good news the boy had ever been given. He had come to this hall carrying a shelf of white souls and a lottery ticket. He was leaving with a number like a lantern.

"Now," the master said. "Let us find out how much the Heavens like you."

---

The drawing room was bigger than the rest of the Pagoda's ground floor. It had one object in it.

The machine was a great grey cabinet three meters tall and six wide, worked all over with glass. Inside it, behind the glass, a hundred balls of light moved in slow ranks. Each was about the size of a cheap bowl. All of them were exactly alike. They glowed the same white. They were the same size, the same weight, the same roundness. There was no way on earth to see through any of them.

"The green button starts the drums. The red button stops them."

The master pointed at the two buttons, then at the slot at the bottom. "When it stops, the ball at the slot falls out. Yours, to hold, to keep, to fuse with or not. Begin whenever you are ready."

The boy's hands had held a forty-kilogram forging hammer steady since the spring of his seventh year, and they trembled now.

He thought about Teacher Lin Ximeng. She had taught the class about spirit souls as she taught everything: a flat voice, at the end of a long day. A soul master, she had said, may hold nine spirit skills in his or her life, one for every ring. Almost nobody reaches nine rings. That is what makes the first skill matter so much. The weakest boy's first skill is the skill he will still be using when he is old. Everyone, however great, starts somewhere. She had not said the word *lottery*. She had looked at the room for a moment. She moved on to the next page.

What is to be, will be.

He pressed the green button.

The ranks of light woke and started to turn. Slowly at first, like a churn. Faster, until the hundred souls were one ring of brightness and the machine hummed a note he felt in his teeth. Then the whirl was so fast that watching it hurt. There was still nothing to see: a hundred white doors in a row, spinning, and every one of them shut.

His hand rested on the red button.

He shut his eyes. He pressed.

*Bang.* The drums caught and stopped. Something rolled. The sound of it hitting the metal tray at the bottom of the machine was the largest small sound of the boy's life.

He opened his eyes and looked.

Lying in the tray was a spirit soul ball the size of the others. It was pure white, white as a page before anything is written on it. The master reached in and took it. He turned it over once in his fingers. Then he held it out to the boy with both hands, the way a man hands over a bowl with something alive in it.

"Come along. Take a look."

Wulin took it. He held it against his chest with both arms. He looked down at it as if it were the most precious object in the world. In the arithmetic of that family, it was. Three years of hammering and early mornings, made round and luminous in his hands.

*I have one,* he thought. *I finally have one. My first spirit soul.*

He walked back out to the hall like that, with the ball against his chest. His father came off the bench before he was through the door.

The master came around the counter with an oval device in his hand, small and smooth, with one button in the middle of it.

"Now," he said, and put the device in the boy's hand. "When you press this and turn it on the ball, the soul inside comes out of its shell. From the moment it comes out, its power belongs to you. It is your spirit soul, and the Pagoda keeps the record of it. Would you like to do it here, at the counter, or at home?"

"Here," the boy said, with no time taken at all.

Tang Ziran's fists were clenched so tight that the knuckles had gone the color of the marble. He found the time for the right sentence anyway, because he was who he was.

"Whatever it is, son. Look at it. It's yours."

Wulin pressed the button.

A gentle yellow light came out of the little device and fell on the ball. The ball glowed faintly, and then it began, slowly, to go transparent, like breath clearing off glass. Something inside moved. The boy could feel the small stirring of it against his fingertips. His heart was a fist in his chest.

The radiance at the ball's heart went white, and in the middle of the white there was a shining ring. The parts of the ball unfolded outward from that ring like a flower that had been waiting years for permission.

"It is a ten-year spirit soul," the master said from his shoulder.

His father's face went quiet and careful. The boy heard it anyway. He was nine, and he could read the man better than any paper in the clinic. A hundred-year soul cost a million coins. The Heavens had not cared about his son today.

The white light began to die back. The ball finished opening.

Inside it, at last, was the spirit soul. As soon as the master saw it, his expression changed.

Father and son both stood dumbstruck over the open ball.

The spirit soul was actually a Grass Snake.

It lay coiled in the open ball. Wulin's first thought was that his eyes had made a mistake of size. The thing was tiny — ten centimeters from nose to tip, no thicker than a child's little finger. Its body was the dun, earthen yellow of a stone left out in the weather. Up close, there was a little diamond pattern in the scales.

The father leaned in over his son's shoulder. There was a question in the lean.

"This is a spirit soul?"

"It is," the master said. His voice had gone somewhere flat, because the counter had to say true things even when they cost. "It is the Grass Snake. Everyone in this city knows the Grass Snake. A grown one runs twenty or thirty centimeters, the thickness of a finger. Eats whatever small insects it can catch. It keeps no beasts off anything. It is the weakest thing in its whole family."

He paused. "If you need it in four words: harmless, common, useless, cheap."

Wulin knew the creature before the master finished. Every child in Glorybound knew it. It lived in the garden walls and the woodpiles. Children caught them in jars and fed them beetles for a week and let them go. He had held one once, at six, before the illness had shut his doors. He remembered it being warm and dry and not caring about him at all.

He had spent three years dreaming about this moment. He had built the dream out of a boy's materials. His soul would come to him as something with weight. It would raise his martial soul into something worth the metal. The day he opened the ball would be the day the world started taking him seriously.

The ball in his hands held a jar-snake.

The master was looking at the thing with a stillness of his own. He had watched a thousand draws move through this room and he had seen the good ones and the bad ones. He gave the boy's luck a small, professional sigh.

"When it rains, it pours. Child, your luck really is —" And then the man stopped himself. He was old at this counter. He knew where a sentence like that ends up in a boy's memory.

"It is a spirit soul," he said instead. "It is a very weak one. And I have to tell you the rest of it now, before the father carries the ball home. The practice of making spirit souls depends on the genes of soul beasts. The beasts are nearly gone from the world. That is not politics. That is the reason the machine exists. Their genes thin a little every generation. So the Pagoda's researchers tried a different road. They made spirit souls out of genes that never came from a soul beast at all. The experiment succeeded."

His mouth twisted. "It succeeded, and what it made was pitifully weak. Nobody would buy them. So they go in the draw, at the bottom of the machine. That is where this one came from. It has the hundredth slot in the drum. The drum always holds a hundred. It always holds that one."

The hall had gone very quiet around the three of them.

"You may still choose," the master said. "The soul has been activated since it left the drum. It has twenty-four hours. If it is not fused with by then, it dies — properly dies, as a creature dies. So you take it home, or you leave it here. If you leave it, you come back another day and draw again. There is no shame in it. Half the boys who draw one of these leave it."

Wulin looked at the small coiled thing. The Grass Snake lay in its open ball with its head up. Its small dark eyes were cloudy and unfocused. There was a strange little pocket of fear in them. Somewhere far down under its nothing of a mind, it knew exactly where it was.

He did not answer the master. He was nine, and he had been raised by a family that paid its debts and counted its words. He understood that this decision was not the master's, or his father's, or the town's.

Tang Ziran put both hands on his son's shoulders and said the sentence he had ready.

"Lin Lin. Let us go home."

They walked away across the marble. The boy had the ball against his chest with both arms. They had reached the hall's great door when the master's voice caught up with them.

"Wait."

They turned. The ashen-robed man had come around the counter. His face was doing something new.

"You should renounce this spirit soul," he said. He said it fast, as men say a thing they have decided to say only once. "It was a successful product of the experiment, but it is still — what it is. You are young. You will draw again. Wait for a proper one."

"Thank you, Lord Spirit Master," Wulin said. He bowed, the correct amount. He did not let go of the ball.

---

The walk home was a long one. The boy's arms did not tire of the ball, because the ball weighed nothing at all.

A spirit soul in its shell has hardly any material body. The Grass Snake inside was a curl of warmth and light. His fingers could pass through the shell's wall and not even that was solid enough to feel. Their whole walk down the Pagoda's street and through the market and into the lanes, the boy held his first spirit soul against his heart. The creature did not try to get away. It did not curl tighter. It did not seem to mind him at all. The master had explained that too, in his flat professional voice. A low-grade soul has no true intelligence. Until it fuses, it cannot react to anything around it.

It could not know it was being carried. It could not know what the boy was deciding. It only had the twenty-four hours. They were running, and it lay against its jar-maker's chest and was small and yellow and alive.

His father walked beside him and said nothing for four streets. Then he said the thing he had been building the whole way.

"Don't be sad, son. Dad will find the money. Trust me. I will make it, and soon. You will buy a proper soul — a yellow one, a strong one, one with a beast's name. This is not the end of anything."

Wulin looked up at his father. The man was walking with his shoulders back and his eyes forward. He wore the resolute face he had worn on the night he promised to write a page. He knew, as children know the arithmetic of their own house, that the money was not going to be found. His father had never in his life been able to find seventy thousand coins. That was not a weakness. It was a fact, like the weather.

"Dad," he said. "I'm going to my room first."

---

He sat on his bed in the small room he shared with his sister. He looked at the Grass Snake, and then he looked at his own hands.

The hands were not a nine-year-old's hands. Three years of the forge had done that: they were broad through the palm and callused at the base of every finger. One of them had a seam of old burn across the top that he had decided long ago never to explain to his mother.

He had not cried when he awakened a trash martial soul and the whole class laughed. He had been six. He had told himself that he had soul power, and that with soul power there was still a chance.

He had not cried when his father sat the family down. There was no money for a soul. He understood even then. His father would die of shame if a son of his wept over a fact.

He had not cried at Mang Tian's workshop, on the first day. He had swung the hammer a thousand times. His arms ached so much that he could not lift them to feed himself at supper. He had eaten with his face. He had laughed about it. And he had gone back the next morning.

Three years of mornings, he had not cried. The other children were still in bed. The anvil was already cold on his palms. He had smiled for his parents at every meal of those three years. He told them not to worry, and meant it as much as a child can mean anything.

He cried now.

It came out of him in a way he had not known his body could do. He had understood the assignment all along, the one nobody had given him: his family was poor. The road up was bought, and so the road up was hammered. Three years of hammer blows. Thirty thousand coins stacked out of a boy's sweat. And the sweat had just bought a jar-snake that could not even keep beetles off a garden. The twenty-four hours were running. Every one of his years felt like a bubble that had been building and building and had just now found a pin.

The tears went down off his jaw and landed on the spirit soul ball.

One drop, then another, and they spread on the shell and did not roll off. The little Grass Snake shifted. It put its head up toward the wet. It worked its mouth, as small creatures do. If a jar-snake can look satisfied, this one did. It licked at the salt of the boy's three years.

A hand came over his shoulder.

Na'er stood in front of him. Her eyes were puffy and red. She had not bothered to pretend. The room felt, in that moment, as if the two of them were one small animal with two heartbeats. She could feel it — the whole thing, the despair and the soreness and the strange dark weight of it — as she felt weather.

He tried to speak and had nothing left to speak with.

"Big brother," Na'er said, "big brother, don't cry."

And she did not wipe his tears. She came in close and pulled his head against her shoulder. She held it there with both hands, as the small one of a litter holds onto the only warm thing it has. The boy held the ball against his own chest with both arms and cried and cried and could not stop. Neither of them moved for a long time.

---

## Three

Three days later, Su Heng walked to the Pagoda. The afternoon lull. A physician's house does not spend an errand and a morning on the same trip. He stood at the same counter in the same marble hall. The room held nobody else.

The ashen-robed master looked up from his forms, and then he looked up again. He got to his feet, which he had not done for the previous customer and did not do for everyone.

"Physician Su."

"Lord Soul Master."

The father's courtesy was the shallow, exact kind that comes out of a man who has spent twenty years bowing once and precisely. "You have the shelf for spirit souls. I would like to understand it."

The master looked at the town's physician and understood, as the trade understands things, exactly what season of a family's life had walked in. He stood a little straighter. His voice did the thing it had not done for the father in the morning, which was to become a voice that answered questions.

"The boy is at ten, then."

"The boy is at ten."

"Then here is the shelf, Physician."

The master laid it out as he had laid it out a hundred times, with the numbers this time and no performance. "Ten-year white souls, seventy-three of them. Seventy thousand coins each. Hundred-year yellows, eleven of them. One million. And the draw — thirty thousand, and it gives what it gives. The draw is where the town's boys go."

Su Heng took out the clinic's small book. A pencil, and wrote the three lines down, top to bottom, as he took a history. The master watched him do it and said nothing. The counter had seen fathers weep at those numbers. This one had brought a pencil.

The physician asked two more questions. The first was the one the room had been waiting for: "Of the three, what does thirty thousand honestly buy?"

"A gamble," the master said, "and the wager is worth making, which is why the counter still says it. The draw can give a hundred-year soul to a lucky child. It can also give him a defective. Both of those are written into the price."

"And the defect," the father said. "What does it do to the boy?"

The master thought about the patients this man must keep alive in a bad winter and answered him with the whole truth, plainly. "Nothing. It fuses. It gives the first ring and it opens the tenth rank. Then it sits in him like a small weak thing for the rest of his life. A strong soul would have grown with him. This one will not. There is no cure, because it is not a sickness. It is just less."

The physician wrote that down too.

"The second question," the master said.

"The second question is a physician's," Su Heng said. "When a body takes a thing it was not made to hold, the body keeps the memory. Does the fusing mark the boy? Any boy. Does it cost the body something I should be watching for, in the month after?"

The master was quiet for a moment. The counter's line of customers had asked him many things. Almost nobody asked him the second question.

"They run hot for a day," he said, at last, precisely. "Some of them."

The man in front of him had a rank. It made plain speech possible between them. "The ones who carry a strong soul run hotter. Bring him after, if the boy is one of mine. We take records here, Physician. I will not pretend to you that I am allowed to see the records of children who buy on other counters. For yours, I would ask."

Su Heng bowed, once and exactly, and closed the book.

"Then that is everything the counter sells," he said. "Thank you, Lord Soul Master."

"Physician."

The master's last sentence came at the father's back. Quietly. He had watched two counter-visits of one family in one week. He had done the arithmetic the town would do by supper. "The shelf does not get cheaper. And a boy at ten is at ten. There is no hurry written anywhere, and no hurry is the same thing as a hurry somewhere you cannot see."

---

The reckoning was done the next afternoon. Market-day daylight, and the door open. That had been the promise. Zhou Hui did not care who walked past. Let the lane see. A family counting its money with the door open is a family that intends to still be standing when the counting is over.

The boy brought the tin.

His father brought the clinic's small book. He laid it open at the page with three lines on it, top to bottom, in a physician's hand.

His mother brought the ledger of the house. She sat with her two hands flat on either side of it, and waited.

"Count," she said.

He emptied the tin on the cloth. He counted it once. Then he counted it again, out loud, in a voice that did not hurry. His mother had taught him years ago that the second count was the honest one.

"Three hundred and eighty-two coins."

"Good."

She did not write it down. The second count did not need writing. It needed hearing, and the room had heard it. "Now your father's lines."

Su Heng turned the little book around so that the table could read it. The three lines were neat, and short. Each of them had a number on it like a coin laid flat.

*White, ten-year — 70,000.*
*Yellow, hundred-year — 1,000,000.*
*Draw — 30,000.*

"Read me the third one," the mother said.

"Thirty thousand coins," the boy said.

"Say what our tin is, in the money the shelf uses."

He did the sum as his father had taught him to do sums. Slowly, in his head. Then out loud, in the daylight. A number said out loud in this house was a number that could be stood on.

"Three hundred and eighty-two coins. Counted twice, then said out loud."

"Three hundred and eighty-two," his mother said, and let the table hold the number for a moment, as she let the pot hold a boil. "Against seventy thousand for a bought soul, or thirty thousand for the gamble. Well. There it is."

Nobody at the table said anything for a while. Outside, a cart went past. Somebody's chickens complained, and the sun went on doing its rounds of the afternoon with its usual indifference to the market.

Then the boy did the thing that made the room take his weight. It was the reason that this meeting, out of all the meetings this house ever held, is the one the lane still tells stories about.

He picked up the pencil and wrote one more line under his father's three, in his own hand, in his own page:

*30,000 ÷ one year of the tin = a hundred and fifty years. So the tin is not the road. Find the road.*

His mother read it upside down, which was how she read most things. She did not smile, because she never did, and her eyes did anyway.

Su Heng looked at the line for a long time. Then he took the pencil and added three words under it in his surgeon's writing.

*Then find it.*

"The sum is wrong somewhere," he said, to the table. "I have been sitting with it since the Pagoda. The tin gains two hundred coins in a good year. Thirty thousand coins is a hundred and fifty good years. A hundred and fifty years is not a road, it is a joke. This house does not pay the wall in jokes. So the wrong part of the sum is what we think a read is worth. What did the dockman pay you for one night's walk of a manifest?"

"Nothing. He paid me in a sentence. He said his pig had better manners than most men he owes money to."

"Then he paid you in the wrong currency, and you were too young to know it, and now you are ten."

His father closed the little book and tapped it once, flat, like a man closing a case. "The harbor's money is not the kind you carry, boy. Tonight a man paid you nothing for a read that saved him a boat's worth of trust. Tomorrow a man who stands to lose three of your coins for one bad crate will pay you one of them for a night of your pig. And he will think himself lucky. And a man who stands to lose three hundred will pay thirty. There are a hundred such men within a day's walk of this table."

"And the wall costs thirty thousand," the boy said.

"And the wall costs thirty thousand," his father said. "The wall is not wrong. It is simply the wall. Your arithmetic was right that it is the whole of your life as you are now. So change what you are now."

The mother had not moved her hands.

"There is one more law in it," she said, "and your father will leave it to me to say. I am the one who counts the nights in this house. If the trade is to climb, it will climb up nights. Nights are not for sale in this house. A morning is. The pig may walk a boat; the boy does not vanish. You will be home to be counted, or the trade will be the thing your mother sells."

"Yes, Mother."

"And the tin has a new law as of this afternoon."

She reached over and put the lid on it. She set it in front of him. She set the pencil on top. "Every month, the sums page will be shown to your father. Once a season, to me. No total will be kept from the house again. Not because we think you lie. Because a road needs two sets of eyes. Your father does not have a whole pair, and I do not have any. That makes us both harder to fool."

There was a snort from the doorway, which was the pig's way of voting.

"The pig stays on the book," his father said, deadpan, without turning around. "I said what I said."

"The pig is staff," the mother said.

Later that night, when the house had gone quiet, Su Heng stayed up with the big book. The practice's ledger lay open at the page that began with two words at the top. The hand that wrote them had been steadier then.

*The boy.*

He drew a line under the last entry. He wrote the day. He wrote it short, because a physician writes a page as a physician makes a record. The record of this day did not need verbs.

*Ten. On the machine, in the hall, read aloud by his father in the daylight, one rank short of nothing now.*

Then, on the next line, he copied his son's three lines out of the little book. He used the ruler's exactness, the one he gave to a compounding table. Under them he wrote the only comment the page would carry for years:

*The price list of the door. The machine agrees with our number. The machine has no opinion about the price.*

---

The dockman came the next morning. He did not come to the boy.

He came to the clinic. He stood with his hat in his hands at the counter where the physician's son was grinding a week of bark. He made his case to the father as a man makes his case to a physician. Plainly, and without flattery. Flattery does not lower a fever.

"There is a night boat in tonight," he said. "Salt out of the south. The seller wants one price for the whole hold, and the hold's papers say the whole hold is one grade. I have bought out of that man's hold nine times in nine years and I have never liked how he rounds a page. Your boy's pig walked my pier once and told me the truth of a manifest."

He turned his hat a quarter turn. "I want the hold walked before the auction. Tonight, under lantern, while it is still his to sell."

The boy had put the pestle down halfway through the third sentence.

"And what do you want for the walk?"

Su Heng said.

"The pig and the boy, an hour of the dark and an hour of my lantern. Name the fee."

The father looked at his son. The son looked at his father. He understood. The naming had just been handed to him. This was what had come home from the Pagoda in the man's small book.

"What does the load stand to lose you?" the boy asked the dockman, "if it is wrong?"

The dockman looked at him a while. "Truth? If I buy that hold on the seller's word and it is a wrong hold, I lose three hundred coins. And a season of face, which is worse. If it is right and I buy right, I make six hundred."

"Then a hundred coins," the boy said.

The man put his hat on.

"A coin," he agreed, without blinking, as men agree to prices that are neither generous nor greedy but simply correct. "Pier four. Be there when the lanterns go up."

The mother stood in the doorway of the back room with her arms crossed. She said nothing until the man had gone. Then she said it once.

"You will be home before the house is up. Your father walks you to the water. If the boat is late, you leave it and come home."

"Yes, Mother."

"And the pig eats before it works," she said, "and it eats after."

---

Pier four at night was a county of its own.

The harbor road ran along the water. Lanterns went up one by one. Beyond the road the piers went out into the black like fingers. At the ends of them the boats sat low and yellow-lit. The boy had walked this road at dawn with fish on his back and at noon with the fishwife's crates. He had never once been on it at night. The night harbor was a different country with the same laws.

His father walked him to the corner where the lamp was. There he stopped and did not pretend to be casual about it.

"I stand here," Su Heng said, "where you can see me. That is the whole of my conditions."

The boy went out the pier with the pig at his heel.

The boat was a fat, low-bellied thing, salt-crusted. Two men were aboard. They were tired in the manner of men who have been working all evening, and expect to keep working. The hold was open. The smell that came up out of it was salt. Under the salt, further down, faint and patient. The smell that lives at the bottom of a crate that has been wet.

The dockman hung his lantern on a hook and stood back.

"Walk it."

The boy put his hand on the pig's back. He did that at the start of every read. He could never have said why. Then he let it go and stood aside. A read belongs to the reader. A crowd is a crowd even if the crowd is one.

The creature went down into the hold.

It went slowly, which was its way. It walked the forward rows first. The boy counted the rows in his head as his mother counted the week. The creature's snout worked at the crate seams. Its ears moved. Its heavy body went between the crates with the unhurried care of an old clerk through a ledger. Past the first row. Past the second, the third, the fourth. In the light that spilled down the hatch it was a slow grey shape. It had a ridiculous dignity. It walked a stranger's cargo like an inspector out of a story for children.

It stopped at the first crate of the fifth row.

It sat down.

The boy heard the boat settle, and the water below, and his own heart. The dockman, above, said nothing, which was the most professional thing he could do. The creature sat two steps short of the crate. Its ears went back and its face turned away from the load. It was the no-shape, exactly as it had been on the stones of the fish row twice before. The boy had learned in three years what the no-shape was worth.

Then the creature did a thing it had never done before.

It got up, and walked on.

The boy had read with the pig for years and he knew its arithmetic. A bad thing stops the walk. A wrong thing here is a wrong thing there. But the creature got up off its haunches and went on into the aft rows without reading them at all. Nose high, ears easy. It stepped between the crates like a man walking past a house that was already burning. The pig only read what could still be saved. It came back up the ladder and put its chin on the boy's shoe, and that was its whole report.

The boy stood at the hatch with the smell coming up around him. He did the reading as he did his sums. Slowly, twice, and then aloud. A read that stays in a head is worth nothing to anybody.

"The front four rows are what the papers say. Good salt, good wood."

He took a breath. "The fifth row is the line. It is dry on top, and it took water underneath — not this voyage. Before. Everything from that crate aft is one wet thing, kept quiet with the dry on top of it. He read the front like a ledger. He walked the back like a man crossing a room. The papers round the whole hold off as one grade. It is two grades."

The dockman picked up his lantern and lowered it into the hold. He stood there looking at the dry faces of the crates, as a man looks at a page he now has to rewrite. He was silent long enough for the boy to wonder if he had done something wrong.

Then he said, "You are nine years old."

"Yes."

"Right."

He hauled the lantern up. "One grade on the papers. Two in the wood. Tonight I buy the front four and let the aft go to the man who trusts him."

---

At dawn the grading men came out along the pier with their own lanterns and their clipboards. The market's quiet morning machinery took hold of the boat: the good rows counted out and priced, the aft rows opened in front of witnesses.

The fifth row's first crate came up and was opened on the boards.

The smell came out of it and stood in the morning like a verdict read aloud.

The seller talked, and the dockman let him talk, and then paid for the front four rows and not one crate behind them. The seller's face went through the arithmetic and arrived at the truth. He looked down the pier and found the boy at the end of it, with the pig beside him. He gave them both a long, level look that boys remember and men keep.

"You said it would be two grades," the dockman said, counting coins out of his purse into the boy's hand. "It is two grades. That is a hundred coins for the walk."

The coins went across, warm from the purse. Then the dockman did something the pier did not expect, which was to add another hundred on top of it. "And that one is not for the walk. That one is for the first time I have ever seen a hold read to the row. Bring the beast again."

The boy looked at the two hundred coins in his hand. He thought, briefly and hard, as his father had taught him. Then he did the honest thing.

"A hundred coins was agreed. The second one was not."

"No," the dockman said, "it wasn't. Keep it anyway. The day I start paying boys only what I agreed, boys stop telling me what I need to hear."

He looked out at the water. "The pig has better manners than the men I lend money to. It has since the pier read. Now it has walked a hold in front of witnesses. Put your coins away and go home before your mother comes down that pier herself."

---

His mother was in the doorway when he came up the lane, with the lamp behind her and her shawl around her shoulders. The house was dark behind her and the sky was grey at the edge. The boy had carried his whole life in his hands. He had counted his sentences the whole way home. He stopped, and said the only thing that mattered.

"One coin," he said. "And one more he would not take back. The load was two grades and the papers said one, and it came out true in front of the graders."

His mother looked at him for a long moment. She had stood in this doorway once before, on a fog night, when he was seven. Her face was the color of the fog. She had one sentence that she never repeated. He had been small then, and wrong, and lucky.

"You were counted," she said now. "Come in and eat."

The pig ate like a magistrate, twice, as the law required. Before he slept, the boy wrote two lines in the sums page, in a hand that had spent the night learning a trade.

*Night boat, hold read — two hundred coins.*

Then, under it, honest as his father had taught him: *Feed, two extra ladles — one coin.*

Then the total, which was the thing the page existed for: *Left: five hundred and eighty-one coins.*

Two hundred coins of the road, in the tin.

---

That same week, the town finished moving its two stories into their next shape, as the town always did, at once and without a meeting.

The market said it over the fish: the physician's boy was at ten. The tea stand improved it twice by noon and had him at eleven by the evening. The clinic, which had been quiet for a season, found three new customers with complaints that could have waited and questions that could not. The questions were all about the same thing, which was how a boy's number had moved when his father's treatment had not. His mother closed the ledger on the third of them and said the family's one sentence about the outside world. She said it in company, which was the point.

"We are still richer than most."

And the tin went to five hundred and eighty-one coins inside one market week. It had held three hundred and eighty-two on the afternoon the price was spoken. It was the first time in the history of that page that the column had moved for a reason worth the ink.

The number was ten. The door was thirty thousand. The first coins were in the tin.

# Chapter 7: The Refusal

## One

The Su house woke before the sun did.

Fang Zheng opened the clinic shutters before dawn. He was sixteen, the clinic's apprentice, and he had been opening those shutters for two years. He swept the counter, set out the mortar, and put the small stove under the water pot. By the time the lane was grey, the clinic was ready, and the house behind it was awake.

Su Heng came down with his sleeves rolled. He was forty-seven years old. He was the city's physician, and he was a Soul Ancestor with four rings, which in Glorybound meant two things: he was the first man of his trade, and he was one of the strongest people in the city. He never said either fact out loud. He did not need to.

The first patient was already waiting. Old Tong mended nets at the east pier, and his right arm had been wrong since a winter fall: the soul-vein in it had seized, and the fingers had gone cold and slow.

"Sit," Su Heng said. "Arm on the cloth."

Old Tong put his arm on the cloth. He kept his eyes on the counter, because he was a poor man and poor men watch their money, but he could not keep his eyes off the physician's hands either.

Su Heng put two fingers on the inside of the wrist. Then he let out his soul power.

Four rings of light came up around him and turned slowly. One white, two yellow, and a purple. The room did not become loud. It became still, which is what real power does in a small room.

The four rings were not for show. His martial soul came with them: a long, bright silver needle that appeared between two fingers of his right hand, thin as a splinter of ice.

"Hold still," he said. "This will be warm."

The needle went in at the wrist. The old man flinched and then let his breath go, because the warmth had already arrived, and it was moving up his arm in a slow line, opening what had closed.

At the back table, Su Yan stood at the mortar and watched the whole thing. He had watched it a hundred times. He still came to the doorway every time his father released his rings.

His father had done the same work on him, six years ago. That was the whole story of the house, and nobody told it out loud. The boy's soul-veins had failed at birth. For six years, this room and this needle had fought for him, and the needle had lost again and again.

"Open and close the fingers," Su Heng said.

Old Tong opened and closed his hand. His eyes went wide. "It is warm," he said. "It has not been warm in three years."

"Twice a day, the broth I give you. Keep the arm out of the water for a month." Su Heng drew the needle out and the rings went away. "Pay Fang Zheng as you leave. Not more than the board says."

The old man got up and bowed three times, which was two times too many, and paid, and left. At the door he turned and said the thing the whole lane said about this house.

"Your boy will be a physician too. It runs in the hands."

Su Heng did not answer that. He looked at his son, who was nine, and who had been at rank ten since the spring. Then he went back to his counter. Some answers are not useful at the counter.

---

The house behind the clinic was already running.

Zhou Hui sat at the long table with the ledger open and the morning light on it. She was forty-four. She was the daughter of the Zhou grain house, whose warehouses had fed this city for three generations. She had also been a soul master once: two rings, earned before she was nineteen.

The lane still remembered those two rings. The lane also remembered the winter she stood in her own doorway with the ledger under her arm and read a cheating delivery man off the street with one sentence, and it never tried her twice. When she chose the ledger over the ladder, her family had not complained. She was the best pair of eyes the grain house had ever grown.

"Nine months of the year paid in full," she said to the room, because the room listened even when it was empty. "The term is paid to the end. Say that again at the counter."

"I said it twice," the boy said. "The clerk wrote it down twice."

The pig came in from the yard on its own time, which was always late. Luo Sanpao walked to its bowl and sat and looked at the pot. It was a fat, pale, dog-faced creature with deep blue eyes, and it had been in this house for four years and paid its way for three.

"Three ladles," the boy said. "You worked the market yesterday. Today you walked nothing, so it is two. Two, and the fish ends from last night."

The pig made the small noise it made for the number three. Nobody in the family had ever worked out how it knew numbers. The boy gave it three. The pig's labor was real, and the house paid its staff.

Two lanes over, his father's mother kept the old Su house with the boy's uncle and aunt. Grandmother Su came by on wash days. She had kept a paper packet of the boy's combings through all six sick years, one lock for every season, and she still brought it out to check his hair against it. "The roots are thicker," she said every time. "That is the whole of my medicine."

The boy had an uncle on his father's side too: Su Rong, who worked the harbor's weighing station with two rings of his own and a back like a door. On the water he was a careful man, and at the family's New Year table he was the loudest laugher in the room. His daughter, Cousin Su Ran, was seven and believed the pig could talk.

Then the boy went out to the yard and did his practice set, the one his father had built from four years of watching him. Breath, slow. Hold, down to the count. Then the wall: three lengths, twice, the second one without stopping at the end. Then the crock: a half-full water jar carried from the yard wall to the kitchen door and back, eight times, with no noise from the water. His mother could hear it from the kitchen. She had ears like an abacus.

When he finished, he stood in the yard and looked at the sky over the harbor. He was nine years old and at rank ten, and the door those two facts opened cost thirty thousand coins.

---

In the little house at the end of the repairman's lane, that same morning, Tang Wulin sat on his bed and cried until the crying stopped on its own.

He had cried on Na'er's shoulder a long time. When it was over, he lifted his head and wiped his face and asked her the question.

"Na'er. Am I weak?"

She shook her head hard. Twice.

He looked down at the spirit soul ball in his hands. Inside it, the little Grass Snake was turning slowly in the light. Its eyes were cloudy, and there was fear in them that it could not name.

"Go out first, Na'er," he said. "I want to fuse with this one."

Her face changed. "But brother. This one is no good."

"I know."

He said it straight, and that was harder than crying. "Mom and Dad work all day for us. Dad said he would find the money for a better one, and I know what finding money means in this house. It means another year. This one is still a spirit soul. It still makes me a real Soul Master. If I fuse with it, Dad does not have to think about it again, and that is worth more to me than a yellow ball."

She stared at him.

He gave her his smile. He was nine, and he had already learned how to smile while he gave something up, which is the kind of thing a house teaches a child without meaning to.

"It was only a dream anyway," he said. "Maybe a master forger is what I am."

She went out, because he asked her to. The door closed softly behind her.

---

A spirit soul in its shell is not much of a body. Wulin's finger went right through the shell's wall. The Grass Snake felt the touch and uncoiled and wound itself once around his finger. It put out its little forked tongue, as if to lick him.

He lifted it to his face. It was ten centimeters long and no thicker than his little finger. Its eyes were cloudy and unfocused, and the fear in them was simple and animal. It did not want to die.

In twenty-four hours, if nobody fused with it, that was exactly what would happen. It would die as a creature dies. It would not be a thing put away on a shelf.

He released his soul power into his palm. The faint blue halo came, and the Bluesilver Grass slid out into his hand. It was a thin, dark blade, the thickness of a hair.

The Grass Snake climbed onto the grass. The soft blue light of the blade met it, and a faint earthen ring of light rose out of the snake's small body and hung in the air.

Then something touched his mind. It was small and warm and frightened, and under the fear there was one clear want: to stay alive.

He opened his soul and let it in.

The snake's mind was too weak to fight him. Wulin's spiritual power had reached the intermediate level of the Spirit Origin realm, and that was more than enough for a creature like this. The fusion took hold at once, and he closed his eyes and went into it.

He did not notice his knees give way. He did not notice the floor.

The changes started in the grass.

The thin blade thickened. Its color went from pale green down to a darker, greyer green. It stopped being a leaf and became a stem, and then the stem became a vine, as thick as a boy's finger, curling around his hand. Small dry plates formed along it, like the scales of a fish made of wood. Careful eyes would have called them snake marks. There was no careful eye in the room.

The Grass Snake changed with it. Its earthen-yellow body took on a blue tint, as if the color were being poured through it from the grass. It grew from six centimeters to eight. Then it lay along the thick stem, calm, as if it had found a road it liked.

The fusion ended without any trouble. The snake had no power to resist. The grass took it as a field takes a light rain.

And then the wall that Wulin's soul power had been pressing against for three months broke.

The power crossed the bottleneck in one surge. It ran down his arms and legs and out to his skin. His cells woke up one by one, like people coming to a door to see what the noise is. The heavy pressure that had sat in his chest all his life lifted, and what came up under it was heat.

The heat started at his tailbone. It climbed his spine like water finding the seam of a floor. It did not hurt. It climbed until it stopped, all at once, at the top of his back.

At the same moment, from his forehead, a faint gold pattern of veins came down. He had carried it, unseen, since the year of his fever. The two lights met at the top of his spine and joined into one.

The gold spread out of his back and through his skin. It ran over his arms and legs. It lay for a moment on the little Grass Snake and on the new vine of Bluesilver Grass, and it wrote on the grass one thin gold line, from root to tip.

The grass changed where the line ran. It had been soft. It gained a spine. Its dull surface took on a gloss, like water on stone in the evening, and a faint blue halo moved under the skin of it, and through the middle went the gold.

The heat changed after a while. It left his bones and turned into a numbness that crawled. It ran down his four limbs and through every bone of him, as if a thousand small insects were walking inside his body. He could not move. He could not make a sound. His mind stayed clear through all of it, and the clearness made every minute worse. At the worst of it, he wished for the furnace back.

If the Soul Master at the Pagoda had put the helmet over his head in that hour, the cabinet would have written down something worth a paper. His spiritual power was rising the whole time. It climbed under the pain as a man's voice climbs when he holds a door shut.

Then that passed too. The cloudiness left the Grass Snake's small eyes, and they came clear. It had grown again while he lay there, one centimeter longer, and the tips of its scales stood up gold for a moment. Then the snake climbed down off the grass and into the hollow of his palm, and from there it went into him, as a tame animal goes into its own house. The vine drew itself back into the blue halo and followed.

Later, the sweat dried on him. Under his shirt, the gold pattern had gone past the skin and into his body, and it lay there, thin and patient, where no one would see it for a long time.

Nobody had seen any of it. He was alone on the floor of his room, face down, breathing slowly. He slept where he lay. Outside the window the morning market opened, and the town went on with its day.

---

Tang Ziran came home in the middle of the afternoon with the money in his fist.

He had done it. He had walked to a man he did not want to ask and he had asked, and the man had said yes, and the coins were heavy in his hand. It was a whole day late, and he knew it was a whole day late, and he told himself that a whole day was not the end of anything.

Na'er stood in the doorway of the house.

She was small and she filled the frame. She looked at him with her hands on both sides of the door, as a person stands when the standing is the point.

"Big brother is fusing with the spirit soul," she said. "You can't go in."

The words went through him like a nail through a board.

"What did you say?" His voice was strange. "He has already started?"

"Big brother said he didn't want to burden Mom and Dad."

Tang Ziran took two steps back and sat down in the chair by the wall. His face changed in front of a child, and he did not have anything left to build it back with.

He struck his own head once.

"Tang Ziran," he said. "Why can you not look at the truth one time? It is because you are weak that your son has to be careful with you."

The big coins slipped out of his hand and rolled across the table. One of them went off the edge and rang on the floor, and he did not pick it up.

Too late. One day too late.

He lifted his head suddenly. "Na'er. How long has he been at it?"

"He started soon after you left."

"Still not finished?" he said. He should be done by now. He stood up. "We should look. Quietly."

Na'er thought for a moment, then nodded, and stepped out of the doorway.

The spirit soul ball lay on the floor beside the bed, open and empty. Wulin was asleep on his back with his clothes soaked in sweat, and his face was calm. Tang Ziran put two fingers on his son's wrist out of pure habit, and counted, and the count came back normal, and he let out the breath he had been holding for four hours.

Then he gathered his son up against his shoulder and cried without making any noise. "I am sorry," he said into his hair. "Your father is sorry. Nine years old, and you already carry this house, and today I sat you down in front of a counter and showed you a wall."

Na'er stood beside the bed and watched. Her pretty purple eyes went strange for a moment, as they did when something everybody else had a name for did not add up. She looked at the money on the table, and at the crying man, and she did not understand it. She put her hand on his sleeve once, and then took it back.

---

Wulin woke in the evening.

He sat up before he was fully back in the world. The first thing he noticed was that his body was light. His clothes were clean; someone had changed him without waking him. When he breathed, the breathing felt good, as if his chest had been widened while he slept.

He put a thought to his soul power and it answered at once, like a full well answering a rope. He knew the feeling from the night he crossed into rank ten, and this was bigger. He sat still and let it be true.

Rank eleven. He had left the Scholars and become a Soul Master in one afternoon.

He thought the honest thought first. The Grass Snake was defective; the Pagoda's own master had told him to give it back; every man at that counter had known it was junk. Then he thought the better thought. The soul power was his. Soul power did not care what a person's martial soul was worth. A man with soul power could still swing a hammer better than a man without, and the world paid for that.

He threw both arms out to feel himself.

*Pa!*

The sound came out of the air of the room like a small balloon popping. It was not a clap. It was the room itself, and it had not been there yesterday.

He stared at his hands.

The door opened before he could do it again, and the room filled with people.

"Son! Son, are you all right?"

Lang Yue came through first and reached the bed and took him, and her tears arrived a moment later. He held her while she came apart on his shoulder.

Behind her came Tang Ziran, carrying a face that was trying to be ordinary. Beside him stood Na'er, who looked at her brother like a person looks at a lamp that was out and now is lit.

Behind all three of them stood a broad, weathered man in a soot-grey work shirt. His forearms were like oak roots and his face was like the wall of a workshop. It was Mang Tian, Wulin's forging master.

The boy had not come to the workshop that day. Mang Tian had called the house at noon to ask why his apprentice was missing, and had heard the whole story, and had come as soon as his day's work was done.

"I'm all right, Mom."

"Son." Lang Yue held him at the arms and said the sentence she had carried all day. "Let us stop trying to make you a Soul Master. It is fine to be ordinary people. Ordinary people are fine."

"Mom, I really am all right." He gave her the smile he had learned for moments like this. "I'm a real Soul Master now. Rank eleven. The fusion went fine. That Grass Snake suits my martial soul. If they had given me a strong one, I don't think I could have carried it. This one and I fit."

Lang Yue's tears came again. Her son had learned to talk like a man who comforts other people, and no mother wants to hear that from a boy of nine.

Tang Ziran let out a long breath. The money was on the table behind him, under a bowl. It would stay there. Telling the boy now would only make the wound worse. Some arithmetic a man learns late, and keeps to himself.

Mang Tian had listened to all of it without moving. Now he spoke, and his voice filled the room as his arms filled his sleeves.

"Rank eleven," he said. "Bring out your martial soul. I want to see it."

## Two


The dim room lit up.

A white ring of light rose slowly from his feet and turned around him, steady as a wheel on a track. Everyone in the room knew what it was, even Na'er. It was a soul ring. White meant ten years, the lowest kind.

Ten thousand years ago, spirit souls had not existed, and the ring was the thing that told the world a person was a Soul Master. It still told them before he spoke.

Then he opened his hand. The grass came out.

The yellow light of the snake showed first. The little Grass Snake rode the air at his fingertips, small and bright. Then the Bluesilver Grass came. It kept coming.

It came out as a vine.

It was as thick as a boy's finger and it did not stop. It unrolled off his hand and looped around the lamp and came back down to the floor and spread. In a moment the little room had a grey-green net in it, and the net was him. He could feel every centimeter of it, as he could his own fingers. Where the vine touched the wall, the wall came back to him. Where it touched his mother's coat, the coat came back to him. And along the whole length of it came a small, bright feeling of happiness that was not his, which was the snake's, glad to be out of the dark and out in the world on the end of his arm.

He stood in the middle of it and understood something for the first time. This was what people meant when they said a Soul Master was not like other men.

Lang Yue stood frozen. Tang Ziran's eyes had something in them that had not been there since the counter. Na'er stood at the edge of the vine with her hands in it, and her face wore the lost look it always wore over things no one explained to her.

Mang Tian walked forward and took hold of a length of the vine.

He pulled it out straight, as a man tests a rope. His hands had been in iron thirty years. They had bent cold metal with grip alone.

The grass came out of his hands straight and smooth and fine, and it did not snap.

Mang Tian held the length up to the lamp and read it as he read metal. "Don't be discouraged," he said. "The soul is nothing much. But you are a real Soul Master now, and that means your soul power has room to grow. Soul power is useful in every trade. Ten thousand years ago the first soul tools came out, and the first people who bought them were the Soul Masters whose martial souls were not worth much. It was soul power that drove them. It is the same way with mechas now. A smith with soul power can feel the metal down in his fingers and strike twice as true. A Mecha Master with a weak martial soul and a steady arm is not a joke."

He let the grass drop. Then he shrugged. "So don't write yourself off."

The grass hit the floor.

It struck the boards with a sound like a dropped tool.

Mang Tian stopped. He looked down at his own feet. Then he reached down and picked up the grass again, and this time he did not read it like a smith reading metal. His brows came together as they did when a billet came out of the forge with a fault in it.

He did not speak. He took a fresh length of the vine in both fists and pulled.

Thirty years of iron came down the vine, and the vine straightened and held.

"Your Bluesilver Grass is a bit abnormal."

The room went quiet. Wulin heard his own voice come out small. "Master. What part of it is abnormal?"

"The weight, to start with." Mang Tian was turning the vine end over end in his fingers, which was a thing he only did with metal. "I did not feel it when I picked it up. I felt it when it hit the floor. A strand of Bluesilver Grass that size should weigh almost nothing. This one weighs as much as a full vine of the same thickness, and heavier than that, I think. Grass with one white ring should not have that attribute at all."

He put the vine down. He looked at the boy. "And it does not tear. I have pulled worse iron than you have lived years. Let me try once more."

His eyes went bright. Four soul rings appeared.

One white, two yellow, one purple. They rose around him and lit the little room like a festival, and the air in the room changed weight. Wulin, who had been a Soul Master for one hour, felt the four rings press against him and took two steps back without deciding to.

His teacher. His forging master. The man who paid him by the day, who drank tea out of a chipped tin cup, and who had come to the house because an apprentice missed one morning.

Wulin stared with his back against the vine.

The ladder he had climbed did not end where he had thought it ended. Rank zero to ten were Scholars. Eleven to twenty were Masters. After that, every ten ranks had its own name and its own country: Soul Grandmaster, Soul Elder, Soul Ancestor, Soul King, Soul Emperor, Soul Sage, and Soul Douluo at the top of the world. And at the very top, with nine rings, stood the Titled Douluo.

His forging master had four rings. He was a Soul Ancestor, and the little room in the repairman's lane was holding him.

Three spirit souls appeared on Mang Tian's shoulders.

The first was small and weak: a tiny white rabbit, crawling around his shoulder with a white ring of light on it. The second was a pair of yellow hammers, turning slowly and shining. Wulin stared at those. A hammer was a tool, and tool spirit souls did not happen in nature. Somebody had made those, which meant a man could buy a spirit soul that was never born.

The third was purple. It was a brown bear about a meter tall, with two arms thicker than a man's leg. It bared its teeth and jumped up onto the master's shoulder and looked around the room as if it wanted a job.

Then Mang Tian called his martial soul, and a hammer appeared in his hand.

It had a dim white glow and it looked exactly like the hammer he worked with every day, with deep brown vein lines running over it like roots.

The Earth Hammer.

Mang Tian's first spirit soul lit up. The little white rabbit turned into a layer of white light and flowed over his body.

"Tenacity," he said. The word landed in the room like a tool set down on a bench. His muscles changed under his shirt, going loose and full, a body that had just been told it could ask for more from itself.

Then he took the Bluesilver Grass in both hands, with his soul power behind him and a ten-year soul skill running through his arms, and he pulled.

Everyone expected the grass to tear.

Instead, the dim body of the Bluesilver Grass began, very slowly, to shine.

---

The school put the graduation list up at the end of that week, and the list changed the shape of the room.

It was one sheet of paper in a wooden frame by the office door. The office wrote out the children of the graduation year who had reached rank ten, in their small exact hand. Six names. There had not been a list like it in four years, because the year had not had six.

Su Yan read it on his way in. Mu Yun's name was one line above his own, exactly where it had been on the spring list, as if the office had decided months ago where the two of them belonged in every document the town would print.

The room had read it before breakfast. A list on a wall is the loudest thing a school owns.

"We get a paper at the end of term," said the boy at the next desk. His name was Deng and he was one tenth of a rank short of the list, and he had been in a mood for a month. "A recommendation. For the intermediate academy."

"For Shrek," Wan Yunchao said from behind them, loud enough for the whole aisle. "That is what the list is for. They send it out to the academies."

"You're on the list," Deng said.

"Of course I'm on the list." Wan Yunchao was one of the six, and the room knew it, because he had told the room. His father owned two fishing boats and a share in a cold store.

He leaned forward and looked down the row at Su Yan. The room's ears turned, because this was the part it had been waiting for.

"Six of us," he said. "Five of us will go to the counter this year. Guess who doesn't."

Su Yan looked up from his book.

"Say it," Wan Yunchao said, grinning. "Your father's the physician. He has money. Everyone says your rank moved because of something he did in that clinic. So when do you go?"

"The counter costs thirty thousand coins." "That is the draw. The shelf above it is seventy thousand, and the shelf above that is a million. My house has the numbers written in a clinic book. I have seen the page."

The room went still. It was the wrong answer for winning, and the right one for everything else.

"So the pig boy stays a pig boy," Wan Yunchao said, and his grin had gone thin at the edges.

"The pig walks back," Su Yan said, quietly.

"That was last year's answer."

"It is still true."

"I hope the pig gets a certificate," Wan Yunchao said. Half the room laughed, at the timing and not at the sentence.

"Sit down, Yunchao."

Teacher Lin came in with her papers. She never raised her voice. She looked at the room for one second.

"The list on that wall is a list of ten ranks," she said. "Not one of them is worth a cup of tea by itself. What the counter costs is your father's business and nobody else's. Yunchao, you are on the list. That is not a trophy. It is a summons. Go and worry about your own counter."

She turned to the blackboard and began. Wan Yunchao sat down. The room went back to being a room, with a little extra quiet in it on one subject.

---

Mu Yun was standing at the sheet when he passed it after the last bell.

She was the only one there. She had stopped in front of the frame and she did not turn around when he came up.

"I looked at our two lines," she said. "The office put us together on the list. Two years ago I did the arithmetic on how that would happen. It has happened exactly that way."

She turned then. Her eyes did sums on people faster than people like to be summed.

"Everyone in the room has read the list," she said. "And under it they are all doing the same sum about you. Six tens, one counter, and a wall."

"Let them."

"The wall is not the interesting part."

She hitched her satchel up. It was heavy with books she chose herself.

"The interesting part is the pig," she said. "Half the school says it cost a dockman a boat. My cousin sweeps at the auction house, and he says the same thing every man on the harbor is saying. A fat grey animal refused a clean hold, and a reader's name went grey."

She watched his face. He let her watch it.

"Is it true?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Was the hold clean?"

"By the papers it was clean salt and dry wood."

"Then what was the pig telling you?"

The bell-rope creaked down the corridor. Nobody came out. The list sat in its frame between the two of them.

"I don't know yet," Su Yan said. "My father asked me the same question. My mother asked it first."

Mu Yun looked at the list once more: six names, her line and his line, the whole graduation year in the office's small exact hand. Then she turned away and dug in her satchel and put something wrapped in paper into his hand.

"You did not ask what everyone else asks," she said.

"What does everyone else ask?"

"How it feels to be poor," Mu Yun said, and walked out of the corridor, and did not look back, which was how she said thank you, and he had known that for two years.

He unwrapped the paper on the walk home. It was a plum sweet, the expensive kind from the shop by the north gate, the kind his family bought twice a year.

He ate half and put the other half in his pocket. That evening the pig smelled it through the paper from across the room, so he gave the other half to the pig, and the pig ate a sweet for the first time in its life, slowly, like a magistrate taking evidence.

---

On the sixth day of the month, his mother sent him to the grain house with the season's page.

The Zhou grain house stood on the market square with its shutters painted green. It had fed the city for three generations, and it was still run from the counter by his mother's elder brother, Uncle Zhou Anmin, a wide man with a wide laugh and a memory for every farmer's debt.

"Little Su," the uncle said. "The physician's boy, at ten. The whole square has heard it."

He took the page and read it as the family read pages, top to bottom, twice.

"Your mother's hand is not on this."

"She says the page is mine now. She reads it at the season's turn."

The uncle grunted, which in that family was a compliment. Then he called back through the doorway. "Father. The boy is here with a page."

Grandfather Zhou came out of the back room. He was seventy-two and moved like a man who had once carried sacks and now carried nothing heavier than the ledger he had run for forty years. He had made the rule the town still used: a hundred coppers makes a coin.

"Show me," he said.

The boy put the page on the counter. The grandfather read it slowly, moving one finger under the columns as he had checked his own books for forty years. Earned, spent, left.

"Nine months," the old man said. "A boy of nine with nine months of his own book. Your mother did that."

"Every month to my father," the boy said. "Every season to her."

"And what does the column say now, at the bottom?"

"Five coins, eighty coppers," the boy said. "It went down a copper this month. There is a line for it."

The grandfather looked at him for a long moment, and his eyes were not soft and were not unkind. "The page of a man goes up and down, boy. The book of a house only goes down when something is learned. Your mother taught you that too, I think."

Behind them, a boy about thirteen came in from the warehouse with a slate. It was cousin Zhou Yuan, second year at Red Mountain Academy, rank six, and sure he was the family's future.

"Heard the news," the cousin said, and put the slate down. "Ten at the spring tests. The whole school has a list. My year has a list too. I am at six and I will be at ten next year, and then the family will have two of us at the counter."

"They will," Su Yan said.

"They will," the cousin agreed, and went to help his father with the noon delivery. He had never once been unkind about the pig, and he had never once understood it either. He was simply the boy of the house who was going the normal way, and the normal way was working.

---

The dockman came to the clinic at the end of that week.

He stood at the counter with his hat in his hands, as he had stood there once before, and made his case to the physician plainly, as men made their cases in this room.

"There is a salt run in tonight." "Out of the south, three days out. The papers call the whole hold one grade. The man selling it is new to me, and the price he wants is a price I only pay when a hold is clean. I want the boat walked before the auction. Under lantern, while the man on the other side of the water is still deciding his number."

"You came to me last time with a night boat as well," Su Heng said.

"This one stands to lose me six coins," the dockman said. "And how the pier watches, more than the coins."

The boy had come to the counter. He did not say anything, because his father had not looked at him yet. But the dockman had. The dockman's eyes went to him once, briefly, as a man checks that his best tool is still on the shelf.

"Six coins," the boy said. "And what do you make if the hold is clean?"

"Twelve, and my name, which is worth more."

The boy did the arithmetic slowly, as his father taught: once in his head, and then again.

"Then two coins," he said.

The dockman put his hat on.

"Two coins," he said. "Pier four. Be there when the lanterns go up."

When the man had gone, Su Heng looked at his son for a while. Then he opened the small book and wrote the day down, and the boy knew the entry had gone under the page that said *The boy*, and that he might read it one day or might not, and either was the same.

At home, his mother stopped them. She had her ledger under her arm and the shawl already off the hook.

"Home before the house is up," she said. "The same law as last time, and it does not change because the boat is bigger. And the pig eats before it works and it eats after."

"Yes, Mother."

She looked at the pig, which stood at the boy's heel with its dignity on. "You are staff. Staff get their supper whatever the boat does."

At the doorway she said one more thing, and she said it to the father, in the tone she used for the ledger.

"If the boy is late, the trade is the thing I sell."

"Yes, Zhou Hui."

The father took his coat. At the lamp on the corner of the harbor road he stopped and stood where the boy could see him, on the same stone as last time.

"The pig does the reading," he said. "You bring the reading home."

"Yes, Father."

"One rule first, then you go." He looked down at his son. In the lamplight his face had the flat patience of a man who had held a hundred hands over a hundred pulses. "A physician is given signs all day long. A sign with no place is a rumor. A sign with a place is a case. If your reader gives you a no tonight, get the place where the no lives, and then come home."

"Yes, Father."

The boy went out along the pier with the pig at his heel. Behind him the flame stood still in the lamp, and the man stood still under it, and the water moved in the dark.

## Three

Pier four at night was the same country it had been the week before, with a bigger boat in it.

The salt boat sat low and full at the end of the boards. Two of the seller's men stood on the deck with their hands in their pockets. One of them smoked, and did not stop smoking when the boy and the pig came up the boards. A clerk from the other side of the water stood at the rail with his hands behind his back, doing the arithmetic of a rival. The auction was at dawn. Everyone on the pier knew what a clean hold was worth before breakfast.

The dockman hung his lantern on the same hook as last time.

"Same job as the other night," he said. "The whole hold, papers say one grade. The man wants thirty coins for it. One grade, and it is worth fifty."

"And if it is not?"

"Then I walk away, the other side of the water buys a bag of south-country trouble, and I eat supper with my name on." He looked at the boy, and his eyes were serious but not unkind. "Walk it."

He put his hand on the pig's back. He did that at every read. Then he took his hand away and stood to the side, because a read belongs to the reader.

The pig went down the ladder.

It went slowly, as it did everything. The lantern above caught the top of its back and let the rest of it go into the dark. The boy counted the rows in his head, because he always counted.

The creature stopped in the second row.

It did not go further. It stood in the dark between the crates with its head low. Then it turned away from the load, came back up the ladder, and sat down against the boy's leg.

That was the whole reading. There was no slow walk down the hold, no working of the crate seams. There was a stop in the second row and a turning away, and then the reader came back and sat on the boy's foot, which was the pig's way of saying that a thing was finished.

On the stones of the fish row, twice, the pig had refused a crate. Here it had refused a whole boat.

The dockman waited.

"The hold," the boy said.

"Say it plain, boy."

"No." His voice came out smaller than he wanted, so he said it again, deeper. "The hold is a no. He will not walk it."

The seller's man took the cigarette out of his mouth. He looked down the pier at the dockman, at a boy, at a fat grey pig sitting on a shoe, and he laughed the honest laugh of a man who has just been handed a gift.

"Two coppers off the price for the entertainment," he said. "Well? Do you still want thirty? Or does the animal carry your purse now?"

The other side of the water's clerk watched from the rail and did not smile. He was doing the arithmetic of an event his morning had not planned for.

The dockman did not answer either of them. He stood with his lantern and looked down into the hold, at the crates he could see and the rows he could not.

"A no is a no," he said. "Not tonight, then."

He said it flat and short, and he did not look at the boy while he said it, and the whole pier heard it.

---

The walk home was short. The boy asked once to go back and walk the hold himself, with the lamp, and the dockman looked at him for a moment and then said no, in the voice men use on a pier when the boards are not a place where a boy argues.

His mother was at the door with the lamp behind her.

"You were counted," she said. There was nothing in her face that said the word good.

"He refused," the boy said. "He would not walk it. He stopped in the second row and sat on my shoe."

His mother stood in the doorway, and the lane behind him was grey, and she asked the only question that mattered.

"Did he say why?"

"No."

She let that sit in the air. Then she stepped back and held the door.

"Then that is the work," she said. "Come in. The pig eats, and then you do."

---

At dawn, the boy was back on the pier, because his mother's law said he came home after the boat was done, and the boat was not done.

The other side of the water's clerk had bought the hold at first light. Papers read aloud, price counted, and the seller's man had offered one crate to be opened on the boards, as a courtesy from a seller to a buyer.

The crate came up the ladder and was opened with a chisel.

The salt inside was white and dry and clean as a page. The man dug his hand down to the middle and brought it up and let it run through his fingers. There was a noise from the pier that was not a laugh and not a cheer. It was the sound men make when a bet they did not make has just been settled the wrong way.

One grade. The hold was one grade. A clean hold, refused in front of every man in the harbor.

The boy stood at the end of the boards with his hands at his sides and did not look away from any of it, because his father had taught him that a man who will not look at his own mistake makes the mistake twice. He watched the crate go back down. He watched the coins go across.

Then he walked to the middle of the pier, where the dockman was standing with his purse already out.

"Two coins was the fee," the dockman said.

"No."

"It was agreed."

"A coin was agreed for a walk," the boy said. His voice held steady. He had not expected that. "The walk bought you nothing. You lost the hold because of what I told you, and if I cannot pay you twelve coins, then I pay the only coin I have, which is that you keep yours."

The dockman looked at him a long time.

Then he put the purse away, slowly, and stood with his hands on his hips and looked out at the water where the other side of the water was counting a clean hold.

"You are the strangest nine-year-old I have ever met," he said. "And I have met some strange ones, because a pier is where strange comes to work."

"Sir."

"Listen now, because I will say this once." The dockman turned and looked down at him. There was no kindness being wasted and no cruelty being saved. "Half this pier came down to watch your pig last night. That is what a name is. Last week your name was worth a coin and a good sentence from me. Last night your name cost me a buy, and the whole harbor watched it. The harbor will tell that story at the auction house and in the chandlery and at the fish row by supper. Men will still hire you. They will just watch the pig instead of trusting it, and the money will come slower. That is the tax on a wrong reading, and every name in this harbor has paid it once."

"So here is the one thing I want out of last night. When he refuses, walk it twice. Bring me the row and the reason. A no with a place and a name is a reading. A no with nothing behind it is a priest's word, and I do not pay priests to walk my holds. Can you do that?"

"No," the boy said. "Not yet."

"Then do it not yet," the dockman said, "and come back when you can."

He walked away down the boards. The boy went home under a sky that was turning the colour of dishwater.

His mother stood in the doorway with the lamp.

"You were counted," she said.

Then she looked at his face, which had learned something in the night, and she did not ask what.

---

That morning, before the market, the boy wrote in the back of his practice book, in the page that held the columns earned, spent, and left:

*Night walk, refused. Fee unpaid by my own refusal. Feed, one copper.*

*Left: five coins, eighty coppers.*

Under that he wrote, because the page did not lie and he did not lie to it:

*The reason: unknown.*

Then he washed his face and ate his breakfast and went to school like any other boy on any other day, with one copper less in a tin and a question riding on his back.

---

The father took him to the boat the next afternoon.

The dockman had given the leave. The boy had asked him at the clinic, and the man had looked at him for a while and then written a word on a chit for the boat's watchman, which is how the pier says yes.

Su Heng closed the clinic at the dead hour and walked him down the harbor road in his ordinary coat. The salt boat still lay at pier four. Her cargo had sold and the unloading had been going all day, so the forward rows were already empty and the hold was a wooden room with an echo in it. The pig went down the ladder first. The boy went after it. The father came last with the lamp, because a physician does not send a boy into a hole in the ground without a lamp behind him.

The pig walked past the empty forward rows without a look. It walked to the second row's line, where a few crates still waited for the winch. It stopped. It sat.

"That is the place," the boy said. "That is the exact board."

The father lowered the lamp. The boards there were old and joined, and the nail heads had gone black with water years ago and been painted over with tar. They looked like every other board in every other hold in the harbor.

"They look like boards," Su Heng said.

"Yes."

They got the boards up. The boy did the work and the father held the lamp. Under the planks was a shallow bilge, dry at this tide, with a long seam running forward under the whole row like a scar under a shirt.

And in the wood there, in the grain of the boat's ribs, there was a colour that was not tar and was not time.

"Do you see it?" the boy said.

"I see it."

The father crouched down, slowly, because crouching was a thing his knees had opinions about now. He put his face near the rib and breathed in, and then did it again as he did a patient: first the near breath, then the slow one. Then he sat back on his heels and was quiet.

"Blood," he said. "Old blood. Years of it, in the wood and under the tar. It has been fed and dried and fed again." He pointed at the seam with his chin. "That drain line is from the old days. Whatever used to come out of this hold's cargo came out through there, over these boards, for a long time."

Above them on the deck, the watchman heard the boy's question and answered it sideways, as pier men answer children, which is honestly and for free.

"Hides," he said. "Two seasons back this boat pulled skins out of the north for a tanner. Cow, mostly, and some deer, and one run of something bigger that the tanner sold cheap and never explained. Then the law on the northern trade changed and the boat went to salt. The salt is fine. The boat is fine. A hold is a room. Rooms keep what happens in them."

"Thank you," the boy said.

They put the boards back. The father held the lamp on the boy's hands while he set the last one.

"So," Su Heng said. "A sign with no place is a rumor. This afternoon you gave the sign a place, and the place has a name. The name is another cargo's history. That is a case, and it is written in your head where it belongs."

He straightened up. "Now tell me the trade's half, because the trade is your half and not mine."

The boy watched the boards.

"His nose is for beasts," he said, slowly. "That is what he has. He is a pig, and he is a dragon that never finished, and the one part of him that finished is the nose. When he walks a hold, he reads what a beast has left in it. Last week the hold was two grades, and that was a beast of a different kind, and he read that too. This week the hold was clean, and the room was not. He told me about the room."

"And what did you sell the pier?"

"A no."

"You sold it as a verdict." "The pier does not buy no's. It buys answers. Those are two different words, and now you know it."

He held the lamp over his son for a moment without saying anything.

"Then write it in the page," Su Heng said at last. "A page that carries a new law is worth the ink. That is what I do."

---

At the end of the month, the sums page came to the table, exactly as the law said it would.

The pot was off the stove. The clinic was closed. His mother sat with her arms crossed and her ledger shut in front of her, because the month's read was the father's read, and her season was the season. The pig lay by the stove with its chin on its own foot.

"Read it to me," Su Heng said.

The boy read the page. He read the columns as his mother had taught him, earned and spent and left, and then the night in question, and then the two lines under it, in his own hand:

*When he refuses: walk it twice. Bring the place and the name.*

*The name this time: hides. The wood keeps what happened in it.*

His father read the page twice, top to bottom, as he read everything. Then he put it down flat.

"Five coins, eighty coppers," he said. "The page went down a copper this month, and the reason column went up by a whole case. Sit down, both of you. The table has two more things on it, and I will say them once each."

The boy sat. His mother did not move, because she had been sitting as a fact for an hour.

"The first thing is the column you cannot read yet." Su Heng turned the practice book around so the page faced the table. "Everything on this page is the road. The road is good. The road is not the door. The door is a different page, and it has two columns of its own. Last month I wrote the first on it. That column is the price: thirty thousand for the draw, seventy thousand for a white soul that suits you, and a million for the yellow. That column is the town's. It moves with markets and with other men's luck, and this week it moved the wrong way for a family three streets from here. I want you to hold that in your hand now."

"The second column is yours, and nobody in this city can sell it to me. It is what you can carry. A strong soul bends a weak reader double. The Pagoda's own master told me the same thing in his own words: the ones who carry strong souls run hotter. So the day this house walks into that marble hall, the boy who walks in will hold a soul that suits what he is. The drum is a drum of defectives, and you are not putting the one soul of your life on a drum."

"He is nine," the mother said. She said it flatly, to the wall.

"He is nine," the father agreed, "and he was at ten at the machine in the spring, and the road he is on is the only one he has. So it is his business to know what the two columns are. It is my business to write them. And it is yours, Zhou Hui, to keep both of us honest."

"Then the tin stays mine to count," the mother said.

"The tin stays yours to count."

The mother reached over and drew the practice book to her side of the table. She read the boy's two new lines with her head tilted, which was how she read, and she read them twice.

"*Bring the place and the name*," she said. "That is not arithmetic. That is a craft. Crafts are slower than arithmetic. They last longer."

She closed the book. She pushed it back across the table to her son, square, with her two hands, as she pushed the ledger when a season's sum had been stood on and was finished.

"Good," she said. "The tin went down a copper and the house is standing. Show me the season's page at the turn, and I will tell you whether the craft is worth more than the arithmetic. That is all I will say about it."

Later that night, when the boy had gone up and the pig had gone to the clinic's outer door, Su Heng stayed at the table with the big book open at the page that began with the two words.

*The boy.*

He drew a line under the last entry and wrote the day, and he wrote it short, because a physician writes a record and this day's record did not need verbs.

*First refusal. The place found: the boat's own wood, a hold of another trade, two seasons old. The boy sold a verdict and bought a craft in the same week.*

Then, on the next line, in the ruler's exactness he kept for tables:

*The door has two columns. He knows both of them now. Bring out the second one when he is ready, and not before.*

---

The town did the rest of it as the town did everything, at once and without a meeting.

The fishwife had the story by the middle of the week and gave it out in pieces, as a good woman deals fish: the good parts first.

"Eleven, is it," she said, over the Tangs' boy's ring, and the fish row heard her and carried it up the lanes. "A ring at nine years old, and a white one. He took a snake out of a drum and it suits him. So the Heavens do answer, sometimes, late."

The other half she kept for the customers who bought and stayed.

"And the physician's boy's pig turned down a clean boat, and a man lost a harbor on it. That is the end of the fat grey animal's fame." She weighed a basket and did not look up. "Or the start of it. I sell fish."

By the end of the week the two sentences were traveling the same lanes and had begun to touch. There is a boy in this town with a ring at nine. There is a boy in this town whose pig refused a boat that was fine. The town kept both, because a town keeps what it can use.

At school, the six names stayed on the wall, and the room's sentence about Su Yan settled into its next shape, which was quieter than the last one and had more listening in it. Wan Yunchao stopped saying pig boy where the room could laugh. Teacher Lin's class kept its hour. Mu Yun's name stayed one line above his, and neither of them moved it.

And in the tin by the bed of a nine-year-old reader of holds, the money sat at five coins and eighty coppers, one copper fewer than the week before. In the back of the practice book the column of losses had its first line, written small and clean, and next to it a case, and next to that a new law.

The walk was refused. The boat was clean. The reason was not for sale.

*End of Chapter 7.*

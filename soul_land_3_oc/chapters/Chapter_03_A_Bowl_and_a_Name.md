# Chapter 3: A Bowl and a Name

The gates of Red Mountain Academy let the first day out at last, and Tang Wulin came through them with his head down.

It had been a long day. It had also been a bad one, and the two are different things. The children had tried his martial soul on their tongues and found it funny. The teacher had spent the least time of anyone in the room on his meditation.

He walked a hundred steps with all of it on his back. Then, being six years old, he did what six-year-olds do with a heavy day. He put it down and kept walking.

*So my soul is no good. Fine. Then I put in more effort.*

His father had told him once that success was part effort and part talent, and that the effort was the bigger part. Wulin had liked the sound of that. He had made it a rule. Rules were easy to carry, and rules did not care what other children said.

*One day I'll be a famous Soul Master. Everyone will see.*

That settled, he noticed the next thing.

He was starving.

The academy gave the Soul Master class one meal, and that meal had no limit woven into it. At noon Wulin had eaten enough for half a dozen students. By the end of the lunch hour the room had a name for him, and the name had stuck for good. Rice Bucket. Now it was the middle of the afternoon, and his body had put in a new order.

Hunger was new to him. He had always loved food. This week he had started to need it. He walked a little faster, because there was food at home.

Then the afternoon sun caught on something silver.

---

There was a small figure squatted by the roadside, half in the shade of a wall.

Silver hair. Short, and bright even dusty, and the sun had found it from a long way off.

Wulin slowed. The girl was smaller than him, wrapped in clothes that had gone past shabby into gray, and her face was dirty. But her eyes, when she raised them, were not like anything he had ever seen up close. They were large and clear, and their color was a clean deep violet, like the last edge of the evening. He could see himself in them.

Neither of them said anything. Neither of them looked away.

He never could say afterward why he had stopped.

A few loose words carried down the street behind him — older boys, several of them, walking like a wall — and then they were there, standing over the girl like weather over a field.

"Little girl. Where are your parents?"

She lowered her head and said nothing.

One of them said something to another about her hair. Silver was rare, he said. Silver was probably from one of the other two continents, and there were dealers who paid for silver hair and eyes like that, and the rest of them nodded like men doing sums.

The first boy crouched down in front of her and made his voice friendly.

"Hey. Are you hungry? Big brother will take you to eat something good. How about it?"

She shook her head.

He reached out anyway and took her arm, and lifted her up over his shoulder while his friends closed around him, and the girl let out a scream.

"What are you doing?!"

The voice came out of a small boy at the edge of the group, and it was full of fury, and for one second it worked. The boys turned and found a pretty child a great deal shorter than any of them, and their faces changed shape.

The one at the back kicked out, and Wulin went off his feet and rolled more than two meters, through the dust of the road, and came up with his clothes brown.

"You villains!"

He charged them.

The one whose shoulder the girl was on turned around with a knife suddenly in his hand.

"Get lost, if you don't want to die!"

Wulin planted his feet and glared up at him.

"Villains don't get good ends." His voice was higher than he wanted it to be and it did not shake. "I'm a Soul Master. I'm not afraid of you. Put her down!"

He raised his right hand.

A faint blue light gathered in his palm and stood there, waving — a single blade of grass, green and thin, giving off a thread of energy. Rank 3 did not make him a fighter. It made him a little stronger than boys his own size, and no ring had ever been fed into his soul, and what stood in his palm could not have frightened a cat.

The boys went still anyway, and it was not the grass that did it.

Soul power was a thing the whole Federation counted. Every child who carried it went into the record — the government kept it, and so did the Spirit Pagoda — and anything that happened to a child in that record would be dug at until there were answers, by people whose questions cost a great deal. And half the street had watched.

The leader of the boys said one bitter word about his luck, and let the girl down, and walked away with his friends, fast, and did not look back.

The girl stumbled when her feet hit the ground and sat down hard, and Wulin hurried over and crouched beside her.

"Don't be scared." He said it with everything he had. "I'll protect you."

She looked up at him, and for a moment he thought her eyes had gone misty, and he panicked.

"Don't cry, don't cry. I chased the villains off, it's all right." He put his hand out flat, as his father did when he was selling something honest. "My name is Tang Wulin. What's your name?"

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she spoke.

"My name is Na'er."

---

Across town that same afternoon, a boy came home with a basket on his hip.

The gates had let out more than one class. Su Yan had walked his own way down from the school with the list his mother had left on the table: dried fish from the fish row, the small bag of rice from his uncle's counter at the grain house, and the radish basket, which was now a standing item and no longer a treat.

It was good to walk with weight on his hip. Six months ago he had been a boy who got carried up the same street, and he did not plan to forget it.

At the corner above the fish row his feet stopped on their own.

Two streets down, the market noise had changed shape. Something had drawn a knot of grown-ups, as a dropped crate draws ants, and voices were going back and forth in the particular rhythm of a thing that has just happened.

His basket was heavy. His mother's list did not include other people's afternoons. He did not go and look.

And then the warm place under his ribs turned over.

It happened once — the same single turn it had made in the Chamber on the morning of his reading, from asleep to awake and back. Nothing came with it. No picture, no voice, no words. Only a wide low warmth that lasted three steps, like a stove still warm after the fire has gone out.

The pig slept on under his ribs like a folded cloth.

Su Yan shifted the basket to his other hip and went on to the fish row.

---

"That's a good name," Wulin said. "Your voice is nice too."

Na'er lowered her head and said nothing.

"Where are your mother and father? Where is your home?"

She shook her head.

Then a sound interrupted them that did not come from Wulin at all. His own belly had opinions, but this one was higher and emptier, and it made the girl's dirty cheeks go as red as they could get.

"Are you hungry?" he said, delighted, because hunger he understood. "If you can't find them, come home with me. My mom's cooking is really good."

He put his hand out. She looked at it, and at his face — still pink from the fight, and bright, and large-eyed — and she looked for long enough that a grown-up would have called it thinking. Then she put her hand in his.

The walk home took a while. She was half a head shorter, and she tired in the middle of it. He slowed down and gave her his day in exchange. His martial soul, which was grass. His rank, which was three. The boy with the knife soul, whose knife was a cheap knife. His father, who fixed soul machines. His plan, which was to become a famous Soul Master and buy the family delicious food.

"Mom, I'm home!"

"Child, inside voice," said Lang Yue, pulling the door open. "The neighbors —" She stopped.

She had found her son first, dusty from head to shoes. Then she found the small gray girl her dusty son was holding by the hand.

"Mom, there were villains," Wulin said, and told her the whole thing, livelier than it had happened, with the knife bigger in the retelling.

By the end of it Lang Yue's breathing had gone uneven. She knew exactly what those young men of the common streets could do, and she had one son, and the story had walked that son directly through the middle of it.

"Don't you know how dangerous that was? How could you —"

"But dad says boys need to be brave and gutsy in front of villains," Wulin said, with his chin up, and it was such an exact copy of his father that she lost the thread for a second.

He ran in and hugged her leg.

"Mom, don't be angry. Na'er and I are hungry. Make us something delicious, please."

She could not stay angry at a child for being right. She crouched down instead, in front of the small girl who had still not said one word.

"Little one. You're called Na'er? Where are your mother and father?"

Na'er shook her head.

"All right. Look at the state of the two of you. Wash first. Then we'll see about food."

---

Lang Yue washed the road off one child and then the other. Wulin asked, halfway through, a question about the two of them — the sort of question six-year-olds ask — and his mother laughed and did not answer, and Na'er hid behind her arm. The girl came out of it transformed.

The same rag clothes had hidden a child who had been lovely under the dust, and Wulin's cast-off shirt and trousers hung around her like a tent and showed it anyway. Her hair, clean, was silver from root to tip and soft as silk floss, and her face was white and fine, and the soap she had used left a light clean smell in the room, like rain on warm stone.

Wulin sat at the table with his chin on both hands and stared at her.

"Wah! Na'er, you're so pretty!"

Na'er raised her head and looked at him, and did not say anything, and it seemed she had decided against speaking as a general policy.

It was not dinner time yet, so Lang Yue put out two small plates of biscuits and two cups of milk, and set the girl's plate down first.

Na'er ate hers fast. There was no other word for it. The biscuits were gone in a way that had nothing to do with manners and everything to do with arithmetic, and she had drained the milk before Wulin had chewed his second biscuit.

He watched her look at his plate.

"Here. Have mine." He pushed it across. "It's fine, you eat it. I ate a lot at noon."

She hesitated. Then the biscuits were gone too.

Lang Yue sat down with them at the end of it and asked the questions a mother asks. Where was her home. Did she know any way to find her family. Anything at all.

No. No. No.

"How old are you, then?"

The girl thought about that one longer than the others.

"Five and a half."

"Then I'm your older brother!" Wulin sat up straight enough to add an inch. "I'm six. I'm older than you."

Lang Yue gave him the look that says *later*, and stood.

"I'll take Na'er to the Administrative Office and see if anyone is looking for her. You stay here and behave yourself. Understood?"

"Oh."

He did not know why the idea sat so cold in his chest. It was probably because she was the prettiest thing that had ever happened to the house, and houses do not keep pretty things; houses give them back.

They left. The house went quiet, and Wulin, with nothing else to do about the day, sat down on his mat in the middle of his room and tried the other thing the teacher had taught them, which was meditation.

---

It turned out to be easier than he had expected, and he did not stop to ask why.

Sit straight. Hands on the knees. Breathe down and in. The teacher had said that most children would feel nothing at all today, and that nothing was fine, and that what was being built was the habit. Wulin did not want the habit. He wanted the thing itself, and so he went after it as he went after everything.

He shut his eyes, and let the house's small sounds pass, and went down.

Bluesilver grass was waiting for him.

It swayed in his mind as it swayed in his palm, one thin blade with a stubborn green in it, and the strange thing was how much more of it there suddenly was. He had thought of his soul as a plant a child had pulled up by mistake. Down here it was a whole field of the same plant, gone soft and green and patient. It grew everywhere on the continent — lawns, ditches, cracks in the pavement. It died every winter and came back every spring, and it had been doing that since before there were names for things. The world's great monsters had not managed what grass managed.

He thought he heard voices. Bits and pieces, far off, like a market heard through a wall. He could not hold one long enough to know it, so he stopped trying, and did the one thing the teacher had said to do, which was to stay.

Something was happening to the air.

The tiniest existences he had ever brushed against were coming into him — not through his nose or his mouth, just in, quiet and slow, like warmth coming off a stove into a cold room. They did not feel like power. They felt like rain going into soil. They sank down into the weak small pool of his soul power and stayed there and became part of it, and the pool did not splash or jump. It rose.

Not fast. Nothing about it was fast. It was like watching a plant grow — he was sure that if he opened his eyes he would see nothing had changed. But it did not stop, either, and he hung in that feeling, which was the best feeling he had ever had, and he stayed there until he forgot to count and forgot where he was.

A long time later, he came up out of it.

His body felt cool and light.

He was not alone.

His father was sitting opposite him on the floor, coat still on, watching him with a face Wulin could not read.

"Dad," Wulin said, startled. "How come you're home so early?"

"I'm not early." Tang Ziran shook his head slowly, the slow shake of a man who has watched a machine do something clever on its own. "It's already night."

---

"Did you just learn to meditate? In one day?"

"I don't know," Wulin said honestly. "It felt like… little things came into me. And then my power went up a little. Is that meditation?"

His father was quiet for a moment.

"Yes," he said. "That's meditation."

He did not say the rest of it. He had been this age once, and he had found the same things himself, and it had taken him a great deal longer than one day. What had happened in this small room tonight was the kind of thing that made teachers look twice at a child, and then start writing letters to other cities.

What he said instead was:

"You've got good comprehension, my son. That brain of yours will carry you further than grass."

Wulin puffed up, which was his natural posture, and his father let him enjoy it. Then Tang Ziran leaned forward and put his elbows on his knees, and Wulin came down off the cloud, because he knew that posture.

"Men have to be brave," his father said. "You did a brave thing today. But I need you to learn the second half of it."

"What's the second half?"

"Being wise." Tang Ziran looked at him levelly. "Listen. When you know that you don't have the power to save someone, and you go in anyway, that isn't being brave. That's being reckless, and it puts two people in danger instead of one. The brave thing, the wise thing — is to get help. There are streets in this town where a boy alone is a boy who gets hurt, and your mother and I have one son."

Wulin took a long time with that. His father let him, which was the gift his father always gave.

"I was wrong, dad," he said at last.

"Good." Tang Ziran's face did not soften; it sharpened into a kind of approval. "Men don't make that kind of mistake."

"En."

And then, out of nowhere, his father smiled.

"Now. Let's talk about the other matter. You turn six years old, and on your very first day of school you bring a girl home." He shook his head as if the arithmetic of it troubled him. "The hero who saves a beauty, at six. Not bad."

Wulin remembered everything at once.

"Dad! Has Na'er found her family?"

"I'm afraid not. Your mother brought her to the Administrative Office to ask, and no one is looking for a girl who matches her. There's no record of her at all."

"Then what's going to happen to her?"

"That," his father said, "is the interesting question. You tell dad. What do *you* want to do?"

Wulin thought about it. He thought about Na'er eating biscuits like a soldier, and Na'er turning out to be pretty under the dirt, and Na'er asleep somewhere in a building with other children who had no one, which he had never seen and could already picture, and did not like.

"How about we let her live with us, dad? Is that all right?"

His father gave him a long look with a smile half made.

"Live with us? Mom and dad can't take care of two children."

"I can take care of her!" Wulin was up on his knees. "I can look after her after school, every day. I'll watch her. I'll —"

"All right, all right." Tang Ziran stood up and put a hand on his head, and his voice had gone mild in that way that meant the door was open a crack and would open further if nobody pushed too hard. "Let's eat first."

---

Na'er was already at the table when they came out, sitting with her hands folded, wearing Wulin's clothes and an expression of great docility, and Lang Yue was watching her over the pots with an expression Wulin did not know yet.

"Na'er!"

He bounced across the room and took her hand like it was his, and then he looked up at his mother, because he was six, and he could do father's looks but not mother's arithmetic.

Lang Yue laid the bowls out and spoke to the table at large.

"We couldn't find any record of her. Without a record and without family, the office can only send her to the orphanage."

"No!"

Wulin was on his feet with his arms spread out in front of the girl like a fence.

"Don't send her to the orphanage, mom! Can't she stay here with us? At worst —" he did the sum at speed, wildly, having no idea what a family costs, "— at worst, I'll eat a little less."

Na'er raised her head.

She had seen him only twice and heard him for part of a day, and she was five and a half, and nobody had ever stood in front of her like that. Her eyes went glassy.

Lang Yue looked from one child to the other and sighed the sigh of a woman whose house was already being rearranged by forces beyond her vote.

"You can't decide a thing like that for her, and neither can I." She crouched down and turned to the girl. "Little one. This isn't a small thing, so listen with your whole self. Do you want to stay in this house with us? Or do you want us to take you to the orphanage and let them find you a family?"

Wulin did not wait for translation.

"Na'er. Stay with us!" He crouched down in front of her. "My mom's cooking is really good. My dad is nice — he's strict, but he's nice. And I'll definitely protect you. I've always wanted a little sister, and now that I have one I'm going to keep you. Stay and be my little sister, all right?"

The room held still.

Na'er looked at him — at the dusty boy with the grass soul and the enormous plans — for a long moment.

Then she nodded.

"Yay! I have a little sister now!"

The roof very nearly came off.

---

Dinner had been made bigger than usual, because Lang Yue had been a mother for some years and had seen both children eat.

It was not enough.

It was not remotely enough. They ate the vegetables and the fish and the pot of soup. They ate the extra pot of rice. Then Na'er pointed at the lunch Lang Yue had packed for her husband to carry to work tomorrow — the small, careful point of a person asking for something she does not dare to ask for — and Wulin, an older brother of ten minutes, gave it to her.

At the end of it the table looked like weather had passed through, and the two children sat with the exhausted dignity of small animals, neither of them fat enough in any way to explain what had just happened to the kitchen.

Tang Ziran looked at Lang Yue over their heads.

Lang Yue looked back.

"We were going to need a bigger plan anyway," she said, and got up to boil water for nothing at all, just to have done something with her hands.

The house had only ever had the two small rooms, and it did not grow that evening. What it got instead was a divider — an old frame and a hanging cloth, a door that was not quite a door — which Tang Ziran and Wulin raised together in the middle of Wulin's room, in front of the window, and behind which a bed was made up with the second blanket and, at Wulin's insistence, his second-best pillow.

Na'er watched the whole operation from the doorway in her tent of a shirt, and when the divider was up, she walked behind it, lay down without a word, and was asleep inside two minutes.

Wulin stood looking at the cloth, and at the shape of the small sleeper behind it, and listened to the house: his father's boots in the other room, his mother banking the stove, the sea working at the harbor wall under the whole town.

He felt, for the first time in his life, rich.

He wanted more. He wanted it in a way he could not lie down under. He crossed the room to his own mat in the corner. "Dad. I'm going to meditate now." Then he sat down, and shut his eyes, and reached for the feeling again, because now he knew how to reach it.

He wanted to become a Soul Master and make money and buy delicious food, always.

Now he also wanted to be strong.

Before, being strong had been a story to live in. Tonight it was a job with reasons, and the reasons were heavier and better than the stories, and there was one of them breathing behind a cloth in his own room, and she had nobody in the world who knew she existed except one small family and one record in an office.

*I'm her brother now.*

He settled his hands on his knees like the teacher had shown him, and he went down into the grass, and this time he did not come up until morning.

---

The weeks after that went by as weeks do in a small town: quickly, and all at once.

At the academy, the class settled down around its own history. There were the boys with rank and the boys without. This year there was the grass boy, and the pig boy, and the town's newest story, which had run the streets in two days — the repairman's son who had fought older boys over a silver-haired girl and brought her home to keep. And there was the girl herself: two paces behind Wulin everywhere, a small serious shadow, walking him to the gates in the morning and waiting at the gates when they opened.

The class did not know what to do with Na'er, so it did what classes do. It filed her. The file said *the little sister*, and the little sister, in her new old clothes, sat on the wall at every break near enough to hear Wulin's voice and far enough to be forgotten, and did not talk to anyone, and ate everything anyone gave her.

Su Yan watched her from the yard with the professional interest of a boy who had spent his life at the edge of rooms.

She reminded him of himself. That was the whole of the thought, and he put it away and did not build anything on it, because the bell rang.

The meditation hour was where the school turned out to matter.

Lin Ximeng taught it the same way every week. Sit straight. Hands on the knees. Breathe down and in. And every week, four rows back, there was a boy who went down into it like a stone into a well, and came up when the hour ended with his hands warm and his face empty of anything the town thought a boy's face should be doing.

The teacher stopped at his mat the first week, and the second week, and said nothing either time. In the third week he said one thing.

"Your hands are right." He was already moving on. "Keep them there."

It was the longest sentence a teacher had spent on Su Yan, and he held it as he held everything, quietly, for later.

In the third week, the small warm thing under his ribs did something new.

He was sitting his evening hour on his own floor. The thread of warmth that had once been a spoonful and then a line was moving along its course — slow, one way, like water in a channel dug once and then forgotten. And instead of running out at the ends, as it always had before, it met itself coming back.

One circle.

Then another.

He sat very still. The house ticked around him. Somewhere in the clinic his father's voice said a price and a reason, and up through the floorboards the stove kept its heat, and the creature under his ribs slept with its weight against the curve of the warmth like a pig against a warm wall.

The circle closed, and closed again, and kept closing, and it did not stop when he opened his eyes.

He did not tell anyone. There was no one to tell, and no words yet, and it was his.

---

Noon break at the water tap had become a fixture, though neither boy had arranged it.

"You have a lot of friends," Wulin said one day, meaning it as a kindness, having watched three children walk past Su Yan into the yard.

"I have a pig," said Su Yan.

Wulin drank from his hand, and considered the two facts with his whole self.

"Does yours eat a lot?"

"More every week."

"Mine too." Wulin's face went bright, because it had found a road. "Mine's called Na'er."

"Mine's called Luo Sanpao."

"Luo Sanpao?" Wulin tried it twice. "That's a funny name."

"It's an old word," said Su Yan.

The bell rang, and the grass boy went one way with the day's other story waiting on the wall for him, and the pig boy went the other way, up four rows to a desk where a margin line said *ask later*, and stayed a while.

---

At home, the feeding economy had gone into its second stage, and the household was adjusting, as households do, by doing things without announcing them.

The bowl was bigger. Where the creature had taken one radish a night and then two, it now took two radishes and the bones and the fish ends and a potato if the potato was soft, and stood by the stove at feeding hour with the gravity of a magistrate taking his seat. The broth had become the boy's standing work of an evening: bones, water to cover, a long low simmer with the lid cracked, and the lid, if he had judged the heat right, lifting and settling like a slow breath.

His sums page was filling. Radish basket, fish ends, grain-house seconds, the week's total — he was buying his soul's dinner out of money that was a household's money, and he wrote it down because his mother wrote everything down, and because a number that is written is a number you can argue with.

The pig's coat had gone past glossy into something that caught the light at the hip. It slept deeper and longer and woke in the small hours and paced twice around the stove, and it answered the boy's voice the moment he spoke, from any room in the house, like a thing that had decided the question of whose it was.

One evening the boy came to the pot and found a small paper twist sitting on the counter beside it. Inside were dried slices of something brown and warm-smelling.

His father was in the clinic doorway with his sleeves rolled, watching the bowl.

He did not say what the slices were and did not say why. He did not have to. Su Yan knew the smell from the clinic shelves well enough to name it and old enough not to.

What he did say, eventually, in the tone of a man giving directions to a stranger, was: "Half in the broth. It won't keep."

And he went back to work.

The pig ate that night with unusual ceremony.

That was the week the pig also began coming with him.

It had been a stroll first, and a waddle, and then a habit. When the lamps came on along the harbor, Su Yan went down the lane to the breakwater to run, as he had run every evening since the winter the sickness stopped. The pig came out of the house and traveled behind him on its four short legs at its own speed, which was the speed of a full sack of rice with opinions.

So there were two of them on the lane: a small boy running out to the end and back, and a purple creature the size of a barrel arriving in its own time, nose down, reading the ground.

The breakwater was the pig's country. It worked the whole length of it every evening with its nose an inch off the stone, huffing and tasting and filing, and Su Yan noticed over a week that the pig's reading of the harbor was better than his own. It knew where the day's fish crates had been set down and carried. It knew which way the wind had gone at noon. It knew — he tested this three times before he believed it — which of the two boats on the near moorings had brought animals and which had brought fish.

The boy had a brain that kept everything, and he fed it everything the creature gave him, and somewhere in those weeks the arithmetic of the pair changed without a single word being said about it: it was not a boy walking a pig. It was two noses on the breakwater, one of them better.

The town noticed. The town noticed everything; it was the one talent it truly had. A physician's boy and a purple pig walking the breakwater at dusk became one of the town's standing pictures, and the older boys at the academy had two or three jokes about it, and Su Yan let them have the jokes, as he let the weather be weather.

The nose also cost him money, once, which was the lesson he kept.

Out of curiosity, on a market morning, he had let the pig's nose decide a purchase at the fish row. Two crates of fish ends. The pig's attention went to the left crate fast and hard, and the boy paid, feeling clever. The crate had been stored badly for two days, and it smelled the strongest because it was the worst. The fish went into the pot gray and came out gray, and two coins went down the drain with it. His mother asked one question and did not need to ask two.

*The nose knows smells,* he wrote in the back of the book that night, in the terrible handwriting. *It doesn't know sums. The sums are mine.*

The week after that, the nose paid the money back with interest, and it did it in the grain house.

His mother had business with her family's stores — sacks in long rows, the year's wheat and the year's rice, the whole throat of the town's eating. Su Yan had come along to carry. The pig had come along to be a pig, because the creature's idea of a good time had turned out to be anywhere the boy went.

It was in the back row, among the older sacks, that the pig stopped.

It stood still in a pose Su Yan had learned to recognize — the pose of a thing whose smell was wrong — and did not bark or push, because it did not bark or push. It set its nose against one sack in the middle of a row of six and turned an eye on the boy, and waited, with the patience of a thing that has never once been in a hurry.

Su Yan looked at the sacks. They were identical.

He found the grain-house man anyway, because on the strength of one pig and two spoilt fish, a boy says what he has to say fast or not at all.

"That one's going," he said, and pointed.

The man came and looked, and humored the physician's boy because he was the physician's boy, and put a knife in the sack out of pure habit, and a breath came up out of it that a nose did not have to be a pig's to catch.

The middle of the sack had gone warm and damp and was beginning to think about turning.

They cut the row open an hour later, at the old man's insistence, sack by sack — and one more, two over from the first, had gone the same way, and it was a week from being a loss and a week and a half from being a much bigger one.

The grain-house man looked at the pig. The pig looked at the grain-house man.

"Huh," said the man.

That evening at the table, Zhou Hui recounted the affair in the flat voice of a woman reading a delivery note. The family heard all of it. Su Heng, who had watched the pig be fed for two months without giving an opinion, got up and put half a bowl of milk down by the stove. Unprompted. Without a word.

The pig drank it and lay down.

"The pig earns its bowl," said Zhou Hui, and closed her ledger, and that was the highest review the house gave anything.

---

That was also the week the town's new story finally arrived at the Su table, like all of the town's stories: by way of the grain house, which heard everything before it happened.

The repairman's boy — the pretty one, the one with the grass soul — had brought home a girl. She had been sitting by the roadside with nothing and no one. Men had wanted her for the black market because of the hair and the eyes, and the boy had stood in front of her with his little blade of grass until they went away. The office had searched its records and found no family, no missing-child notice, nothing. The family had kept her anyway. A meal a day more. A mouth, a bed, a blanket, a divider.

The table took it in.

"One more mouth and one more pair of hands," said Zhou Hui. "That is what a house is. Both."

"Good for her," said Su Heng, from over his bowl, and it was more than he said about most things.

Su Yan listened to the story and chewed.

He thought about the girl at the roadside, who nobody knew and who now had a house, and about the men who had wanted her, and about records. He had heard the fact, that afternoon at the corner, that the government and the Pagoda keep files on children with soul power — his own file said: *one, Luo Sanpao,* and had been written the same morning hers had not been written at all. And in the end a house had decided to keep her, which is the only thing a record cannot do.

He did not know her. He was not going to meet her. She was a story, and he had three sums due and half a bowl of broth still on the fire.

He thought about being a small boy a house had decided to keep, through six bad winters, out loud every day and never said. Somebody had found Na'er at the right moment, and somebody had found him, and between the two there was no difference worth writing down.

Then his mother put more rice in his bowl and asked him about the week's total, and the evening went on with its own business, as evenings do.

---

The weeks went by.

At the academy, the class kept its charts and its ranks, and the soul hours deepened, and the afternoon meditation ceased being a joke the boys made and became a thing they did, most of them badly, a few of them well. Su Yan's sit lengthened from an hour to an hour and a half at home without anyone deciding it should. The circle of warmth under his ribs closed and closed again, and his legs, on the breakwater, went out to the fifth lamp post before they wanted to stop, which was two more than the winter, which was one more than last month.

The pig's bowl grew, and the sums grew with it, and the ledger his mother kept and the page his father never once read both stayed balanced, which was the house's version of a miracle, and it kept being repeated, which was the house's version of faith.

And Wulin's little sister, on the wall by the yard, learned which bell meant Wulin and, at that bell, was already standing.

---

In the little house in the commoner's district that night, the house was dark and the divider in the middle room rose and fell with two small sleepers. In the corner, a boy sat on his mat with his eyes shut and his hands on his knees, and went down into the blue-green grass. At the end of it, the door of the whole world stood open. He did not come up until morning.

Across town, the bowl was washed and hung on its nail, and the pig slept warm by the banked stove, and above it, in the room where a boy's practice book lived on a shelf by the bed, one line was drying in the dark where no teacher would ever look:

*Today the pig earned its bowl.*

The town had three stories now, and told all three at once, and got a little of each of them wrong, which is how towns are.

Under the floorboards of both houses, the sea went on working at the harbor wall.

It had no opinion about any of them, and it never stopped.

# Chapter 3: A Bowl and a Name

The gates of Red Mountain Academy let the first day out at last.

Tang Wulin came through them with his head down.

It had been a long day. It had also been a bad one, and those are different things.

The children had tried his martial soul on their tongues and found it funny. Grass. The teacher had spent less time at his mat than at anyone's.

He walked a hundred steps with all of it on his back. He was six years old. Six-year-olds put a heavy day down and keep walking.

*So my soul is no good. Fine. Then I put in more effort.*

His father had told him once that success was part effort and part talent. The effort was the bigger part. Wulin had liked the sound of that. He had made it a rule. Rules were easy to carry, and rules did not care what other children said.

*One day I'll be a famous Soul Master. Everyone will see.*

That settled, he noticed the next thing.

He was starving.

The academy fed the Soul Master class at noon. That meal had no limit written anywhere. Wulin had eaten enough for half a dozen students, and the room had given him a name for it before the hour ended.

Rice Bucket.

The name had stuck for good. Now it was the middle of the afternoon, and his body had put in a new order.

Hunger was new to him. He had always loved food. This week he had started to need it.

He walked faster. There was food at home.

Then the afternoon sun caught on something silver.

---

There was a small figure squatted by the roadside, half in the shade of a wall.

Silver hair. Short. Bright even under dust, and the sun had found it from a long way off.

Wulin slowed down.

The girl was smaller than him. Her clothes had gone past shabby into gray. Her face was dirty. But her eyes, when she raised them, were like nothing he had ever seen up close.

They were large and clear. Their color was a deep clean violet, the color of the last edge of evening. He could see himself in them.

Neither of them said anything. Neither of them looked away.

He never could say afterward why he had stopped.

Behind him, a few loose words carried down the street. Older boys. Several of them, walking like a wall. Then they were there, standing over the girl like weather over a field.

"Little girl. Where are your parents?"

She lowered her head and said nothing.

One of them said something about her hair. Silver was rare. Silver was probably from one of the other two continents. There were dealers who paid for hair and eyes like that. The rest nodded like men doing sums.

The first boy crouched in front of her. He made his voice friendly.

"Hey. Are you hungry? Big brother will take you to eat something good. How about it?"

She shook her head.

He reached out anyway and took her arm. He lifted her up over his shoulder while his friends closed around him.

The girl screamed.

"What are you doing?!"

The voice came out of a small boy at the edge of the group. It was full of fury. For one second, it worked.

The boys turned. They found a pretty child a great deal shorter than any of them, and their faces changed shape.

The one at the back kicked out. Wulin left his feet and rolled more than two meters through the dust. He came up with his clothes brown.

"You villains!"

He charged them.

The one with the girl on his shoulder turned. A knife was suddenly in his hand.

"Get lost, if you don't want to die!"

Wulin planted his feet and glared up at him.

"Villains don't get good ends." His voice was higher than he wanted and it did not shake. "I'm a Soul Master. I'm not afraid of you. Put her down!"

He raised his right hand.

A faint blue light gathered in his palm. It stood there and waved — a single blade of grass, green and thin, giving off a thread of energy.

Rank 3 did not make him a fighter. It made him a little stronger than boys his own size. No ring had ever been fed into his soul. What stood in his palm could not have frightened a cat.

The boys went still anyway. It was not the grass that did it.

Soul power was a thing the whole Federation counted. Every child who carried it went into the record. The government kept the record. So did the Spirit Pagoda. Anything that happened to a child in that record would be dug at until there were answers. The people who dug charged a great deal.

And half the street had watched.

The leader of the boys said one bitter word about his luck. He let the girl down. Then he walked away with his friends, fast, and did not look back.

The girl stumbled when her feet hit the ground. She sat down hard.

Wulin hurried over and crouched beside her.

"Don't be scared." He said it with everything he had. "I'll protect you."

She looked up at him. For a moment he thought her eyes had gone misty, and he panicked.

"Don't cry, don't cry. I chased the villains off. It's all right." He put his hand out flat, as his father did when he was selling something honest. "My name is Tang Wulin. What's your name?"

She looked at him a moment longer. Then she spoke.

"My name is Na'er."

---

Across town that same afternoon, a boy came home with a basket on his hip.

The gates had let out more than one class. Su Yan had walked his own way down from the school with the list his mother had left on the table. Dried fish from the fish row. A small bag of rice from his uncle's counter at the grain house. And the radish basket, which was now a standing item and no longer a treat.

It was good to walk with weight on his hip. Six months ago he had been a boy carried up the same street. He did not plan to forget it.

At the corner above the fish row, his feet stopped on their own.

Two streets down, the market noise had changed shape. Something had drawn a knot of grown-ups, as a dropped crate draws ants. Voices were going back and forth in the rhythm of a thing that had just happened.

His basket was heavy. His mother's list did not include other people's afternoons. He did not go and look.

And then the warm place under his ribs turned over.

It happened once. It was the same single turn it had made in the Chamber on the morning of his reading, from asleep to awake and back. Nothing came with it. No picture. No voice. No words.

Only a wide low warmth that lasted three steps, like a stove still warm after the fire has gone out.

The pig slept on under his ribs like a folded cloth.

Su Yan shifted the basket to his other hip and went on to the fish row.

---

"That's a good name," Wulin said. "Your voice is nice too."

Na'er lowered her head and said nothing.

"Where are your mother and father? Where is your home?"

She shook her head.

Then a sound interrupted them that did not come from Wulin. His own belly had opinions, but this one was higher and emptier. The girl's dirty cheeks went as red as they could get.

"Are you hungry?" he said, delighted, because hunger he understood. "If you can't find them, come home with me. My mom's cooking is really good."

He put his hand out.

She looked at it. She looked at his face — still pink from the fight, and bright, and large-eyed. She looked long enough that a grown-up would have called it thinking.

Then she put her hand in his.

The walk home took a while. She was half a head shorter, and she tired in the middle of it. He slowed down and gave her his day in exchange. His martial soul, which was grass. His rank, which was three. The boy with the knife soul. His father, who fixed soul machines. His plan, which was to become a famous Soul Master and buy the family delicious food.

"Mom, I'm home!"

"Child, inside voice," said Lang Yue, pulling the door open. "The neighbors —"

She stopped.

She had found her son first, dusty from head to shoes. Then she found the small gray girl her dusty son was holding by the hand.

"Mom, there were villains," Wulin said. And he told her the whole thing, livelier than it had happened, with the knife bigger in the retelling.

By the end of it, Lang Yue's breathing had gone uneven. She knew exactly what those young men of the common streets could do. She had one son. The story had walked that son straight through the middle of it.

"Don't you know how dangerous that was? How could you —"

"But dad says boys need to be brave and gutsy in front of villains," Wulin said, chin up. It was such an exact copy of his father that she lost the thread for a second.

He ran in and hugged her leg.

"Mom, don't be angry. Na'er and I are hungry. Make us something delicious, please."

She could not stay angry at a child for being right. She crouched down instead, in front of the small girl who had still not said one word.

"Little one. You're called Na'er? Where are your mother and father?"

Na'er shook her head.

"All right. Look at the state of the two of you. Wash first. Then we'll see about food."

---

Lang Yue washed the road off one child and then the other.

Wulin asked, halfway through, a question about the two of them. It was the sort of question six-year-olds ask. His mother laughed and did not answer. Na'er hid behind her arm.

The girl came out of it transformed.

The rag clothes had hidden a child who had been lovely under the dust. Wulin's cast-off shirt and trousers hung around her like a tent, and they showed it anyway. Her hair was silver from root to tip and soft as silk floss. Her face was white and fine. The soap left a light clean smell in the room, like rain on warm stone.

Wulin sat at the table with his chin on both hands and stared at her.

"Wah! Na'er, you're so pretty!"

Na'er raised her head and looked at him. She did not say anything. It seemed she had decided against speaking as a general policy.

It was not dinner time yet. Lang Yue put out two small plates of biscuits and two cups of milk. She set the girl's plate down first.

Na'er ate hers fast. There was no other word for it. The biscuits were gone in a way that had nothing to do with manners and everything to do with arithmetic. She had drained the milk before Wulin chewed his second biscuit.

He watched her look at his plate.

"Here. Have mine." He pushed it across. "It's fine, you eat it. I ate a lot at noon."

She hesitated. Then the biscuits were gone too.

Lang Yue sat down with them at the end of it and asked the questions a mother asks. Where was her home. Did she know any way to find her family. Anything at all.

No. No. No.

"How old are you, then?"

The girl thought about that one longer than the others.

"Five and a half."

"Then I'm your older brother!" Wulin sat up straight enough to add an inch. "I'm six. I'm older than you."

Lang Yue gave him the look that says *later*, and stood.

"I'll take Na'er to the Administrative Office and see if anyone is looking for her. You stay here and behave yourself. Understood?"

"Oh."

He did not know why the idea sat so cold in his chest. It was probably because she was the prettiest thing that had ever happened to the house, and houses do not keep pretty things. Houses give them back.

They left.

The house went quiet. Wulin had nothing else to do about the day. He sat down on his mat in the middle of his room and tried the other thing the teacher had taught them.

Meditation.

---

It turned out to be easier than he had expected. He did not stop to ask why.

Sit straight. Hands on the knees. Breathe down and in. The teacher had said most children would feel nothing at all today. He had said that nothing was fine, and that what was being built was the habit.

Wulin did not want the habit. He wanted the thing itself. He went after it as he went after everything.

He shut his eyes. He let the house's small sounds pass. He went down.

Bluesilver grass was waiting for him.

It swayed in his mind as it swayed in his palm, one thin blade with a stubborn green in it. The strange thing was how much more of it there suddenly was. He had thought of his soul as a plant a child had pulled up by mistake.

Down here it was a whole field of the same plant, soft and green and patient. It grew everywhere on the continent. Lawns. Ditches. Cracks in pavement. It died every winter and came back every spring, and it had been doing that since before there were names for things. The world's great monsters had not managed what grass managed.

He thought he heard voices. Bits and pieces, far off, like a market heard through a wall. He could not hold one long enough to know it, so he stopped trying. He did the one thing the teacher had said to do. He stayed.

Something was happening to the air.

The tiniest existences he had ever brushed against were coming into him. They did not use his nose or his mouth. They were just in, quiet and slow, like warmth coming off a stove into a cold room.

They did not feel like power. They felt like rain going into soil. They sank into the small weak pool of his soul power and stayed there and became part of it. The pool did not splash. It rose.

Not fast. Nothing about it was fast. If he opened his eyes, he was sure he would see nothing had changed. But it did not stop, either. He hung in that feeling, which was the best feeling he had ever had, until he forgot to count and forgot where he was.

A long time later, he came up out of it.

His body felt cool and light.

He was not alone.

His father was sitting opposite him on the floor, coat still on, watching him with a face Wulin could not read.

"Dad," Wulin said, startled. "How come you're home so early?"

"I'm not early." Tang Ziran shook his head slowly. It was the slow shake of a man who has watched a machine do something clever on its own. "It's already night."

---

"Did you just learn to meditate? In one day?"

"I don't know," Wulin said honestly. "It felt like… little things came into me. And then my power went up a little. Is that meditation?"

His father was quiet for a moment.

"Yes," he said. "That's meditation."

He did not say the rest of it. He had been this age once. He had found the same things himself, and it had taken him a great deal longer than one day. What had happened in this small room tonight was the kind of thing that made teachers look twice. And then start writing letters to other cities.

What he said instead was this.

"You've got good comprehension, my son. That brain of yours will carry you further than grass."

Wulin puffed up, which was his natural posture, and his father let him enjoy it. Then Tang Ziran leaned forward and put his elbows on his knees. Wulin came down off the cloud, because he knew that posture.

"Men have to be brave," his father said. "You did a brave thing today. But I need you to learn the second half of it."

"What's the second half?"

"Being wise."

Tang Ziran looked at him levelly.

"Listen. When you know you don't have the power to save someone, and you go in anyway, that isn't being brave. That's being reckless. It puts two people in danger instead of one. The brave thing and the wise thing are the same thing here. You get help. There are streets in this town where a boy alone is a boy who gets hurt, and your mother and I have one son."

Wulin took a long time with that. His father let him. That was the gift his father always gave.

"I was wrong, dad," he said at last.

"Good." Tang Ziran's face did not soften. It sharpened into a kind of approval. "Men don't make that kind of mistake."

"En."

And then, out of nowhere, his father smiled.

"Now. Let's talk about the other matter. You turn six years old, and on your very first day of school you bring a girl home." He shook his head as if the arithmetic of it troubled him. "The hero who saves a beauty, at six. Not bad."

Wulin remembered everything at once.

"Dad! Has Na'er found her family?"

"I'm afraid not. Your mother brought her to the Administrative Office to ask. No one is looking for a girl who matches her. There's no record of her at all."

"Then what's going to happen to her?"

"That," his father said, "is the interesting question. You tell dad. What do *you* want to do?"

Wulin thought about it. He thought about Na'er eating biscuits like a soldier. He thought about Na'er turning out to be pretty under the dirt. He thought about Na'er asleep somewhere in a building with other children who had no one. He had never seen such a building. He could picture it anyway, and he did not like it.

"How about we let her live with us, dad? Is that all right?"

His father gave him a long look with a smile half made.

"Live with us? Mom and dad can't take care of two children."

"I can take care of her!" Wulin was up on his knees. "I can look after her after school, every day. I'll watch her. I'll —"

"All right, all right." Tang Ziran stood and put a hand on his head. His voice had gone mild in that particular manner that meant a door was open a crack. "Let's eat first."

---

Na'er was already at the table when they came out. She sat with her hands folded, wearing Wulin's clothes and an expression of great docility. Lang Yue was watching her over the pots with an expression Wulin did not know yet.

"Na'er!"

He bounced across the room and took her hand like it was his. Then he looked up at his mother, because he was six, and he could do father's looks but not mother's arithmetic.

Lang Yue laid the bowls out and spoke to the table at large.

"We couldn't find any record of her. Without a record and without family, the office can only send her to the orphanage."

"No!"

Wulin was on his feet with his arms spread out in front of the girl like a fence.

"Don't send her to the orphanage, mom! Can't she stay here with us? At worst —"

He did the sum at speed, wildly, having no idea what a family costs.

"— at worst, I'll eat a little less."

Na'er raised her head.

She had seen him only twice and heard him for part of a day. She was five and a half. Nobody had ever stood in front of her like that.

Her eyes went glassy.

Lang Yue looked from one child to the other and sighed. Hers was the sigh of a woman whose house was already being rearranged by forces beyond her vote.

"You can't decide a thing like that for her, and neither can I." She crouched down and turned to the girl. "Little one. This isn't a small thing, so listen with your whole self. Do you want to stay in this house with us? Or do you want us to take you to the orphanage and let them find you a family?"

Wulin did not wait for translation.

"Na'er. Stay with us!" He crouched down in front of her. "My mom's cooking is really good. My dad is nice. He's strict, but he's nice. And I'll definitely protect you. I've always wanted a little sister, and now that I have one I'm going to keep you. Stay and be my little sister, all right?"

The room held still.

Na'er looked at him — the dusty boy with the grass soul and the enormous plans — for a long moment.

Then she nodded.

"Yay! I have a little sister now!"

The roof very nearly came off.

---

Dinner had been made bigger than usual. Lang Yue had been a mother for some years. She had seen both children eat.

It was not enough.

It was not remotely enough. They ate the vegetables and the fish and the pot of soup. They ate the extra pot of rice. Then Na'er pointed at the lunch Lang Yue had packed for her husband to carry to work tomorrow. It was the small, careful point of a person asking for something she does not dare to ask for.

Wulin, an older brother of ten minutes, gave it to her.

At the end of it the table looked like weather had passed through. The two children sat with the exhausted dignity of small animals. Neither of them was fat enough in any way to explain what had just happened to the kitchen.

Tang Ziran looked at Lang Yue over their heads.

Lang Yue looked back.

"We were going to need a bigger plan anyway," she said. Then she got up and boiled water for nothing at all, just to have done something with her hands.

The house had only ever had the two small rooms. It did not grow that evening. What it got instead was a divider — an old frame and a hanging cloth, a door that was not quite a door.

Tang Ziran and Wulin raised it together in the middle of Wulin's room, in front of the window. Behind it, a bed was made up with the second blanket and, at Wulin's insistence, his second-best pillow.

Na'er watched the whole operation from the doorway in her tent of a shirt. When the divider was up, she walked behind it, lay down without a word, and was asleep inside two minutes.

Wulin stood looking at the cloth, and at the shape of the small sleeper behind it. He listened to the house. His father's boots in the other room. His mother banking the stove. The sea working at the harbor wall under the whole town.

He felt, for the first time in his life, rich.

He wanted more. He wanted it in a way he could not lie down under.

He crossed the room to his own mat in the corner.

"Dad. I'm going to meditate now."

He sat down and shut his eyes and reached for the feeling again, because now he knew how to reach it.

He wanted to become a Soul Master and make money and buy delicious food, always.

Now he also wanted to be strong.

Before, being strong had been a story to live in. Tonight it was a job with reasons. The reasons were heavier and better than the stories. One of them was breathing behind a cloth in his own room. She had nobody in the world who knew she existed, except one small family and one record in an office.

*I'm her brother now.*

He settled his hands on his knees like the teacher had shown him. He went down into the grass, and this time he did not come up until morning.

---

The weeks after that went by as weeks do in a small town. Quickly, and all at once.

At the academy, the class settled around its own history. There were the boys with rank and the boys without. This year there was the grass boy, the pig boy, and the town's newest story. The story had run the streets in two days. The repairman's son had fought older boys over a silver-haired girl and brought her home to keep.

And there was the girl herself. Two paces behind Wulin everywhere. A small serious shadow, walking him to the gates in the morning and waiting at the gates when they opened.

The class did not know what to do with Na'er, so it did what classes do. It filed her. The file said *the little sister*. The little sister sat on the wall at every break, near enough to hear Wulin's voice and far enough to be forgotten. She did not talk to anyone. She ate everything anyone gave her.

Su Yan watched her from the yard. He watched with the professional interest of a boy who had spent his life at the edge of rooms.

She reminded him of himself. That was the whole of the thought. He put it away and did not build anything on it, because the bell rang.

The meditation hour was where the school turned out to matter.

Lin Ximeng taught it the same way every week. Sit straight. Hands on the knees. Breathe down and in.

And every week, four rows back, there was a boy who went down into it like a stone into a well. He came up when the hour ended. His hands were warm, and his face was empty of anything the town thought a boy's face should be doing.

The teacher stopped at his mat the first week and the second week, and said nothing either time. In the third week he said one thing.

"Your hands are right." He was already moving on. "Keep them there."

It was the longest sentence a teacher had spent on Su Yan. He held it as he held everything. Quietly, for later.

In the third week, the small warm thing under his ribs did something new.

He was sitting his evening hour on his own floor. The thread of warmth that had once been a spoonful and then a line was moving along its course. It went slow, one direction, like water in a channel dug once and then forgotten.

And instead of running out at the ends, as it always had before, it met itself coming back.

One circle.

Then another.

He sat very still. The house ticked around him. Somewhere in the clinic his father's voice said a price and a reason. Up through the floorboards the stove kept its heat. The creature under his ribs slept with its weight against the curve of the warmth, like a pig against a warm wall.

The circle closed. It closed again. It kept closing, and it did not stop when he opened his eyes.

He did not tell anyone. There was no one to tell, and no words yet, and it was his.

---

Noon break at the water tap had become a fixture. Neither boy had arranged it.

"You have a lot of friends," Wulin said one day. He meant it as a kindness. He had watched three children walk past Su Yan into the yard.

"I have a pig," said Su Yan.

Wulin drank from his hand and considered the two facts with his whole self.

"Does yours eat a lot?"

"More every week."

"Mine too." Wulin's face went bright, because it had found a road. "Mine's called Na'er."

"Mine's called Luo Sanpao."

"Luo Sanpao?" Wulin tried it twice. "That's a funny name."

"It's an old word," said Su Yan.

The bell rang. The grass boy went one way, with the day's other story waiting on the wall for him. The pig boy went the other way, up four rows, to his desk. A margin note there had said the same small thing since the first day. No teacher had ever looked.

---

At home, the feeding economy had gone into its second stage. The household adjusted, as households do, by doing things without announcing them.

The bowl was bigger. The creature had taken one radish a night, and then two. Now it took two radishes, the bones, the fish ends, and a potato if the potato was soft. It stood by the stove at feeding hour with the gravity of a magistrate taking his seat.

The broth had become the boy's standing work of an evening. Bones. Water to cover. A long low simmer with the lid cracked. The lid, if he had judged the heat right, lifted and settled like a slow breath.

His sums page was filling. Radish basket, fish ends, grain-house seconds, the week's total. He was buying his soul's dinner out of money that was a household's money. He wrote it down because his mother wrote everything down. A number that is written is a number you can argue with.

The pig's coat had gone past glossy into something that caught the light at the hip. It slept deeper and longer. It woke in the small hours and paced twice around the stove. It answered the boy's voice the moment he spoke, from any room in the house. It had decided the question of whose it was.

One evening the boy came to the pot and found a small paper twist on the counter beside it. Inside were dried slices of something brown and warm-smelling.

His father was in the clinic doorway with his sleeves rolled, watching the bowl.

He did not say what the slices were. He did not say why. Su Yan knew the smell from the clinic shelves well enough to name it and was old enough not to.

What he did say, eventually, came in the tone of a man giving directions to a stranger.

"Half in the broth. It won't keep."

Then he went back to work.

The pig ate that night with unusual ceremony.

That was the week the pig also began coming with him.

It had been a stroll first, and a waddle, and then a habit. When the lamps came on along the harbor, Su Yan ran the lane to the breakwater. He had run it every evening since the winter the sickness stopped.

The pig came out of the house and traveled behind him on its four short legs at its own speed. Its speed was the speed of a full sack of rice with opinions.

So there were two of them on the lane. A small boy running out to the end and back. A purple creature the size of a barrel arriving in its own time, nose down, reading the ground.

The breakwater was the pig's country. It worked the whole length of it every evening with its nose an inch off the stone, huffing and tasting and filing. Su Yan noticed over a week that the pig's reading of the harbor was better than his own.

It knew where the day's fish crates had been set down and carried. It knew which way the wind had gone at noon. He tested this three times before he believed it. The pig knew which of the two boats on the near moorings had brought animals, and which had brought fish.

The boy had a brain that kept everything. He fed it everything the creature gave him. Somewhere in those weeks the arithmetic of the pair changed without a word being said about it. It was not a boy walking a pig. It was two noses on the breakwater, one of them better.

The town noticed. The town noticed everything. It was the one talent the town truly had.

A physician's boy and a purple pig walking the breakwater at dusk became one of the town's standing pictures. The older boys at the academy had two or three jokes about it. Su Yan let them have the jokes, as he let the weather be weather.

The nose also cost him money once. That was the lesson he kept.

On a market morning, out of curiosity, he had let the pig's nose decide a purchase at the fish row. Two crates of fish ends. The pig's attention went to the left crate fast and hard, and the boy paid, feeling clever.

The crate had been stored badly for two days. It smelled the strongest because it was the worst. The fish went into the pot gray and came out gray, and two coins went down the drain with it.

His mother asked one question and did not need to ask two.

*The nose knows smells,* he wrote in the back of the book that night, in his terrible handwriting. *It doesn't know sums. The sums are mine.*

The week after that, the nose paid the money back with interest. It did it in the grain house.

His mother had business with her family's stores. Sacks in long rows. The year's wheat and the year's rice, the whole throat of the town's eating. Su Yan came along to carry. The pig came along to be a pig. The creature's idea of a good time had turned out to be anywhere the boy went.

It was in the back row, among the older sacks, that the pig stopped.

It stood still in a pose Su Yan had learned to recognize. It was the pose of a thing whose smell was wrong. It did not bark or push, because it did not bark or push. It set its nose against one sack in the middle of a row of six and turned an eye on the boy. Then it waited, with the patience of a thing that has never once been in a hurry.

Su Yan looked at the sacks. They were identical.

He found the grain-house man anyway. On the strength of one pig and two spoilt fish, a boy says what he has to say fast or not at all.

"That one's going," he said, and pointed.

The man came and looked. He humored the physician's boy because he was the physician's boy. He put a knife in the sack out of pure habit. A breath came up out of it that no nose needed to be a pig's to catch.

The middle of the sack had gone warm and damp. It was beginning to think about turning.

They cut the row open an hour later, at the old man's insistence, sack by sack. One more, two over from the first, had gone the same way. It was a week from being a loss. It was a week and a half from being a much bigger one.

The grain-house man looked at the pig. The pig looked at the grain-house man.

"Huh," said the man.

That evening at the table, Zhou Hui recounted the affair in the flat voice of a woman reading a delivery note. The family heard all of it.

Su Heng had watched the pig be fed for two months without giving an opinion. That evening he got up and put half a bowl of milk down by the stove. Unprompted. Without a word.

The pig drank it and lay down.

"The pig earns its bowl," said Zhou Hui. She closed her ledger. That was the highest review the house gave anything.

---

That was also the week the town's new story finally arrived at the Su table. It came, like all of the town's stories, by way of the grain house. The grain house heard everything before it happened.

The repairman's boy — the pretty one, the one with the grass soul — had brought home a girl. She had been sitting by the roadside with nothing and no one. Men had wanted her for the black market because of the hair and the eyes. The boy had stood in front of her with his little blade of grass until they went away.

The office had searched its records and found no family, no missing-child notice, nothing. The family had kept her anyway. A meal a day more. A mouth, a bed, a blanket, a divider.

The table took it in.

"One more mouth and one more pair of hands," said Zhou Hui. "That is what a house is. Both."

"Good for her," said Su Heng from over his bowl. It was more than he said about most things.

Su Yan listened to the story and chewed.

He thought about the girl at the roadside, who nobody knew and who now had a house. He thought about the men who had wanted her, and about records. His own file said *one, Luo Sanpao*, and had been written the same morning hers had not been written at all. And in the end a house had decided to keep her, which is the only thing a record cannot do.

He did not know her. He was not going to meet her. She was a story, and he had three sums due and half a bowl of broth still on the fire.

He thought about being a small boy a house had decided to keep, through six bad winters. Out loud every day, and never said. Somebody had found Na'er at the right moment. Somebody had found him. Between the two there was no difference worth writing down.

Then his mother put more rice in his bowl and asked about the week's total. The evening went on with its own business, as evenings do.

---

The weeks went by.

At the academy, the class kept its charts and its ranks. The soul hours deepened. The afternoon meditation stopped being a joke the boys made and became a thing they did. Most of them did it badly. A few did it well.

Su Yan's sit lengthened from an hour to an hour and a half at home, without anyone deciding it should. The circle of warmth under his ribs closed and closed again. His legs, on the breakwater, went out to the fifth lamp post before they wanted to stop. That was two more than the winter. It was one more than last month.

The pig's bowl grew, and the sums grew with it. The ledger his mother kept and the page his father never once read both stayed balanced. That was the house's version of a miracle, and it kept being repeated, which was the house's version of faith.

And Wulin's little sister, on the wall by the yard, learned which bell meant Wulin. At that bell, she was already standing.

---

In the little house in the commoner's district that night, the house was dark. The divider in the middle room rose and fell with two small sleepers.

In the corner, a boy sat on his mat with his eyes shut and his hands on his knees. He went down into the blue-green grass. At the end of it, the door of the whole world stood open. He did not come up until morning.

Across town, the bowl was washed and hung on its nail. The pig slept warm by the banked stove. Above it, a boy's practice book lived on a shelf by the bed. One line was drying in the dark where no teacher would ever look.

*Today the pig earned its bowl.*

The town had three stories now. It told all three at once and got a little of each of them wrong, which is how towns are.

Under the floorboards of both houses, the sea went on working at the harbor wall.

It had no opinion about any of them, and it never stopped.

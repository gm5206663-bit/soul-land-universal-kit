# Chapter 1: The Reading

Su Yan woke before the house did.

He had done that every morning for a month, ever since his body came back to him. He lay still and listened to the building. Downstairs, in the front room, his father was opening the clinic shutters. The hinges complained twice, in the same order they always did.

Past the last roofs of the lane, the sea was working at the harbor wall.

It was Awakening Day.

Glorybound City sat where the land gave up: a small town on the border between the ocean and the eastern coast of the Sun Moon Federation. Today it was not small and it was not quiet. Awakening Day came once a year, and on that day every town in the Federation did the same thing. It held its breath.

The rules were the same everywhere.

Every person on the continent was born with a martial soul. It was part of the body, like a hand. It slept inside a child until the year the child turned six, and then the academy woke it up. A farmer with a hoe for a soul could do the work of three men in his field. A girl with a bird for a soul could carry the bird's speed, or its eyes, or its voice, for the rest of her life.

The soul was only half of the gift.

The other half was soul power. A martial soul could be fed with soul power and grown on it. A fire needs wood to keep burning; a martial soul needs soul power. The world weighed its people with it. Rank was counted in it. Doors opened for it.

Only one in a thousand people had any at all.

Su Yan had known all of it since he could sit at the table. He had learned it from his father first, and then from the seasons of waiting. His own season had lasted six years.

The illness had come before he was old enough to remember and had stayed for six winters. It kept his soul-veins shut, and it paid for the shut door out of his body. He had spent those years by the window. He had learned which birds used the roof as a road.

Then, in the last month of winter, the illness stopped. It did not fade or wind down; it stopped. His color came back first. Then his legs. His father still looked at him across the table sometimes, checking a sum he had already checked twice.

Nobody in the house said the word lucky. The house had rules about words.

Su Yan had one plan for the day, and the plan had wings.

He wanted a soul that could fly.

His mother was at the end of the long table when he came down, with the household ledger open in front of her and a brush in her hand. His father was at the stove, sleeves rolled, making porridge. It was the fourth morning in a row that the man who treated the whole city had cooked breakfast for his own family. Su Yan had noticed. He had not said anything.

"You are up early," his mother said, without looking up.

"Today is today," Su Yan said.

"That is true of all days."

"This one more than others."

Zhou Hui wrote one more number in the ledger and closed it. "Eat," she said. "Walking is in front of us."

---

By the time the three of them reached the academy street, the sun was over the roofs and the street was full.

Hawkers worked the edges with trays of fried dough and sour plums. Grandmothers who had no child in the ceremony stood at the walls anyway, arms crossed, because they had stood in that same spot forty years ago and had opinions about how it should be done.

The queue ran from the gate down the hill and around the corner. Three hundred children, maybe four hundred, every one of them six years old, every one of them holding on to somebody.

Families came out of the gate all morning.

Most of them came out quiet. The parents' faces were set. The children looked at their own feet. The crowd did those families the courtesy of looking away.

Once in a long while, a family came out loud. Laughing, crying, a grandmother with both fists in the air, strangers thumping the father on the back. Those families walked down the hill with half the street watching them go.

Nobody had to ask which kind of family it was.

The queue moved. The sun climbed. The morning grew long.

Four families back in Su Yan's line stood a father and a son that half the street knew by sight.

The father was forgettable in every direction: average height, average build, a plain gray coat. His son was not. The boy was just barely six and a little taller than the boys around him, with short black hair and the biggest, clearest pair of eyes on the street. Grown women turned to look at him. He did not notice, which somehow made it worse.

"Dad," the boy said. "What do you think my martial soul will be?"

His father looked down at him. "Dad doesn't know either, Wulin," Tang Ziran said. "It all depends on luck."

He smiled as he said it. The sigh stayed inside, where he kept that kind of thing.

Awakening Day decided the shape of a life. That had been true in every era. In this era, at least, a child who missed the road could still live well, and Tang Ziran had been reminding himself of that since sunrise.

"I'll definitely have soul power, right?" Wulin said. He had his face turned up to the sky, as if the answer might be written there.

His father put a hand on his head. "Whether you have it or not," he said, "you are my son first. You will be my son after."

Wulin nodded, satisfied, and went back to watching the gate.

A little further up the same line stood the three of them: Su Yan, his father, and his mother a step behind, where she could see everything.

Su Yan's father was not a forgettable man. When Su Heng walked through the market, men carrying fish stopped to nod. He was the city's physician, and his soul was a silver needle, and once, at a harbor accident, half the street had watched him stand in the smoke with four rings out and bring a crushed man back to breathing. People remembered that. So did Su Yan, who had been six months old and had been told the story more than once.

Su Yan himself was known for a smaller reason. Everyone in that part of the city knew he had been the sick boy for six years. Everyone knew the sickness had stopped in the last month of winter. Some of the grandmothers at the wall had watched him most mornings of his life, from a distance. It was the watching people give a lamp that keeps burning in a bad wind.

He stood in the line with his weight forward. He was ready to be called.

"Su Yan," his father said, quietly. "What are you going to be, after today?"

"I don't know yet," Su Yan said. "But I'll be something with wings."

Su Heng looked at him for a moment. Then he nodded, like a man who had received a professional opinion and found it sound.

The two fathers knew each other from the harbor market, where they had stood at the same stalls over the years. Su Heng asked about the wait. Tang Ziran asked about the crowd. Both men said the things men say outside a big door.

The two boys looked each other over properly. Boys do this in one pass. Shoes, hands, face, done.

"What do you want yours to be?" Wulin asked.

"Something with wings," Su Yan said. "Something fast."

"A bird?"

"Maybe."

"Mine is going to be strong," Wulin said. "Whatever it is. I'll be strong with it."

"That's not a plan," Su Yan said. "That's a wish."

"It's a good wish."

The line moved ten steps. The gate came closer. Somewhere inside the walls, a child came out of a door, and the door of the round building opened and shut fifty times a day, and this was one of the fifty.

"They call us by family name here," Su Yan said. "Mine is Su. Yours is Tang."

"So?"

"So Su comes first," Su Yan said. "I go in before you."

Wulin considered this with the full weight of six years.

"Then I'll go in second," he said, "and come out better."

They shook hands, small hands and serious grips, two boys sealing something that mattered more than they could say.

Then a teacher came out of the gate with a list in his hand. The man looked a hundred years tired. He had been calling names since morning.

"Su Yan!"

Su Yan was moving before he finished hearing his own name. His mother caught him by the collar and turned him around.

She straightened the collar. She looked at him once, up and down, the whole of him — a grain house look, the one she had given every delivery in the years she kept its books.

"Come home with whatever you come home with," Zhou Hui said. "We are still richer than most."

"Yes, Mother."

His father walked him through the gate.

---

Inside the gate, the noise stopped.

Red Mountain Academy held two thousand students in term time. On Awakening Day the term had not started, and the grounds were empty, and the emptiness made them bigger. The buildings were simple: white roofs, red walls, swept steps. Someone had cut the lawns that morning. The lawns were bluesilver grass — the same common grass that grew in every ditch and field on the continent, and even this school had not bothered to replace it with anything finer.

A teacher walked Su Yan along a small path to a round building older than the rest of the academy.

"Parents wait outside," the teacher said.

His father crouched down to his height. "I will be here when you come out," Su Heng said. "Go on."

Su Yan went in.

The inside of the round building was dark for three steps. Then it was not. The floor was carved. The walls were carved. Even the ceiling was carved, in long lines of old writing that spiraled around and around the room, patient as an old man's account book.

A man sat on a stool by the wall with a low desk in front of him. On the desk there was a ledger, a brush, a small inkstone, and a row of blank brass plates. The man was middle-aged, in a simple orange robe with the shape of a soul beast stitched across the chest. His fingers were stained with ink. He looked like a man who had already read sixty children today and expected sixty more before dinner.

Su Yan had grown up on stories about these people. The Spirit Masters of the Pagoda kept the beasts and the souls and the doors to the whole profession. In his father's stories they were tall and terrible.

The man on the stool did not look terrible. He looked like work.

"Come here child. Stand in the middle."

Su Yan walked to the middle of the room and stood on a stone slab that ten thousand pairs of feet had worn smooth before his.

"Hands at your sides," the master said. "This is quick and it does not hurt. Whatever happens, you let it happen. Do not fight it."

He set down his brush.

Then he put his palm on top of Su Yan's head.

The world went warm.

Light rose off the floor. It circled Su Yan's ankles, cold and strange, and climbed in threads through the air, and the old writing on the walls drank it and gave it back brighter, line by line, until the whole room was reading itself.

Under Su Yan's ribs, in the deep place where six years of sickness had lived and then left, something turned over once.

The light came up through him, past his collar, past his own eyes.

Then the writing on the walls stood still.

There was a creature standing on the floor in front of him.

It looked like two animals had agreed to share one body. A dog's face on a hog's frame. Four short, thick legs. Better than a meter of it, nose to tail. A round bulge sat on top of its head like a closed fist. Its ears were small and folded down.

Its fur was a soft, faded purple — the color of the flowers that grew along the harbor wall. Its eyes were large, and deep blue, and calm.

It looked at Su Yan. Su Yan looked back.

"Well," the master said.

Then he stopped.

The man came up off his stool without seeming to decide to. His pen hung above the ledger and did not move. For one long breath, the oldest and tiredest man Su Yan had ever met did nothing at all.

"...Luo Sanpao," he said at last, quietly.

He said it slowly, a word he had not needed in years.

The creature's ears came up. It made a low sound in its chest — a soft, two-part sound, *luo luo* — and walked its slow, wide walk to Su Yan and pressed the side of its head against his hip. Like a cat choosing a leg. Like the decision had been made somewhere else, long ago, and was only being delivered today.

It was warm. It smelled faintly of grass.

Su Yan put both hands on it without deciding to.

"Is that its name?" he asked. "Luo Sanpao?"

"It is an old word," the master said.

"Is it a good one?"

The master looked at the boy for a moment. "It is old," he said. "That is all I said."

His face shut like a shop front coming down, in order, and he picked up his pen and went back to work. He wrote the name in the ledger, slowly, taking more time than a name should take.

"Now the measure," he said. "Keep your hands on it. Do not let go."

He set his palm flat above the creature's head and closed his eyes.

The room waited.

Warmth came up Su Yan's arms, slow and even, like the sea coming in. The creature breathed under his hands. For the length of the measure, the boy, the soul, and the old room were one quiet thing.

The master took his hand back.

"One," he said.

Su Yan waited. There had to be more. The word was too small for how much room it had just crossed.

The master wrote it down. "One," he said again. "That is the number."

"Is one good?" Su Yan asked.

"Most of the children I read today will leave with nothing," the master said. "You will leave with one. That means you can cultivate. That means the world may one day have business with you."

"So it is good."

"It is small," the master said. "It is real. In this room, real is rare."

He picked up one of the blank brass plates and stamped it. Then he cut two things into it with a fine chisel, quick and exact: SU YAN, and under the name, the character for one.

"Ten is the first ring," he said, and held the plate out. "Everything before ten is preparation. Go and prepare."

---

His father was standing in the corridor exactly where the door had left him, hands behind his back.

"Well," Su Heng said.

He did not ask. He looked at his son's face for a while, then at the door of the chamber, then back. Whatever he found did not break anything in his own face.

"A pig," Su Yan said. "A dog's face on a pig, purple, with a bump on its head. It knows its name. It sounds like *luo luo*. And the number is one."

His father took it as he took everything: in order, one item at a time.

"A pig with a dog's face," he repeated. "And one."

"Yes."

Su Heng nodded once, the nod he used for difficult cases in the clinic. "Then you can work," he said. "That is all we needed."

They walked back down the path. Behind them, the master was already writing again.

Outside the gate, the town's noise came back all at once.

And that was when the teacher at the list called the next name loud enough to carry over the wall.

"Tang Wulin!"

"I'm here!"

Su Yan turned his head.

The pretty boy from the line pulled his father through the gate, up on the balls of his feet, bright again all at once, the whole long morning of waiting gone off his face like it had never been there.

The gate closed behind him.

Su Yan watched it close. Then his mother took his hand, and the three of them went home through the crowd, past the hawkers, up the hill, into the ordinary streets where they lived.

---

Inside the academy, the teacher with the list did not slow down for anyone.

"Follow me."

He did not look back twice. The day had worn him thin, and this was his hundredth child. He walked ahead along the small path, and Wulin followed with his hand in his father's, taking everything in. The white walls. The red roofs. The swept steps. Past the wall behind them the street roared, and in here it was quiet and green and clean, and Wulin's excitement, which had climbed all morning, settled down into something more serious.

The teacher stopped in front of a round building.

"Would the child's parent please wait here for a moment?" he said.

Tang Ziran crouched down to his son's height. "Do what the master tells you," he said. "And good luck, son. I will be right here."

"I'll get a strong one, Dad," Wulin said. "Wait and see."

He went in.

Tang Ziran stood outside with his hands at his sides and watched the door close. He remembered standing in a line like today's, a street of boys and fathers, thirty years back, on his own small road. The building in front of his own academy had looked the same kind of round. The hope had felt the same size. He had carried that hope in and walked it home, and here he was, watching his son carry the same weight through the same kind of door.

Every elementary academy had one of these buildings. That was not an accident. The Federation built them first and then the schools around them.

Inside, the stairs went up. Seven floors, one awakening chamber on each. Wulin was taken to the third.

He had never seen anything like the room. The floor, the walls, even the ceiling were covered in wonderful carved lines, curving and crossing, an elegant design that made the room feel like the inside of a decorated box.

Standing by the desk was a middle-aged man in a simple orange robe, with the shape of a soul beast stitched across his chest in gray thread.

The same orange robe. The same ink-stained fingers. The same ledger and the same row of blank brass plates.

Wulin had grown up on his father's stories, and Spirit Masters walked through every good one of them. Powerful, mysterious, respected across the whole continent. This was the first one he had ever seen in real life, and the man looked up and smiled at him in a way that made the whole morning easy.

"Hello," Wulin said, shyly.

The master's smile gentled. "Come here child. Stand in the middle."

Wulin blinked, walked over, and stood in the middle.

Then he could not help himself.

"Lord Spirit Master," he asked, "what kind of martial soul will I have?"

The master smiled again. "That I cannot tell you. After it is awake, we will both see it. But look at those eyes on you — with eyes like that, I hope it is something about eyes."

Wulin's eyes went wide. "Soul eyes? I heard that the first master of the Spirit Pagoda had spirit eyes…"

"Quiet down," the Spirit Master said. "I'm about to begin."

Gentle white light shone from his hands.

Wulin stared at it. Could that be the fabled soul power that only Spirit Masters possessed?

The man raised his right arm a little. The white light shook, then flew up and bloomed into fireworks, and the room filled with splendor. The carved lines lit from the ceiling down — ceiling, walls, floor, one current running through all of it like a life — until every line in the room came together and converged beneath Wulin's feet.

Something comfortably numb crept up his legs and went into his body through his heart.

His whole body trembled. It felt like lightning with the pain left out.

His sight blurred. An unclear image flickered in his mind, close, closer. He wanted to look at it properly. It flowed away like water. At the very end he almost saw it.

Then he could not remember what he had seen.

From where the master stood, the boy was wrapped in light and giving off light of his own.

The martial soul had awakened.

"Ah —!"

Wulin screamed.

The master went from his stool to the boy's side in one step, hands out, ready to stop the ceremony and pull the child free if this was going wrong.

It was not going wrong.

He looked — and his breath stopped.

Golden lines were flickering on the boy's forehead.

They spread down his arms and legs, joining, branching, until they made a whole net of gold under the skin. The master had awakened children for his entire life. He had read thousands of them, and tens of thousands. He had never seen this. Not once.

A faint energy rose and fell in the boy. It came from his lower abdomen — the ordinary place, the common place — and it was weak, but there was no mistaking it.

The master's heart lifted.

Soul power.

Some days not one child in the whole line had any. This morning he had already read a boy with one. One, and now this. Today the luck was very good.

Then the golden lines vanished without a trace, as if they had never been there at all.

Wulin's eyes were squeezed shut. His face said he was in terrible pain. Slowly, he raised his right arm.

His palm turned up.

Pale blue light gathered in it. A small blade of light-blue grass appeared, quietly rotating, extending, and swaying in his hand with a delicate motion. A very weak energy came off it.

"Bluesilver Grass?"

Disappointment filled the master's voice — and then, in one breath, amazement pushed it aside.

He had seen this soul so many times. Grass was the most ordinary martial soul on the continent. Grass grew everywhere: in fields, in ditches, on the lawn right outside this building. Grass was trash. Everyone knew grass was trash.

How could trash give birth to soul power?

Wulin heard none of it.

The pain had him.

It was as if his body were being ripped apart — bone, muscle, flesh, nothing spared. Heat pushed up out of the bones of his four limbs and rolled through him, and his stomach felt bloated and about to burst, and he could not breathe, and then it went on like that for a very, very long time.

Then it ended.

A warm thread of feeling came into the hollow of his palm.

Wulin opened his eyes.

A small light-blue grass, green and full, was waving gently in his hand.

"...Bluesilver Grass?" he said. "This is my martial soul?"

He had not studied spirit beasts. He did not need to. Some things a child picks up by living, and every child on the continent knew which souls were trash. Bluesilver Grass was the worst of them all.

"En. It is." The Spirit Master smiled. There was pity behind the smile, and he did not quite manage to hide it. "This is your martial soul."

Wulin's lips trembled.

He had waited so long. He had told his father to his face that he would get a strong one. And it was grass.

"Child, don't be sad," the Spirit Master said quickly. "You possess soul power."

Wulin's head came up. "I — I have soul power?"

"I measured it. You have rank 3 innate soul power." The Spirit Master nodded. "It cannot be called amazing, but it is real. It means you can cultivate. As long as you put in great effort, you will be able to become a Soul Master too."

"But," Wulin said, "but my martial soul is Bluesilver Grass..."

The Spirit Master laughed.

"Have you heard the tale of the Tang Sect? The founding patriarch of the Tang Sect had Bluesilver Grass for a martial soul! He took the soul the world calls trash and reached the heavens with it, and he defeated the wicked Martial Soul Hall twenty thousand years ago."

Wulin blinked.

Of course he knew the tale of the Tang Sect's founding patriarch. The legends of the Tang Sect and the Spirit Pagoda were the most told stories on the whole continent.

But something was wrong with the story.

"But wasn't the Tang Sect's founding patriarch's martial soul the Bluesilver Emperor?" he asked, very earnestly.

"Eh..."

The Spirit Master was somewhat embarrassed. Clearly, this little guy was not so easy to fool.

"The Bluesilver Emperor is the equivalent of an evolved Bluesilver Grass," he said. "If you wish to become a Soul Master, then you must put in your greatest effort."

Wulin bit his lip.

"Thank you, Lord Spirit Master," he said.

He came down the stairs holding his own palm, looking at it.

His father was exactly where the door had left him, hands behind his back, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all.

---

Su Yan's evening was small, because the family had decided without discussing it that the day did not deserve a bigger one.

His mother cooked more than she had at breakfast. The three of them ate. His mother asked four questions — how many children were ahead of him in the line, whether the master was old or young, whether the room smelled of anything, and what the plate was made of — and did not ask the fifth, which was *will my son be all right*. The answer was at the table eating fish. She looked at it, and did not ask, and that was her way of answering it.

"Luo Sanpao," she said at one point, tasting the name as she tasted everything that came into the house — once to hear it, once to weigh it. "In the grain house, we once bought a whole warehouse of rice from a man everyone called Nine Fingers. Best rice of the year. Nobody remembers the fingers."

She filled his bowl again. "Eat."

His mother set down the ladle.

"One thing," she said. "Tomorrow, first light, I go to the school office. The term gets paid then, and it gets paid in advance." She looked at the two of them. "If this family is going to be counted, it will be counted standing up."

Nobody argued. Nobody asked which money. The accounts were kept by the person who had just spoken, and the boy at the table was not yet old enough to be shown the page.

Su Yan ate, and answered, and watched his father.

Halfway through the meal he noticed the hands. His father was eating with his left. The right lay flat on the table beside his bowl, fingers loose, doing nothing at all.

In this house, a right hand doing nothing at the table meant it had been doing something all day.

"Your hand," Su Yan said.

His father looked down at his own right hand, as if surprised to find it there.

"It waited with me," Su Heng said. "It is resting."

His mother looked at the hand, and at his father's face, and said nothing, which was her way of writing something down.

Su Yan finished his fish.

When he went up to bed, there was a saucer on the floor by his door. On the saucer sat a radish, washed and peeled. Nobody had mentioned it. This was not a house that mentioned things twice. It was a house that put a radish on a saucer.

His parents went quiet in the front room below. Su Yan lay in bed and thought about the creature with all the concentration he kept for the things he wanted, and the warmth came up through him, and the creature stood on the floor beside the bed.

It did not glow. It was simply there, more there than the furniture. It walked its slow, wide walk around the room, inspecting, professional, like a physician making a house call. Corners first. The window. The shelf where two years of unpracticed toys stood at attention.

Its nose worked the whole time, twitching, tasting. The whole world was probably one long smell to a thing that lived in it through a face like that.

Then it found the saucer.

It considered the radish with its deep blue eyes and ate it in slow, serious bites, like a thing eating something it intended to remember.

Getting it back in turned out to be exactly as easy as getting it out. Su Yan wanted it near, and it came. He said, out loud, into the dark, "Go on, then," and felt it fold down into the warm place under his ribs, a cat going in off the street.

He lay back and did his arithmetic in the dark.

"One," he said. "Nine ranks to go."

A year, maybe two, maybe more. He did not care how many years it took. The master had said real was rare, and he had it, and the rest was only walking.

Two boys had gone through the same gate on the same morning.

One of them lay in the dark in a big house on the physician's lane, warm, with a pig asleep under his ribs and nine ranks to climb.

The other lay in a plain room across town, with his right hand open on the blanket, a blade of grass standing somewhere only he could see it, a rank of 3, and one word — *effort* — that he had decided to like.

Below, the front room went dark. His father bolted the clinic door.

Past the roofs, the sea worked at the harbor wall.

The boy in the bed was warm.

*End of Chapter 1.*

# Chapter 1: The Reading

> **Canon reference:** Soul Land 3, chapter 1, and the era's verified pages — the **annual Awakening Day**; **Glorybound City**, the small town on the border between the ocean and the eastern coast of the Sun Moon Federation; **Red Mountain Academy** (elementary, two thousand students, white roofs and red walls); the **Awakening Chamber** — a circular building every elementary academy has, seven floors, an awakening chamber on each, floor and walls and ceiling carved with inscriptions; the **Spirit Pagoda's spirit master** who conducts the ceremony (a middle-aged man in a simple orange robe embroidered with the form of a soul beast); the reading of **innate soul power**. Canon rows: `foundation/CANON_SPINE.md` LSP-01..LSP-10, AW-01..AW-08. All family-side people are `[design]`, listed in `canon_coverage/Canon_Coverage_Chapter_01.md`. **Author rulings folded in (2026-10-02):** Glorybound (same town as Tang Wulin); the illness as soul-veins and body, one problem; the boy's first spirit soul, far ahead, is the **Sun-Branded Wyrm**.
> **Timeline:** the boys' sixth year. Glorybound City. Tang Wulin's Awakening Day is today; so is Su Yan's.

---

On the morning of Awakening Day, Su Yan woke first.

He lay still under the blanket and took inventory, as he had every morning since spring. Feet: warm. Hands: warm. Breath: easy. He ran the check like a man checking a boat before the harbor.

Everything answered.

He still checked.

For six years of his life, the illness had owned this bed. It started before memory. His soul-veins had never taken root properly. The part of a person a soul grows in, built one size too small. His body had paid the bill in weight, warmth, and running. Other children spent those three things without counting. Su Yan had counted them all his life.

Then, in the last month of winter, the illness stopped.

Not slowly. It just stopped.

He ate supper cold one night and woke up warm the next morning. By the first week of spring he was running the lane to the harbor wall and back. His mother stood in the doorway with both hands pressed over her mouth. For a week, nobody in the house said anything about it. Saying it might call the cold back.

His father, who was the best physician in Glorybound, asked two other doctors and three old books.

The books said nothing.

*Why now?*

Nobody in the house asked that question out loud. It lived in the floorboards next to the smell of medicine. Su Yan kept it in his pocket, with the other things worth keeping.

This morning, though, the pocket could wait.

Today the whole city would find out what he was.

---

"Su Yan. Breakfast. Now."

His mother's voice came down the stairs in the exact tone that meant she had been awake for an hour.

Zhou Hui had the grain house's books open at one corner of the table and a pencil behind her ear. She could read, write, and count at the same time and still notice everything else in the room. That was her talent, and she had never needed a martial soul to prove it.

His father came in from the clinic side, rolling his sleeves down. He washed before every meal. Wrists and nails, patient as the tide. Su Heng was the physician people crossed the harbor to see. In this house he was mostly the man who made everyone eat.

On the table sat flatbread, yesterday's fish, and one boiled egg in its cup, watching over the meal like a small official.

"I have a question," Su Yan said.

"You always have a question. Ask it while you eat."

"What if my soul is something embarrassing?"

"Like what," said his mother, writing.

"A barnacle."

His father sat down and gave the egg the respectful attention he gave all small things.

"Barnacles are tenacious," he said. "I have known barnacles to outlast ships."

"That is not comforting."

"It was not meant to be comfort. It was meant to be true." He picked up his chopsticks. "Eat. The walk is long and the hall is cold."

"The hall is not cold. It is the third month of summer."

"The Awakening Chamber is cold in every season. Carved stone and old air. Eat."

Su Yan ate.

He knew what the table was not saying. He had heard it said for six years in other houses, at the market, through the clinic door. *The physician's son. The one who was sick. The one who nearly —*

Nobody finished that sentence in front of him. Everyone finished it behind him.

His mother had said her own piece about today exactly once, with her shops voice on: "Half the powerful men in this city have made their families miserable. You come home with whatever you come home with, and we are still richer than most."

His father had said nothing.

For his father, that was a kind of speech.

Su Yan had his own plan, and it fit in one sentence.

He wanted something with wings. Something fast.

---

Awakening Day emptied Glorybound into the streets.

Everyone on the continent had a martial soul. A farmer with a hoe for a soul could till like three men. A child with a bird for a soul might carry that bird's speed, or its voice, or its eyes, for the rest of his life. But the soul was only half of the reading. The other half was soul power — the energy a soul could be fed and grown on — and soul power was rare.

Only one in a thousand people might possess soul power.

Everyone else kept their souls the way a family keeps an old sword: real, inherited, and never once drawn.

So once a year, every elementary academy on the continent invited a Spirit Master up from Spirit Pagoda. Every town under the Sun Moon Federation held its breath.

Glorybound was holding its breath this morning.

Red Mountain Academy stood at the top of the hill behind its white roofs and red walls. The grounds were swept clean enough to eat off. Su Yan had walked past the gate his whole life and never once been through it. This morning, every road in the city was pouring in.

His mother straightened his collar at the corner and then pretended she had not. His father carried nothing and walked like a man going to work, which he was. Twice before the gate, people stopped him. A woman with a market basket asked about her mother's drops. A harbor hand with one arm in a sling asked, low, whether the wrist would hold for the autumn catch.

Su Heng answered both in short, calm sentences.

After each one, his hand came down and rested on Su Yan's shoulder for a moment.

Checking that what he was responsible for was still there.

Families came out through the side rail as the morning went on. Most came out quiet. Parents' faces set. Children looking at their own feet. The whole crowd gave those families the mercy of looking away.

Every so often a different family came out — laughing, crying, walking too fast, a grandmother with both hands in the air.

Nobody needed to ask about that family.

The envious looks followed them down the hill and out of sight.

Su Yan watched the last one go, and that was when the size of the day finally sat on him.

Then, in the line, he saw him.

A boy his own size, in a coat mended so many times that the patches had patches. He was talking up at a tired man in his shirtsleeves, hands going, eyes huge, explaining at length exactly what he expected today to give him.

The man's hand rested on his son's head.

The same weight. The same check.

The patched boy laughed at something, head back, laughing with his whole face.

*He laughs like he has never once been sick*, Su Yan thought. Then he felt a small, immediate shame. The boy had done nothing to him.

Their eyes never met.

The line moved. A teacher by the inner path read a name off the list. A common name. Two notes the wind took. The patched boy went through the gate pulling his father's hand.

That was all of it. One minute. Two strangers on the same morning.

"Su Yan," called the teacher with the list.

His mother kissed the top of his head. She did that in public only on the first day of a new term and the two worst days of his life. This morning counted. Nobody commented.

"Go on," she said.

His father walked him through the gate.

---

Every elementary academy has one. A round building, older than the rest of the school, standing a little apart. Walls a man could not put his arms around. One door.

Red Mountain's stood at the end of a gravel path. White stone gone gray at the base. It had no sound in it at all.

Inside, the silence went up seven floors.

A teacher led Su Yan up one flight of worn stairs, then down a corridor where the inscriptions began. Not on signs. Cut into the building itself. Lines of old writing ran along the floor, up the walls, and over the ceiling in long spirals. Two hundred years of shoes and weather had worn them shallow.

"Everybody says the marks are the old test," Su Yan said. He needed to fill the last of the corridor. The building was doing something to him, and he did not want it to see.

"The marks are the old test," the teacher agreed.

That was not helpful.

The chamber was a round room. Same spirals. At the center of the floor lay one round slab, worn dark. The only part of the building that was not carved. It did not need to be.

That was the spot.

Every child in the world stood on a spot like that one at six years old, and everything that happened after started there.

The master from the Spirit Pagoda sat by the wall. Low desk, ledger, a row of blank brass plates, and a cup of tea that had given up being warm an hour ago.

He was middle aged and wore a plain orange robe with a soul beast embroidered across the chest in gray thread. His fingers were ink stained. His eyes had the specific patience of a man who had awakened fifty children before breakfast and expected a hundred more before dinner.

"Parents wait outside," the master said, without looking up. "The boy is enough. Su Yan. Family name Su."

He wrote something in the ledger.

"Come here child. Stand in the middle."

The door closed behind his father.

Su Yan walked through the quiet, through the spirals, and stood.

"Hands at your sides. Feet still." The master set his dead tea down. "This does not hurt and it does not take long. What happens happens. Do not fight it."

Then he put his hand on the top of Su Yan's head.

The world went warm.

It came from the outside in. A gentle pressure over his scalp, patient, like water rising in a jar. The spirals on the walls drank a slow brightness, as if the room had waited years for its own writing to be read.

Something behind Su Yan's eyes turned over. Some deep thing under his ribs, in the place where six years of illness had lived and then left.

Once.

He could feel it coming up through the middle of him. Past his collar. Past his own face.

Then the brightness on the walls stood still, the master's hand lifted, and Su Yan opened his eyes.

There was a creature standing on the floor in front of him.

It had a face much like a dog and a body rather like a hog. There was more of it than Su Yan had expected of anything belonging to him. Well over a meter of it, on four short, thick legs. A round bulge sat on the top of its head like a small melon had decided to live there. Its ears were small and drooped.

Its fur was a soft, faded purple from nose to tail, the color of the flowers along the harbor wall.

Its eyes were large and deep blue, gentle eyes, and they looked at Su Yan and did not look away.

"Well," said the master.

And stopped.

The man had come off his stool without seeming to decide to. The ledger lay open under his hand and his pen was not moving.

"...Luo Sanpao," he said at last, quietly.

He said it like a man reading a word out of an old book. Not a greeting.

The creature's ears came up.

It made a sound low in its chest. A soft *luo luo*. Then it shuffled two steps on its short, slow legs and pressed the side of its head against Su Yan's hip like a cat claiming a leg.

It was warm.

Warm like the stove in the third month. Su Yan, who had been cold for more of his life than he liked to remember, put both hands on it without deciding to.

"Is that its name?" he asked. "Luo Sanpao?"

"It is an old word," the master said.

He had his voice back. His face shut like a shop front closing, in order. He sat back down, picked up his pen, looked at the creature for one more long second, and then did his job.

"Now. The measure. Stand still, keep your hands on it, do not let go."

He set his palm flat against the air over the great round head and closed his eyes and listened.

The whole room waited.

Su Yan held the warmth. It came up his arms, slow and even, like the sea coming in. The creature breathed under his hands.

For the length of the measure, the boy and the soul and the old room were one quiet thing.

The master took his hand back.

"One," he said.

Su Yan waited. There had to be more of it. The word was too small for the room.

"That is the whole of it," the master said. "One."

The look he gave the boy was not cruel and was not kind. It was the look of a man at the end of a long morning, giving the true weight without adding any to it.

"Soul power, boy. The measure that decides whether the world counts you. One is one." He picked up his brush and wrote something small in the ledger. "Do you know what it means?"

"No," Su Yan said.

"It means you have the power to cultivate." The master said it flatly. "Most children leave this room with none at all. You have one. That is a door. It is a narrow one, and you will find the world has very little to say to narrow doors."

He set the brush down.

"Ten is the first ring."

"Ten," Su Yan said.

"Ten." For the first time, the dry voice had something under it. Not warmth, exactly. Maybe just a professional's respect for the sentence he was about to say. "I have awakened boys with a whole thundercloud of a soul and a big number who spent their lives telling everyone about it. You have a pig and a one, and both of them are yours. The rest is arithmetic and years."

He took one of the brass plates, stamped it, and cut a single character into it, deep and plain, and held it out.

SU YAN.

And under the name, the character for one.

---

His father was waiting in the corridor, hands folded behind his back, exactly where the door had left him.

"Well," Su Heng said.

He did not ask. He looked at his son's face for a while. Then at the door of the chamber. Then back. Whatever he found did not break anything in his own face.

That was his father's whole art, Su Yan thought later. He could hold a whole verdict behind his teeth and still make his face say that the verdict mattered less than dinner.

"A pig," Su Yan said. "A purple pig that says luo luo. And one."

His father nodded slowly. The nod he used for difficult calls in the clinic.

"A pig that says luo luo," he repeated. "I have met worse patients."

They walked out into the light.

The town's day washed over them. Banners, buns, gulls, families drifting home in ones and twos. A woman whose children Su Heng had pulled through a bad cough saw them and came over. She heard, from three words, everything there was to hear. Then she did what kind hearts do with news they cannot fix.

"He is well, though," she said, looking at Su Yan with such determined cheer that it landed on his shoulders like a bag of rice. "After everything, the doctor's boy is well. Isn't that the gift. Souls come and go, but health, health is —"

"That is long past true," Su Heng said, mildly. "The boy is six. He has been healthy since spring, and he takes after his mother, which is bad for nobody."

The woman laughed and went off to tell the market.

Su Yan spent the rest of the walk home blinking hard and could not entirely have said why.

At the turn for their street, his father spoke again. Quietly. Just to the two of them.

"For the record, I do not care what the man in the robe stamped on your plate."

They turned the corner, out of the crowd.

"You came into this world carrying a body that would not hold you. You held it anyway. Six years, in a bed, without once asking me why the medicine was not stronger. That is the only reading that has ever mattered in this house. The other one is arithmetic."

He was the calmest man in Glorybound. His voice did not shake. Walking beside him, Su Yan knew exactly what it had cost his father to say that on a public street.

"Nine more," Su Yan said. "Nine, and then the first ring. That is what the master said. Ten is the first ring."

His father was quiet for a moment.

"That," he said, "is also arithmetic."

---

The evening at home was small, because the family had decided without discussing it that the day did not deserve a bigger one.

His mother cooked more than she had at breakfast. A bowl of the bay's best shellfish appeared. Either the harbor hand with the wrist had paid his bill, or his mother had decided this was the day to spend some of the family's stored-up luck.

The three of them ate.

His mother asked four sharp questions. The room. The robe. The man's name. Whether the slab had been cold. She did not ask the fifth question. *Is my son going to be all right.*

Su Yan, who had decided on the walk home to be a person who answers truthfully, answered truthfully.

A purple pig. A one. An old word.

"Luo Sanpao," his mother repeated.

She turned the word over the way she turned over a coin at the market.

"It is not a lucky-sounding name. But I once bought four hundred weight of salt from a man whose name was Cow Leg, and the salt was good."

She took his plate and put a second helping on it without being asked.

"Eat. Tomorrow we go to the school office and pay the term in advance. If there is counting to be done in this family, we do it standing up."

Su Yan ate.

Between the second and third helping, he noticed something. He did not say it out loud. He had been raised to watch, not to be watched.

His father was eating with his left hand.

He did that when the right was needed elsewhere. The right hand lay on the table, curled loosely around nothing, resting, steady. The hand of a physician who had spent the evening not looking at his instruments.

There was soup. There was his mother's sharp sums. There was his father's quiet hand.

And past the kitchen window, past the roofs and the masts and the dark harbor, there was the rest of their lives.

---

In his own small room, the boy did his sums and went to bed rich.

He owned, in the official sense: a brass plate with his name and the character for one, an old word he did not understand, and a pig.

Also — though no doctor had told him so, no teacher, and no robe — he owned a spring and a summer. He owned the lane down to the harbor wall, and the breath to run it, and a body that had stopped being a cage.

The reading said one.

The reading did not count the small, stubborn warmth under his ribs. It had kept him alive through six winters without ever once announcing what it was.

He was six. Nobody had taught him that sum yet.

What he did know, by full dark, was that the pig could come out and stay out.

That was the discovery of the night, and it was his own. His parents had gone quiet in the front room. He lay in bed and thought about the creature. He gave it all the concentration he kept for things he wanted. The warmth came up through him. The creature stood on the floor beside the bed.

It did not glow. It was just there, more there than the furniture, taking up the dark as if the dark belonged to it. It walked its slow, expert waddle around the room, inspecting like a physician making a house call. Corners first. The window. The shelf where two years of unpracticed toys stood at attention.

Its nose worked the whole time, twitching and tasting. The whole world was probably one long smell to a thing that lived in it through a face like that.

By the door, on the floor, there was a washed white radish sitting on a saucer.

His mother had left it there before turning in. No note. No mention. As with anything that did not need discussing.

Su Yan put the saucer by the window.

The creature considered the radish for a moment with its deep blue eyes, and then ate it in slow, serious bites. Like a thing eating something it intended to remember.

Getting it back in, later, turned out to be exactly as easy as getting it out. He wanted it near, and it came. He said, out loud, into the dark, "Go on, then." The creature folded down into the warm place under his ribs like a cat going in off the street.

He was six years old.

It was the best trick anyone in the history of that house had ever owned.

He lay back and looked at the ceiling and said the numbers once more, quietly, because the room deserved a decision and not a wish.

"One. Then nine. Then ten."

A year, maybe two, maybe more. He did not care how long. The boy who had spent six years being carried up the clinic stairs knew exactly what to do with stairs.

He slept the way he had slept all spring. Fast, and deep, and without dreams. The sleep of a body that had learned how to build.

Under the floorboards, the sea kept working at the harbor wall.

The boy in the bed was warm.

# Chapter 1: The Reading

> **Canon reference:** Soul Land 3, chapter 1, and the era's verified pages — the **annual Awakening Day**; **Glorybound City**, the small town on the border between the ocean and the eastern coast of the Sun Moon Federation; **Red Mountain Academy** (elementary, white roofs and red walls, two thousand students); the **Awakening Chamber** — a circular building every elementary academy has, seven floors, an awakening chamber on each, floors and walls and ceiling carved with inscriptions; the **Spirit Pagoda's spirit master** who conducts the ceremony (canon: a middle-aged man in a simple orange robe embroidered with a soul beast); the reading of **innate soul power**; and *"only one in a thousand people might possess soul power."* The martial soul and its canon rows: `foundation/CANON_SPINE.md` LSP-01..LSP-10, AW-01..AW-08. All family-side people are `[design]`, listed in `canon_coverage/Canon_Coverage_Chapter_01.md`. **Author rulings folded in (2026-10-02):** Glorybound (same town as Tang Wulin); the illness as soul-veins and body, one problem; the boy's first spirit soul, far ahead, is the **Sun-Branded Wyrm**.
> **Timeline:** the boys' sixth year. Glorybound City. Tang Wulin's Awakening Day is today; so is Su Yan's.

---

The morning of the Awakening Day, the sea below Glorybound was the color of tin, and Su Yan was awake before it.

He lay in bed and listened to the house. Below him, in the two front rooms his father kept for the clinic, the stove ticked as it cooled from the night. Somewhere past the wall, gulls were working the harbor the way gulls did, loud and unimpressed. The smell that lived in the floorboards — dried root, clean cloth, the ghost of a hundred medicines — was the smell of his whole childhood, and it had never once frightened him, because it meant his father was near.

He had spent more mornings of his life in this bed than out of it. That was finished now. It had been finished since the winter, and he still woke up like a boy testing a knot in a rope, waiting to see if the day would hold his weight.

The day held. He swung his legs out and stood.

From the kitchen, his mother's voice, pitched exactly at the level that meant *I know you are awake*: "If you are planning to be late to the largest day of your life, at least eat first."

---

Breakfast was on the table with the seriousness of a festival. Flatbread, fish from yesterday's catch, a boiled egg set in a cup like something in a shrine. His mother, Zhou Hui, had her accounts ledger open at the corner of the table, because the grain office did not stop for feelings, but her hair was done in the way she did it for funerals and weddings and everything that mattered.

His father came in from the clinic rooms, rolling his sleeves down. Su Heng was the town's physician — the good one, the one people rowed over from the far side of the harbor to see — and he did the last thing before every meal that his trade demanded: he washed his hands down to the wrist, unhurried, as if the day could wait.

It could not, today, but he took the time anyway, which was one of the two or three ways Su Yan's father said everything he had to say.

"Eat," his father said. "The walk is long and the hall is cold."

"The hall is not cold," Su Yan said. "It is the third month of summer."

"The Awakening Chamber is cold in every season. It is carved stone and old air." His father sat, and blinked at the table, and picked up his chopsticks. "Eat."

Su Yan ate. He was aware, at six years old, of the thing this table was not saying out loud, because he had been raised by these two people and he was quick, and because it had been said out loud for six years in other people's houses and at the market and through the clinic door.

*The physician's son. The one who was sick. The one who nearly —*

The winter had ended it. That was the whole sentence. In the last month of frost, when the illness that had lived in him since before he could walk was supposed to be at its worst, it simply did not come. He had eaten. He had slept. He had stood. The spring had come, and he had run the lane down to the harbor wall and back. His mother had watched from the door with both hands pressed flat against her mouth. Ever since, the house had been different in a way no one could say out loud: lighter, the way a boat is lighter when the water it has been taking on for years finally stops.

The illness had a name his father used with other physicians — he had heard it once, through the clinic door: *the veins never took root.* As if the part of a person that a soul grows in had been built a size too small, and the body had spent six years paying the bill. His father's needles — and his father's needle was a real martial soul, a silver thread of it he could put through a man's pain with four rings of strength behind it — could only ever hold the line.

Then the line had held on its own, and no one, including the physician with the four rings, could tell him why.

There was one more thing the table was not saying, and it was not about being well.

Today, the whole city found out what Su Yan's soul was.

Everyone had a martial soul; the world was made of them. But what you got, you got. The general's daughter got whatever the general's daughter got, and the fishmonger's boy got whatever he got, and a soul was like a face, handed to you before you could be consulted about it. The deciding thing, the thing families whispered about all year like a weather forecast, was not even the soul. It was the other measure, the one the master would take after: whether there was **power** in it. One child in a thousand had power enough to cultivate. The rest of the world carried a soul like an heirloom in a locked cabinet — real, inherited, useless.

His mother had her own opinion, which she had expressed exactly once, in her shops voice, with a pencil behind her ear: "Half the powerful men in this city have made their families miserable. You come home with whatever you come home with, and we are still richer than most."

His father had said nothing, which for his father was a kind of speech.

Su Yan had decided, privately, that he wanted something with wings.

---

Glorybound was a small town with a harbor, a fish market, one long street, and the sea on three sides of its imagination. Half of it stood in line with them that morning.

Awakening Day emptied the town. The streets around Red Mountain Academy were packed the way they were packed for the harvest festival — a thousand parents and their six-year-olds, slowly squeezing toward the white roofs and red walls of the school. Hawkers had set up on the corners. A woman was selling sweet buns out of a steam basket and calling blessings at every child who passed, which was a business only an optimist would run, because most of the children who came out of that gate came out with their chins up and most of the families came out quiet.

Su Yan walked between his parents and took it all in — the noise, the yellow banners, the smell of the buns and the harbor behind everything — and the whole way, he was aware of his body the way a boy is aware of a recovered tooth: still there, still new, still not to be trusted all the way.

His father had been stopped twice before the gate. A woman with a basket took his sleeve and asked about her mother's drops; a harbor hand with one arm in a sling asked, low, whether the doctor thought the wrist would hold for the autumn catch. Su Heng answered both the way he answered everything, in short sentences that left people calmer than they had been. But Su Yan watched his father's hand come down, both times, to rest on his shoulder at the end — the same small weight, as if the physician were checking that his son was still there.

At the gate, a teacher with a list was calling names by the family name, and the line shuffled forward, and that was where Su Yan saw him.

A boy about his own size, in a coat that had been mended so many times the patches had patches, talking with his whole face at a tired man in his shirtsleeves. The boy was telling his father — with his hands, at length, with his eyes enormous and bright — exactly what he expected to get today. The father's hand rested on his son's head the way Su Yan's father's hand rested on his shoulder: the same weight, the same check.

The patched boy laughed at something, head back, entirely certain of the day.

*He laughs like he has never been ill*, Su Yan thought, and then felt a small shame for the thought, because the boy hadn't done anything to him.

Their eyes did not meet. The line moved. The patched boy's name — a common one, three notes that went by too fast to hold — was called toward the academy's inner path, and his father went with him, and Su Yan's family kept their place in line, and that was all of it. One minute. Two strangers on the same morning.

"Su Yan," called the teacher with the list, and it was his turn.

"Go on," his mother said, and kissed the top of his head, which she did in public only on day one of a new term and the two worst days of his life, and this was neither, so it had surprised her as much as anyone.

His father walked him through the gate.

---

Every elementary academy has one: a round building, older than the rest of the school, standing a little apart, with walls a man could not put his arms around and one door.

Red Mountain's stood at the end of a gravel path, white stone gone gray at the base, and it was quiet the way a stone is quiet. The inscriptions began past its door — not on signs, but cut into the building itself, low lines of old writing running along the floor, up the walls, over the curve of the ceiling in long patient spirals, worn shallow by two hundred years of shoes and weather and children.

"Everybody says the marks are the old test," Su Yan said, to fill the last of the corridor, because the building was working on him and he did not want it to see that.

"The marks are the old test," his father agreed, which was not helpful.

Inside, it was the same quiet, doubled. Seven floors of it — he had heard the stories — each floor with its own chamber, each chamber the same: a round room, the spiral writing, and at the center of the floor a round stone slab, worn dark, which was the ONLY piece of the room that was not carved, because it did not need to be. That was where you stood. Every child in the world stood on a spot like that one at six years old, and the difference between everything in the world happened on it.

The master from the Spirit Pagoda sat on a stool by the wall with a low desk, a ledger, and the expression of a man in his third hour of children.

He was middle aged, with a plain orange robe and a soul beast worked in gray thread across the chest, and his hands were ink-stained, and he did not look powerful and did not need to. Su Yan knew what he was. Everyone knew what the orange robes were. Their kind raised the eggs and the souls of the beasts and kept the door of the whole profession, and this man had spent his morning watching six-year-olds find out who they were, and would spend his afternoon doing it fifty more times, and had probably seen every face that could be made about it.

"Fathers wait outside," the master said, without looking up. "The boy is enough. Su Yan. Family name Su." He wrote something. "Stand on the stone."

The door closed behind his father. Su Yan went to the slab — through all the quiet, through all the spirals — and stood.

"Hands at your sides. Feet still," the master said. "This does not hurt and it does not take long. What happens happens. Do not fight it."

And then, still without ceremony, the man set his hand on the top of Su Yan's head.

The world went warm.

It came from the outside in: a pressure over his scalp, gentle, patient, like water rising in a jar. The spirals on the walls drank a slow brightness, as if the room had been waiting years for its own writing to be read. And something behind Su Yan's eyes — some deep thing under his ribs, in the place where six years of illness had lived and then, unaccountably, left — turned over, once.

*Oh*, he thought. *There you are.*

It rose. He could not see it yet but he could feel it, a weight coming up through the middle of him, past the collar of his shirt, past his own face — and then the brightness on the walls stood still, and the master's hand lifted off his head, and Su Yan opened his eyes.

There was a creature standing on the floor in front of him.

It had a face much like a dog and a body rather like a hog, and there was more of it than Su Yan had expected of anything belonging to him. Well over a meter of it stood on four short, thick legs. A round bulge like a small melon sat on the top of its head, and its ears were small, and drooped. Its fur was a soft, faded purple from nose to tail, the color of the flowers that grew in the lane by the harbor wall. And its eyes were very large, and deep blue, and they looked at Su Yan with an expression that had no business on a stranger's face.

It looked at him the way his father's hand rested on his shoulder.

"Well," said the master, and stopped.

Something in the room had changed, and it was the master. The man had come off his stool without seeming to decide to, and stood there looking at the creature the way Su Yan's mother looked at the harbor before a weather turn. The ledger was open under his hand and his pen was not moving.

"...Luo Sanpao," he said, quietly, and it was not a greeting. He said it the way a man says a word he has only read in old books and never once expected to stand on his own floor.

The creature's ears came up. It made a sound, low in its chest, a soft *luo luo*, and then it shuffled two steps on its short, slow legs and pressed its head — the side of it, the way a cat presses — against Su Yan's hip.

It was warm. That was the first thing. Under all the strangeness, it was warm the way the fireplace was warm in the third month, and Su Yan, who had been cold for more of his life than he liked to remember, put both hands on it before he had decided anything at all.

"Is that its name?" he asked. "Luo Sanpao?"

"It is an old word," the master said. He had his voice back; the man's face closed the way a shop front closes, in order, and he sat back down on the stool and picked up his pen and looked at the creature for one more long second, and then he did his job. "Now. The measure. Stand still, keep your hands on it, do not let go."

He set his palm flat against the air over the great round head, and closed his eyes, and listened.

The whole room waited. Somewhere above them, in the spiral writing on the ceiling, nothing moved at all.

Su Yan held the warmth. It flooded up his arms, slow and even, like the sea coming in — not loud, not grand, just inexorable, the way the sea came in — and the creature breathed under his hands, and for the length of the measure, the boy and the soul and the old room were one very quiet thing.

The master took his hand back.

"One," he said.

Su Yan waited, because there had to be more of it. The word was too small for the room. Above the desk, on a shelf, a row of brass plates sat ready with the year's names on them.

"That is the whole of it," the master said. "One." He looked at the boy, and the look was not cruel and was not kind; it was the look of a man at the end of a long morning, giving the true weight without adding any to it. "Soul power, boy. The measure that decides whether the world counts you. One is one." He picked up his brush and wrote something small in the ledger. "Do you know what it means?"

"No," Su Yan said.

"It means you have the power to cultivate." The master said it flatly. "Most children leave this room with none at all. You have one. That is a door. It is a narrow one, and you will find that the world has very little to say to narrow doors." He set the brush down. "Ten is the first ring."

"Ten," Su Yan said.

"Ten." For the first time, the dry voice had something under it — not warmth, exactly; maybe just the professional's grudging respect for the sentence he was about to say. "Boys have stood on that stone with a whole thundercloud of a soul and a big number, and spent the rest of their lives telling everyone about it. You have a pig, and a one, and they are both yours. The rest is arithmetic and years."

He stamped a small brass plate and held it out. SU YAN, and under it, cut deep and plain, a single character that the boy could not read yet but would never forget the shape of.

One.

---

His father was waiting in the corridor with his hands folded behind his back, exactly where the door had left him.

"Well," Su Heng said. He did not ask. He looked at his son's face for a while, and then he looked at the door of the chamber, and then back, and whatever he found in the boy's face did not break anything in his own. That was his father's whole art, Su Yan thought later. He could hold a whole verdict behind his teeth and still make his face say that the verdict did not matter as much as dinner.

"A pig," Su Yan said. "A purple pig that says luo luo. And one."

His father nodded slowly, the way he nodded at difficult calls in the clinic. "A pig that says luo luo," he repeated. "I have met worse patients."

They walked out into the light, and the whole town's day washed over them — banners, buns, gulls, the crowd thinning as families drifted home in ones and twos. A woman whose children Su Yan's father had pulled through a bad cough saw them and came over and heard, from three words, everything there was to hear, and did what kind hearts do with news they cannot fix:

"He is well, though," she said, looking at Su Yan with such determined cheer that it landed on his shoulders like a bag of rice. "After everything, the doctor's boy is well. Isn't that the gift. Souls come and go, but health, health is —"

"That is long past true," Su Heng said, mildly. "The boy is six. He has been healthy since spring and he takes after his mother, which is bad for nobody."

The woman laughed and went off to tell the market, and Su Yan walked the rest of the way pressurizing his eyes not to do anything embarrassing, and could not entirely have said why.

At the turn for their street, his father said, in a voice pitched exactly to the two of them:

"For the record, I do not care what the man in the robe stamped on your plate." They turned the corner, out of the crowd. "You came into this world carrying a body that would not hold you, and you held it anyway, for six years, in a bed, without once asking me why the medicine wasn't stronger. That is the only reading that has ever mattered in this house. The other one is arithmetic."

He was the town's calmest man, and his voice did not so much as shake, and Su Yan, walking beside him, was almost seven and knew exactly what it had cost him to say it out loud on a public street.

"Nine more," Su Yan said. "Nine, and then the first ring. That is what the master said. Ten is the first ring."

"That," his father said, after a moment, "is also arithmetic."

---

The evening at home was small, because the family had decided without discussing it that the day did not deserve a bigger one. His mother cooked more than she had at breakfast. A bowl of the bay's best shellfish appeared, which meant the harbor hand with the wrist had paid his bill in the morning, or his mother had decided this was a day for withdrawing from the family's small stores of stored-up good luck. The three of them ate, and his mother asked four sharp, specific questions — the room, the robe, the man's name, whether the stone floor had been cold — and did not ask the fifth question, which was *is my son going to be all right*.

Su Yan, who had decided on the way home to be the kind of person who answers truthfully, answered truthfully: a purple pig, a one, and an old word.

"Luo Sanpao," his mother repeated. She had a head for ledgers and an ear for nonsense, and she turned the word over the way she turned over a coin at the market. "It is not a lucky-sounding name. But I once bought four hundred weight of salt from a man whose name was Cow Leg, and the salt was good." She took his plate and put a second helping on it without asking. "Eat. Tomorrow we go to the school office and pay the term in advance, because if there is arithmetic to be done in this family, we do it standing up."

Su Yan ate. Between the second and third helping, he noticed something he did not say out loud, because he had been raised to watch and not to be watched:

His father was eating with his left hand, which he did when his right was needed elsewhere, and his right hand was on the table, curled loosely around nothing, resting, steady — the hand of a physician who had spent the evening not looking at his instruments.

That was the whole evening, really. There was no tragedy at the table. There was soup, and a mother's arithmetic, and a father's quiet hand.

There was also, somewhere past the kitchen window, past the roofs and the masts and the dark harbor, the rest of their lives.

That was the shape of it. That was all it was.

---

At home, in his own small room, the boy did the arithmetic and went to bed a rich person.

He owned, in the official sense: a brass plate with his name and the shape of a one, an old word he did not understand, and a pig.

Also, though no doctor had told him so, no teacher, and no robe — he owned a spring and a summer. He owned the lane down to the harbor wall, and the breath to run it, and a body that had stopped being a cage. The reading said one. The reading did not count doors, and it did not count the small warm thing under his ribs that had kept him alive through six winters without ever once announcing what it was. He was six. He did not know that arithmetic either.

He did know, by full dark, that the pig could come out and stay out.

That was the discovery of the night, and it was his own. His parents had gone quiet in the front room. He lay in bed, and he thought, the way he thought about everything he wanted — concentrated, entire — about the creature, and the warmth came up through him like it had all day, and stood on the floor beside his bed.

It did not glow, exactly. It was just there, more there than the furniture, taking up the dark the way the dark belonged to it, with its drooping ears and its round head, and its slow, expert, waddling walk.

It inspected the room the way his father inspected a patient: the corners first, then the window, then the shelf where two years of unpracticed toys stood at attention. Its nose worked constantly, all of it, twitching and tasting — the whole world was probably one long smell to a thing that lived in it through a face like that. It found the tail of the dried fish Su Yan had smuggled in for a purpose he now understood he had been planning all week, and looked at him over its shoulder with the deep blue patience of something that had never once been lied to about food, and Su Yan gave up the fish on the spot.

Getting it back in, later, turned out to be exactly as easy: he wanted it near, it came; he said, out loud, into the dark, "Go on, then," and felt it fold down into the small warm place under his ribs like a cat going in off the street.

He was six years old. It was the best trick anyone in the history of that house had ever owned.

He lay back and looked at the ceiling, and said his new arithmetic into the dark, one word at a time, the way his mother read out salt totals.

"One."

"Nine left."

"Ten."

Nine ranks, then the first ring. The master had said it; the whole world ran on the same great stairs, and he had just found out his feet were on them. He was the boy who had spent six years being carried up the stairs at the clinic. Now he knew what his hands were for.

He did not know — no one had told him, and no one could — what the round room had actually seen that morning, or why an old word out of very old books had come to rest in a physician's house by the sea. The master in the orange robe had not told him, because the master did not know either. No one did.

That was all far away. Tonight there was a boy, a brass plate, a fish thief asleep in the warm place under his ribs, and the sea doing its slow work outside the wall.

He slept the way he had slept all spring: fast, and deep, and without dreams — the sleep of a body that had learned to build, and could not wait to get back to it.

Under the window, the harbor bell knocked once for the hour. The little room held nothing but two breathing things and the sea.

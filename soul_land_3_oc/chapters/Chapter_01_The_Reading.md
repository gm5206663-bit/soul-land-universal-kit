Glorybound City woke early on Awakening Day, all at once and in every direction — as it did for the harvest festival and the tide races.

By the time the sun cleared the eastern headland, the street that ran down to the harbor was packed three deep. Parents, six-year-olds, grandparents with no child in the ceremony who had come anyway, hawkers working the crowd with sweet buns and paper flags. The fishwives had done half their trade early so they could stand on the seawall and watch the families pass, because on this one morning the whole town had the same business.

Everyone had a martial soul; the world was built on it. A hoe for a soul meant a farmer who could till like three men. A bird for a soul meant a child who would carry some part of the bird for life — its speed, or its voice, or its eyes. But the soul was only half of the reading. The other half was the power under it. Soul power was the energy a soul could be fed with and grown on, and it was rare: only one in a thousand people might possess soul power. For everyone else the soul stayed exactly what it was, like a good tool kept in a drawer.

By law and custom the ceremony ran the same everywhere in the Sun Moon Federation: the year a child turned six, the academy invited a Spirit Master up from the Spirit Pagoda, and on the annual Awakening Day the town found out what its children were worth.

Halfway up the hill from the fish market, above a physician's clinic with a green door, a boy was eating the largest breakfast of his life.

---

Su Yan had been awake since before the gulls, and he still did not take that for granted. Six winters of that boy's life had been spent in the bed in the corner room with the fever for company. This spring he had gotten up early every single morning and done nothing with the mornings except have them.

Below the floorboards, the clinic's two rooms were dark and orderly. Up through the boards came the smell that was the whole of his childhood — dried root, clean cloth, the ghost of a hundred medicines — and it was the smell of his father being near.

On the shelf by the door, the family's old soul communicator chirped and lit. The academy's recorded voice gave the day's notice for the fourth time since yesterday: gates open at the eighth hour; one parent per child; do not crowd the yard. His mother had replayed it three times at supper with her arms crossed. She had replayed it once more at midnight, when she thought the house was asleep, and nobody had ever mentioned that, and nobody ever would.

That was how the house had been since spring. Nobody said the word hope out loud. It simply kept appearing in small places, the way a coin appears in a coat lining.

"Eat," said his mother.

Zhou Hui sat with the grain-house ledger open at her elbow. She was the only person in that house who could read, write, and count at the same time while noticing everything else in the room. The table had the seriousness of a festival on it: flatbread, yesterday's fish, one boiled egg in its cup.

Su Yan said, "I have a question."

"You always have a question. Ask it while you eat."

"What if my soul is something embarrassing."

"Like what," said his mother, writing.

"A barnacle."

His father came in from the clinic side, rolling his sleeves down over his wrists. Su Heng was Glorybound's physician — the good one, the one the far side of the harbor rowed across to see — and he did the same thing before every meal in that house: washed his hands down to the wrist, unhurried, as if time were something the family owned plenty of.

"Barnacles are tenacious," his father said, drying his hands. "I have known barnacles to outlast ships."

"That is not comforting."

"It was not meant to be comfort. It was meant to be true." He sat down and looked at the egg with the respectful attention he gave to all small things. "Eat. The walk is long and the hall is cold."

"The hall is not cold. It is the third month of summer."

"The Awakening Chamber is cold in every season. It is carved stone and old air." He picked up his chopsticks. "Eat."

Su Yan ate. He knew what the table was not saying, because he had been raised by these two people, and because he had heard it said for six years in other houses, at the market, through the clinic door: *the physician's son. The one who was sick. The one who nearly —*

The winter had ended that sentence. In the last month of frost, when the illness that had lived in him since before he could remember should have been at its worst, it had simply not come. He had eaten. He had slept. He had stood up out of bed one morning and stayed up. By the first warm week he was running the lane down to the harbor wall and back while his mother stood in the doorway with both hands pressed flat against her mouth.

The illness had a name his father used with other physicians, quiet, through the clinic door. *The veins never took root.* As if the part of a person that a soul grows in had been built one size too small, and the body had spent six years paying the bill. His father's needle — a real martial soul, a silver thread he could put through a man's pain with four rings behind it — could only ever hold the line.

Then the line had held on its own. No one, including the physician with the four rings, could say why, and no one wanted to say so out loud. Su Yan kept the question privately, in a pocket of his mind, checked on often — a loose tooth he did not want pulled.

There was one more thing the table was not saying, and it was not about being well.

Today the whole city found out what Su Yan's soul was.

His mother had an opinion on that, and she had expressed it exactly once, in her shops voice, with the pencil behind her ear: "Half the powerful men in this city have made their families miserable. You come home with whatever you come home with, and we are still richer than most."

His father had said nothing, which for his father was a kind of speech.

Su Yan had decided, privately, that he wanted something with wings.

---

Awakening Day emptied the town into the streets around Red Mountain Academy.

Su Yan had walked past the academy his whole life and never been through its gate. Even from the outside it was the cleanest place he knew: the two thousand students' worth of buildings sitting behind their white roofs and red walls, grounds swept like a physician's table, everything in order for the first day of the new term. It was one of the few buildings in Glorybound that was not in a hurry, and this morning every road in the city was pouring into it.

His mother straightened his collar at the corner and then pretended she had not. His father carried nothing and walked like a man going to work, which he was: twice before the gate, he was stopped. A woman with a market basket caught his sleeve about her mother's drops. A harbor hand with one arm in a sling asked, low, whether the wrist would hold for the autumn catch. Su Heng answered both in short sentences that left people calmer than they had been.

At the end of each answer, his father's hand came down and rested on Su Yan's shoulder for a moment — the physician checking that what he was responsible for was still there.

At the gate, a teacher with a list was calling family names, and the crowd moved at festival speed, in slow sighs.

Families came out through the side rail as the morning went on. Most of them came out quiet — the parents' faces set, the children looking at their own feet, and everyone in the crowd giving those families the mercy of looking away. Every so often a different kind of family came out: laughing, crying, walking too fast, a grandmother with both hands in the air. Nobody needed to ask what that meant. The envious glances followed those families down the hill and out of sight, and Su Yan watched them go and felt the size of the day sit on him properly for the first time.

Then, in the line, he saw him.

A boy about his own size, in a coat that had been mended so many times the patches had patches, talking at a tired man in his shirtsleeves. The boy was telling his father, with his hands, at length, with his eyes enormous and bright, exactly what he expected today to give him. The father's hand rested on his son's head — the same weight, the same check as Su Yan's father's hand on his shoulder.

The patched boy laughed at something, head back, entirely certain of the day.

*He laughs like he has never been ill*, Su Yan thought, and then felt a small, immediate shame, because the boy had done nothing to him.

Their eyes never met. The line moved. A teacher by the inner path read a name off the list — a common name, two notes the wind took — and the patched boy went through the gate pulling his father's hand, and Su Yan's family kept their place, and that was all of it. One minute, two strangers on the same morning.

"Su Yan," called the teacher with the list.

His mother kissed the top of his head — something she did in public only on the first day of a new term and the two worst days of his life, which meant that this morning counted, and nobody commented on it.

"Go on," she said.

His father walked him through the gate.

---

Every elementary academy has one: a round building, older than the rest of the school, standing a little apart, with walls a man could not put his arms around and one door.

Red Mountain's stood at the end of a gravel path, white stone gone gray at the base, and it was stone-quiet. Inside, the quiet doubled. The teacher led him up one flight of worn stairs, then down a corridor where the inscriptions began. Not on signs — cut into the building itself. Low lines of old writing ran along the floor, up the walls, and over the curve of the ceiling in long patient spirals, worn shallow by two hundred years of shoes and weather and children.

"Everybody says the marks are the old test," Su Yan said, to fill the last of the corridor, because the building was working on him and he did not want it to see that.

"The marks are the old test," the teacher agreed, which was not helpful.

The chamber was a round room. Same spirals. At the center of the floor, one round slab, worn dark — the only part of the whole building that was not carved, because it did not need to be. That was the spot. Every child in the world stood on a spot like that one at six years old, and the difference between everything that followed happened on it.

The master from the Spirit Pagoda sat by the wall with a low desk, a ledger, a row of blank brass plates, and the expression of a man in his third hour of children.

He was middle aged, in a plain orange robe with a soul beast worked in gray thread across the chest. His fingers were ink stained and his tea had gone cold an hour ago, and he did not look powerful, and did not need to. Su Yan knew what the orange robes were. His father's stories had plenty of them in the useful parts: the Spirit Pagoda kept the eggs and the souls of the beasts and kept the door of the whole profession, and the man at the desk had spent his morning watching six-year-olds find out what they were, and would spend his afternoon doing it fifty more times. He had probably seen every face a child could make about it.

"Parents wait outside," the master said, without looking up. "The boy is enough. Su Yan. Family name Su." He wrote something in the ledger. "Come here child. Stand in the middle."

The door closed behind his father. Su Yan walked, through all the quiet, through all the spirals, and stood.

"Hands at your sides. Feet still." The master set his cold tea down. "This does not hurt and it does not take long. What happens happens. Do not fight it."

Then he put his hand on the top of Su Yan's head.

The world went warm.

It came from the outside in: a gentle pressure over his scalp, patient, like water rising in a jar. The spirals on the walls drank a slow brightness, as if the room had waited years for its own writing to be read. And something behind Su Yan's eyes — some deep thing under his ribs, in the place where six years of illness had lived and then, unaccountably, left — turned over, once.

*Oh*, he thought. *There you are.*

It rose. He could not see it yet, but he could feel it, a weight coming up through the middle of him, past his collar, past his own face — and then the brightness on the walls stood still, the master's hand lifted, and Su Yan opened his eyes.

There was a creature standing on the floor in front of him.

It had a face much like a dog and a body rather like a hog, and there was more of it than Su Yan had expected of anything belonging to him: well over a meter of it, on four short, thick legs. A round bulge like a small melon sat on the top of its head. Its ears were small and drooped. Its fur was a soft, faded purple from nose to tail, the color of the flowers that grew along the harbor wall. And its eyes were large, and deep blue, and they looked at Su Yan with an expression that had no business on a stranger's face.

It looked at him, and the look had the same weight as his father's hand on his shoulder.

"Well," said the master, and stopped.

The man had come off his stool without seeming to decide to, and stood looking at the creature with the stillness Su Yan's mother kept for the harbor before a weather turn. The ledger lay open under his hand and his pen was not moving.

"...Luo Sanpao," he said at last, quietly, and it was not a greeting. He said it the way a man says a word he has only read in old books and never once expected to stand on his own floor.

The creature's ears came up. It made a sound low in its chest — a soft *luo luo* — and shuffled two steps on its short, slow legs and pressed the side of its head against Su Yan's hip like a cat claiming a leg.

It was warm. That was the first thing. Under all the strangeness, it was warm — the plain, banked warmth of the stove in the third month. Su Yan, who had been cold for more of his life than he liked to remember, put both hands on it before he had decided anything at all.

"Is that its name?" he asked. "Luo Sanpao?"

"It is an old word," the master said. He had his voice back. His face shut — a shop front closing in order — and he sat back down and picked up his pen and looked at the creature one more long second, and then he did his job. "Now. The measure. Stand still, keep your hands on it, do not let go."

He set his palm flat against the air over the great round head and closed his eyes and listened.

The whole room waited. Somewhere above them, in the spiral writing on the ceiling, nothing moved at all.

Su Yan held the warmth. It came up his arms, slow and even, like the sea coming in — not loud, not grand; just the sea, coming in — and the creature breathed under his hands, and for the length of the measure the boy and the soul and the old room were one very quiet thing.

The master took his hand back.

"One," he said.

Su Yan waited, because there had to be more of it. The word was too small for the room.

"That is the whole of it," the master said. "One." The look he gave the boy was not cruel and was not kind; it was the look of a man at the end of a long morning, giving the true weight without adding any to it. "Soul power, boy. The measure that decides whether the world counts you. One is one." He picked up his brush and wrote something small in the ledger. "Do you know what it means?"

"No," Su Yan said.

"It means you have the power to cultivate." The master said it flatly. "Most children leave this room with none at all. You have one. That is a door. It is a narrow one, and you will find the world has very little to say to narrow doors." He set the brush down. "Ten is the first ring."

"Ten," Su Yan said.

"Ten." For the first time, the dry voice had something under it — not warmth exactly; maybe the professional's grudging respect for the sentence he was about to say. "Boys have stood on that stone with a whole thundercloud of a soul and a big number, and spent their lives telling everyone about it. You have a pig and a one, and both of them are yours. The rest is arithmetic and years."

He took one of the brass plates, stamped it, and cut a single character into it, deep and plain, and held it out.

SU YAN. And under the name, the character for one.

---

His father was waiting in the corridor with his hands folded behind his back, exactly where the door had left him.

"Well," Su Heng said. He did not ask. He looked at his son's face for a while, and then at the door of the chamber, and then back, and whatever he found did not break anything in his own face. That was his father's whole art, Su Yan thought later: he could hold a whole verdict behind his teeth and still make his face say that the verdict mattered less than dinner.

"A pig," Su Yan said. "A purple pig that says luo luo. And one."

His father nodded slowly, the nod he used for difficult calls in the clinic. "A pig that says luo luo," he repeated. "I have met worse patients."

They walked out into the light, and the town's day washed over them — banners, buns, gulls, the crowd thinning as families drifted home in ones and twos. A woman whose children Su Heng had pulled through a bad cough saw them and came over, and heard, from three words, everything there was to hear, and did what kind hearts do with news they cannot fix.

"He is well, though," she said, looking at Su Yan with such determined cheer that it landed on his shoulders like a bag of rice. "After everything, the doctor's boy is well. Isn't that the gift. Souls come and go, but health, health is —"

"That is long past true," Su Heng said, mildly. "The boy is six. He has been healthy since spring, and he takes after his mother, which is bad for nobody."

The woman laughed and went off to tell the market, and Su Yan spent the rest of the walk home blinking hard, and could not entirely have said why.

At the turn for their street, his father said, in a voice pitched exactly to the two of them:

"For the record, I do not care what the man in the robe stamped on your plate." They turned the corner, out of the crowd. "You came into this world carrying a body that would not hold you, and you held it anyway, for six years, in a bed, without once asking me why the medicine was not stronger. That is the only reading that has ever mattered in this house. The other one is arithmetic."

He was the town's calmest man, and his voice did not so much as shake, and Su Yan, walking beside him, was almost seven and knew exactly what it had cost him to say that on a public street.

"Nine more," Su Yan said. "Nine, and then the first ring. That is what the master said. Ten is the first ring."

"That," his father said, after a moment, "is also arithmetic."

---

The evening at home was small, because the family had decided without discussing it that the day did not deserve a bigger one. His mother cooked more than she had at breakfast. A bowl of the bay's best shellfish appeared, which meant the harbor hand with the wrist had paid his bill, or that his mother had decided this was the day to spend some of the family's stored-up good luck. The three of them ate, and his mother asked four sharp, specific questions — the room, the robe, the man's name, whether the stone slab had been cold — and did not ask the fifth question, which was *is my son going to be all right*.

Su Yan, who had decided during the walk home that he would be a person who answers truthfully, answered truthfully: a purple pig, a one, and an old word.

"Luo Sanpao," his mother repeated. She had a head for ledgers and an ear for nonsense, and she turned the word over as she would a coin at the market. "It is not a lucky-sounding name. But I once bought four hundred weight of salt from a man whose name was Cow Leg, and the salt was good." She took his plate and put a second helping on it without being asked. "Eat. Tomorrow we go to the school office and pay the term in advance, because if there is counting to be done in this family, we do it standing up."

Su Yan ate. Between the second and third helping he noticed something he did not say out loud, because he had been raised to watch and not to be watched: his father was eating with his left hand, which he did when the right was needed elsewhere. The right hand lay on the table, curled loosely around nothing, resting, steady — the hand of a physician who had spent the evening not looking at his instruments.

There was soup, and a mother's sharp sums, and a father's quiet hand.

There was also, somewhere past the kitchen window, past the roofs and the masts and the dark harbor, the rest of their lives.

---

In his own small room, the boy did his arithmetic and went to sleep rich.

He owned, in the official sense, a brass plate with his name and the character for one, an old word he did not understand, and a pig.

Also — though no doctor had told him so, no teacher, and no robe — he owned a spring and a summer. He owned the lane down to the harbor wall, and the breath to run it, and a body that had stopped being a cage. The reading said one. The reading did not count the small stubborn thing that had kept him alive through six winters without ever once announcing what it was. He was six. Nobody had taught him that sum yet.

What he did know, by full dark, was that the pig could come out and stay out.

That was the discovery of the night, and it was his own. His parents had gone quiet in the front room. He lay in bed and thought about the creature with the whole concentration he kept for things he wanted — entire, patient — and the warmth came up through him like it had all day, and stood on the floor beside the bed.

It did not glow, exactly. It was just there, more there than the furniture, taking up the dark as if the dark belonged to it, with its drooping ears and its round head and its slow, expert, waddling walk. It inspected the room like a physician making a house call: the corners first, then the window, then the shelf where two years of unpracticed toys stood at attention. Its nose worked constantly, twitching and tasting — the whole world was probably one long smell to a thing that lived in it through a face like that.

From the kitchen end of the house came a small, definite sound: his mother setting something down on the floor of the corridor. Just outside his door.

Su Yan got up and looked. It was a white radish from the crock, washed, sitting on a saucer, like an offering left for a household ghost.

He looked down the dark corridor. The last lamp was out.

"Thank you," he said, to the corridor, quietly.

He put the saucer on the floor by the window. The creature considered the radish for a moment with its deep blue eyes, giving it the respectful attention his father gave the egg at breakfast, and then ate it with the unhurried gravity of a small official.

Getting it back in, later, turned out to be exactly as easy as getting it out: he wanted it near, and it came; he said, out loud, into the dark, "Go on, then," and felt it fold down into the warm place under his ribs, like a cat going in off the street.

He was six years old. It was the best trick anyone in the history of that house had ever owned.

He lay back and looked at the ceiling, and said his new numbers into the dark, one word at a time, in the cadence his mother used for salt totals.

"One."

"Nine left."

"Ten."

Nine ranks, then the first ring. The master had said it; the whole world ran on the same great stairs, and he had just found out his feet were on them. He was the boy who had spent six years being carried up the stairs at the clinic. Now he knew what his hands were for.

He did not know — no one had told him, and no one could — what the round room had actually seen that morning, or why an old word out of very old books had come to rest in a physician's house by the sea. The master in the orange robe had not told him, because the master did not know either. No one did.

That was all far away. Tonight there was a boy, a brass plate, a radish thief asleep in the warm place under his ribs, and the sea doing its slow work outside the wall.

He slept as he had slept all spring: fast, and deep, and without dreams — the sleep of a body that had learned to build, and could not wait to get back to it.

Under the window, the harbor bell knocked once for the hour. The little room held nothing but two breathing things and the sea.

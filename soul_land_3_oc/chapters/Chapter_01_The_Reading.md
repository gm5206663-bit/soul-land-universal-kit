# Chapter 1: The Reading

> **Canon reference:** Soul Land 3, chapter 1, and the era's verified pages — the **annual Awakening Day**; **Glorybound City**, the small town on the border between the ocean and the eastern coast of the Sun Moon Federation; **Red Mountain Academy** (elementary, two thousand students, white roofs and red walls); the **Awakening Chamber** — a circular building every elementary academy has, seven floors, an awakening chamber on each, floor and walls and ceiling carved with inscriptions; the **Spirit Pagoda's spirit master** who conducts the ceremony (a middle-aged man in a simple orange robe embroidered with the form of a soul beast); the reading of **innate soul power**. > **Timeline:** the boys' sixth year. Glorybound City. Tang Wulin's Awakening Day is today; so is Su Yan's.

---

Glorybound City had one long street, two hills, a harbor, and a habit of waking early. On Awakening Day it woke before the sun.

By first light the street in front of Red Mountain Academy was packed three deep — parents, six-year-olds, grandparents with no child in the ceremony and no excuse to be standing there, hawkers working the crowd with sweet buns and paper flags. The fishwives had finished half their trade before dawn and moved up to the seawall to watch, because on this one morning the whole town had the same business.

Everyone on the continent had a martial soul. A farmer with a hoe for a soul could till like three men. A child with a bird for a soul might carry that bird's speed, or its voice, or its eyes, for the rest of his life. But the soul was only half of the reading. The other half was soul power — the energy a soul could be fed and grown on — and soul power was rare. Only one in a thousand people might possess soul power. Everyone else kept their souls the way a family keeps an old sword: real, inherited, and never once drawn.

So once a year, every elementary academy on the continent invited a Spirit Master up from the Spirit Pagoda, and every town under the Sun Moon Federation held its breath.

Glorybound was holding its breath this morning. Halfway up the hill from the fish market, above a physician's clinic with a green door, one boy was too busy eating to notice.

---

Su Yan was six, and he had spent six winters in bed.

The illness was there before he was old enough to remember it. His soul-veins had never taken root — the part of a person a soul grows in, built one size too small — and his body had spent six years paying the bill. Weight. Warmth. Running. Three things other children spent without counting, and three things he had learned to count.

His father was the best physician in Glorybound, and the best physician in Glorybound had spent six years holding a line he could not push. Needle by needle. Winter by winter. The whole town knew the boy above the clinic: the physician's son, the one who was sick, the one who nearly —

Then, in the last month of frost, the illness had simply not come.

He had eaten. He had slept. He had stood up one morning and stayed up. By the first warm week he was running the lane down to the harbor wall and back again, while his mother stood in the doorway with both hands pressed flat against her mouth. The physicians found nothing left to treat and no explanation to write. His father asked the doctors. The doctors asked their books. The books said nothing.

Nobody in the house ever said the question out loud. It stayed where the medicine smell stayed — in the floorboards, in the walls — and Su Yan kept it in his own pocket, where he kept everything worth keeping.

The breakfast on the table that morning had the seriousness of a festival on it. Flatbread, yesterday's fish, one boiled egg sitting in its cup like something under guard.

"I have a question," Su Yan said.

"You always have a question. Ask it while you eat."

"What if my soul is something embarrassing?"

"Like what," said his mother, writing. Zhou Hui ran the grain house's books and could read, write, and count at the same time while missing nothing in the room.

"A barnacle."

His father came in from the clinic side, rolling his sleeves down after the hand-wash he did before every meal in that house — wrists and under the nails, patient as the tide.

"Barnacles are tenacious," Su Heng said. "I have known barnacles to outlast ships."

"That is not comforting."

"It was not meant to be comfort. It was meant to be true." He sat, and gave the egg the respectful attention he gave all small things, and picked up his chopsticks. "Eat. The walk is long and the hall is cold."

"The hall is not cold. It is the third month of summer."

"The Awakening Chamber is cold in every season. Carved stone and old air. Eat."

Su Yan ate. He knew what the table was not saying, because he had heard it said for six years in other houses, at the market, through the clinic door. Today the whole city would find out what he was.

His mother had an opinion about that. She had stated it exactly once, with a pencil behind her ear and her shops voice on: "Half the powerful men in this city have made their families miserable. You come home with whatever you come home with, and we are still richer than most."

His father had said nothing, which for his father was a kind of speech.

Su Yan himself had decided, privately and completely, that he wanted something with wings. Something fast.

---

Awakening Day emptied the town into the streets around the academy.

Red Mountain sat behind its white roofs and red walls like the cleanest thing in Glorybound — grounds swept like a physician's table, everything in order for the first day of the new term. Su Yan had walked past its gate his whole life and never once been through it, and this morning every road in the city was pouring in.

His mother straightened his collar at the corner and then pretended she had not. His father carried nothing and walked like a man going to work, which he was. Twice before the gate he was stopped — a woman with a market basket asking about her mother's drops, a harbor hand with one arm in a sling asking, low, whether the wrist would hold for the autumn catch. Su Heng gave both of them the same short, calm sentences he gave everyone, and after each answer his hand came down and rested on Su Yan's shoulder for a moment, checking that what he was responsible for was still there.

Families came out through the side rail as the morning went on. Most came out quiet — parents' faces set, children looking at their own feet, the whole crowd granting those families the mercy of looking away. Every so often a different family came out: laughing, crying, walking too fast, a grandmother with both hands in the air. Nobody needed to ask. The envious glances followed those families down the hill and out of sight, and Su Yan watched the last one go and finally felt the size of the day settle on him.

Then, in the line, he saw him.

A boy his own size, in a coat mended so many times the patches had patches, talking up at a tired man in his shirtsleeves. The boy was telling his father, with his hands, at length, with his eyes enormous and bright, exactly what he expected the day to give him. The man's hand rested on his son's head — the same weight, the same check, as Su Heng's hand on Su Yan's shoulder.

The patched boy laughed at something, head back, entirely certain of the morning.

*He laughs like he has never once been ill*, Su Yan thought, and then felt a small, immediate shame, because the boy had done nothing to him.

Their eyes never met. The line moved. A teacher by the inner path read a name off the list — a common name, two notes the wind took — and the patched boy went through the gate pulling his father's hand, and that was all of it. One minute. Two strangers on the same morning.

"Su Yan," called the teacher with the list.

His mother kissed the top of his head — something she allowed herself in public only on the first day of a new term and the two worst days of his life, which meant this morning counted, and nobody commented.

"Go on," she said.

His father walked him through the gate.

---

Every elementary academy has one: a round building, older than the rest of the school, with walls a man could not put his arms around and one door.

Red Mountain's stood at the end of a gravel path, white stone gone gray at the base, and it had no sound to it at all. Inside, the hush doubled. A teacher led Su Yan up one flight of worn stairs and down a corridor where the inscriptions began. Not on signs — cut into the building itself. Low lines of old writing ran along the floor, up the walls, and over the curve of the ceiling in long patient spirals, worn shallow by two hundred years of shoes and weather and children.

"Everybody says the marks are the old test," Su Yan said, to fill the last of the corridor, because the building was working on him and he did not want it to see that.

"The marks are the old test," the teacher agreed, which was not helpful.

The chamber was a round room. Same spirals. At the center of the floor, one round slab, worn dark — the only part of the whole building that was not carved, because it did not need to be. That was the spot. Every child in the world stood on a spot like that one at six years old, and the difference between everything that followed happened on it.

The master from the Spirit Pagoda sat by the wall with a low desk, a ledger, a row of blank brass plates, and a cup of tea that had given up being warm an hour ago.

He was middle aged, in a plain orange robe with a soul beast worked in gray thread across the chest. His fingers were ink stained. His eyes had the specific patience of a man in his third hour of children and his fiftieth child of the day, and Su Yan understood at once that this man had seen every face a six-year-old could make about the biggest day of their life, and had stopped being impressed around the fourth.

"Parents wait outside," the master said, without looking up. "The boy is enough. Su Yan. Family name Su." He wrote something in the ledger. "Come here child. Stand in the middle."

The door closed behind his father. Su Yan walked through the quiet, through the spirals, and stood.

"Hands at your sides. Feet still." The master set his dead tea down. "This does not hurt and it does not take long. What happens happens. Do not fight it."

Then he put his hand on the top of Su Yan's head, and the world went warm.

It came from the outside in. A gentle pressure over his scalp, patient, like water rising in a jar. The spirals on the walls drank a slow brightness, as if the room had waited a very long time for its own writing to be read. And something behind Su Yan's eyes — some deep thing under his ribs, in the place where six years of illness had lived and then, unaccountably, left — turned over, once.

*Oh*, he thought. *There you are.*

It rose. He could not see it yet, but he could feel it — a weight coming up through the middle of him, past his collar, past his own face. Then the brightness on the walls stood still, the master's hand lifted, and Su Yan opened his eyes.

There was a creature standing on the floor in front of him.

It had a face much like a dog and a body rather like a hog, and there was more of it than Su Yan had expected of anything belonging to him: well over a meter of it, on four short, thick legs. A round bulge like a small melon sat on the top of its head. Its ears were small and drooped. Its fur was a soft, faded purple from nose to tail, the color of the flowers along the harbor wall. And its eyes were large, and deep blue, and they looked at Su Yan with an expression that had no business on a stranger's face.

It looked at him, and the look had the same weight as his father's hand on his shoulder.

"Well," said the master, and stopped.

The man had come off his stool without seeming to decide to, and stood looking at the creature with the stillness Su Yan's mother kept for the harbor before a weather turn. The ledger lay open under his hand and his pen was not moving.

"...Luo Sanpao," he said at last, quietly, and it was not a greeting. He said it the way a man says a word he has only ever read in old books, and never once expected to find standing on his own floor.

The creature's ears came up. It made a sound low in its chest — a soft *luo luo* — and shuffled two steps on its short, slow legs and pressed the side of its head against Su Yan's hip like a cat claiming a leg.

It was warm. That was the first thing. Under all the strangeness, it was warm — the plain, banked warmth of the stove in the third month. Su Yan, who had been cold for more of his life than he liked to remember, put both hands on it before he had decided anything at all.

"Is that its name?" he asked. "Luo Sanpao?"

"It is an old word," the master said. He had his voice back. His face shut — a shop front closing in order — and he sat down and picked up his pen and looked at the creature one more long second, and then he did his job. "Now. The measure. Stand still, keep your hands on it, do not let go."

He set his palm flat against the air over the great round head and closed his eyes and listened.

The whole room waited. Somewhere above them, in the spiral writing on the ceiling, nothing moved at all.

Su Yan held the warmth. It came up his arms, slow and even, like the sea coming in — not loud, not grand; just the sea, coming in — and the creature breathed under his hands, and for the length of the measure the boy and the soul and the old room were one very quiet thing.

The master took his hand back.

"One," he said.

Su Yan waited, because there had to be more of it. The word was too small for the room.

"That is the whole of it," the master said. "One." The look he gave the boy was not cruel and was not kind; it was the look of a man at the end of a long morning, giving the true weight without adding any to it. "Soul power, boy. The measure that decides whether the world counts you. One is one." He picked up his brush and wrote something small in the ledger. "Do you know what it means?"

"No," Su Yan said.

"It means you have the power to cultivate." The master said it flatly. "Most children leave this room with none at all. You have one. That is a door. It is a narrow one, and you will find the world has very little to say to narrow doors." He set the brush down. "Ten is the first ring."

"Ten," Su Yan said.

"Ten." For the first time, the dry voice had something under it — not warmth exactly; maybe the professional's grudging respect for the sentence he was about to say. "Boys have stood on that stone with a whole thundercloud of a soul and a big number, and spent their lives telling everyone about it. You have a pig and a one, and both of them are yours. The rest is arithmetic and years."

He took one of the brass plates, stamped it, and cut a single character into it, deep and plain, and held it out.

SU YAN. And under the name, the character for one.

---

His father was waiting in the corridor with his hands folded behind his back, exactly where the door had left him.

"Well," Su Heng said. He did not ask. He looked at his son's face for a while, and then at the door of the chamber, and then back, and whatever he found did not break anything in his own face. That was his father's whole art, Su Yan thought later: he could hold a whole verdict behind his teeth and still make his face say that the verdict mattered less than dinner.

"A pig," Su Yan said. "A purple pig that says luo luo. And one."

His father nodded slowly, the nod he used for difficult calls in the clinic. "A pig that says luo luo," he repeated. "I have met worse patients."

They walked out into the light, and the town's day washed over them — banners, buns, gulls, the crowd thinning as families drifted home in ones and twos. A woman whose children Su Heng had pulled through a bad cough saw them and came over, and heard, from three words, everything there was to hear, and did what kind hearts do with news they cannot fix.

"He is well, though," she said, looking at Su Yan with such determined cheer that it landed on his shoulders like a bag of rice. "After everything, the doctor's boy is well. Isn't that the gift. Souls come and go, but health, health is —"

"That is long past true," Su Heng said, mildly. "The boy is six. He has been healthy since spring, and he takes after his mother, which is bad for nobody."

The woman laughed and went off to tell the market, and Su Yan spent the rest of the walk home blinking hard, and could not entirely have said why.

At the turn for their street, his father said, in a voice pitched exactly to the two of them:

"For the record, I do not care what the man in the robe stamped on your plate." They turned the corner, out of the crowd. "You came into this world carrying a body that would not hold you, and you held it anyway, for six years, in a bed, without once asking me why the medicine was not stronger. That is the only reading that has ever mattered in this house. The other one is arithmetic."

He was the town's calmest man, and his voice did not so much as shake, and Su Yan, walking beside him, was almost seven and knew exactly what it had cost him to say that on a public street.

"Nine more," Su Yan said. "Nine, and then the first ring. That is what the master said. Ten is the first ring."

"That," his father said, after a moment, "is also arithmetic."

---

The evening at home was small, because the family had decided without discussing it that the day did not deserve a bigger one. His mother cooked more than she had at breakfast. A bowl of the bay's best shellfish appeared, which meant the harbor hand with the wrist had paid his bill, or that his mother had decided this was the day to spend some of the family's stored-up good luck. The three of them ate. His mother asked four sharp, specific questions — the room, the robe, the man's name, whether the slab had been cold — and did not ask the fifth question, which was *is my son going to be all right*.

Su Yan, who had decided during the walk home that he would be a person who answers truthfully, answered truthfully: a purple pig, a one, and an old word.

"Luo Sanpao," his mother repeated. She turned the word over as she would a coin at the market. "It is not a lucky-sounding name. But I once bought four hundred weight of salt from a man whose name was Cow Leg, and the salt was good." She took his plate and put a second helping on it without being asked. "Eat. Tomorrow we go to the school office and pay the term in advance, because if there is counting to be done in this family, we do it standing up."

Su Yan ate. Between the second and third helping he noticed something he did not say out loud, because he had been raised to watch and not to be watched: his father was eating with his left hand, which he did when the right was needed elsewhere. The right hand lay on the table, curled loosely around nothing, resting, steady — the hand of a physician who had spent the evening not looking at his instruments.

There was soup, and a mother's sharp sums, and a father's quiet hand.

There was also, somewhere past the kitchen window, past the roofs and the masts and the dark harbor, the rest of their lives.

---

In his own small room, the boy did his sums and went to sleep rich.

He owned, in the official sense: a brass plate with his name and the character for one, an old word he did not understand, and a pig.

Also — though no doctor had told him so, no teacher, and no robe — he owned a spring and a summer. He owned the lane down to the harbor wall, and the breath to run it, and a body that had stopped being a cage. The reading said one. The reading did not count the small stubborn warmth under his ribs that had kept him alive through six winters without ever once announcing what it was. He was six. Nobody had taught him that sum yet.

What he did know, by full dark, was that the pig could come out and stay out.

That was the discovery of the night, and it was his own. His parents had gone quiet in the front room. He lay in bed and thought about the creature with the whole concentration he kept for things he wanted — entire, patient — and the warmth came up through him, and the creature stood on the floor beside the bed.

It did not glow, exactly. It was just there, more there than the furniture, taking up the dark as if the dark belonged to it, with its drooping ears and its round head and its slow, expert, waddling walk. It inspected the room like a physician making a house call: the corners first, then the window, then the shelf where two years of unpracticed toys stood at attention. Its nose worked constantly, twitching and tasting. The whole world was probably one long smell to a thing that lived in it through a face like that.

By the door, on the floor, there was a washed white radish sitting on a saucer.

His mother had left it there before turning in — no note, no mention, as with anything that did not need discussing. Su Yan put the saucer by the window. The creature considered the radish for a moment with its deep blue eyes, and then ate it in slow, serious bites, like a thing eating something it intends to remember.

Getting it back in, later, turned out to be exactly as easy as getting it out: he wanted it near, and it came; he said, out loud, into the dark, "Go on, then," and felt it fold down into the warm place under his ribs, like a cat going in off the street.

He was six years old. It was the best trick anyone in the history of that house had ever owned.

He lay back and looked at the ceiling and said it once more, quietly, because the room deserved a decision and not a wish.

"One. Then nine. Then ten."

A year, maybe two, maybe more — he did not care how long. The boy who had spent six years being carried up the clinic stairs knew exactly what to do with stairs.

He slept as he had slept all spring: fast, and deep, and without dreams — the sleep of a body that had learned to build, and could not wait to get back to it.

Under the window, the harbor bell knocked once for the hour, and the little room held two breathing things, a brass plate, and the whole future.

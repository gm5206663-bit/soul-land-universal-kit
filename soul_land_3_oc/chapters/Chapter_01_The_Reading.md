# Chapter 1: The Reading

Glorybound City was a small town sitting on the border between the ocean and the eastern coast of the Sun Moon Federation, and on Awakening Day it woke with the whole continent.

Everyone on the continent had a martial soul. The soul was part of the body, and at six years old, every child went to the academy and woke it up. A farmer with a hoe for a soul could till like three men. A boy with a bird could carry the bird's speed, or its eyes, or its voice, for life.

The soul was only half of it.

The other half was soul power — the energy that a soul could be fed with and grown on. Only one in a thousand people might possess soul power.

One in a thousand.

So once a year, on Awakening Day, every town in the Federation packed its streets and held its breath.

---

The streets in front of Red Mountain Academy were packed before the sun cleared the roofs.

Parents and children filled the road three deep. Hawkers worked the crowd. Grandmothers who had no child in the ceremony stood at the walls anyway, because they had stood there forty years ago, and because they knew what the day did to families.

A small boy stood in the middle of it with his hand in his father's.

He was just barely six, a little taller than the children around him, with short black hair and a clean, commoner's coat. His father was a middle-aged man of medium build and average height, and everything about him was just average — an average man holding the hand of a boy who was not.

It was mostly the eyes. Large and clear and black, with long lashes a girl would have envied. Even in plain clothes, the boy drew looks like a lamp draws moths. He did not notice any of it, which made it worse.

"Dad," the boy said. "What do you think my martial soul will be?"

His father looked down at him.

"Dad doesn't know either," Tang Ziran said. "It all depends on luck."

He said it with a smile. Inside, his heart sighed.

Awakening Day decided lives. That was true in every era. A child with a soul and no soul power stayed where he was born; a child with even a little power walked a road that led out of town. In this era, at least, the ones who missed the road could still live well. That comfort had been doing a lot of work in Tang Ziran's chest since sunrise.

"I'll definitely have soul power, right?" Wulin said. His eyes were shining straight up at the sky, as if the answer might be up there.

His father put a hand on his head.

"Regardless of whether you have soul power," he said, "Dad will always love you."

Wulin nodded, accepting the promise, and went back to watching the door.

---

Families came out of the academy all morning.

The overwhelming majority came out quiet. Parents with their faces set, children looking at their own feet, and the whole crowd granting those families the mercy of looking away.

Every so often a different family came out — laughing, crying, walking too fast, a grandmother with both hands in the air.

Nobody had to ask about that family. The envious looks followed them down the hill and out of sight.

Time passed. The sun climbed and grew strong, and the enthusiasm in the pretty boy's face slowly wore down at the edges. Kids do not notice much, but they notice that.

Four families back in the same line stood another father and another son.

That boy was smaller, and he stood with his weight forward, like a person prepared to be called at any moment. He had been sick for six years — everyone in his part of the city knew it — and then, in the last month of winter, the sickness had stopped, the way a bad wind stops. His color had come back. His weight had come back. His father, who was Glorybound's physician, still looked at him across the breakfast table sometimes like a man double-checking a sum.

The boy himself did not think about it much. He had a plan for today, and the plan had wings.

His name was Su Yan.

The two fathers had met at the harbor market a few times over the years. They nodded to each other. Su Heng asked after the queue, Tang Ziran asked after the wait, and both men said the things men say at the door of a decisive day.

"Long morning," Su Heng said.

"The longest," Tang Ziran agreed.

The two boys looked each other over.

"What do you want yours to be?" Wulin asked.

"Something with wings," Su Yan said. "Something fast."

"Fire bird?"

"Maybe."

"Anything can be a martial soul," Wulin said, with deep authority, "but mine is going to be strong. I'll definitely have a powerful one."

"Teacher will call us by family name," Su Yan said. "So you only get called after me if we're in the same line."

"We're in the same line."

"Then good luck to you too."

They shook hands, small and serious, the way boys shake hands when the day is bigger than they are.

Then a teacher came out of the gate with a list.

"Su Yan!"

Su Yan's mother, Zhou Hui, had come out of the crowd and caught him by the collar before he could bolt. She straightened it and let him go.

"Come home with whatever you come home with," she said. "We are still richer than most."

His father walked him through the gate.

---

Inside Red Mountain Academy, the noise fell away.

The grounds were swept clean. The buildings were simple and unadorned, white roofs and red walls, and the lawns between them were green with common bluesilver grass, because even an academy that could hold two thousand students had lawns like everyone else.

Awakening Day was right before the start of the new term, so the school itself was empty and quiet, and the quiet did something to Su Yan's stomach.

The teacher led them along a small path to a round building, older than the rest of the school, with walls a man could not put his arms around and one door.

"Parents wait here," the teacher said.

His father nodded and crouched down to his level. "I'll be right here."

Su Yan looked up at the building.

Every elementary academy had one. His father had told him the name for it long ago, as parents did: the Awakening Chamber.

Inside, the chamber took his breath.

The floor was carved. The walls were carved. The ceiling was carved. Lines of old writing ran along all of it in long patient spirals, and when Su Yan stepped onto the center slab, worn dark by two hundred years of shoes, he stood in the middle of a room that had been waiting for him since before his grandparents were born.

A Spirit Master from the Spirit Pagoda sat by the wall with a low desk, a ledger, and a row of blank brass plates.

He was middle aged, in a simple orange robe with a soul beast embroidered across the chest in gray thread. His fingers were ink stained, and he had the patient eyes of a man who had already awakened sixty children this morning and expected sixty more before dinner.

Su Yan had heard about these people his whole life. In his father's stories, the Spirit Masters of the Pagoda were the ones who kept the beasts and the souls and the door to the whole profession, powerful in a way an ordinary boy could not even picture.

The man looked up and did not look powerful at all.

"Come here child. Stand in the middle."

Su Yan was already in the middle. He stayed very still anyway.

"Feet apart a little. Hands at your sides." The master picked up his brush, dipped it, and looked at him properly. "This does not hurt and it does not take long. What happens happens. Do not fight it."

He set the brush down and put his hand on top of Su Yan's head.

The world went warm.

Light rose from the floor. It circled Su Yan's ankles, cool and strange, then climbed in threads through the air, and the spirals on the walls drank a slow brightness, as if the room had waited a very long time for its own writing to be read.

Something under Su Yan's ribs — the deep place where six years of sickness had lived, and then left — turned over once.

Su Yan forgot every instruction except one.

*Breathe.*

The heat came up the middle of him, past his collar, past his own eyes.

The brightness on the walls stood still.

There was a creature standing on the floor in front of him.

It had a face much like a dog and a body rather like a hog, and there was more of it than Su Yan had expected of anything belonging to him. Well over a meter of it, on four short, thick legs. A round bulge sat on the top of its head. Its ears were small and drooped.

Its fur was a soft, faded purple from nose to tail, the color of the flowers along the harbor wall. Its eyes were large and deep blue and gentle.

It looked at Su Yan, and it did not look away.

"Well," the master said.

And stopped.

The man had come off his stool without seeming to decide to. The ledger lay open under his hand and his pen was not moving.

"...Luo Sanpao," he said at last, quietly.

He said it like a man reading a word out of an old book. Not a greeting.

The creature's ears came up. It made a sound low in its chest — a soft *luo luo* — and shuffled two steps on its short, slow legs and pressed the side of its head against Su Yan's hip like a cat claiming a leg.

It was warm.

Su Yan put both hands on it without deciding to.

"Is that its name?" he asked. "Luo Sanpao?"

"It is an old word," the master said.

He had his voice back. His face shut like a shop front, in order. He sat, picked up his pen, looked at the creature for one more long second, and then did his job.

"Now. The measure. Keep your hands on it, do not let go."

He set his palm flat against the air over the great round head and closed his eyes and listened.

The whole room waited.

The warmth came up Su Yan's arms, slow and even, like the sea coming in. The creature breathed under his hands. For the length of the measure, the boy and the soul and the old room were one quiet thing.

The master took his hand back.

"One," he said.

Su Yan waited. There had to be more of it. The word was too small for the room.

"That is the whole of it," the master said. "One."

The look he gave the boy was not cruel and was not kind. It was the look of a man at the end of a long morning, giving the true weight without adding any to it.

"Soul power, boy. The measure that decides whether the world counts you. One is one." He picked up his brush and wrote something small in the ledger. "Do you know what it means?"

"No," Su Yan said.

"It means you have the power to cultivate." The master said it flatly. "Most children leave this room with none at all. You have one. That is a door. It is a narrow one, and you will find that the world has very little to say to narrow doors."

He stamped a small brass plate and cut a single character into it, deep and plain, and held it out.

SU YAN.

Under the name, the character for one.

"Ten is the first ring," the master said. "The rest is arithmetic and years."

---

His father was waiting in the corridor with his hands folded behind his back, exactly where the door had left him.

"Well," Su Heng said.

He did not ask. He looked at his son's face for a while. Then at the door of the chamber. Then back. Whatever he found did not break anything in his own face.

"A pig," Su Yan said. "A purple pig that says luo luo. And one."

His father nodded slowly, the nod he used for difficult calls in the clinic.

"A pig that says luo luo," he repeated. "I have met worse patients."

They walked back down the path, past the gate, and out into the town's noise — and that was when the teacher at the list called the next name loud enough to carry over the wall.

"Tang Wulin!"

Su Yan turned his head.

The pretty boy from the line was pulling his father through the gate, jumping up on the balls of his feet, bright again all at once, the whole long morning of waiting gone from his face like it had never happened.

"I'm here!"

Su Yan watched him go in.

Then his mother took his hand, and they went home.

---

Inside the academy, the teacher with the list did not slow down for anyone.

"Follow me."

The teacher did not slow down or look back twice; the day had worn him thin, and this was his hundredth child. He walked ahead along the small path, and Wulin followed with his hand in his father's, taking everything in. The path ran between the white walls and red roofs. The grounds were clean and quiet, peaceful in a way the streets outside had not been, and Wulin's excitement, which had climbed all day, seemed to settle into something more serious.

The teacher stopped in front of the circular building.

"Would the child's parent please wait here for a moment?"

Tang Ziran nodded.

"Listen to the teacher," he told his son. "Good luck, son! Dad will be waiting right here for you."

Wulin nodded hard. "I'll definitely have a powerful martial soul, dad!"

He followed the teacher in.

Tang Ziran watched him go, and for a moment there was disappointment on his face — old disappointment, decades old, standing exactly where he was standing now. Every elementary academy had this building. He had walked into one at six years old himself, with the same hopes in the same order.

The teacher took Wulin up to the third floor.

The building had seven floors, and every floor had its own awakening chamber. Wulin was brought to the one on the third. He had never seen anything like it in his life. The floor, the walls, and even the ceiling were covered in wonderful inscriptions, carved and curving, an elegant design that made the room feel like the inside of a decorated box.

Inside stood a middle-aged man in a simple orange robe with the embroidered form of a soul beast on his chest.

Wulin had grown up on his father's stories, and Spirit Masters walked through every good one. The Spirit Pagoda's people were powerful, mysterious, and respected across the whole continent. This was the first one Wulin had ever seen in real life, and the man looked up and smiled at him in a way that made the whole morning easy.

"Hello," Wulin said shyly.

The Spirit Master's smile gentled.

"Come here child. Stand in the middle."

Wulin blinked, walked over, and stood in the middle.

Then he could not help himself.

"Lord Spirit Master," he asked, "what kind of martial soul will I have?"

The Spirit Master smiled again.

"Ah, I don't know either! After you've awakened, you'll naturally be able to see what it is. Anyway, your eyes are so pretty — it would be just perfect if you got a martial soul related to your eyes."

Wulin's eyes went wide.

"Soul eyes? I heard that the first master of the Spirit Pagoda had spirit eyes…"

"Quiet down," the Spirit Master said. "I'm about to begin."

---

Gentle white light shone from the Spirit Master's hands.

Wulin stared at it. Could that be the fabled soul power that only Spirit Masters possessed?

The man raised his right arm a little. The white light shook, then flew up and bloomed into fireworks, and the chamber filled with splendor. Starting from the ceiling, the inscription lines lit — floor, walls, ceiling, in order, one current running through all of it like a life — until every line came together and converged below Wulin's feet.

Something comfortably numb crept up his legs and went into his body through his heart.

His whole body trembled. It felt like being electrocuted.

His sight blurred. An unclear image flickered in his mind, close, closer — he wanted to look at it properly, but it flowed away like shadow. At the very end he saw it. Then he could not remember what he had seen.

From where the Spirit Master stood, the boy was wrapped in light and giving off light of his own.

The martial soul had awakened.

"Ah —!"

Wulin screamed.

The Spirit Master vanished from his place and appeared in front of the boy, ready to stop the ceremony and pull him out of it if this was going wrong.

It was not going wrong.

He looked, and was astonished.

Golden lines were flickering on the boy's forehead.

They spread down his arms and legs, joining, branching, until they made a whole network of gold under the skin. The Spirit Master had awakened thousands and tens of thousands of children in his career. He had never seen this before.

A faint energy rose and fell, drifting out of the boy. It came from his lower abdomen — the ordinary place, the common place — and it was weak, but there was no mistaking it.

The Spirit Master's heart lifted.

Soul power. A child with soul power. Days of ceremony could pass without meeting one; today's luck was very good.

Then the golden lines vanished without a trace, as if they had never been there at all.

Wulin's eyes were squeezed shut. His face said he was in terrible pain. Slowly, he raised his right arm.

His palm turned up.

Pale blue light gathered in it, and a small blade of light blue grass appeared, quietly rotating, extending, and swaying in his hand with a delicate motion. A very weak energy came off it.

"Bluesilver Grass?"

Disappointment filled the Spirit Master's voice — and then, in one breath, amazement pushed it aside.

He had seen the Bluesilver Grass soul so many times. Grass was the most ordinary martial soul on the continent; grass grew everywhere, on the whole of the Douluo Continent, on lawns, in ditches, on the ground right outside this academy. Grass was trash. Everyone knew grass was trash.

How could trash give birth to soul power?

Wulin did not hear any of that.

The pain had him.

It was as if his body were being ripped apart — bone, muscle, flesh, nothing spared. Heat pushed up out of the bones of his four limbs and rolled through him, and his stomach felt bloated and about to burst, and he could not breathe, and then it went on like that for a very, very long time.

Then it ended.

A warm thread of feeling came into the hollow of his palm.

Wulin opened his eyes.

A small light blue grass, green and full, was waving gently in his hand.

"...Bluesilver Grass?" he said. "This is my martial soul?"

He had not studied spirit beasts. He did not need to. Some things a child picks up just by living, and every child on the continent knew which souls were trash, and Bluesilver Grass was the worst of them all.

"En. It is." The Spirit Master smiled. There was pity behind the smile, and he did not quite manage to hide it. "This is your martial soul."

Wulin's lips trembled.

He had waited so long. He had promised his dad to his face that he would definitely be strong. He had shaken a stranger's hand over it. And it was grass.

"Child, don't be sad," the Spirit Master said quickly. "You possess soul power."

Wulin's head came up. "I — I have soul power?"

"I tested it. You have rank 3 innate soul power." The Spirit Master nodded. "It cannot be called amazing, but it is real. It means you can cultivate. As long as you put in great effort, you will be able to become a Soul Master too."

"But," Wulin said, "but my martial soul is Bluesilver Grass..."

The Spirit Master laughed.

"Have you heard the tale of the Tang Sect? The founding patriarch of the Tang Sect had Bluesilver Grass for a martial soul! He took the soul the world calls trash and reached the heavens with it, and he defeated the wicked Martial Soul Hall twenty thousand years ago."

Wulin blinked.

Of course he knew the tale of the Tang Sect's founding patriarch. The legends of the Tang Sect and the Spirit Pagoda were the most told stories on the whole continent.

But something was wrong with the story.

"But wasn't the Tang Sect's founding patriarch's martial soul the Bluesilver Emperor?" he asked, very earnestly.

"Eh..."

The Spirit Master was somewhat embarrassed. Clearly, this little guy was not so easy to fool.

"The Bluesilver Emperor is the equivalent of an evolved Bluesilver Grass," he said. "If you wish to become a Soul Master, then you must put in your greatest effort."

Wulin bit his lip.

"Thank you, Lord Spirit Master," he said.

---

He came down the stairs holding his own palm, looking at it.

His father was exactly where the door had left him, hands behind his back, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all.

---

Su Yan's evening was small, because the family had decided without discussing it that the day did not deserve a bigger one.

His mother cooked more than she had at breakfast. The three of them ate. His mother asked four sharp questions — the room, the robe, the man's name, whether the slab had been cold — and did not ask the fifth, which was *is my son going to be all right*, because the answer was sitting at the table eating fish.

Su Yan ate and answered and watched his father.

Somewhere between the second and third helping he noticed it: his father was eating with his left hand. He did that when the right had been needed elsewhere. The right hand lay on the table, curled loosely around nothing, steady. The hand of a physician who had spent the evening not looking at his instruments.

"A pig," his mother said, tasting the word. "Luo Sanpao. It is not a lucky-sounding name. But I once bought four hundred weight of salt from a man whose name was Cow Leg, and the salt was good." She took his plate and put a second helping on it. "Eat. Tomorrow we go to the school office and pay the term in advance, because if there is counting to be done in this family, we do it standing up."

Su Yan ate.

Then, full dark, his room.

His parents went quiet in the front room. Su Yan lay in bed and thought about the creature with all the concentration he kept for things he wanted, and the warmth came up through him, and the creature stood on the floor beside the bed.

It did not glow. It was just there, more there than the furniture, walking its slow waddle around the room and inspecting like a physician making a house call. Corners first. The window. The shelf where two years of unpracticed toys stood at attention.

Its nose worked the whole time, twitching and tasting. The whole world was probably one long smell to a thing that lived in it through a face like that.

By the door, on the floor, there was a washed white radish sitting on a saucer.

His mother had left it there before turning in. No note. No mention. As with anything that did not need discussing.

Su Yan put the saucer by the window. The creature considered the radish with its deep blue eyes and ate it in slow, serious bites, like a thing eating something it intended to remember.

Getting it back in turned out to be exactly as easy as getting it out. He wanted it near, and it came. He said, out loud, into the dark, "Go on, then," and felt it fold down into the warm place under his ribs like a cat going in off the street.

He lay back and did his sums in the dark.

"One. Then nine. Then ten."

A year, maybe two, maybe more. He did not care how long.

Two boys had walked out of that gate on the same morning.

One of them lay awake in a house by the harbor, warm, with a pig asleep under his ribs and nine ranks to climb.

One of them lay awake in a house across town, in a plain room, with a blade of bluesilver grass standing somewhere only he could see it, and a rank of three, and a word — *effort* — that he had decided to like.

Both of them were at the start.

Under the floorboards, the sea kept working at the harbor wall.

The boy in the bed was warm.

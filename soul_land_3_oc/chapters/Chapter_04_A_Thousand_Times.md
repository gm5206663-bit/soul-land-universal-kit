# Chapter 4: A Thousand Times

"Wait a moment."

Tang Wulin's fingers were already on the door frame. He stopped and looked back.

His father was still at the table. The lamp was still burning. A man in that house did not call a tired boy back across a room for nothing.

"What is it, dad?"

"Come and sit down. Dad has something to discuss."

Wulin came and sat. He put both hands on his knees and gave his father the long, patient look that boys save for a grown-up who has been quiet all evening.

His father did not begin. He turned his tea bowl a quarter turn. Then he turned it back. His hands did that when his mind was working.

"Son. You chose the road of a Soul Master. Dad will support you." He looked up. "But before you walk far down it, you should know what it costs."

"I know it costs work."

"Work. Yes." His mouth moved. It was not quite a smile. "And money. Listen, son. When a Soul Master reaches rank ten, the first ring is waiting. That is the first real step of the whole life. In the old days, a person broke through that door by hunting a soul beast in the wild. We have learned better ways since."

"Better ways?"

"Spirit souls." His father set the bowl down. "A spirit soul beats a soul ring. A good one grows along with its master. These days they can even be made to order, like a cabinet from a carpenter. One spirit soul can carry one ring. It can carry more. That is the road. And for plain people — no sect, no clan, no great name — there is exactly one place to buy one. The Spirit Pagoda."

"I know that building. The tall one near the market."

"That's the one."

Wulin waited. His father had opened the subject. So his father had to finish it.

"This is the limit of what dad can do." Tang Ziran said it plainly, because plainly was the only voice he owned. "My wage feeds this house. It pays the shop's rent. With you and Na'er at the table — and the two of you eat like the sea — dad could not buy you the lowest grade spirit soul by the time you turn ten. That is the truth. You are old enough to hear it."

Wulin sat very still.

He had never once turned this stone over. Rings belonged to the future. The future was a bright wide room with open doors. Nobody had ever told him that a door could carry a price.

"There are two ways in at the pagoda." His father lifted one finger. "The first is for geniuses. Children who climb so fast that the pagoda itself takes notice. Those children get their first spirit soul for nothing."

"Then I'll—"

"That road is real. It is not your road." Tang Ziran looked at the lamp, not at his son. "Bluesilver Grass will never make them look twice. Whatever a father might wish."

The last words came out on a dry breath. Wulin understood, as children do, that the sigh had nothing to do with grass.

"The second way is to buy one. So that is our way. Which means money is now a thing your future stands on."

Wulin's idea of money was still a small boy's idea. Coins left his mother's hand at the market. Food came back home. Coins were weather. Now his father had put a price on the sky.

"What should I do then, dad?"

His father laughed. It was a short, dry sound with no meanness in it. Then he was quiet a moment. Then he said the thing he had called the boy back to say.

"An old friend of mine can help. You remember Uncle Mang Tian? He visited us once. A huge man, tall, with a beard a bird could nest in."

"He carried me on his shoulder. Mom told him not to." Wulin's eyes came up. "He smelled like hammers."

"He is a first-rate blacksmith. I explained our situation to him. He has agreed to give you a chance." His father held up two fingers. "Two hours a day, after school, for three months. If you can forge simple things by the end of that, you can begin to earn. A smith's work is honest work. It pays by the piece."

Wulin was up on his feet before the sentence finished.

"I want to do it, dad. When can I begin?"

His father studied him for a long moment. It was not a look that doubted the boy. It was a look that wanted him certain.

"Forging is the hardest trade on this street and every street. The hammer will not care that your hands are small. Do you truly want this?"

"I can do it." Wulin said it like one of his rules, repeated until it came true. "I'll earn the money myself. I'll buy my own spirit soul."

His father smiled then. He hid it by standing up.

"Good. Try it. If it doesn't suit you, you stop. That is all a father asks of a trial, son. That it be honest."

"Then I'll go meditate, dad."

"Go on."

Wulin went.

---

In the kitchen doorway, behind the lamp, his mother had stood long enough for the tea in her hands to lose its steam.

When the boy's door clicked shut, a drop landed on Tang Ziran's shoulder. He turned.

"Why did you have to go and do that?" Lang Yue's voice was quiet, because two children were down the hall. Quiet is not the same as calm. "He is six years old. Tighten our belts and we can save him a coin at a time, as we always have. Why does my son have to be beaten at by a furnace to pay for his own dream?"

"Because the dream will not wait for my wage, Ah Yue."

"It's too hard. With Bluesilver Grass—"

"It is too hard either way." He took her hand, the one holding the cold tea, and kept it. "Listen. Lin Lin is small. He is not soft. That boy fought a fever war and walked out of it. He never once asked us what would become of him. A boy like that finds his own door. And this — this is a craft. If he can't climb high as a Soul Master, a craft feeds him for life. He'll have hands he can be proud of. Smithing tempers a person. It lets a boy stand among strong men and not be pushed aside. Nothing in it costs him except sweat."

"And if the work hurts him?"

"Then it stops. My word."

"The sweat. That is the part I am asking about."

"I know." It came out gentler than anything else he had said all night. "I only fear, sometimes, that one day we will—"

There he stopped. He stopped at the edge of what he could not lift. He held his wife's hand instead, and she let him.

She had never once fought him in front of the children. Tonight she had not even fought him in front of the lamp.

So. Two hours a day. The house would fold itself around the two hours.

What the forge would ask in return had not been priced yet.

---

Next morning, Tang Wulin said goodbye to his mother and to the girl at the stove. He went out with his bag half open.

He had meditated half the night, or it felt that way. When he called the little grass up inside his mind now, it came to him like a hand he knew in the dark. Brave and mild at the same time. He had decided it was a small, quiet thing that carried courage.

School kept two kinds of days. One kind belonged to the Soul Master studies — the meditation hour, and the great chart that started with beast souls and tool souls and ended in regions nobody in the room could name. The other kind belonged to letters, numbers, and the long history of the continent. The class called those the cultural days.

Today was a cultural day, and Wulin liked his chances. The teacher asked them to recite a passage. He knew it. He said it without a stumble and collected the small praise that teachers hand out like bread, and he kept it in his pocket all morning.

At the gate, when the day ended, his mother was waiting. Not Na'er.

"Mom? Where's Na'er?"

"Home with her own bowl of rice. She is a very reliable girl." Lang Yue swept him up in her arms as if he were three.

"Mom! Put me down. I'm big now. Everyone is looking."

"I will never put you down. You will simply have to survive it." She laughed. She was already walking, so there was nothing to be done. Then, over his shoulder: "I talked with your father. Before we go home, I am taking you to Uncle Mang Tian."

Wulin's arms went still on her neck. "You are?"

"Do you really want the hammer? It is hot in there, son. Smoke, sparks, and arms that will hate you by the second morning."

"I do. Dad says a man stands on his own feet. I'm going to buy my own spirit soul."

Lang Yue stopped in the middle of the street. She set him down and crouched until their eyes were level. Both her hands stayed on his shoulders.

"Hear me first, then. If it turns out to be too much — ten million difficulties and you with nothing left — mom will find work, and we will buy that spirit soul between the two of us. That is not losing. That is a family."

"No." He shook his head with every inch of chin he owned. "I want to earn it. Then it's mine."

She looked at him a moment. Then she kissed his face. He allowed it, sighing, in the middle of the street, because refusing seemed more expensive than permitting.

She took his hand, and they went on.

The walk took a good twenty minutes. Past the market. Down toward the harbor's edge. Then inland, into the lane where the trades kept their houses.

Long before they reached the end of that lane, Wulin could smell where they were going. Iron. Heat. Something dark under both, coming straight through the walls.

The workshop sat in a row of shabby fronts. From outside it was not much: a broad, low building with rust on the gutters and a door the color of soot. But the sign above it had been cut and set by somebody with pride in the letters. Three words. MANG TIAN'S WORKSHOP.

Lang Yue rang the bell. They did not wait long.

The door opened, and the man filling it was tall and dark, with a full beard and the stillest shoulders Wulin had ever seen on a person. Fierce was the word for his face. The look he gave you was a second door all by itself.

"Younger sister has arrived."

The voice was low and it carried weight. Standing close, Wulin felt it in his chest, like the edge of a struck drum.

"Uncle Mang Tian." He said the name politely and stood straight, because that face did not invite anything else.

The master nodded once and showed nothing.

Then, to Lang Yue: "You go home. Pick him up in two hours."

"Big Brother Mang Tian. Thank you for your trouble." She let go of Wulin's hand one finger at a time.

There was a sentence she wanted to say. He could see it sitting in her. It was the mother's sentence about small shoulders and heavy work. He watched her set it back down, uncarried, and go.

Mang Tian gave the boy one last look. A man judging which length of a bar of metal was the good length.

"Come in."

---

Inside was nothing Wulin had imagined.

He had imagined one enormous fire and a hammer twice the size of his head. He got a long, crowded hall instead. Every flat surface was heaped with metal — brackets, rods, toothed wheels, housings with their screws still in. A boy could not have named one piece in ten. Much of it, he would learn later, was the bones of soul machines, waiting their turn.

Mang Tian walked and did not wait to be followed. Wulin hurried after him — around a bench of cold parts, past a tall shape under a sheet — into an inner room.

It was small. One window, gray with soot. A workbench stood against the wall, taller than Wulin's chin.

On the floor beside the bench, on a low table, lay a round lump of metal. Set into the table under the lump waited a small soul-machine screen. Dark and quiet, like an eye that had not decided whether to open.

Mang Tian stopped. He turned around.

"Do you know what forging is?"

Wulin thought about the question honestly. Then he shook his head.

The master's face did not move. Wulin was beginning to suspect this was a face that almost never moved.

"I did not want to take you," he said. "You are too young. No boy of six has the shoulders for this trade. Your father would not let the matter alone, so I said I would give you a trial. If I find you are no good, you go home. And you do not stand in my workshop and cry about it. Understood?"

"I won't cry, Uncle Mang Tian."

"Good." The master pointed at the low table. "Then here is today's trial."

Only now did Wulin see that the lump was not alone. Two small hammers lay beside it, dull-headed and businesslike. Mang Tian picked them up and set one into each of the boy's hands.

"You see that lump? Strike it a thousand times. The screen below counts the hits with real strength behind them, so swings that dodge the work count for nothing and help no one. Every strike takes everything you have. Finish the thousand, and I will tell you what forging is. Fail to finish, and do not come back tomorrow."

Wulin looked down at what he was holding.

The hafts were a third of a meter. The heads were short and fat — as long as his palm, thicker than his fist. They belonged to a bigger room and a bigger person. In a six-year-old's hands they had no business being anything but boulders on sticks.

He lifted them.

They were not boulders.

They had weight, plenty of it. But the weight behaved like a dog on a chain. Real. Not angry. Wulin turned one over once, and suspicion crossed his face.

Hollow? Uncle Mang Tian looks like a wolf, and he keeps a door a small boy can open. He's kind. He only doesn't want anyone to find out.

He grinned up at the master. Then he raised the right hammer and brought it down.

Bang.

The lump did not dent. The trial was not about denting. But the room threw the sound back at him, and Wulin jumped as if the floor had bitten him. Under the metal, the little screen woke with one quiet green number.

1.

He raised the left hammer and struck.

2.

This isn't too hard.

He set his feet and found a rhythm. The hammers rose and fell like a pair of old rowers. Bang. Bang. Bang. The numbers climbed. Neither hammer came down crooked. The metal took every blow and stayed a lump, patient as a stone in shallow water.

At a hundred, his hair was wet. The master had said nothing and was no longer in the room. The boy had been too busy to notice him leave.

At three hundred, his arms were sore in a place he had not known arms had. Soreness was old company. When a boy has been ill a long time, pain stops being an event and becomes weather. Wulin had walked through weather before.

I must persevere. Dad said so. Persevere, and the door opens.

At five hundred, the soreness had gone down into his shoulders as an ache, and the rowers kept rowing anyway. His forearms had turned pink, and then red. He kept going.

Forging means money. Money means a spirit soul. And a spirit soul means the day I can protect Na'er and buy the good cuts at the market for mom and dad.

At seven hundred, his arms stopped belonging to him. He could lift them and feel nothing. The rhythm slowed. Sweat ran off him like soup off a ladle, and his tunic glued itself to his back. His own breathing sounded like a bellows, and the beats grew longer, and longer—

And then something moved.

It began at the base of his back. It was not pain, exactly. It was not warmth, either. It climbed his spine like a long ripple of water — up to his shoulders and down again — and everywhere it passed, the ache did not leave. The ache simply stopped being in charge.

His spine went numb in a way that was almost pleasant. His grip felt light. The hammers that had been hanging off his fists came up as if they had been empty all along.

He did not stop to wonder. He had a hundred-odd blows left of his seven hundred, and then three hundred more, and he took all of them faster than anything he had managed that morning.

Bang. Bang. Bang.

The boy never saw the line of golden tracery that ran the length of his back. There and gone. Written under the skin, wiped clean before his next breath. Nobody else in the workshop saw it either. It left nothing behind except a small boy with steady hands.

One thousand.

Wulin lowered the hammers, chest heaving. His palms were red. His arms had swelled until they looked like somebody else's. And instead of finished, he felt — he hunted for the word, being six — invigorated. His back was humming in slow circles, from the middle of his spine out to the edges of him, like hands working dough.

It took him five long breaths to remember that he was supposed to report.

He went looking. He found the master in the far room, doing something delicate to a machine part with a tool smaller than a chopstick.

"Uncle Mang Tian." Wulin straightened his back, which complained, and straightened it anyway. "I'm done."

The master looked up. He took in the boy in the doorway — the soaked tunic, the red hands, the absurd straightness — and then he checked the wristwatch on his own arm.

Half an hour. It had been half an hour.

"You hammered the thousand?"

"Yes."

Mang Tian stood, and the two of them went back to the inner room together. The screen by the low table waited with its quiet green number.

1000.

The master looked at it for a long moment. He had set the screen himself. He had wired it with his own hands. No six-year-old invents a lie that little eye will tell. The number was the number.

Then he looked at the boy again. This time he looked with a smith's eyes.

Both hammers weighed five kilograms. They were not hollow. Mang Tian knew that without weighing them, because he had made them. A grown man's arms would hang limp after a thousand clean strikes from that pair. A grown man would need an hour, if he could do it at all.

The trial had been built to be impossible. Mang Tian had built it. His friendship with Tang Ziran was a real thing, and there had been no polite sentence available for it: bring me a boy with shoulders, not a boy with hope.

The trial had been made to send a child home. This lump of a boy had rowed straight through it in half an hour and reported it standing up.

Mang Tian did not believe what his eyes were telling him. There was only one way to make an old blacksmith believe anything.

"Hammer it a few more times." His voice had gone heavy. "Don't stop until I tell you."

"Yes."

---

Wulin picked the hammers up again. He had rested a moment. The dull ache had settled. His hands came up.

He struck with no technique at all. There was none inside him to use. No wrist work. No turn of the hip to borrow power from the ground. It was all arm and all force, and that was exactly what the man was reading.

Fifty blows. Twenty more. Thirty. Every one went down square and hard on the patient lump.

A blacksmith with thirty years in the trade does not need a machine to judge iron. Bone reads plainer than iron.

This child's strength was enough. Not for the trade. Not yet. Enough to be worth years of teaching.

Was this what they called a gifted child? A legendary one?

He did not say so. He let the boy keep swinging. He stood over him with his arms crossed, still as a cooling bar.

Fifty. Eighty. A hundred.

The sweat returned like a tide. The ache returned with it, worse than before, because the morning's work was spent and the arms were being asked now for coin they did not have. His arms heated until every beat of his heart was audible in them. His scalp swelled. Between blows he moved in small sways, like a man leaning into a stiff wind.

At a hundred and fifty, the room started sliding sideways, gently, like a boat deck. His arms were so far away they might as well have belonged to the furnace.

I can persevere. I have done harder things than this, by not dying. I'm a man. A man does not hand back the hammer.

He lost count. The counting belonged to the screen, and the screen was behind him. After a while there was only the boy, the lump, and the red dark behind his eyes.

"Stop."

A hand caught the hammer. A shoulder caught Wulin. Without it he would have gone down to the boards with the grace of a dropped sack.

Mang Tian took the little hammers out of his hands, one and then the other. Then he looked at what he was taking them from. The hafts had worn the palms red. The hands had swollen until they looked bought from somewhere else.

The fierce man stood there with both small hammers in one fist. Something moved through him that had not moved in years.

It was not the strength alone, though strength was rare enough. It was this: three hours ago, this child had been handed a task designed to send him home. And he had ground himself against it until a man who did not want to teach him had to make him stop.

Talent was a seed. Anyone might be given one. That stubbornness — in a six-year-old, on the first day, for no money — was the soil.

"When your mother comes," Mang Tian said, "you will tell her you did well. Because you did."

Wulin was too tired to ask what that meant. He sat down on the floor where he stood, because the floor was holding still and he was not.

---

Lang Yue arrived at the second hour exactly. The man who met her at the door wore a face she had never seen on him before. Gentle.

"You two have raised a good child," Mang Tian said. "I'll take him as my disciple. Same time every day, starting tomorrow. And when you get him home, rub this on his arms."

He placed a small dark bottle into her hands. The bottle appeared to be the entire explanation.

After an hour of sitting, Wulin had most of himself back. He could walk. He could not raise his arms above his shoulders, and he told nobody about that.

Something was still turning in his head, round and round. It was a thing said between hammer blows. On the walk home, he spent it at last.

"Mom. Do you know what forging is?"

"You're going to tell me."

"Uncle Mang Tian says it isn't casting. Casting is pouring metal into a shape and grinding it down. Forging is a person standing over the metal from the first blow to the last. He says a machine can pound metal all day. But metal is alive, and a machine can't feel the veins in it. That's why every first-rate part in the world is made by hand. He says a good blacksmith stands as high as a Spirit Master. That's what he said."

"Did he now."

"He did. A Spirit Master, mom. Imagine it."

For a boy that age, the two dream trades were Spirit Master and Machine Master. Everyone knew it. Wulin carried the idea the whole walk back. A man in a workshop, standing as high as a Soul Master. On his own two feet. With his own two hands.

Then his mother took his hand at a crossing, and he made a sound.

"Ouch—"

She looked down. She turned his palm up. The walk home stopped being a walk home.

The skin had blistered, opened, and blistered again. Every hour he had put in was written in his hand.

"Heavens. He — did he do this to you?"

"He didn't do anything!" Wulin pulled the hand back, which took courage, because it was his mother, and because his arms hurt. "Uncle Mang Tian gave me a test and I passed it. I'm strong, mom. Aren't I strong? Don't cry. It doesn't hurt."

"We are going home." Her voice shook. She walked fast. Her eyes were far too bright.

"Mom. I passed his test. Aren't you happy for me? Isn't this the sense of achievement dad talks about?"

She stopped. She bent down and laid her hand on his head and looked at him for a long moment. Her boy, with his blistered hands and his ridiculous chin.

"I'm happy," she said. Her eyes were angry at nobody and full of tears at the same time. "I'm really happy."

They went home.

---

Na'er was sitting against the wall with her legs folded, in the one patch of floor that caught the afternoon.

Wulin came through the door and bounced over to her. He was carrying his arms like two awkward parcels.

"Na'er! Guess what. Uncle Mang Tian tested me. I passed. I'm going to be his disciple. Two hours every day." He sat down across from her, carefully. "When I earn money at the forge, I'll save it, and I'll buy my spirit soul. And I'll buy you things, Na'er. So many things. There was a candy cart standing by the forge door —"

He talked for a long time. She listened with her chin on her knees, her habit for everything. Twice her eyes went somewhere far off and came back empty, and he did not see either time, because he was busy describing hammers.

"— and I got past seven hundred and my back went all strange and after that it got easy. And he says I'm to come every day, Na'er. Every single day."

She nodded once.

In the small pause he made to breathe, he asked the question that had been sitting on a shelf in his chest since the night she arrived.

"Na'er. You really don't remember anything about your family?"

She shook her head. Small as she was, the shake had no story in it. It was only the truth, small and neat, like a pebble.

"I really don't remember. I only remember my name is Na'er. Everything else is fog." She looked up at him. "Big Brother Lin. Am I stupid?"

"No!" It came out far too loud. He caught himself. "No. Of course you're not stupid, Na'er. It doesn't matter that you can't remember. This is your home now. My mom and dad are your mom and dad. You're my little sister."

Na'er looked at him. Then, slowly — as a window warms when the sun comes round to it — she smiled.

It was the first smile since she had come to the house.

Wulin forgot his arms entirely.

"Na'er! Your smile is really pretty, do you know that? I'll tell you a secret, then. Big brother is going to work hard and grow into a strong Soul Master. And then, in the future, I'll be able to protect you. All right?"

"En."

She said it like a promise she intended to keep.

Tang Ziran came home while the lamp was being lit. Dinner was already on the table. Their mother took their father down the little hallway into the inner room and shut the door behind them.

"You children eat first," she said over her shoulder. Her face was like calm water.

Wulin shrugged at the closed door. Dads and closed doors were weather, not events.

Na'er, who never asked questions without food at the end of them, looked at the table.

Then she stopped looking, and looked at him.

Wulin had got the spoon halfway up. Then he had to stop and twist his whole body like a man shifting a wardrobe, because his arms had arrived at the stage where they were only along for the ride. He had made this discovery on the walk home. He had not made the discovery of a remedy.

So he sat there starving — he was always, always starving now; the forge appeared to have opened a hole in him — and unable to feed himself, which for him was a brand-new species of tragedy.

Na'er watched him wrestle the problem for a while, head tilted.

"Big brother. What's wrong?"

"I can't lift anything. My arms are soup." He tried again. The spoon rose a hand's width and trembled like a leaf in rain. "I'm so hungry, Na'er."

Na'er set down her own bowl. She came around the table, small and deliberate, and took the spoon out of his hand.

"Then I'll feed you."

Wulin's face lit like a festival. "Yes! All right!"

And so that is what happened. A girl of five and a half stood at a boy of six. She held the spoon in two hands because food made it heavy. She carried it up one wobbling spoonful at a time. Some of it went in. Some of it went on his chin. None of it went toward her own mouth. Her patience with a task was bottomless, provided the task had been handed to her.

On the other side of the wall, their mother's voice was doing its angry-quiet work. The words coming through the wood were the words wood lets through. Not wanted. No more of this. My son. Six years old.

Then, clear enough to land in the middle of the table: "I don't care what kind of master he is. I won't have my boy beaten at again."

And their father's voice, lower, going on like water under a bridge.

"Without going through thick and thin, how does anyone ever see the rainbow? I am not asking him to do as he pleases. I am asking him to carry one hard thing now so that he doesn't have to carry ten of them later. When I went to Mang Tian, he didn't want this either. A master smith, agreeing to take a child of six — his pride argued against it in every sentence. Our son won his approval anyway. Do you understand what sort of boy we raised? On my walk home that day, Mang Tian stopped me in the street. He said the boy is unusually gifted. He said our son has strength — real strength, more than a boy should carry. And what he liked even more than the strength is that the boy does not give up. He has Bluesilver Grass, Ah Yue. At the top of the Soul Master world he will never stand. That is the truth that was handed to us. But let him become an outstanding smith, and he will never worry about food, clothing, or shelter for his whole life. Our child is not weak. Are the two of us going to stand here and be weaker than he is? Mang Tian is a six-star blacksmith. He will not break a child. He is not that kind of man. Let the boy try. And if I ever find the boy is being harmed, I will stop it myself. Nobody will have to argue with me."

After that, the angry-quiet voice said nothing for a while. Then it said one word. It was quiet in a different way, and it ended the argument.

The door opened. The parents came out — braced, both of them, for whatever had happened to the food — and stopped.

Na'er was in the middle of the fifth spoonful. She wore a stripe of soup on her own chin like a campaign medal. Wulin was chewing with his entire face, grinning like a lamp.

"You're the best, Na'er."

Tang Ziran looked at the two of them for a long moment. Two thin children in one small room, feeding each other, glowing. He looked at his wife. His wife looked back.

"We'll adopt this child then," he said. To the room, or to no one, or to everybody. "Growing up beside another child will do Lin Lin good."

"Mm." Lang Yue said it, and something that had been sitting on her shoulders since the door closed got up and left. "Na'er. Eat more. I made plenty today."

She had made plenty. It did not survive.

The pair ate like the tide coming in. Tang Ziran's chopsticks slowed and then stopped altogether — the hand of a man who has just finished arithmetic he does not like. When dinner ended, the children were round as ticks, the table showed bare boards, and their father sat smiling at the middle distance where his supper used to be.

"I liked the fish," Na'er informed him gravely, as if this settled everything.

"It is very hard not to like the fish in this house," he said.

Later, while Lang Yue did the bowls, the two of them talked it over in the small voice that grown-ups keep for money. His wage. The rent. Two appetites that had both come in off a road. What they arrived at was that she would look for work. They arrived at it in the tone of two people who had already decided and were only choosing the words now. The winter's bread was long gone. What they were out of money on was two children.

Wulin, who had been sent to bed and had — unusually for him — actually gone, could not sleep. He lay there, and because he was proud and had no other use for it, he raised one complaining hand and showed Na'er the small blue grass turning in his palm.

It spun in its faint light. One blade of lawn that had decided to have a life of its own. The air around it had the gentle feel of something breathing.

Na'er came close and studied it for a long time. She tilted her head to follow it around.

"Big brother. Will I have a martial soul in the future too?"

"Of course you will. Everyone has one. Every single person. It's just that most of them don't wake up properly." He said it like an old professional. "You wait until you're six. Then next Awakening Day you go up, and the master wakes your soul. Then you'll have your own. Then you can be as strong as you want."

He was already sagging. His arms lay beside him like two dead fish. His eyes were closing the shop.

"I'm tired. I can't lift anything. I'm going to sleep. You sleep early too, Na'er."

"Mm."

He was gone inside a few breaths, cheek on his arm.

Na'er sat and looked at him for a while. Then she lay down and tried, seriously, as she tried every night, to remember something from before. A house. A face. A hand.

The fog was exactly where the fog always was. It no longer frightened her. She watched the shape of the boy in the next bed until her eyes grew heavy, and then she slept.

---

Dead of night.

The door of the children's room opened without a sound. Doors open like that for people who have spent years being careful in a small house.

Tang Ziran came in carrying the bottle Mang Tian had sent home. He knelt beside his son's bed as he knelt at work over machines too delicate to lift. He turned his shoulder until the little lamp in it woke. A thin, cool beam lay down across his boy's arm.

Then he stopped, the sleeve still caught in his fingers.

There was no bruise. That was the first word he found for it. He had seen those arms at dinner — red and swollen like meat from a butcher's block — and had spent the evening working out how to work the ointment in without waking a child who needed sleep more than medicine.

He drew the sleeve up to the shoulder. Then the other. He opened the small fingers one by one and laid the palms under the lamp, and the palms were clean. Not healed. Clean, like the inside of a wrist, like a jade cup in a shop window. New bright skin with no mark anywhere on it.

Tang Ziran had watched his wife cry for two streets of a walk home. What he was looking at now was not what she had seen.

Maybe his wife was wrong.

No. Lang Yue did not cry over invented things. What she had described through the door was what he had seen at the table. And what had been at the table was this.

Could it be the martial soul?

There were soul masters whose souls bent toward healing their own bodies. He had repaired their machines; every repairman hears the stories. Souls that knit a body's marks away like rain running off a roof. He had never heard of Bluesilver Grass doing anything of the kind. It was the weed in every field on the continent, and it had never knitted so much as a paper cut.

But the boy's own last ten days argued with him, and the boy's father did not always believe the continent.

Or it could be the medicine. Ointments did work, sometimes, faster than the world expected. It could be the medicine.

He did not notice — the beam lay on the arms, and the boy lay in the dark — that under the short black hair, in the middle of the sleeping forehead, a dim golden veined pattern was quietly fading out. The last of it went under the skin like water going down through sand. Drawn in. Gone.

He stayed a while longer, thinking it over. A repairman learns one lesson early. When a machine does something you did not build it to do, the worst possible answer is to invent one. You look again in better light.

He put the bottle away, still full. He rose off his knees and pulled the blanket up to his son's chin. He had never once managed that without waking the boy. He had never once been able to leave it undone.

Then he went out and shut the door without a sound, and stood in the little hallway a moment.

It had been a long time since the house had held this much noise. Broken things make no noise. Machines at rest make no noise. For six years he had listened, in hallways exactly like this one, to a small quiet room, and the worst sound in the world is a small room being quiet.

Now the room behind him was full of two sleeping children who had eaten his dinner. There was a cost. He intended to pay it. It was the first arithmetic of his life that had ever made him glad.

---

Wulin woke early. On some internal schedule, his body had decided this was simply what bodies did now. He washed, ran to the kitchen, and got himself employed carrying bowls.

"Lin Lin." Lang Yue looked at him over the steam. "Do your arms still hurt?"

Wulin considered the question. Then he swung both arms briskly to check. It cost him nothing, as though the pair of them had not been soup the night before.

"Huh. They don't hurt at all." He swung them again, thinking about it. "It's nothing. Honestly, they feel better than they did before."

The inch of worry came off Lang Yue's shoulders, and her face opened out into morning. "Then the medicine Uncle Mang Tian sent must be very good. If it's like this, mom can rest easy." She ladled broth into his bowl. "You fell asleep so early last night. Dad went in and rubbed your arms after you were out."

In the doorway, Tang Ziran had stopped walking.

He had not rubbed any part of anybody last night.

The bottle was in the drawer, full as the day it arrived.

He stood in his own doorway with his rice bowl and watched his son — swinging his arms around like a windmill, choosing a fourth fold of bread — and he thought again, in the morning light, the thing he had not been able to think in the dark.

Something inside that boy healed him. It healed him fast, and it put strength back in the place of what it healed. And whatever that something was, the boy's soul — one patch of common grass, the town's favorite joke — did not match it.

He had no name for it. He decided, in the ordinary manner of a man with a shop to open and two children to raise, to keep his eyes open.

"Aiya!" Wulin was suddenly upright in his chair, struck by a thought. "Dad! Hurry up and take me to school! We're learning about martial souls today. And listen — I was so tired last night that I forgot to meditate. Remind me when I get home, all right?"

"You forgot the one thing most important to you."

"I know! Remind me." Wulin filled his mouth and spoke through it, a crime his mother punished with a look. "I, Tang Wulin, will definitely become a powerful Soul Master!"

The small house broke into cheers and laughter. It had started doing that around the time a second child came to the table, and nobody under that roof had any complaints about the noise.

---

In the Su house, three streets away, the season turned.

Su Yan earned his first wage on the school's rest day. His father put him to work in the clinic — counting, grinding, lifting — which suited the boy more than it should have. Counting was his best sport. Lifting had stopped being hard.

The work was less simple than it looked.

"The back of the hand for heat," his father said. He laid the boy's hand on a feverish woman's brow, then on a healthy arm, then back again, until the difference had moved into the boy's skin. "A palm is a polite liar. The back of the hand never lies."

"Follow the pulse with the fingers, not the thumb." That sounded obvious and took three tries.

Then the smoke test, which became his favorite. A pinch of dried root burned at the stove's edge, and the smoke was read. True root — thin, straight, smelling of the field it grew in. Old root — a sulk that smelled of dust.

The boy learned all of it on the first pass. He remembered all of it forever. That pleased his father no more than it would have pleased him to find his own tools already where he meant to look.

He was paid in the evening. Five coins, counted into his hand, both of them serious about it.

"This is for the day's work," Su Heng said. "Not for being my son. Those are two different wages, and you should know which one you are being paid."

By the end of the week he had a jar for it, a tin that had once held tea. His practice book grew a new column on the sums page. Earned. Spent. Left.

The shelf gave the week its adventure.

A jar of smoke-roots came back from the market wrong. The restock man had mixed it with harbor root, which looked exactly like it and was worth a ninth of the price. The boy found it because the pig wandered in through the clinic's outer door. The creature had been waiting out there for the walk home. It stood with its nose a hand's width from the shelf and one ear forward. That was the stance the boy had learned to watch for.

"Dad. This jar."

His father came and ran the burn test himself, twice. He stood over the two spirals of smoke, thinking whatever a physician thinks about the man who sells him his stores. Then he looked at the pig — still at the shelf, nose working, personally offended on behalf of the field.

"The market owes us a refund and an apology," Su Heng said at last. "Your pig is welcome at work any time. Tell him his wages will be a bone."

The pig, who did not need telling, took the bone, the walk home, and the evening hour. All three with the same grave air of an employee.

The pot had work too. His mother was teaching him the broth properly now. Skim the grey. Keep the lid cracked so the steam does not fall back in. Learn the hour when a bone turns from giving to holding. And the pinch of dried herb at the very end, so that what the pot gives is smell and not only soup.

"Anyone can boil water," Zhou Hui said. "A cook decides what deserves the fire."

The broth had gone to two ladles a night. The creature had stopped being thin in the lantern light. Its coat came up under the boy's hand. It slept deeper. It woke, always, at the hour he came home, and waited by his step.

On the fourth day of that week, the boy bought the heads himself. The fish row sold heads and fins cheap to the boiling houses. He put down three coins of his own and carried home a basket that smelled like everything the sea had ever discussed.

His father looked at the basket. Then at his son.

"So the pig gets your first wage."

"He works," the boy said. "The pig earns its bowl."

His mother, watching from the stove with the wooden spoon, laughed. One short sound, entirely in her throat. She never said one word about it, then or afterward. That was how she gave the boy his right to stand behind what he had said.

The hollow in the world the two of them had come out of had a spoon in it now. That was all. The bowl paid for itself.

---

At the academy, the days went by in their two kinds.

The class learned the chart and the letters. The meditation hour arrived in the afternoon as it always arrived, and the room's children sat and breathed and mostly achieved nothing. That was the expected result and the expected lesson.

The chart was the class's quiet trouble. It hung at the front with a row for every kind of soul the world had ever known. It was neat. The boy liked it, except for the trouble he never said out loud.

A beast soul shows itself on the body. A tool soul shows itself in the hand. His own soul showed itself beside him — a small dog-faced creature on four short legs, squinting at the world. The chart had no row for that.

He had decided, after some thought, that charts were like price boards. Correct about what they sell. Silent about everything else. And the silence was nobody's business unless he chose to make it so.

The memorization hour was his sport.

The teacher set them to learn the ten kinds of the lower classifications by heart. Most of the room got three, or five, and chewed the rest again. Su Yan read the list twice and said it once, without hurrying. The room turned its head. Reciting was usually done with a face.

The girl who sat by the window got seven kinds and then her face closed like a drawer.

He thought about it for a moment. Then he did for her the thing his mother had done for him with sums.

"Don't keep them up in the air," he said. "Put them in your hands. Beast souls live on the body. Tool souls live in the hand. Swords, hammers, books — hand. Ears, tails, horns — body. After that, the last two kinds aren't even trying to hide."

She looked at her own open hands with new suspicion. Then at the wall where the chart hung. On the next pass she had all ten.

Teacher Lin watched the two of them over the top of her page, a moment longer than the answer needed. Then she called the class on to the next line.

At supper that week, the town's news came home in his mother's mouth before her coat was off.

"Mang Tian has taken an apprentice."

Su Heng set down his bowl. "Which apprentice?"

"The Tang boy. The one from the awakening. The Bluesilver Grass boy. Two hours a day, they're saying. His mother walks him down and brings him back."

"Hm." The physician turned that over slowly, with both hands, which was how he turned over his own opinions. "The forge on the lane has better hands than this town gives it credit for. A good smith stands as high as a Spirit Master. The Association says so, and the Association does not say things like that for free."

"Maybe." Zhou Hui poured. "The boy is six."

"The boy is feeding a smith's fire with two hours and a hope." Su Heng said it without meanness, in the flat voice of a price board. "That is what a family does when the door has a price on it."

Su Yan ate and listened. He knew the Bluesilver Grass boy as the whole class knew him. By the nickname the noon line had given him. By the grass. By a tap and a habit on which he had once stood on the polite end.

So when the talk came around to forging, he found himself listening hard.

"What does a forge make?" he asked.

His father considered the question seriously. He did that with all questions, including the obvious ones.

"Hinges," he said. "Hoops. Hooks. The bands on the water barrels. The shoes on the runners of boats. The frames that hold the machines that fix the machines. Everything a town never thinks about — which is to say, everything a town could not survive without for a single day."

Su Yan turned that over. He looked at his own hands. That week they had ground medicine, skimmed a pot, and carried the flat of seconds up the grain house's back stair. Small hands, growing stronger on a schedule that the town's price boards knew nothing about.

He had no forge, no hammer, and no master. He had a chipped tin with five coins in it. He had a column in a book. He had a pot owed to him every night, and a pig with a wage.

It was a road too, he decided. A quieter one.

---

The fog came off the water in the last week of the month and stayed. That was the season's habit in that town. The lamps began being lit before supper, like an admission.

The clinic caught the first round of coughs, which always began at the docks. Su Heng came home with the tiredness of a physician standing at the start of a season of small plagues. Every evening he put the back of his hand to his own son's forehead without noticing, as a man locks his own shop door without noticing.

Her boy did not cough.

The neighbors' children coughed. The boy at the next desk coughed. The fog came in around the window frames and sat down in the bedding of the whole lane. Su Yan, who used to spend every winter as the house's most guarded possession, ran out in the wet and came home warm. He ate. He slept like a stone set down for the night.

Once, in the second week, his mother stopped in the middle of folding a blanket and looked at him longer than the folding required. Then she went back to the blanket.

There was a line under what had happened that neither parent said aloud. Both of them kept it in the same place, like a coin set aside. Their son had spent six years ill. The books said some children simply grow out of it. Neither of them could point to the page that said grown out of it into what, exactly.

So they kept the coin. They said nothing. They let the boy be warm.

On the breakwater, the fog softened the world's edges. The boy went out every evening anyway, because the run was his. The creature came, because the creature had opinions about wet weather and all of them were positive.

It was on one of those evenings that he set the day's question on purpose.

The fog had eaten the harbor. From the wall, three piers' worth of nothing. The water was a rumor underneath.

Su Yan thought it over. Then he crouched beside the pig and asked it with a walk, which was how he asked everything now.

"Come on. Let's find out who's moored in this."

He walked him along the wall, slow. Past the second pier. Past the third. The pig's nose worked the whole length of the walk. Its ears moved like two small shutters.

Between the third pier and the fourth, where the moorings were three black ropes coming out of the fog, it stopped. It stood with its nose aimed out over the water at a point where the boy could see nothing at all. Its tail went still. That was the stillness he had learned to read as a man saying there.

The boy watched the fog a while. Then he kept the pig's read, went home late, and got scolded for a cold pot — and was forgiven, because the pot was not cold. She had kept it warm and hidden that from him for the whole meal, which is a mother's finest work.

After the fog lifted, he walked down to the piers before school. That was the other half of a read. A read you never check is only a guess.

On the third pier, behind the grain barge, a small coastal boat sat low and heavy. Above it hung the smell of goats.

He stood and looked at it a moment. He did not jump. He did not cheer. He did not tell anyone. He was the only person who would know what had been checked and what it meant. It was a private sort of feast, and he had been eating at that table his whole life.

The other thing the season brought, the grain house brought.

A delivery ran late on a wet evening. The boy was at the counter with his mother when the cart came in. The half-crate of seconds that had needed both his arms at the start of the season went up the back stair under one arm. His other hand rode the rail. The stair did not slow him.

His mother's pen stopped on the ledger for exactly one line's worth of time.

She wrote the next line and said nothing. She was a woman who trusted arithmetic and mistrusted predictions, and her son, whatever else he had turned out to be, had arithmetic in the column where it counted.

That night, back home, the boy lay in the dark and did what the academy had taught them to do — what he had kept doing every night since the first circle closed. He took hold of the thread of warmth and let it travel its round. Down his right side. Up his left. Over his heart. Back to where it had started, as water walks a channel it trusts.

And then, at the end of the round, it did not stop.

It went round again. A second circle inside the first, close and quiet and unpushed, as if the path had widened while nobody was watching and could hold two rows of walkers now. It kept going, round and round, while the boy lay with his heart going like a bird in a hand.

Not frightened. Nothing about it was frightening.

He had no name for it. He told nobody. Nobody had ever given him a word for it, and a boy who has been ill a long time develops an instinct for things that must be carried unspoken.

He lay still and let it walk. When he slept, it was still walking.

It came back the next night. And the next.

---

At the forge on the lane, some evenings, a small light burned late. The boy who lived three streets and one left turn away could not see it from his window. The town could, and the town had a story for it.

It was the same town that had a story for the silver-haired child down at the Tang house. The same town that had noticed the physician's boy had not been seen coughing once all year.

Three stories lived in one small city, and not one of the three was told correctly. The town would be right about that later, in a way it would never manage to prove.

In the Su house, the house closed itself for the night in its usual order. His father marked a page and set the lamp low to save the oil. His mother wrote the day's last number in the grain-house book and unpinned her hair. Downstairs the pig slept by the stove with its chin on the boy's discarded shoe. The pot ticked as it cooled, the small patient clock of the week.

Su Yan lay in the dark with the second circle going its rounds. He thought the thought he thought every night, which was a fine thought and got finer every week.

Nine ranks to go.

Under the floorboards, the sea kept working at the harbor wall.

The boy in the bed was warm.

*End of Chapter 4.*

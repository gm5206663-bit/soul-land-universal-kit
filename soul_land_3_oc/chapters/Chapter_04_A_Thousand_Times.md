# Chapter 4: A Thousand Times

"Wait a moment."

Tang Wulin stopped with his hand already on the door frame. His father was still at the table, and the lamp was still lit, and grown-ups did not call a boy back after a long day for nothing.

"What is it, dad?"

"Come and sit down. Dad has something to discuss with you."

Wulin sat with both hands on his knees and gave his father the long look a boy keeps for a man who has been quiet all evening.

His father did not begin at once. He turned his tea bowl a quarter circle, then back. His hands did that when his mind was working.

"Son. You chose the path of a Soul Master, and dad will support you. But you should know what the path costs before you walk far down it."

"I know it costs work."

"Work, yes." Tang Ziran's mouth moved, not quite a smile. "And money. Listen. Once a Soul Master reaches rank ten, the first ring is waiting. The first real step. In the old days a person broke through that door by hunting a soul beast. We have learned better ways since. A spirit soul is better than a soul ring, and a good one grows with its master. They can even be made to order now, like a chair from a cabinetmaker. One spirit soul can carry one ring, or more than one. That is the road. And for plain people — no sect, no clan — there is one place to buy one. The Spirit Pagoda."

"I know the pagoda. The tall building near the market."

"That's the one."

Wulin waited. His father was the one who had said it, so his father had to finish it.

"This is the limit of what dad can do." He said it plainly, because plainly was the only way he knew. "My wage feeds this house and pays the shop's rent. With you and Na'er at the table — and the two of you eat like the sea — dad could not buy you even the lowest grade spirit soul by the time you turn ten. That is the truth, and you are old enough to hear it."

Wulin sat very still. He had never once thought about this. Rings belonged to the future, and the future was a place with wide doors and bright rooms, and he had not known that doors had prices.

"There are two ways in at the pagoda," his father went on. "The first is for geniuses — children who climb so fast the pagoda itself takes notice. Those get their first spirit soul for nothing. That road is real. It is also not your road. Bluesilver Grass will never make them look twice, whatever a father wishes."

The last of it came out on a dry breath, and Wulin understood, as children do, that the sigh was not about the grass.

"The second way is to buy one. So that is our way. Which means money is now a thing your future stands on."

Wulin's idea of money was still a six-year-old's idea: coins left his mother's hand at the market, and food came back. Coins were weather. Now his father had put a price on the sky, and Wulin found his voice needed a push to come out.

"What should I do then, dad?"

His father laughed. It was a short, bitter sound with no cruelty in it, and then he was quiet a moment, and then he said the thing he had called the boy back to say.

"An old friend of mine can help. Do you remember Uncle Mang Tian? He visited us once — a big man, tall, with a beard that could hide a bird."

"He carried me on his shoulder and mom told him not to." Wulin's eyes came up. "He smelled like hammers."

"He is an outstanding blacksmith. I told him about our situation, and he has agreed to give you a chance. Two hours every day, after school, for three months. If you learn to forge simple things by the end of it, you can start to earn. Smith's work is honest work. It pays by the piece."

Wulin was already on his feet. "I want to do it, dad. When can I begin?"

His father looked at him a long moment. It was not a look that doubted him. It was a look that wanted him to be sure.

"Forging is the hardest profession in the trades. The hammer does not care that your hands are small. Do you truly want this?"

"I can do it." Wulin said it like one of his rules: to make it so. "I'll earn the money and buy my spirit soul myself."

His father smiled then, and covered it by standing. "Good. Try it. If it doesn't suit you, you can stop. That is all a father asks of a trial — that it be honest."

"Then I'll go meditate, dad."

"Go on."

Wulin went.

Behind the lamp, in the kitchen doorway, his mother had stood long enough for the tea in her hands to lose its steam. When the boy's door closed, a drop fell on Tang Ziran's shoulder, and he turned.

"Why did you have to go and do that?" Lang Yue's voice was quiet, because the children were down the hall. Quiet is not the same as calm. "He is six. If we tighten our belts we can save for him a coin at a time. We have done it before. Why must my son be beaten at by a furnace to pay for his own dream?"

"Because the dream will not wait for my wage, Ah Yue."

"It is too hard. With Bluesilver Grass—"

"It is too hard either way." He took her hand, which held the cold tea, and kept it. "Listen to me. Lin Lin is small, but he is not soft. The boy fought a fever war and walked out of it, and he never once asked us what would happen to him. A boy like that finds his door. Now — blacksmithing. It is a craft. If he cannot go high as a Soul Master, a craft keeps him fed his whole life, and he will have hands to be proud of. It tempers a person. It lets him stand beside strong men without being pushed. There is nothing in this that costs him but sweat."

"And if the work hurts him?"

"Then he stops. My word."

"The sweat is what I am asking about."

"I know." It was gentler than anything else he had said all night. "I only fear, sometimes, that one day we will—" There he stopped, at the edge of what he could not carry, and held his wife's hand instead. She let him. She had never once fought him in front of the children. Tonight she had not even fought him in front of the lamp.

Two hours a day, then. The house would arrange itself around it.

None of them knew yet what the forge would ask for.

---

The next morning Tang Wulin said goodbye to his mother and to the girl by the stove and hurried out with his bag already half open.

He had meditated half the night, or it felt like it. When he called the little grass up in his mind now, it came like a hand he knew in the dark — brave and mild at once. A small quiet thing, he had decided, that brought courage.

School had two kinds of days. Some days the class worked on Soul Master studies — meditation hours, and the great chart that began with the beast souls and the tool souls and ended in places nobody could read. Other days were for letters and numbers and the long history of the continent. Those were the cultural days.

Today was a cultural day, and Wulin liked his chances. The teacher asked them to speak a passage aloud. He knew it, and said it without a stumble, and got the small praise teachers hand out like bread. Wulin kept it in his pocket all morning.

At the gate, when the day ended, his mother was waiting instead of Na'er.

"Mom? Where's Na'er?"

"Home, with her own bowl of rice. She is a very reliable girl." Lang Yue swept him up in her arms as if he were three.

"Mom! Put me down. I'm a big kid now. Everyone's looking."

"I will never put you down. You are going to have to get used to this." She laughed, and she was already walking, so there was nothing to do about it. Then, over his shoulder, she said it. "I spoke with your father. I am taking you to Uncle Mang Tian before we go home."

Wulin's arms went still on her neck. "You are?"

"Do you really want to learn the hammer? It is hot in there, son. It is smoke and sparks, and your arms are going to hate you by the second day."

"I want to. Dad said a man can only stand on his own feet. I am going to buy my own spirit soul."

Lang Yue stopped in the street and set him down, and crouched until her eyes and his were level. Her hands stayed on his shoulders.

"Then hear this first. If it turns out to be too much — ten million difficulties, and you with no strength left — mom will find work, and we will buy the spirit soul between us. That is not losing. That is a family."

"No." He shook his head with all of his chin. "I want to earn it myself. Then it's mine."

His mother looked at him a moment. Then she kissed his face, which he permitted, sighing, in the middle of the street, because it seemed cheaper than arguing. She took his hand and they went on together.

The walk was a good twenty minutes — past the market, down toward the harbor's edge, then inland again, into the lane where the trades kept their houses. Long before they reached the end of it, Wulin could smell where they were going. Iron and heat and something dark, and it came through the walls.

The workshop stood in a row of shabby fronts. It was not much from the outside: a broad low building with rust on the gutters and a soot-colored door. But the sign above it had been cut and set by somebody with pride in the lettering. Three words. MANG TIAN'S WORKSHOP.

Lang Yue rang the bell. They did not wait long.

The door opened, and the man in it was tall and dark, with a full beard and a way of standing that filled the frame. Fierce was the word for his face, and the look he gave you was a second door of its own.

"Younger sister has arrived."

The voice was low and it had weight in it. Standing close, Wulin felt it in his chest like a struck drum's edge.

"Uncle Mang Tian." He said it politely, and stood straight, because the man's face did not invite anything else.

The master nodded once, expressionless. Then, to Lang Yue: "You go home. Pick him up in two hours."

"Big Brother Mang Tian. Thank you for your trouble." She let go of Wulin's hand one finger at a time. She had a sentence she wanted to say — he could see it in her, the mother sentence about him being small and the work heavy. He watched her put it back down, uncarried, and go.

Mang Tian gave the boy a last look, like a man judging which part of a length of metal is the good part.

"Come in."

---

Inside, the workshop was not what Wulin had pictured. He had pictured one big fire and a hammer twice the size of his head. What he got was a long crowded hall, every flat surface heaped with pieces of metal — brackets, rods, wheels with teeth, housings with screws still in them. A boy could not have named one piece in ten. Most of it, he would learn later, was the bones of soul machines, waiting their turn.

Mang Tian walked without waiting. Wulin hurried after him, around a bench of cold parts, past a tall thing under a sheet, and into an inner room.

The room was small, with one window gray with soot. A workbench stood against the wall, just taller than Wulin's chin. On the floor beside it, a low table held a round lump of metal; set into the table beneath the lump was a little soul-machine screen, dark and quiet, like a waiting eye.

Mang Tian stopped. He turned, and asked the boy a question.

"Do you know what forging is?"

Wulin thought about it honestly, and shook his head.

The master's face did not move, which Wulin was starting to suspect was a face that almost never moved. "I did not want to take you," he said. "You are too young. No boy of six has the shoulders for this trade. Your father, though, would not let it alone, so I said I would give you a trial. If I find you are no good, you go home. And you don't stand here and weep about it. Understood?"

"I won't cry, Uncle Mang Tian."

"Good." The master pointed at the low table. "Then here is your trial for today."

On the table, Wulin now saw, was more than a lump. Two small hammers lay beside it, dull-headed and businesslike. Mang Tian picked them up and put one in each of Wulin's hands.

"You see that lump of metal? Strike it a thousand times. The screen below counts hits that have real strength behind them, so swings that dodge the work don't count and don't help you. Each strike takes everything you have. Finish the thousand, and I will tell you what forging is. Do not finish, and do not come back tomorrow."

Wulin looked down at the hammers: handles a third of a meter, heads short and fat, as long as his palm and thicker than his fist. They belonged to a bigger room and a bigger person. For a six-year-old they should have been boulders on sticks.

He lifted them.

They were not boulders. They had weight, but the weight sat in them like a dog on a chain — real, but not angry. Wulin turned one over, once, and suspicion crossed his face.

Hollow? Uncle Mang Tian looks like a wolf and keeps a door a boy can open. He's kind. He just doesn't want anyone to know.

He smiled up at the master, raised the right hammer, and brought it down.

Bang.

The lump did not dent — the trial was not about denting — but the room said the sound back to him, and Wulin jumped as if the floor had bitten him. Under the metal, the small screen woke with one quiet green number.

1.

He raised the left hammer and struck.

2.

This isn't too hard.

He set his feet and found a rhythm, and the hammers went up and down like a pair of old rowers. Bang, bang, bang, bang. The numbers climbed. Neither hammer came crooked. The metal took every blow and stayed a lump, patient as a stone in shallow water.

At a hundred his hair was wet. The master had said nothing and was not in the room anymore; the boy had been too busy to see him go.

At three hundred his arms were sore in a place he had not known arms had. Soreness was old company. When you have been ill a long time, pain stops being an event and becomes weather, and he had walked through weather before.

I must persevere. Dad said so. Persevere, and the door opens.

At five hundred the soreness had become an ache that went into his shoulders, and still he kept the rowers rowing. His forearms had gone pink, then red, and he kept going.

Forging means money. Money means a spirit soul. And a spirit soul means the day I can protect Na'er and buy mom and dad the good cuts at the market.

At seven hundred his arms were no longer his. He could not feel them when he lifted them, and the rhythm slowed, and his sweat ran down him like soup off a ladle. His tunic stuck to his back. He could hear himself breathing like a bellows, and the beats got longer, and longer —

And then something moved.

It started at the bottom of his back. It was not hurt, exactly, and it was not warmth. It went up his spine like one long ripple of water, to his shoulders and down again, and where it passed, the ache did not leave. It only stopped being the boss of him. His spine went numb in a way that was almost pleasant. His grip felt light. The hammers, which a moment ago had dragged in his fists, came up as if they had been empty all along.

He did not stop to wonder. He had a hundred and some blows left of the seven hundred, and then three hundred more, and he took them faster than anything he had done that morning.

Bang. Bang. Bang.

The boy did not see the line of golden tracery that ran the length of his back, there and gone, written under his skin and washed away before his next breath. Nobody in the workshop saw it. It left nothing behind but a boy with steady hands.

One thousand.

Wulin lowered the hammers, chest heaving. His palms were red, his arms swollen till they looked like borrowed goods. And instead of finished, he felt — he searched for the word, being six, and found it — invigorated. His back was humming in slow circles from the middle of his spine out to the edges of him, like hands working dough.

It took him five long breaths to remember that he was supposed to report.

He went looking, and found the master in the far room, doing something delicate to a machine part with a tool smaller than a chopstick.

"Uncle Mang Tian." Wulin straightened his back, which complained, and did it anyway. "I'm done."

The master looked up. He looked at the boy in the doorway — the soaked tunic, the red hands, the ridiculous straightness — and then he checked the wristwatch on his own wrist.

Half an hour. It had been half an hour.

"You hammered the thousand?"

"Yes."

Mang Tian stood, and they went back to the inner room together. The screen by the low table waited, holding its quiet green number.

1000.

The master looked at it a long moment. He had set the screen himself, wired it with his own hands; no six-year-old invents a lie that little eye will tell. The number was the number.

Then he looked at the boy again, and this time it was with a smith's eyes.

The two hammers were five kilograms apiece, and not hollow. Mang Tian knew that without weighing them, because he had made them. A grown man's arms would be limp after a thousand clean strikes from that pair. A grown man would take an hour, if he managed it at all. The trial had been built to be impossible. He was the one who had built it — his friendship with Tang Ziran was a real thing, and there was no tactful sentence for it: bring me a boy with shoulders, not a boy with hope.

The trial had been made to fail. And this lump of a boy had rowed through it in half an hour and reported it standing up.

Mang Tian did not believe in what his eyes were telling him, and there was only one way to make an old blacksmith believe anything.

"Hammer it a few more times." His voice had gone heavy. "Don't stop until I tell you."

"Yes."

Wulin picked up the hammers again. He had rested a moment and the dull ache had settled; his hands came up. He struck without any technique. There was none in him to use — no wrist work, no turn of the hip to borrow from the ground. It was all arm, all force, and that was exactly what the man was reading. Fifty times, and twenty more, and thirty, each blow down straight and hard on the patient lump.

A blacksmith with thirty years in the trade does not need a machine to judge iron, and bone reads plainer than iron.

This child's strength was enough — not for the trade, not yet, but enough to be worth the years of skill.

Was this what they called a gifted child? A legendary one?

He did not say so. He let the boy keep swinging, and stood over him with his arms crossed, still as a cooling bar.

Fifty. Eighty. A hundred.

The sweat came back like a tide, and the ache came with it, worse than before, because the morning's work was spent and the arms were being asked for coin they did not have. His arms heated until he could feel every beat of his heart in them. His scalp swelled. He moved in small sways between blows, like a man in a stiff wind.

At a hundred and fifty the room began to slide sideways, gently, like a boat deck. His arms were so far away they might as well have belonged to the furnace.

I can persevere. I have done harder things than this by not dying. I'm a man. A man does not hand back the hammer.

He lost count. The numbers were the screen's job, and the screen was behind him, and after a while it was only him and the lump and the red dark behind his eyes.

"Stop."

A hand caught the hammer, and a shoulder caught Wulin, and without it he would have gone down to the floor with the grace of a dropped sack.

Mang Tian took the little hammers out of his hands, one and then the other, and looked at what he was taking them from. The palms had been worn red by the handles. The muscles had swollen till the hands looked like borrowed goods.

The fierce man stood there with the pair of small hammers in one fist and something moved through him that had not moved in years. It was not only the boy's strength, though strength was rare enough. It was this: three hours ago this child had been handed a task built to send him home — and he had ground himself against it until somebody who did not want to teach him had to make him stop.

Talent was a seed. Anyone might get one. That stubbornness — in a six-year-old, on the first day, for no money — was the soil.

"When your mother comes," Mang Tian said, "you will tell her you did well. Because you did."

Wulin was too tired to ask what that meant. He sat down on the floor where he was, because the floor was standing still and he was not.

---

Lang Yue came at the second hour exactly, and was met at the door by a face she had never seen on the man: gentle.

"You two have raised a good child," Mang Tian said. "I'll take him as my disciple. From tomorrow, same time every day. And when you get him home, rub this on his arms."

He put a small dark bottle into her hands, and it was as if the bottle was the entire explanation.

After an hour of sitting, Wulin had most of himself back. He could walk; he could not lift his arms above his shoulders, and he told nobody about that. Something was still turning in his head, round and round, a thing said between hammer blows, and on the walk home he spent it at last.

"Mom. Do you know what forging is?"

"You're going to tell me."

"Uncle Mang Tian says it isn't casting. Casting is where you pour metal into a shape and grind it down. Forging is where a person stands over the metal from the first blow to the last. He says you can pound metal with a machine, but metal is alive, and a machine can't feel the veins in it, so all the first-rate parts in the world are made by hand. He says a good blacksmith stands as high as a Spirit Master. That's what he said."

"Did he now."

"He did. A Spirit Master, mom. Can you imagine."

For a boy that age, the two dream trades were Spirit Master and Machine Master, and everyone knew it. Wulin turned the idea over the whole walk back — a man in a workshop, standing as high as a Soul Master, on his own two feet, with his own two hands.

Then his mother took his hand at a crossing and he made a sound.

"Ouch —"

She looked down, and turned his palm up, and the walk home stopped being a walk home.

The skin had blistered, opened, and blistered again. She found the marks of every hour he had put in, written in his hand.

"Heavens. He — did he do this to you?"

"He didn't do anything!" Wulin pulled his hand back, which took courage, because it was his mother, and because his arms hurt. "Uncle Mang Tian gave me a test and I passed it. I'm strong, mom. Aren't I strong? Don't cry. It doesn't hurt."

"We are going home." Her voice was shaking, and she started walking fast, and her eyes were much too bright.

"Mom. I passed his test. Aren't you happy for me? Isn't this the sense of achievement dad always talks about?"

She stopped. She bent down and put her hand on his head and looked at him a long moment, her boy with his blistered hands and his ridiculous chin.

"I'm happy," she said, and her eyes were angry at nobody and full of tears at once. "I'm really happy."

They went home.

---

Na'er was sitting by the wall with her legs folded, in the one patch of the room that caught the afternoon. Wulin came through the door and went to her in one bounce, holding his arms like two uncomfortable packages.

"Na'er! Guess what. Uncle Mang Tian tested me and I passed, and I'm going to be his disciple. Two hours every day. When I earn money with forging, I'm going to save up and buy my spirit soul — and I'll buy you things, Na'er. So many things. There was a candy cart by the forge —"

He talked for a long time. She listened with her chin on her knees, as she listened to everything, and twice her eyes went somewhere far off and came back empty, and he did not see, because he was recounting the hammers.

"— and I went past seven hundred and my back went all strange and then it got easy, and he says I'm to come every day. Every day, Na'er."

She nodded once. Then, in the small pause he made to breathe, he put the question that had sat on the shelf in his chest since the night she came.

"Na'er. You really don't remember anything about your family?"

She shook her head, and small as she was, the shake had no story in it. It was just the truth, small and neat, like a pebble.

"I really don't remember. I only remember my name is Na'er. Everything else is fog. Big Brother Lin, am I stupid?"

"No!" It came out much too loud, and he caught himself. "No. Of course you're not stupid, Na'er. It doesn't matter if you can't remember. This is your home now. My mom and dad are your mom and dad. You're my little sister."

Na'er looked at him. And then — slowly, like a window warming when the sun comes round to it — she smiled.

It was the first time since she had come to the house.

Wulin forgot his arms entirely. "Na'er! Your smile is really pretty, do you know that? I'll tell you a secret, then. Big brother is going to work hard and become a strong Soul Master — and then, in the future, I'll be able to protect you. All right?"

"En." She said it like a promise she planned to keep.

Tang Ziran came home while the lamp was being lit, and dinner was already on the table, and their mother took their father by the road of the doorway into the inner room and closed the door behind them.

"You eat first, children," she said over her shoulder, with a face like calm water.

Wulin shrugged at the closed door — dads and closed doors were weather, not events — and Na'er, who never asked questions that had no food at the end of them, looked at the table.

Then she stopped and looked at him.

Wulin had got the spoon halfway and had to stop and twist his whole body like a man moving a wardrobe, because his arms had reached the stage where they were only along for the ride. He had discovered this on the walk home. He had not discovered a way around it. He sat there, starving — he was always, always starving now, the forge seemed to have opened a hole in him — and unable to feed himself, which for him was a new kind of tragedy.

Na'er watched him wrestle with the problem for a while, tilting her head.

"Big brother. What's wrong?"

"I can't lift anything. My arms are soup." He tried again. The spoon made it a third of the height and shook like a leaf in rain. "I'm so hungry, Na'er."

Na'er put down her own bowl. She came around the table, small and deliberate, and took the spoon out of his hand.

"Then I'll feed you."

Wulin's face lit like a festival. "Yes! All right!"

And that is what happened: a girl of five and a half standing beside a boy of six, holding the spoon in two hands because it was heavy with food, moving it up one wobbling spoonful at a time. Some went in. Some went on his chin. None of it went near her own mouth — her patience with a task was bottomless as long as the task had been handed to her.

Behind the closed door, on the other side of the wall, their mother's voice was doing its angry-quiet thing, and the words coming through the wood were the words a wall lets through: not wanted, no more of it, my son, six years old. Once, clear enough to land in the middle of the table: "I don't care what kind of master he is. I won't have my boy beaten at again."

And their father's voice, lower, going on like water under a bridge:

"Without going through thick and thin, how do you ever see a rainbow? I'm not asking him to do as he pleases. I'm asking him to bear one thing now so that he doesn't have to bear ten later. When I went to Mang Tian that day, he didn't want this either — a man with a master smith's pride, taking a child of six. But our son got his approval anyway. Do you understand what kind of boy we raised? On my way home from the shop, Mang Tian stopped me in the street. He told me the boy is unusually gifted. He said our son has strength — real strength, more than a boy should — and what he liked even more is that the boy doesn't give up. He has Bluesilver Grass, Ah Yue. His chances of standing at the top of the Soul Master world are almost nothing; that is the truth we were handed. But if he becomes an outstanding smith, he will never worry about food or clothing or shelter in his life. Our child isn't weak. How can we two stand here and be weaker than he is? Mang Tian is a six-star blacksmith. He will not break a child. He's not that kind of man. Let the boy try it. If one day I find the boy is being harmed, I'll stop it myself, and no one will have to argue with me."

After that, the angry-quiet voice had nothing to say for a while. Then it said one word, quiet in a different way, and that was the end of the argument.

The door opened. The parents came out — ready, the two of them, for whatever the table had done to the food — and stopped.

Na'er was in the middle of the fifth spoonful, wearing a stripe of soup on her own chin like a campaign medal. Wulin was chewing with his whole face, grinning like a lamp.

"You're the best, Na'er."

Tang Ziran looked at the two of them a long moment: two very thin children in a very small room, feeding each other, glowing. He looked at his wife, and his wife looked back.

"We'll adopt the child," he said, to the room, or to no one, or to everyone. "Growing up beside another child will do Lin Lin good."

"Mm." Lang Yue said, and if there had been a weight on her shoulders since the door closed, the boy and the girl at the table had just carried it off for her. "Na'er. Eat more. I made a lot today."

She had made a lot today. It did not survive.

The two of them ate like a tide at a beach. Tang Ziran's chopsticks slowed and stopped entirely, the hand of a man who has just done arithmetic he does not like. When dinner ended, the children were round as ticks, the table showed its bare boards, and their father sat smiling at the middle distance where his supper used to be.

"I liked the fish," Na'er informed him, gravely, as if this settled everything.

"It is very hard not to like the fish in this house," he said.

Later, while Lang Yue did the bowls, the two of them talked it over in the small voice grown-ups use for money. His wage. The rent. Two appetites that had both come off a road. What they arrived at was that she would look for work — and they arrived at it in the tone of two people who had already decided, and were only choosing words now.

It had been a hard winter's worth of bread that they were out of money. It was two children out of the sea.

Wulin, who had been sent to bed and had — unusual for him — actually gone, could not sleep. He lay there and, because he was proud and could not do anything else with it, he lifted one hand with much complaining and showed Na'er the small blue grass turning in his palm.

It spun there in its faint light, a blade of lawn that had decided to have a life of its own. Around it the air had the gentle feel of something breathing.

Na'er came close and looked at it a long time, tilting her head to follow it around.

"Big brother. Will I have a martial soul in the future too?"

"Of course you will. Everyone does. Every single person has one. It's just that most of them don't wake up right." He said it like an old professional. "You'll wait until you're six, and then next Awakening Day you'll go up and the master will wake yours. Then you'll have your own, and then you can be as strong as you want."

He was already sagging. His arms lay beside him like two dead fish, and his eyes were closing shop.

"I'm tired. I can't lift anything. I'm going to sleep. You sleep early too, Na'er."

"Mm."

He was gone in a few breaths, cheek on his arm.

Na'er sat and looked at him a while. Then she lay down and tried, seriously, as she tried every night, to remember something from before — a house, a face, a hand — and found the fog exactly where the fog always was. It did not frighten her anymore. She looked at the shape of the boy in the next bed, and her eyes grew heavy, and she slept.

---

Dead of the night. The door of the children's room opened without a sound — as doors open for people who have spent years being careful in a small house.

Tang Ziran came in carrying the bottle Mang Tian had sent home. He knelt beside his son's bed as he knelt at work over machines too delicate to lift. He turned his shoulder until its little lamp woke, a thin cool beam, and laid it across his boy's arm.

Then he stopped, with the sleeve still in his fingers.

There was no bruise. That was the first word for it. He had seen the arms at dinner, red and swollen like something bought from a butcher, and had spent the evening working out how to rub in the ointment without waking a child who needed sleep more than medicine.

He pulled the sleeve up to the shoulder. Then the other. He opened the small fingers one by one and laid the palms under the lamp, and the palms were clean. Not healed — clean, like the inside of a wrist, like a jade cup in a shop window. The skin was bright and new and it did not have a mark on it.

Tang Ziran had watched his wife cry for two streets of a walk home. What he was seeing now was not what she had seen.

Maybe his wife was wrong? — no. Lang Yue did not cry about invented things. What she had described through the door was what he had seen at the table. And what was at the table was now this.

Could it be the martial soul?

There were soul masters with souls that bent toward self-healing — he had repaired their machines, and every repairman heard the stories. Souls that knit a body's marks away like rain off a roof. He had never heard of Bluesilver Grass doing anything of the kind. It was the weed in every field on the continent, and it had never once knit so much as a paper cut.

But the boy's own last ten days said otherwise, and the boy's father did not always believe the continent.

Or it could be the medicine. Ointments did work, sometimes, faster than the world expected. It could be the medicine.

He did not notice — the beam was on the arms, and the boy lay in the dark — that under the short black hair, in the middle of the sleeping forehead, a dim golden veined pattern was quietly fading out, the last of it going under the skin like water under sand, drawn in and gone.

He stayed a while longer, considering. A repairman learned one thing young: when a machine does something you did not build it to do, the worst answer is to invent one. You look again in the light.

He put the bottle away, still full, and rose off his knees, and pulled the blanket up to his son's chin, which he had never once managed to do without waking him and had never once been able to leave undone.

Then he went out and closed the door without a sound, and stood in the little hallway a moment.

It had been a long time since the house had held this much noise. Broken things made no noise; machines made no noise at rest. For six years he had listened, in hallways like this one, to a small quiet room, and the worst sound in the world is a small room being quiet.

Now the room behind him was full of two sleeping children who had eaten his dinner. There was a cost. He intended to pay it. It was the first arithmetic of his life he had ever been glad to make.

---

Wulin woke early, because on some internal schedule his body had decided that was simply what bodies did now. He washed, ran to the kitchen, and got himself employed carrying bowls.

"Lin Lin." Lang Yue looked at him over the steam. "Do your arms still hurt?"

Wulin thought about it, and swung both arms briskly to check, which cost him nothing, as if the two of them had not been soup the night before.

"Huh. They don't seem to hurt at all." He swung them again, considering. "It's nothing. They feel better than they did before, actually."

Lang Yue's shoulders came down an inch, and her face opened out into morning. "It must be that the medicine Uncle Mang Tian sent is very good. If it's like this, mom can rest easy." She ladled out broth. "You fell asleep so early last night — dad went in and rubbed your arms after you were out."

Right at that moment, in the doorway, Tang Ziran had stopped walking.

He had not rubbed any part of anybody last night.

The bottle was in the drawer, full as the day it came. He stood in his own doorway with his rice bowl and looked at his son — swinging his arms around like a windmill, choosing a fourth fold of bread — and thought again, in the morning light, the thing he had not been able to think in the dark.

Something in that boy healed him — healed him fast, and put strength back in the place of what it healed. And whatever the something was, the boy's soul — a patch of grass, the town's favorite joke — did not match it.

He had no name for it. He decided, in the ordinary way of a man who had a shop to open and two children to raise, to keep his eyes open.

"Aiya!" Wulin was suddenly upright in his chair, struck by a thought. "Dad! Hurry up and take me to school! We're learning about martial souls today — and listen, I was so tired last night I forgot to meditate. Remind me when I get home, alright?"

"You forgot the thing most important to you."

"I know! Remind me." Wulin filled his mouth and spoke through it, a crime his mother punished with a look. "I, Tang Wulin, will definitely become a powerful Soul Master!"

The small house broke into cheers and laughter. It had begun to do that around the time a second child came to the table, and nobody in that house had any complaints about the noise.

---

In the Su house, three streets away, the season turned.

Su Yan's first wage came on the school's rest day, when his father put him to work in the clinic — counting, grinding, lifting — which suited the boy more than it should have; counting was his best sport, and lifting had stopped being hard.

The work was less simple than it looked. "The back of the hand for heat," his father said, laying the boy's hand on a fevered woman's brow, then on a healthy arm, then back, until the difference lived in his skin. "The palm lies. The palm is polite. The back of the hand never lies." "Follow the pulse with the fingers, not the thumb," which sounded obvious and took three tries. Then the smoke test, which became his favorite: a pinch of dried root burned on the stove's edge, and the smoke read. True root — thin, straight, smelling of the field it grew in. Old root — a sulk that smelled of dust.

The boy learned all of it at the first pass and remembered all of it forever, which pleased the father no more than it pleased him to find his own tools where he meant to look.

He was paid in the evening: five coppers, counted into his hand with both of them serious about it.

"For the day's work," Su Heng said. "Not for being the son. Those are different wages, and you should know which one you're being paid."

He had a jar for it by the end of the week, a chipped tea tin, and a new column in the back of his practice book where the sums lived. Earned. Spent. Left.

The shelf gave the week its adventure. A jar of smoke-roots had come back from the market wrong: the restock man had mixed it with the harbor root, which looked exactly like it and was worth a ninth. The boy found it because the pig wandered in from the clinic's outer door, where it had been waiting for the walk home, and stood with its nose a hand's width from the shelf, one ear forward — the stance the boy had learned to watch for.

"Dad. This jar."

His father came and did the burn test himself, twice, and stood over the two spirals of smoke thinking whatever a physician thinks about the man who sells him his stores. Then he looked at the pig, still at the shelf with its nose working, personally offended on behalf of the field.

"The market owes us a refund and an apology," Su Heng said at last. "Your pig is welcome at work any time. Tell him his wages will be a bone."

The pig, who did not need telling, took the bone, and the walk home, and the evening hour, all with the same grave air of an employee.

The pot had work too. His mother was teaching him the broth now, properly. Skim the grey. Keep the lid cracked so the steam does not fall back in. Learn the hour a bone turns from giving to holding. And the pinch of dried herb at the very end, so that what it gives is smell and not just soup.

"A pot is a small arithmetic," Zhou Hui said. "Anyone can boil water. The trick is to boil only what is worth boiling."

The broth had gone to two ladles a night, and the creature had stopped being thin in the lantern light. Its coat came up under the boy's hand. It slept deeper — and woke, always, at the hour he came home, and waited by his step.

On the fourth day of the week, the boy bought the heads himself. The fish row sold heads and fins cheap to the boiling houses, and the boy put down three coppers of his own and came home with a basket that smelled like everything the sea had ever discussed. His father looked at the basket, then at his son.

"So the pig gets your first wage."

"He works," the boy said. "The pig earns its bowl."

His mother, watching from the stove with the wooden spoon, laughed — one short sound, entirely in her throat — and never said a word about it, then or afterward, which was how she gave the boy his right to stand behind what he had said.

The hollow in the world where the two of them had come from had a spoon in it now, that was all. The bowl paid for itself.

---

At the academy the days went by in their two kinds. The boy's class learned the chart and the letters; the meditation hour came in the afternoon as it always came, and the room's children sat and breathed and mostly achieved nothing, which was the expected result and the expected lesson.

The chart was the class's quiet trouble. It hung at the front with a row for every kind of soul the world had known. It was neat. The boy liked it, except for the trouble he never said out loud.

A beast soul shows itself on the body. A tool soul shows itself in the hand. His own soul showed itself beside him — a small dog-faced thing on four short legs that squinted at the world. The chart did not have a row for that. He had decided, after some thought, that charts were like price boards: right about what they sell, silent about the rest — and the silence was nobody's business unless he chose.

The memorization hour was his sport.

The teacher set them to memorize the ten kinds of the lower classifications by heart. Most of the room got three, or five, and chewed again. Su Yan read it twice and said it once, without hurrying, and the room turned its head, because reciting was usually done with a face.

The girl who sat by the window got seven kinds and then her face closed, like a drawer.

He thought about it a moment, and did for her the thing his mother had done for him with sums. "Don't keep them in the air," he said. "Put them in your hands. Beast souls live on the body. Tool souls live in the hand. Swords, hammers, books — hand. Ears, tails, horns — body. Then the last two kinds aren't even trying to hide."

She looked at her own open hands with new suspicion, and then at the wall where the chart hung, and got all ten on the next pass.

Teacher Lin looked at the two of them over the top of her page for a moment longer than the answer needed. Then she called the class to the next line.

At supper that week, the town's news came home in his mother's mouth before her coat was off.

"Mang Tian has taken an apprentice."

Su Heng set down his bowl. "Which apprentice?"

"The Tang boy. The one from the awakening — the Bluesilver Grass boy. Two hours a day, they're saying. His mother takes him and brings him."

"Hm." The physician turned that over slowly, with both hands, which was how he turned over his own opinions. "The forge on the lane has better hands than the town gives it credit for. A good smith stands as high as a Spirit Master, the Association says, and they don't say things like that for free."

"Maybe." Zhou Hui poured. "The boy is six."

"The boy is feeding a smith's fire with two hours and a hope." Su Heng said it without meanness, in the flat voice of a price board. "That is what a family does when the door has a price on it."

Su Yan ate and listened. He knew the Bluesilver Grass boy as the whole class did — by the nickname the noon line had given him, by the grass, and by a tap and a habit of which he had once been on the polite end. So when the talk came round to forging, he found himself listening hard.

"What does a forge make?" he asked.

His father considered the question seriously, which he did with all questions, including the obvious ones.

"Hinges," he said. "Hoops. Hooks. The bands on the water barrels and the shoes on the boats' runners. The frames that hold the machines that fix the machines. Everything a town doesn't think about, which is to say everything a town would not do without for one single day."

Su Yan turned that over. He looked at his own hands, which that week had ground medicine, skimmed a pot, and carried the flat of seconds up the grain house's back stair. Small hands, getting stronger on a schedule the town's price boards knew nothing about.

He did not have a forge, or a hammer, or a master. He had a chipped tea tin with five coppers in it, and a column in a book, and a pot that was owed to him every night, and a pig with a wage.

It was a road too, he decided. A quieter one.

---

The fog came off the water in the last week of the month and stayed — the season's habit in that town — and the lamps started being lit before supper, like an admission.

The clinic caught the first round of coughs, which always began at the docks. Su Heng came home with the tiredness of a physician at the start of a season of small plagues, and every evening he put the back of his hand to his own son's forehead without noticing — as a man locks his own shop door without noticing.

The boy did not cough.

The neighbor's children coughed. The boy at the next desk coughed. The fog came in through the window frames and sat in the bedding of the whole lane. Su Yan, who used to spend every winter as the house's most guarded possession, ran in the wet and came home warm, and ate, and slept like a stone put down for the night. Once, in the second week, his mother stopped in the middle of folding a blanket and looked at him longer than the folding needed. Then she went back to the blanket.

There was a line under what had happened that neither parent said out loud, and both kept in the same place, like a coin put aside. Their son had spent six years being ill. The books said some children simply grow out of it. Neither of them could find the page that said into what, exactly. So they kept the coin, said nothing, and let the boy be warm.

On the breakwater the fog softened the world's edges, and the boy went out each evening anyway, because the run was his, and the creature came, because the creature had opinions about wet weather and all of them were positive.

It was on one of those evenings that he set the day's question on purpose.

The fog had eaten the harbor. From the wall, three piers' worth of nothing, and the water a rumor below. Su Yan thought it over, then crouched next to the pig and asked it with a walk, which was how he asked everything now.

"Come on. Let's find out who's moored in this."

He walked him along the wall, slow, past the second pier, past the third. The pig's nose worked the whole length of the walk, and its ears moved like two small shutters — and between the third pier and the fourth, where the moorings were three black ropes coming out of the fog, it stopped. It stood with its nose aimed out over the water at a point where the boy could see nothing at all, and its tail went still — the stillness he had learned to read as a man saying there.

The boy watched the fog a while. Then he kept the pig's read, and went home late, and got scolded for a cold pot — and was forgiven, because the pot was not cold. She had kept it warm and concealed that from him the whole meal, which is a mother's finest work.

After the fog lifted, he walked down to the piers before school, because that was the other half of a read: unless you check, all you own is a guess. On the third pier, behind the grain barge, a small coastal boat sat low and heavy, and above it hung the smell of goats.

He stood and looked at it a moment. He did not jump, or cheer, or tell anyone, because he was the only one who would know what had been checked and what it meant. It was a private sort of feast, and he had been eating at that table all his life.

The other thing the season brought, the grain house brought. A delivery ran late on a wet evening, and the boy was at the counter with his mother when the cart came in. The half-crate of seconds that had needed both his arms at the start of the season went up the back stair under one arm, the other hand on the rail, and the stair did not slow him.

His mother's pen stopped on the ledger for exactly one line's worth of time.

She wrote the next line and said nothing, because she was a woman who trusted arithmetic and mistrusted predictions, and her son, whatever else he had turned out to be, had arithmetic in the column where it counted.

That night, back home, the boy lay in the dark and did what the academy had taught them to do, and what he had kept doing every night since the first circle closed. He held the thread of warmth and let it travel its round: It went down his right side, up his left, over his heart, back to where it had started — as water walks a channel it trusts.

And then, at the end of the round, it did not stop.

It went round again — a second circle inside the first, close and quiet, unpushed, as if the path had widened while no one was watching and could hold two rows of walkers now. And it kept going, round and round, while the boy lay with his heart going like a bird in a hand — not frightened, because nothing about it was frightening.

He did not know what to call it. He did not tell anyone, because nobody had given him a word for it, and a boy who has been ill a long time has an instinct for things that must be carried unspoken. He lay still and let it walk. When he slept, it was walking still.

It came back the next night. And the next.

---

At the forge on the lane, on some evenings, a small light burned late. The boy who lived three streets and one left turn away could not see it from his window, but the town could, and the town had a story for it — the same town that had a story for the silver-haired child down at the Tang house, and for the physician's boy who had not been seen coughing once all year.

Three stories, one small city, and not one of them understood. The town was right about that, later, in a way it would never be able to prove.

In the Su house, the house closed itself for the night in its usual order. His father marked a page and set the lamp low to save the oil. His mother wrote the day's last number in the grain-house book and unpinned her hair. Downstairs the pig slept by the stove with its chin on the boy's discarded shoe, and the pot ticked as it cooled, the small patient clock of the week.

Su Yan lay in the dark with the second circle going its rounds, and thought the thought he thought every night, which was a fine thought and got finer every week.

Nine ranks to go.

Under the floorboards, the sea kept working at the harbor wall.

The boy in the bed was warm.

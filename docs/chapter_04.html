4. The Still Well

Morning came, and the world was the same world, and the water in him ran cleaner.

That was the whole of it, and it took him a week to trust it. The Still Well Method was not faster than the road before it. It was deeper. The same hour of sitting drew more water, and drew it cleaner, and lost less of it on the way up, and no panel announced any of that, because there was nothing to count. The crystal in the headmaster's office counted levels. It could not weigh.

But the costs knew. An apple poured cheaper than it had in the autumn, the same rest in it for less of the well. The method hour ended with the well fuller behind it than the hour before. He had spent a year learning that a method has seams, and where every seam had been, there was now just water, going down and coming up, and the seams were what the missing parts had turned out to be for.

The panel said one thing all summer, and said it at dawn, and said it in the dark.

「Technique — The Still Well Method: 100%」

The meters that still moved were the ring's, quietly, and the lists', on plain life, and the level, which was not the system's number and had never been. Sixteen by the first frost. Eighteen at New Year, read in private, kept off every list. Nineteen by the thaw. Twenty between the spring planting and the first mowing, and this time, when the water stood, he was not afraid of it. He knew the shape of a wall now. A wall was a door that cost something.

The autumn term brought the displays, and the yard gathered for the first soul-class as it always did, and Su Ping brought the apple up over his palm with the ring turning above it, as he had done the year before.

The ring was not white.

It was the palest yellow — young straw, holding light — and the yard made a sound he had never heard the yard make. Not the snort. Something lower and quieter, from forty throats at once, because a first ring was white or, for the richest and the luckiest, a proper yellow off a hundred-year beast, and every child in the yard knew the difference, and every child in the yard could see his had been white a year ago.

"Rings do not age," said Headmaster Lin, to the yard, in the register-voice, and then he looked at Su Ping for exactly as long as he had looked at the crystal the year before, and added nothing at all.

That evening, in the office, with the door shut: "Say it back to me."

"Rings do not age, headmaster."

"No." Lin sat down behind the register, and his hands were very still on it. "They do not. In the whole of the Hall's records, on the whole of this continent, a ring does not gain years after the taking. There are stories." He said the word as if setting it down at arm's length. "Stories are what the Hall calls the things it cannot file. I have now watched a food-type move five levels in a year and a ring cross from white to yellow in its seat, and one of those things I have a column for, and the other one I do not." He looked at the boy. "You will not display the soul at assembly again. The yard's memory is short and kind. The Hall's is neither. Do you understand what I am keeping for you?"

"I think so, headmaster."

"Then think so a little harder every year," said Lin, and opened the register, and that was all the office had to say about it, then or after. Not yet had a new weight on it now. It had a color.

In the third month of spring, Ma Fei hit his own wall.

He had come to the school at level four, the highest reading in five years, and he had climbed the honest way, three hours in the morning and three at night, every day, the diligence of a boy whose soul was a hammer and who knew what hammers were for. Ten came to him at nine years old, and the water stood, and Ma Fei walked around the yard for two days like a man carrying a full pail he could not set down, and on the third day Lin took him east with a permit and the school's second-best hoe.

They came back three days later, and Ma Fei was a Spirit Master.

The ring over his iron hammer was white, and the beast had been the big boar of the east slope, forty years if it was a day — the oldest thing Lin would put a nine-year-old under. The yard turned out to see it, and somebody at the back snorted, a plain white boar ring, all that noise for a boar. And this time Ma Fei turned around by himself.

"It's the biggest boar on Saddleback," he said. "I asked for the biggest one his body could hold, and he took me to it, and I hit it." He looked around the yard, slow, as a hammer looks around. "Su Ping took the top of the white. I took the top of what was mine. Somebody around here better explain what's funny."

Nobody explained. The yard had learned that arithmetic a year ago, from the same porch rails.

Su Ping brought the apples for the celebration, and paid for them, and stood at the edge of it, and understood something he had no panel for. He had just watched the ordinary road walk past him in the other direction, at its own pace, in good order. Ma Fei at one ring and nine was fast — genuinely fast, the best the school had produced in a decade. And the engine in the dark was already four levels past him and pulling away, and would be past the second wall while Ma Fei's second wall was still a rumor. He was glad, and the gladness was clean, and underneath the gladness was a small cold stone of arithmetic, which he put away with all the other feelings, carefully, where he could find it later.

The wall-class met in the evenings now, the two of them, Ma Fei recovering from his ring and Su Ping sitting at his wall, and in the spring Lin did something he did for no class below the wall: he opened the school's second book.

"The method you were taught is the first road, and the first road is the same everywhere — the Hall prints it, and the villages hand it down. It will carry a man to the tenth level, and no further, with any honesty." He stood at the front of the room as he stood at assemblies, and his voice was the plain one. "Above it there is a second road. Every academy keeps one. They differ in small ways and agree in the large one: the second road draws deeper water, and it does it by a different turn of the breath. Here is the turn. Watch once."

And he showed them the turn. Once, slow, in the lamplight, a half-turn of the breath that bent the water in the room as a stone bends a stream — and Su Ping felt the system take it in, silently, as it took in everything: seen, filed, held. He had a full technique seat and no second one, and the new road had nowhere to sit, and he could feel that it did not care, and would wait. One ring from now, there would be three seats.

"You will both learn it properly when your walls are broken," said Lin, and closed the second book. "The world above the tenth level is a wider country than this one. It has more roads in it, and none of them are free."

None of them are free. Su Ping had heard the shape of that truth all year, in pieces, from the same mouth. The ladder above Yunxi: the intermediate academies in the prefecture cities, testing at twelve. The advanced academies in the great cities, testing on a stone the size of the spirit hall itself. The Hall's own schools above everything, testing on nothing so crude as a stone at all. Every gate on the continent opened the same way — sit down, show the crystal, be measured — because the continent had one currency and spent it at every door. "The world is sorted by speed," Lin had said, one evening, to the wall-class, with the lamplight on the register. "You will hear people say it is sorted by blood, or by money, or by the soul a child wakes with. Blood and money buy speed. The soul sets the first pace. But the ladder itself is climbed in one currency, and the Hall's tests exist to count it, and no test on this continent can be bribed with anything a family owns."

And then, because it was the wall-class and the book was open, he had taught them what the world's treasures cost — the real lesson, the one the seniors got in their last year. "There are things that buy rank outright," Lin had said. "Herbs, for the most part, grown where the earth is strange. The Silk Tulip of the far south, one bloom, conservatively ten ranks to the man who eats it, and a body remodeled into the bargain. The Orchid that gave a spirit master in the Hall's own registry five ranks from a single plant. The sac of a ninety-thousand-year beast is worth a full rank to a Titled Douluo. And a Titled Douluo, boys, is a country's treasure — there are perhaps ten alive." He had let that sit, and then he had said the rest of it, plainly, because it was the plain truth and he did not flinch from plain truths: "All of those are once. The herb is eaten and the herb is gone, and the man who built his ladder on herbs is standing, at ninety, three steps below the man who climbed. The Hall has measured this. The world's whole treasury cannot buy what a talent is, because treasury is quantity and talent is speed, and speed is the only thing on this continent that does not spend."

Su Ping had sat in the wall-class with his face exactly still, and inside the stillness he had done his own arithmetic, and then put the answer away with the cold stone, in the same careful place.

Because he had a herb that was not a herb. He had a spring. Every night the green apple came down out of the soul, and every night the engine ran at a better best, and the well refilled itself on the method's new cleanness, and none of it was eaten and gone — it was drawn and drawn again, from a tree that stood in him, and the tree was getting older. The Silk Tulip was lightning, once. What he carried was water, and water was the one thing the world's whole treasury could not buy, and the only safety it had ever had was that no one alive knew a food-type could carry it. Not the Hall. Not Lin. Not the stories. The closed hand was not greed. The closed hand was the wall around a spring. He had known that for a year, and sitting in the wall-class with the treasure-lesson in his ears, he knew it for the first time with his whole body: the day the world learned what the green apple was, the world would come for it. Politely at first, with the Hall's seal on the politeness.

Not yet.

The year's list came at the year's end, quietly, all at once, as it always came.

「Soul Power Control: 28%」

「Observation: 40%」

「Reading: 24%」

「Writing: 15%」

「Body Control: 31%」

「Walking and Running: 35%」

The letters had done that — a letter a month home, a letter a month to Wen Bo in the town, the reading of the second book with Lin's permission on long evenings. His father's reply that spring had been the longest letter the Su family had ever produced, four pages of a farmer's careful characters: the lower meadow was leased off Old Shi, the ditches were redug, the sapling stock was in the ground. The trees take five years to bear, his father wrote. The village says I am a fool to wait on trees.

Apple trees are patient, Su Ping wrote back. We are patient. It runs in the family.

Wen Bo's tenth of every queue was a purse of its own by then, and he counted it with the ox-calf patience he brought to everything, and in the spring he said — out of nowhere, over the second basket — that a man could carry fruit to the prefecture city and come home with the price of a shop. He said it carefully: the shape of a plan, not a dream. Su Ping, who had watched the porters take apples over the hill for two years, said nothing at the time. But he thought: the orchard is growing a merchant.

And at the end of the year, the office, the door shut, the crystal between the two teacups. Lin read the number and did not say it at once.

"Twenty," he said, finally. "And sitting at the wall."

"Yes, headmaster."

"At nine." Lin put the crystal away, and took out the other book — the Hall's book, with the Hall's seal dried into the cover — and opened it to a page Su Ping had never seen. "The second seat has more room in it than the first. The Hall's arithmetic says under seven hundred and sixty-four years, and the Hall's arithmetic is a fence for cattle, and you are not cattle, and a body is still a body, and bodies have limits as wells have bottoms." He turned the book so the boy could see the page: the second-seat column, the permits, the licensed forests. "Saddleback has nothing old enough. What you need is east of the river, in the deep licensed stands, and the permit for a second seat costs more than the first, and the academy's fund will stand behind it, because the academy has never in its history had a boy at the second wall at nine years old."

He closed the book, and folded his hands on it.

"The question is never whether," said Headmaster Lin. "It is which."

Outside, the yard was loud with summer, and in its seat the ring counted quietly on.

# Chapter 07 — The Cold Broke

The cold broke on a Tuesday, five weeks and one day after he came home from the basin.

He knew it had broken because the eaves stopped ticking at night, and because the woman in the locked room under the Hall went into labour four hours later, which he did not think was a coincidence and did not say aloud.

He had prepared for five weeks. He had prepared as a man prepares for a thing he has already watched happen once, in a book, at the age of fourteen, by lamplight, in another world.

There were two midwives, and not one, because he had read somewhere that the second one is for the mother and not for the child. There was hot water, and clean linen boiled twice, and a brazier that he had lit himself because he did not want anybody else's hands on it. There was a bowl of honey and a knife laid out on a tray in case of the thing he had no word for and did not want to think about. There was a nurse he had chosen from four candidates for the reason that she had raised six children of her own and had no opinions about the Saintess.

And there was the box of pills, and the two soul bones, and the jade he had bought from a merchant in the western market who had no idea what he was selling.

He had done all of it in secret, through three hands, and the Hall of Accounts believed the expenditure was for a shrine.

---

He stood outside the door for the first hour.

He stood with his hands behind his back, which was how he stood when he did not know what to do with them, and he listened, and he counted the bells in the city, and he understood that he was frightened in a way he had not been frightened at the basin.

At the basin he had known what would happen if he failed. He would die, or a ring would go, and both of those were things a man could picture.

He could not picture this.

He had read the child's whole life. He had read it at fourteen, in a book with a cracked spine, and he had read it as boys read, which is to say quickly and for the exciting parts, and he had not understood at the time that he was memorizing a person.

She awakens at six. She goes into the palace of the Heaven Dou Empire at nine, wearing a dead prince's face. She poisons two boys. She becomes a god at twenty-six, and she is the first person in ten thousand years to do it. And then she saves her mother's life at a mountain pass and the god in her breaks, and she comes down to eighty-nine and stays there, and she can never go up again, and she lives a long time after that with a hole in the middle of her.

He had read all of it. And he was standing outside a door.

The nurse came out and asked him, in the tone of a woman who has done this many times and has no patience for men, whether he intended to come in or to guard the wood.

He went in.

---

The room was hot, and it smelled of blood and of the honey on the tray, and Bibi Dong was on the bed with her hair loose and her hands gripping the frame, and she did not look at him.

She had not looked at him in seven months. She had looked through him twice and past him many times, and she had taken the food he sent and the light he had put into her bruises in the first month, and she had never once looked at him.

He went and stood at the foot of the bed, because there was nowhere else to stand.

The birth took three more hours.

He was not useful. He was the strongest man in the building and the second strongest on the continent. He had two rings of gold at his feet and a core in his chest that no living soul master had. There was not one single thing in him that could help a woman push a child into the world, and he understood that with total clarity, and stood still, and held a wet cloth.

He spoke to her twice. Once to tell her the nurse said she was doing well, which the nurse had not said in those words. Once to say her name.

She did not answer either time, and he did not expect her to.

---

The child came at the fourth hour, and the room went quiet in a way that rooms do.

The nurse cleaned her, and turned her, and looked at her, and then looked at the other midwife, and the two of them had a conversation with their eyebrows.

He said, "What."

"She is warm," the nurse said. "Not fever warm. Warm like a banked forge. And her grip." She put a finger into the child's hand and the child closed on it, and the nurse's face changed. "Your Grace, she has a grip on her."

He took her.

She was seven months of his life and nine days of the cold breaking. She was eight pounds of something that had never existed before and would never exist again. She was warm as the nurse had said, and when he put his arm under her head she turned toward him with her eyes shut, because newborns turn toward heat.

That was all it was. He knew that. He had read that somewhere too.

The gold came up around him anyway.

Both rings, and the layer over them, and the halo, and the light that he had spent seven months learning to keep down in front of other people, and none of it answered to him. It came up out of his chest as the second thing had come up at the basin, because the blood decided to. It stood in a locked room under the Hall with a woman who hated him and two midwives who would be paid well and told nothing.

He did not try to put it down. He had tried that at the basin and learned what it cost. He stood there and let his own blood make a fool of him in front of his daughter, and she slept through it.

---

Bibi Dong sat up.

She had been lying back with her eyes shut, and she sat up, and the nurse moved to help her and was waved away with a hand that had no strength in it and no patience either.

She looked at the child.

He watched her face the whole time, because he had learned in seven months to read that face at a distance, and because he had read in a book what this woman would one day do.

There was nothing in her face at all. That was the frightening part. Not hatred. He could have worked with hatred. Hatred was a thing a man could stand in front of. What was in her face was the absence of anything, and it went on being absent for a long moment, and then her hands came off the bedframe.

He turned.

He did not decide to. His body did it, as it had done everything for seven months. He put his shoulder between the bed and his daughter, and he did not move the light down, and he did not say anything.

"Give her to me."

The voice was low, and even, and it was the voice of a woman who has spent her whole life enduring things and has become extremely good at it.

"No."

"Give her to me."

"No."

She looked at him then. It was the first time in seven months that she had looked at him, and it was not the look he had been waiting for and it was not the look he had been afraid of either, and he had no idea what to do with it.

"You did this," she said.

"Yes."

He did not say anything else. He had thought for seven months about what he would say in this room, and he had prepared four versions of it, and every one of the four was a speech, and a speech was the last thing that belonged in that room.

"I know," he said. "And I am not going to tell you it was anything other than what it was. And I am not going to ask you for anything about her. You are her mother and you will be for as long as you live. If you want her in this room or out of it, if you want her named or unnamed, if you want me in this building or on the other side of the continent, then it is yours to decide and I will do it."

She did not answer.

She lay back down and turned her face toward the wall, and she did not ask for the child again that night, and he did not offer.

What she did after that was hers. He had decided that a long time ago, in a different room, about a box of pills, and the decision held here as well.

---

Qian Daoliu came at dawn.

He came alone, which was not a thing he did, and he came into a locked room under the Hall without knocking, and he looked at his son standing by the brazier with a child against his shoulder, and he stopped.

He was not looking at the child.

He was looking at the two gold rings around his son's ankles, which were out, because his son had not been able to put them down, and the light on them was doing something in the low grey dawn that it did not do by lamp.

"So it is true," the old man said.

"It is true."

"And that is hers."

"No. That is mine. She has nothing yet and she will have nothing until she is six years old and it wakes up in her."

The old man came closer, and he looked at the child for a while, and his face did something that his son had not seen on it in seven months and did not have a name for. Then he put out one finger, and the child closed on it exactly as she had closed on the nurse's, and the old man's breath went out of him.

"Her hands," he said.

"Yes."

"Her mother's hands are like that."

"Yes."

The old man was quiet for a long time. Then he said, "She cannot stay in this room."

"I know."

"She cannot be in this building. There are eleven men in the Elder Hall who have wanted the Saintess dead for three years, and they will hear about this child before the week is out. Six of them will understand exactly what a six-winged angel in this family means. Two of those six will be frightened, and frightened men in this hall do not write letters."

"I know that too."

"Then you know what I am going to say."

"You are going to say that she goes to the Worship Hall," his son said. "And that you will raise her. And that she will be hidden, and she will be safe, and she will have every resource the family has, and she will not know her mother's face."

The old man looked at him.

"You have thought about it."

"I have thought about nothing else for seven months."

"And."

"And you are right," his son said. "She goes to the Worship Hall."

---

He carried her up the hill himself, at the hour when the bells go for morning prayer, with two gold rings around his ankles and a light on him that he could not put down and a city full of people who would have knelt in the street if they had known what he was carrying.

The Worship Hall is cold. It is cold in every season, because it is stone and it is on the highest ground and nobody has ever thought to put a fire in it, and the Angel God stands at the end of the hall in gold and has stood there longer than the family has had a name.

He stood under the statue with his daughter against his shoulder.

He had stood in this exact place nine days ago and listened to his father explain to him what the gold ring on that statue was. He had stood here and heard the words *it is the seat itself, wearing a colour so that men can see it.*

His daughter was four hours old.

He looked up at the gold, and he looked down at the child. He did the thing he had been doing since the first night in this body. He took a piece of knowledge that belonged to a boy of fourteen in another world, and he held it up against the thing in front of him, and he waited until one of them gave.

He knew her whole life. He knew the palace and the two poisoned boys and the nine trials and the six years and the mountain pass, and he knew the number she would stop at.

He did not know whether any of it was still true.

His blood was not the blood that had made her in the book. He had spent seven months making it into something else, twice over, in a basin in the eastern forest, and the second time it had cost him a meridian and a rank. Whatever she had inherited, she had inherited out of that and not out of the man in the book.

So the road he had memorized at fourteen was a road that might not exist any more.

He found, standing under the Angel God at the hour of morning prayer with a four-hour-old child on his shoulder, that this was the best thing he had heard in seven months, and the worst.

"You are going to be raised in this hall," he told her. "By your grandfather, who is a good man and a hard one. You are going to be taught that you come from something, because you do, and that it is yours to carry, because it is, and you are going to be told very little about your mother, which will be a mistake and I will not be able to stop it, because I will not be here enough to stop it."

The child made a small sound against his collar.

"But I am going to be here," he said. "Not enough. I know that already. But here, and I am going to teach you the one thing nobody in this hall is going to think to teach you, and I am going to teach it badly, because I never had it myself and I am making it up as I go along."

The bells went for morning prayer all over the city below them.

He stood there until the child slept properly, and then he gave her to the nurse who had raised six of her own, and he walked back down the hill with two gold rings around him and the light finally going down, into a Spirit City that did not know that anything at all had happened.

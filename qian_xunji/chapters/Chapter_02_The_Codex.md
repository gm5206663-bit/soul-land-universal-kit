# Chapter 02 — The Codex

Pope Hall smelled of old paper and holy light, and the desk was stone and heavy under his hands, carved with the six wings that watched everything in this hall without ever blinking.

He sat down and let the body settle.

Chest warm. Dantian solid, the new core turning with its patient swallow, drinking the world's power in and handing it back stronger. The six wings showed faint behind the chair, white-gold at the edges, and the nine rings moved with his breathing instead of hanging outside him.

Ninety-six. One level above the man the Hall remembered, and worth that man a hundred times over.

The world had no scale for him yet, and he did not need one. What he needed was paper.

He picked up the brush, dipped it, and wrote the first sheet of the first book.

He began with kinds.

Two, and only two, because a third kind would not exist for twenty thousand years and a page describing it would have read as the raving of a sick man. Beasts: the old familiar shapes, tiger and wolf and dragon and rabbit, the soul worn on the body, the man taking on the beast's features when he let it out. Tools: hammers and blades and towers and lamps, the soul held in the hand and worked like an instrument, and every plant on the continent folded in among them whether the plant-soul holders liked it or not.

And within the beasts, a rank of its own at the top, which almost nobody alive had ever seen and which he wrote down plainly because it was the plain truth.

The divine class. Six-winged angel. Sea God. The souls that carried a god's shape in them.

His own was the first of those, and he did not soften it, and he did not boast about it either. He wrote it in the same hand as the rest.

Then the grades, low to god, seven steps of a ladder that most of the continent climbed blind and argued about loudly.

He paused longest over one name.

The Clear Sky Hammer. Every schoolchild under two empires could recite the claim about it: strongest weapon soul under heaven.

It was not.

Its true grade was Top, which was high and rare and not the highest thing in the world. What made it terrible was the nine generations of men who had taught it to be terrible — technique stacked on technique, art laid over art, until the metal outlived its own grade and a stronger soul in careless hands would have lost to it.

He wrote that exactly, and his brush did not hesitate.

And then he wrote one more line under it, because the Hall's own records were still warm and because a book that flinched was not worth the paper.

*Three hundred li east of Heaven Dou City there is a valley full of that family, and a man of forty-four out of it broke the last Pope with one blow.*

The sentence would travel. He let it travel.

He wrote the rings next.

White for a decade. Yellow for a century. Purple for a thousand years, black for ten thousand, and red for a hundred thousand — old enough to be something more than a beast, and called so. Past the red, orange at two hundred thousand years, and gold at a million. The books named three beasts in all the ages that had reached a million, and he wrote the number three down, because a number that small teaches a man more than a legend does.

Then he wrote what a ring was worth, which no book in the Hall's own library had ever carried.

One skill, up to ten thousand years. Two skills at a hundred thousand. Four at a million.

A hunter who did not know that would trade a red ring for three black ones and go home believing he had won the trade of his life.

Then the mechanism that none of the old broken books had ever once explained, and which he had known since he was nine years old in another world.

When a beast that old died to a successful hunter, it left a bone behind, and the bone did not go to the parts of the body the hunter already owned. It filled what was empty.

He wrote it in one line: *what you are missing is what you are offered.*

Then the bones themselves.

Six places a bone could sit — torso, head, both arms, both legs — and he ranked them, because a man who will only ever hold one in his whole life should know which one to pray for. The torso first. The head next. The arms after that, and the legs last.

He wrote the rare growths outside the six as well, which could grow with their owner and never once stopped being rare. And he wrote the odds, because odds are what keep a man from ruin: one beast in a thousand leaves a bone at all, and a thousand-year beast leaves one only by luck.

A bone once absorbed came out of a living man never. It left when he died, and then it belonged to whoever picked it up. And there was one exception, which the Hall knew and the continent did not: a Titled Douluo could cut his own limb off and pay ten levels for the privilege, and tear the bone out of it.

He wrote the prices too. A book that teaches without prices is a book that lies.

Then the two columns everybody forgot — the body, and the spirit.

Physique first: how much of a ring a body could carry before it tore itself apart on its own ambition, and why a high ring should be fed to a body that could hold it instead of to a body that deserved it.

And spiritual power.

Here he slowed down, because here the world had nothing at all.

He gave the ranges in plain words. The spirit every child is born with. The spirit that can be held and turned, where control begins. The spirit wide as a sea, where an expert begins and where the sixth and seventh rings stop being a gamble. The abyss, where most men stop for good, and where a Titled Douluo stands. And above the abyss a domain of its own, the ground a man must be standing on before the highest walls will open to him.

He did not write the names.

The world had no words for them yet, and would not have believed words it did not have. He kept the ladder in his own head, where it lived with everything else he had read, and he wrote only what a man could measure against himself.

He wrote nothing at all about cores.

That was deliberate, and it was the hardest page he turned. There were truths a continent could simply be handed, and truths a continent would break itself on, and the core belonged to the second kind until the ground underneath it had been raised. He meant to come back. He was not absent.

And what he left out was longer than what he put in, and he knew every line of it by heart.

No cores. Nothing of the beasts' own road, or the towers that would one day bargain for their souls and call it mercy. Nothing of the god seats, or how few of them there were, or which of them stood empty and waiting. Nothing of the twenty thousand years he had read to the last page of.

He wrote the ground and he kept the sky, and he did it because a man who hands out the sky on the first day is a man nobody listens to on the second.

He wrote how to choose a ring.

Compatibility first, and he was blunt about it: the Angel's light would have nothing to do with poison, and a poison soul should keep its distance from the light, and a man who ignored that would spend his life fighting his own hand.

Absorption second — and here he gave the numbers, because numbers are the one part of this art a poor man can use.

A first ring from a beast under four hundred and twenty-three years. A second under seven hundred and sixty-four. A third under eighteen hundred. A fourth under five thousand. A fifth at twelve thousand. A sixth at twenty-five thousand. A seventh between thirty and fifty thousand. An eighth between fifty and a hundred thousand. And the ninth from a beast of a hundred thousand years or more.

Under the numbers he wrote the shape the ages had settled on, which was three purple, five black, and one red.

And last, the rule the Hall itself had been breaking for a century: quality over quantity. The old Hall had swallowed rings for decades and mistaken appetite for strength, and the book would teach it to eat with a purpose.

Then three rules that were in no book anywhere on the continent.

A beast soul is the stronger of the two until the true body comes at seventy. After that the tool soul is the stronger, because a tool takes the soul into itself and a beast only wears it.

A plant soul does not have to take a plant beast's ring, and a beast soul may take a plant's. The ring answers to what the man is missing, and not to what the beast was.

And a man stopped at a bottleneck has not stopped. Without a ring he cannot step up a stage, but the power keeps filling in behind the wall, and all of it shows on the day the ring arrives.

Near the front of the book, where a poorer writer would have put a preface about himself, he put the thing the Hall had been teaching wrong for a hundred years.

A weak soul is not a dead end. A weak soul with the right ring, the right bone, and a chosen direction will outlive a strong soul that has none of the three. What the continent called a trash soul was almost always a soul that nobody had bothered to read.

He wrote one last thing, at the back, where a reader would find it only after he had earned the finding.

There is a thing above skills. A field of a man's own, which he carries into the fight with him, and which no ring gives and no bottle buys and no teacher hands over. Almost nobody has one. Among Titled Douluo, only a handful ever do.

He did not write how it was won, because he did not intend to give that to anyone yet. He wrote only that it existed, so that men would stop believing the ninth skill was the end of the road.

He wrote until his hand hurt, and then the ink ran out and he called for more, and the paper stack grew sheet under sheet until it stood beside the desk like a low white wall.

Ghost came in near the end of it, black and silent, and bowed.

"Your Holiness. The elders are waiting."

He nodded and stood. The papers stayed where they were. Chest warm, dantian solid, core turning — he walked out through the corridors with that quiet radiance around him, and the servants stepped aside twice as fast as they used to.

The Elder Hall was round and cold even in summer, with twenty stone seats in a ring and seven raised at the front. Qian Daoliu's seat stood empty, because the Grand Worship rarely left his statue. Golden Crocodile sat at the front of the front — old, scarred, ninety-eight, one step from the last wall — with the eyes of a man who had buried every opponent he ever respected.

Chrysanthemum and Ghost took their places behind the Pope's seat.

He walked in, and the chamber felt him arrive.

Six wings faint behind him, white-gold, and the aura dense and heavy. Golden Crocodile lifted his eyes slowly, and something crossed the old man's face that a lifetime of war had taught him to hide at once — but it had crossed, and they both knew it.

He had never in ninety-eight years felt a holy aura sit in a body like this one sat. He did not have the word for it, and not having the word disturbed him more than the aura did.

Whispers moved around the ring and died.

He sat down in the Pope's seat and let the room come to order.

"I have called you for a while," he said. "I intend to write strict rules, and end the taking in this Hall. That is the first foundation."

Silence went through the round hall like cold air.

An elder with a grey beard leaned forward. "Your Holiness, the taking—"

Another cut across him from further down the ring. "Spirit Hall has always—"

Chrysanthemum glanced at Ghost. Some of the seats in that ring took offerings from the branch halls. Some sold soul-beast sightings to the highest purse. Every man there knew which seats the others meant, and the Pope held three votes, and twenty elders speaking one way together could unseat him.

Everyone had read the same page of the same law.

Golden Crocodile raised one hand, and the hall went quiet.

"Let His Holiness speak," he said, in a voice that was old and hoarse and used to being obeyed. "What strict rules?"

He laid it out for them.

"Branch halls take extra taxes. Main Halls sell hunting spots ahead of the public lists. Elders take a cut of both. That weakens the Hall."

He let that sit a moment.

"The Hall is sacred to soul masters because the Pope Hall and the Douluo Hall stand in it. Because the dead are honoured here, and the living are certified here, and every child on this continent has his soul read by a clerk we pay. If the ones teaching are corrupt, the people stop believing. And if they stop believing—"

He stopped, and let the room finish it.

"—the Angel God loses faith."

He did not say the other reason, and he kept that folded where it lived. In this world belief had weight. Faith stored up in one place, over long years, in a hall a whole continent truly believed in, might in time raise something out of itself. The first Angel God had been a man who drew a sword he should not have drawn, and spent himself removing evil to atone for it, and became a god through the faith he earned.

He did not intend to leave that lying unclaimed.

But it stayed in his chest, and what he said out loud was only the truth any man in the ring could check for himself.

Golden Crocodile looked at him for a long moment.

"Then write them," he said. "And bring them here."

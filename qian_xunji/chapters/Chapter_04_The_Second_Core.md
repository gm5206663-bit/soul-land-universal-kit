# Chapter 04 — The Second Core

Winter came down on Spirit City and the plum trees along the east wall went black and thin, and in the secret chamber under the Worship Hall a man sat with his eyes closed and tried to build a second centre in his own head.

He had been at it five nights.

The first core sat in his dantian and turned, and it had been comparatively easy — the books had described it, the age was right for it, and the body had wanted it. The second was another matter entirely.

The second did not go in the dantian.

It went higher. Into the spiritual sea between the eyebrows, where the spirit was wide enough to hold it and deep enough to keep it. That was where the books put the second and third centres, in the head and in the middle, and he had known the location since he was nine years old in another world.

Knowing where a thing goes and building one are not the same work.

The first night he had pressed too hard and the sea had closed on him like a hand, and he had come out of it with blood on his teeth and a headache that lasted until noon.

The second night he had pressed too gently, and nothing had happened at all, which was worse, because a man can learn from a failure and cannot learn from an absence.

The third night he had found the shape of it — soul power drawn up out of the dantian, carried along a channel too fine to see, and held in the sea while the sea learned to hold it back. He had held it for the length of four breaths and lost it on the fifth.

The fourth night, eleven breaths.

Tonight he intended to stop counting breaths and start counting nights.

---

The work had three parts, and he had written them down on a sheet and pinned the sheet to the wall, because a man alone with a hard problem will lie to himself by the third day.

Compression. The power had to be packed as the first had been packed, liquid pressed toward solid, and the packing had to be even, because an uneven centre cracks under the first real use of it.

Holding. The sea had to be taught that this was a thing and not a wound.

And the cut.

That was the part nobody had ever written about, because nobody in this era had ever done one, and the book that described it would not be written for twenty thousand years. A core is a centre, and a centre has to have an edge, and the edge is not grown — it is cut, in one movement, at the moment the packing is right, and a man who hesitates at the cut gets a lump of compressed power in his head and no core at all.

He had known that for eleven years.

Knowing it did not make the cutting easier. It made it worse, because he knew exactly what a hesitation cost.

---

He worked at it until the lamps burned low, and then he stopped, because a tired sea makes bad edges, and went up into the hall.

There was a plain sword on a stand by the chair where he read.

No name on it, no history, no rank. Steel, a grip wrapped in old cord, and a weight that told the truth about itself when he picked it up.

He had bought it in the third week, from a Hall armourer who had assumed it was for ceremony, and every morning since, before the light came, he had stood in the cold courtyard and cut with it.

Not forms. He had no forms and there was nobody in this Hall who could have taught him any, and he had asked nobody, because the last thing this story needed was another old master arriving to hand a Pope his art.

He cut. Straight down. And then he cut again, and tried to make the second cut land where the first had landed.

The books had been plain about this. The sword road was a road a man could walk without a sword soul in him — a discipline of the hand and the eye and the timing, climbed in three long stages, the last of which took a lifetime and most men never saw. The Angel's own line had produced one such man in the age to come, ninety-seven years old and called by the world for nothing else.

He was not that man. He was a man who knew the road existed, and who understood that a body which only ever used a domain would go soft at the edges, and that a soft edge was the thing that killed Popes.

So he cut.

He kept a ledger of it, which was the only habit from the other life he had carried over without thinking about it. Two hundred mornings, and beside each one a mark for whether the second cut had landed where the first had. The first forty days were almost all blanks. Then a run of marks, then a week of blanks again when the weather turned and his shoulder went stiff, then marks.

By the two hundredth morning the cut landed in the same place twice out of three times, and he had begun to be able to feel the difference before he saw it — a small resistance in the wrist a half-breath early, which was the whole of the warning a man ever got.

And he had learned one more thing, which had taken him six weeks and a great deal of cold.

The held cut.

A cut that is started and not finished teaches nothing, because the body forgets it. But a cut that is carried all the way to the edge and then held there, with the weight committed and the wrist set and nothing let go, teaches the arm exactly where the edge is. He did fifty of those a morning, and by the end of the second month he could stop the blade a finger's width off a post in the dark.

That was the exercise that mattered, and no book had ever described it, because no book had ever needed to teach a man with a domain how to use a piece of steel.

That was the whole of it. There was no secret in the courtyard, and no voice behind him, and nobody watching except a servant who had learned to walk the other side of the yard.

---

The guild's reader came up to the Hall that week with a question he had not been able to answer downstairs.

He was a young man of twenty-two with ink on both cuffs, and he stood in the doorway holding his cap because he had been told that was what you did, and he had been told wrong.

"Sit down," the Pope said. "What is it."

The reader sat on the edge of a chair like a man expecting to be moved along.

"There is a man in the room, Your Holiness. Sixth ring, forty years old, blacksmith by trade. He read the page about the bottleneck and he has been coming every day since to ask the same question, and I have run out of answers."

"Which question."

"He wants to know whether the book is right." The reader's ears went red. "He has been at fifty-one for six years. He has the money for a ring now, and he is afraid that the page is a comfort written by a man who never had to stand at a wall. He asked me who wrote it, and I told him, and he said a Pope would not know what a wall feels like."

The room was quiet for a moment.

"He is right," the Pope said.

The reader looked up.

"A Pope has not stood at a wall since he was thirty. He has not needed money for a ring in forty years. He has servants." He turned the page he had been reading and marked it. "So tell him this. The book is not a comfort. It is a description. The power is filling in behind his wall whether he believes the page or not, and it has been filling in for six years, and it will all be there on the day he takes the ring."

He paused.

"And tell him to come back and tell me whether it was."

The reader stood up, and sat down again, because he had not finished.

"There is one more thing. He asked why the book gives the numbers at all. He said—" the reader hesitated, and then said it, "—he said the Hall has never given anybody a number in its life, and he wants to know what the Hall gets out of this one."

The Pope looked at him for a moment.

"That is the best question anybody has asked in that room," he said. "Tell him I will answer it in the second volume. And tell him the second volume will be shorter than the first, because the answers are shorter than the questions."

The reader went out backwards, holding his cap, and forgot to put it on until he was halfway down the stairs.

---

The ninth night, the Grand Worship came to the chamber door.

He did not knock, and he did not open it. He stood outside with his hands behind his back, as he had stood five months earlier, and he listened to the light for a while.

Then he said two things, plainly, to a door.

"Five nights."

And after a moment:

"It was mine."

He did not explain either sentence, and the man inside did not ask him to. There was no need. An old man who had spent fifty years watching his son fail had counted the nights of this one, and had worked out what the fifth night meant, and had come to say the only thing that was his to say — that the failure had been his, and not the boy's.

The light behind the door went on turning.

The old man stood there until the cold got into his knees, and then he went back to his statue.

---

On the tenth night he made the cut.

Not with the sword. The sword was standing where it had stood for two hundred mornings, and he did not touch it.

But the cut was the sword's.

That was the thing he had not understood for nine nights and understood on the tenth. The centre he was building was not made of soul power at all. It was made of the sea — of spirit — and spirit has to be given an edge before it will hold a shape. Two hundred mornings of one cut, landing in the same place, had put that edge into him. Not into the steel. Into him.

So he made the cut inside.

It was smaller than he had expected. Not a movement of the arm or the will, but a decision — the packing was right, the sea was holding, and the edge had to be made now or the moment would go, and he made it without consulting himself about it.

There was a sound in his head like a string being drawn tight across a bowl.

And then there was a centre — a spirit's centre and not a soul power's, cold where the first was warm, and as straight as the cut that had made it — and the knowing of it was so plain and so undramatic that he almost laughed at it.

Thin, and not yet solid, and turning slowly between his eyebrows in the wide dark of the sea — but a centre, with an edge to it, drinking in what it was given and giving back a little more than it took.

He sat with it until the lamps went out on their own.

It was not finished. It would take months to solidify, and he knew how many months, because he had read the number in a book written by men who stood on the far side of this from him.

No man alive in this era carried two centres. No man alive in this era carried one.

The method for the ordinary kind would not be invented for twenty thousand years, by a man not yet born, in a school that did not exist — and he had not used it. He had built this one out of his own spirit and his own sword, and there was no book in any age that described the thing he had just done.

---

At the end of the year he walked the walls alone and counted what he had.

The angel at ninety-six, six wings white-gold, the blood running purer than it had ever run in the line. The first core in his dantian, low and warm and finished — the blood's own purity, holding a shape at last. The second, between his eyebrows, thin and straight as a blade's edge, and turning, made of his spirit and his sword.

His spiritual power past the abyss where ordinary men stopped, and standing in the range the books had a name for and this world did not.

Two bones of the Angel set worn all the way through to a hundred thousand years. The Angel Domain, which the blood comprehended and which no teaching could hand over. And a sword cut that landed in the same place twice.

He stood on the wall and looked out over Spirit City under its snow, and he made an assessment of himself, as a man takes stock of a house he has just bought.

Under the gods, he was the strongest thing on this continent.

Not the strongest man. His father was ninety-nine and had beaten the man on Haotian Peak, and there was a woman on an island who had beaten both of them, and there were nine worshipers in this Hall alone who did not answer to him. Strength was not a line you stood at the end of; it was a room with several people in it, and he had walked into the room.

But under the gods — yes. He believed that, and he had checked it, and he had checked it honestly, and the checking was the part that mattered.

He did not say it out loud. He did not write it down. He held it as a man holds a lamp in a dark house — carefully, and without showing it to anybody through the window.

---

He went back inside, and down, and sat in the chamber with two centres turning in him.

The Hall was quiet above him. The Elders were arguing about the second volume in a room he did not attend. Bibi Dong had walked the yard nine times that afternoon and had spoken to no one, and on the eleventh of the month she had written four characters on the wall by her window, in charcoal, where the guard could not help but see them and report them.

*It is not the light.*

He had read the report, and had not answered it, and had left the charcoal on the wall.

She was still there. The child was due when the cold broke. He knew the child's whole life and had decided, some months ago, that knowing it was not the same as being permitted to use it.

He slept in the middle of all of it, that night and every night after — the Hall above him, the guards at the doors, the round hall arguing, the woman under her locks, the daughter not yet born, the continent leaning toward a weight it could not yet name.

He slept in the middle of it. That was the whole art.

# Chapter 06 — The Colour of Gold

He stayed at the basin four more days, and on the fourth night he did the second ring.

He had meant to do it on the second. His body said otherwise. He slept most of a day with the ridge under his first ring throbbing like a bad tooth, and when he woke the cold water felt less cold than indifferent, and that was worse. So he waited, and ate, and did arithmetic, because arithmetic was the only honest thing left.

The first ring had cost him blood he could not make back in a week, and the blood in him was thinner now. The second would want more and not less, because the seam went deeper every time he opened one. He had come here believing he would finish all nine in a month. He was going to finish two, and go home, and come back when the blood had filled in. That was the shape of the road, and it was years long, and the moment he understood it he stopped arguing.

---

The second ring was the other yellow one. Seven hundred and sixty years, out of a bird of prey that had carried light along its wings, and it had given him a shield for fifty years.

He sat against the same stone in the same place, for the same reason: if he went over he would go into the cold, and the cold had already saved him once. He looked at the seam before he touched it, because he had learned that much.

The seam under the second ring was newer than the first, and shallower, and nothing had ever torn it before, and for a while he thought that would make it easier.

It made it worse.

The first seam had a scar in it, and a scar is a place where the body has already learned to let go. This one had not learned anything. He pulled, and it held, and then it tore along its whole length at once instead of in one place, and the pain was so clean and so total that he heard himself make a sound he had not made since he was eleven years old in another world.

Blood came out of his nose and his ears and from under his nails, and he had nothing left in him to be ashamed of the noise.

The years went down into him anyway. The core took them, and the blood weighed them, and the blood kept less of them than it had kept the first time. He felt that happen, and it was the worst part of the night. Not the tearing. The weighing. He could feel his blood go through those years as a merchant goes through a purse by lamplight, setting aside what it could not afford. He was poorer than he had been four days ago and the difference was not coming back.

The core opened and drank, and the pool dipped a finger's width again, and the two waters went on refusing to mix. The seam re-knit. It took most of the night, and twice he stopped breathing and started again without deciding to.

When it was finished, the second ring was gold. Two of them at his feet now, and seven above them still wearing the colours of dead animals.

---

He tried the shield first, because the shield was what he reached for in the dark.

It came. Exactly as it had always come, a wall of gold feathers turning slowly around him, and for a moment he thought the night had cost him nothing but blood.

Then the feathers hardened.

They stopped being light and became something with a grain, and the grain was the grain of his own flesh turned outward, and the shield stopped hanging around him and lay along him instead, and he understood that he was wearing it rather than holding it.

He stood and walked into the cold spring and let the water take him.

The cold did not get in. It stood off him like rain off a roof, and it did not shudder, and it did not thin at the edges. After a while he walked out again and stood on the stone with the frost steaming off his shoulders, and understood that he had not spent anything. His soul power was low and had been low all night, and the shield had not taken from it, because it was not running on it any more.

He called the blessing up.

Fifteen small particles came out of his hands and went slowly around him, exactly as they had done for fifty years, and the light was the same gentle warm gold it had always been. He put it into the crooked meridian in his side and the ache that had been there for five weeks simply stopped.

The meridian stayed crooked. The light could take the pain out of a thing and could not put the thing back as it had been, and he wrote that down as a limitation.

And then a second thing came, which he had not called.

It came up out of his chest instead of out of his hands, and it was not light but heat, and it went along his arms and stood on his skin, and the gold under the skin came with it. He looked down at himself in the dark and saw the grain of it running along his forearms like scale over scale, and he felt his strength go up with it, and not by a little.

He put his hand flat on a boulder the size of a cart and stood up with it, without soul power, which he had not been able to do that morning. And the poison was gone. He had carried a residue of the two poisons for five weeks, a low heat along one side that never went out, and it was not there. Whatever his blood had become, it would not hold poison.

The bird's shield was still his and the sparrow's blessing was still his. Nothing had been taken away. Something had been added, and the thing added was his own blood standing up on the outside of him, and it did not answer to his soul power at all. He let the gold down and the strength went with it, and he was tired in a way he had not been tired since the seclusion month, and the cost had come out of him and not out of his purse of soul power, and he had felt it go.

The golden layer came up, and the halo, and the light, and the pillar over the eastern forest. He had expected the pillar. He had not expected the second one to be taller.

It stood over the trees longer than the first had, and when it went down the wings stayed out a while, and the domain rolled out across the forest and did not want to come back in, and he sat with his hands flat on the stone until they stopped shaking. Then he lay down beside the two waters and slept, and dreamed of a sparrow.

---

He stayed three more days, and on the last of them he made a pill for himself.

He had carried the purple chrysanthemum in oiled cloth for five weeks and had not used it, because he had been saving it for a bottleneck he had not expected to reach so soon. It was a neutral herb with no scent at all, its petals furry to the finger, its stamen standing half a foot above them with a little gold light on the tip. The book said: eaten, it sends the breath out into the limbs and the blood through the eight vessels, and a man may train an indestructible body on it.

He ground it in the iron pot, bound it with honey, rolled one pill the size of a thumb joint, and swallowed it at dusk beside the cold spring, because he had learned what it was worth sitting near water.

The medicine went into him like a rope going through a pulley. It went out along his limbs first, and that was merely enormous. Then it went for the eight vessels, and one of the eight was the meridian along his left side that a poison had opened five weeks ago and that had healed crooked.

It hit the crook as a flood hits a bent pipe.

He went over on the stone. Something in his side tore that had already been torn once, and the pain was not clean this time, it was a tearing with a hook in it, and he bit his tongue through and filled his mouth, and the domain went out of him without his permission.

He lay in his own blood at the edge of the coldest water on the continent and understood that he had done it again. He had taken a thing of the highest grade into a body with a known fault in it, and had not thought about the fault.

Then the wall came down.

It did not break. It came down as a held breath comes down, all at once, and everything he had been holding behind it for seven months went through in the same moment.

Heaven and earth came into him. He had read that phrase in a book and had always assumed it was a book's phrase. It was not. Something came down out of the air and up out of the ground and met in the middle of him. The nine rings went up from his feet. The two cores in him turned together for the first time. His wings came out to their full spread and stayed out, and the domain went over the whole forest and did not stop at the treeline. The pillar over the eastern forest was the tallest of the three.

Ninety-seven.

He lay on the stone until the light went down, and then longer, because his legs were not his legs.

He had been ninety-six for seven months. Every master on this continent above ninety-five measures the next level in decades of his life, and he had crossed one in seven months, on an herb, and the herb was not finished with him. He could feel it still going. It would go on for days, the book said, strengthening the body until it and the medicine were one thing.

A rank was a number. What he had done was take a body that a soul master's arithmetic said was finished and hand it to something that did not read arithmetic.

---

He left on the ninth day and walked west.

He did not fly. He had six wings and the strength of a man twice his rank, and he walked, because a golden man in the sky above the empire was not a thing he wanted anybody describing to anybody else. The country was flat, and frozen, and empty, and he crossed it faster than he had come, because his body had stopped noticing the cold.

Nine slots. Every man on this continent had nine, and the ninth ring was the measure of a man, and the codex said so in the second volume in his own hand. He had not changed that. Two of his nine were gold and seven were still the colours of dead animals, and when the seventh turned there would be no tenth waiting underneath, because blood does not make new slots. What the blood had done was put a second thing over two of the rings he already had, and leave the nine exactly nine, and the bird and the sparrow exactly where they had always been.

On the fourth day he passed a village, and there were men outside the ale house, and one of them was talking with his hands.

"— over the trees, that high, and it stood there like it had a right to. Three nights it was, three separate nights, and each one higher than the last. My wife says it is a god come down to look at something in there."

"Ask the god. I only know it stood there and it was gold and my wife saw it from the yard, and she does not see things that are not there, and after the third one she would not come inside."

---

The guard at the gate straightened when he saw the face. Then he saw the rings. It was a small thing, a man's eyes going down and then up again quickly, as eyes do when they have seen something they will be asked about later. He said nothing at all. He bowed, and he opened the gate, and the bow went lower than a Pope is owed by a man on a gate.

Qian Daoliu was in the hall of statues, not under his own grandfather's face this time but in the middle of the floor with his hands behind him, facing the door, with the look of a man who had been standing there for some hours.

"You are back."

"I am back. Nine days, not six."

"So the roads held. Show me."

He let the rings out.

The hall was quiet for a long time.

"Gold," the old man said at last. "Gold is a million years."

"No."

"Gold is the Angel God's ring." His voice did not change, and that was how his son knew he was frightened. "It is the tenth ring, and it comes down with the god seat, and it has never in the history of this family come down on any man. I have stood under that statue for sixty years and looked at that ring every day of them. It is not a colour a man earns. It is the seat itself, wearing a colour so that men can see it."

"I know what it is."

"Then you know what you have done." The old man took a step closer, and his voice was still perfectly even, which was the worst sound in the room. "The seat does not come down on a man who kills for it. It comes down on blood. It can only ever go to a man of our family who carries the six-winged angel in him, and that is the one law in this hall that has never once been bent, and it is why our family has kept that hall shut to every outsider who ever asked. There is one gold ring in this world and it belongs to a seat, and you have two of them on your feet."

"They are not that ring."

"No," the old man said. "They are worse. That ring is a thing a man may inherit. Those are a thing a man has made out of himself, and I have never seen it and I have never read it and I have no name to put on it."

"They carry no year," his son said. "A ring is a beast's age, and that is what every ring on this continent is, and that is why the ladder is the ladder. Those two are not a beast's age. The bird and the sparrow are still in them and their skills are still mine. What is over the top of them is my blood, and it runs on blood and not on soul power, and it will be as deep as the blood gets and no deeper."

"How deep does the blood get."

"I don't know yet. That is the honest answer and it is the only one I have."

Qian Daoliu was quiet for a while. Then he said the thing his son had been waiting nine days to hear, and had not been sure he would hear.

"Then whose blood do you think you are carrying."

"The Angel God's."

"Yes." The old man sat down on the step beneath his own grandfather's statue, which was not a thing he had ever done in his life. "And you have made it deeper."

"Yes."

"In seven months."

"In seven months."

The old man put his head in his hands for a moment, and then took them away, and looked up, and he was not frightened any longer, and what he was instead was something his son had no name for. Then his eyes went down again, and did not stop at the rings.

"Ninety-seven," he said. "You were ninety-six when you left."

"I was."

The old man looked at him for a long moment, and then he laughed once, without any pleasure in it, and shook his head.

"Every man above ninety-five on this continent measures the next level in decades of his life," he said. "There are four of them in the hall down this hill who have not moved in forty years and who have made their peace with never moving. You went into a forest for nine days. So tell me what you intend to answer, because I will be asked first, and I would like to know what I am lying about."

"The codex," his son said. "I will answer it in the codex."

---

He wrote it that night, in the third volume, in the hand he used when he wanted the Elders to believe he had thought about it for a long time.

A short chapter. A ring is a beast's age, and the ladder is the ladder, and this holds for every ring a man can kill for. There is one other kind. It is not made of a beast and it does not take the beast's place. It lies over a ring a man already holds, and it is made of his blood, and it has no year, and it runs on blood rather than on soul power, and it deepens as the blood deepens, and it brings a skill of its own with it and leaves the ring's own skill where it was. It is gold.

He did not write that he had two of them, and he did not write how it was done, because it could not be done by any man whose blood was not already what his was, and a rule that no man alive can follow is not a rule but a boast. He wrote what the thing was, and he set the chapter beside the chapter on the ladder, and he left it there to be found.

That was the whole of the strategy, and it had taken him nine days of walking and bleeding to see it. A man who does not want to be asked a question should not hide from it. He should answer it in public, and early, and in a voice dry enough that nobody enjoys reading it. The Elders would copy the third volume within a week, and it would reach the two empires by spring, and any man who wanted to know what a gold ring was would find his answer in a book six weeks before he thought to ask. The only man alive who could connect that page to him was his father, and his father would not.

The nine small pills had gone west five weeks ago with a courier who did not know what he carried, and the instructions had reached a servant and not the woman herself, because the woman herself would have thrown them into the fire. The courier had come back. The box was empty. What she did with what was in it was hers.

He sat in the chamber under the Hall with two gold rings around him and eight walls of stone and a daughter due in five weeks, and he was not frightened, and he was not triumphant.

He was counting. Six weeks between one ring and the next, seven rings left, and a blood that had to fill in between them. He did the sum, and the sum came out in years, and he had been alive in this body for seven months.

He had never in two lives been patient about anything.

He put the lamp out and went to bed, because the road was long, and the medicine was still going in him, and the cold would break in five weeks.

# Chapter 08 — What the Sea Sold Cheap

The watch on her door came off on a morning one month after the child was born.

He did it himself, in the corridor, with the two Elder Hall men who had stood there since autumn. He told them the watch was ended. They looked at each other as men do when an order arrives that they will have to explain to someone above them, and then they bowed and went down the stairs. He took their chair and sat in it for a moment with his hands on his knees, because he wanted the fact in the world before he said anything else to anybody. The Saintess of Spirit City was no longer watched. The lock had always been the watch, and the watch had been his, and he had taken it off.

He knocked, and she said come in without waiting, and when he came in she was at the window in full white robes, dressed for the city and not for a room.

"You took the men off my door," she said.

"I did."

"Why."

"Because the reason for the watch died with the child's birth, and because I am done pretending that guarding you is the same thing as keeping you." He set the box he had carried on the table. "Come down to the cellar. There is a great deal of it, and it is easier to show you than to say."

The cellar under the west tower had been his since the third month. Fourteen crates, each the length of a man's arm, sealed with wax and the stamp of a different merchant house. He broke the nearest seal and lifted out a slab wrapped in oilcloth, grey and faintly yellow, heavy as wet stone, smelling of the deep sea and something under it.

"Whale gum," he said. "You know it?"

"Everyone knows it." There was a note in her voice close to amusement and not warm. "The coast sells it to old lords who want to believe things. A brothel drug. That is the only thing in this cellar the merchant houses know how to price."

"It comes out of the brain of a whale-class soul beast, and it is food for the body itself. Muscle, bone, meridian. It rebuilds a person until they can carry what would have torn them open the week before. There was a child in a book I read once who ate it from the time she could walk, and she took a thousand-year ring at level twenty, and everyone thought she was simply gifted." He rested his hand on the crate. "Four months of buying, nine houses, less than one season of the basilica's incense budget. When the coast learns what this is, one brick of the good grey will buy this tower. I learned first. That is the whole of my cleverness."

"And you are telling me this."

"I am giving it to you. All of it. And I am going to need you eating a spoonful of the refined working every night starting tonight, because the gum is not the gift." He said it flatly, without arrangement. "The gum is the armour you will need for the gift."

"For what gift."

"Two of them. One I have to go east for. Six days." He stood. "Do not eat anything from a stranger's hand while I am gone."

He left that night, and this time he did not walk.

He had walked east in the autumn, in a grey coat, a Pope pretending to be a clerk, because the basin was a secret and secrets are kept on foot. But the child was born now, and the woman in the tower was eating whale gum by the spoonful, and there was no more room in him for slow roads. He went up over the city after midnight with the six wings out and the cloud layer under him like a second sea. The crossing that had taken him a season of walking took him three nights of flying. On the fourth morning he stood again on the rim of the basin, sulphur in his teeth, the line of steam rising straight up between the two waters.

Six herbs stood where he had left six standing. He had written down where each of them grew, in a book he kept for a boy who was not born yet, and he stood a long while over the book before he opened his knife.

Two orchids and a lily. The eight-petalled orchid that lifts a soul master five whole levels when it goes into medicine. The tulip that lifts six. And the fragrant immortal that cures no poison but defeats all of them, which is what a body needs when it is standing next to a flood that size. He had left them for the boy. The boy would find three fewer fortunes growing here in twenty-three years, and would never know what had stood in their places.

He weighed a child he had never met against a woman he had wronged, and the scale did not move for long, and he did not pretend to himself that it was only medicine.

He cut the three at the base, carried them downwind in separate folds of cloth, drank one cup from each spring where he stood, and was in the air again before the steam had closed over the rim.

The making of the pill took nine days, and she was there for all nine.

That was not his arrangement and it was not hers. It simply happened, as a room fills when two people keep choosing to stay in it. The orchid had to be ground at dawn while the dew-weight was on it. The tulip went into warm oil and no hotter, and she learned to hold the pan at the height where her wrist said yes and the oil said no. The fragrant immortal went in last, one petal at a time, and three ladles of refined whale gum went under it, and wild honey to bind, and nothing else, because immortal things do not forgive company they were not invited with.

On the eighth night she asked the question she had been holding since the cellar.

"You are eighty-nine," he said, before she asked it, because she had earned the whole arithmetic and he was done keeping ledgers from her. "Eight rings, and two years at the wall of ninety, and the wall of ninety opens for one thing only, and that thing has teeth. That is why you are stuck. Not talent. Not effort. The ring is not there."

"Everyone at the wall is waiting for a ring," she said. "That is not news. That is the world."

"The news is the medicine." He watched the pan. "This pill will not break the wall. Nothing breaks that wall but the ring. What it will do is carry you to the very edge of ninety and then keep pouring. The orchid holds five levels in it. The tulip holds six. At your height the honest arithmetic says five or six between them, and the wall will take none of it, and here is the part nobody knows, because nobody has ever stood where you are about to stand with medicine like this behind them: the power does not vanish at the wall. It piles up behind it. Soul power at a bottleneck keeps growing and cannot be used and waits, and when the ring finally opens the door, everything that waited comes through with it."

She was very still for a moment.

"So the pill is not the rise," she said.

"The pill is the flood behind the dam." He set the stirring stick down. "The rise is the ring, and the ring is in the northern sea, and it is a hundred thousand years old, and it is going to be your ninth. It will burn five days. It will cost you a capillary and a channel and five days you will not get back. And it will buy no rank at all. You will still be eighty-nine when you stand up. You will simply be the most loaded eighty-nine that has ever stood on this continent."

The pill came off the fire on the ninth morning as one pill the colour of old ivory, with three faint gold veins in it where the gum had folded through, and she swallowed it at noon, in her own room, and asked him to stay.

That was the first thing. In seven months she had never asked him to stay anywhere. She said it flatly, looking past him at the wall. *Sit there. I do not want to be alone for this.* He sat, and within an hour he understood that she had not been managing her pride when she asked, she had been managing her fear, because the burning started like a furnace door opening inside her ribs and did not stop opening.

What failed first was the old country of her. Fourteen years of cultivation had packed her channels full to the seam of the wall, and the medicine came down on that packed country like a flood on a silted sluice, and the silt did not want to give. The pressure piled behind her breastbone until the veins stood along her throat. Then the silt let go all at once and the letting go tore. Blood came out of her nose. A capillary burst in her left eye and half her vision went red. Her back arched off the bed and a sound came out of her that he had heard once before, in the basin, out of his own mouth, and his hands were already glowing, already on her, the holy light going in along the tear and holding it while the flood went through.

The room shook. Not a figure: the lamp jumped, the pitcher walked off the table, and a pressure rolled off her in a ring that set dogs barking for six blocks. The eight rings came up through the floor of her soul one behind another, yellow, yellow, purple, purple, black, black, black, black, and they turned faster than they had ever turned, and behind her the Death Spider Emperor reared to its full height with its carapace steaming, and the room was too small for it and it did not care.

It ran five days. He did not leave the room in five days. She came out of the fever on the sixth morning with her hair stuck to her face and her eyes clear, and the first thing she did was count her own soul power with her eyes closed, and the second thing she did was go still in a way he had not seen on her before.

"It is all there," she said. "Every drop of it. Five levels, six, sitting behind the wall, and I cannot spend a copper of it. It is the strangest thing I have ever felt. I am full, and I am poor."

"That is the medicine keeping its word," he said. "The rank comes with the ring, always, and the pill bought no rank. What the pill bought is what comes through the door after the ring. And the ledger for the five days: a capillary gone in that eye, a channel torn and knitted crooked, five days of your life. I am not going to dress any of it up."

"You never do." She looked at him properly then, at the five days in his face, the beard, the hollow eyes, the chair he had not left. "You stayed."

"You asked me to."

"I asked you to sit." She held his look. "Nobody has ever stayed."

The core took nine more days, and it failed the first time exactly as he had told her it would.

He taught it in the cellar at night, off his own body and his own mistakes, because no one alive could teach it and he remembered every error he had made in the basin. The core is what happens when the liquid of a person's soul power is pressed until it refuses to be liquid and turns to a seed, and from that hour the power runs refined through the seed instead of splashing raw against the channels. But she was two souls in one body. When she pressed, the Death Spider Emperor pulled one way and the Soul Devouring Spider Emperor pulled the other, and the half-formed grain between her eyebrows was torn off the press by two hands reaching for it at once. It burst inside her head with a sound like a wet branch breaking. Blood, her nose and mouth together, the channel beside her spiritual sea letting go along a hand's length of itself, her arm out of the world, and him across the floor before she finished falling, holding what he could not mend.

The second press he built with her over nine nights, and the trick was not force. She stopped fighting the two pulls and let each soul pull its own stream, and where the streams met she gave them one narrow throat, and the friction of the two pulls did the pressing her hands never could.

When the grain set, the note of it was felt in the teeth. The wave of it shook dust off the cellar beams and set all fourteen crates humming against each other like struck bells. Over her right shoulder the Death Spider Emperor rose. Over her left the grey devouring one rose with it. The two of them stood in the world at the same hour for the first time in her life, eight rings circling the one and none the other, and she sat between them breathing hard with her hair coming loose.

"Both," she said, and her voice was not steady at all. "I stopped letting the second one out when I was twelve, because it frightened the other children, and now it is standing there, and it is not frightened of anything."

"One furnace," he said. "Both drinking. And when the ninth ring comes through the door, both of you will be strong enough to carry what comes with it, which is the whole reason I taught you this before the sea and not after."

She let the spiders down and the cellar was just a cellar. She sat in the low light with her hands still shaking, and he reached over and put his hand over hers to steady them, the first time in eight months he had touched her without light in his fingers. She turned her hand over under his and held on.

"Do not make this pretty," she said. "I have not forgotten what you did. I want you to know that I have not. And I want your hand there, and I have wanted it there for weeks, and apparently I am a person who can hold both of those at once."

"Apparently you are."

"Then do not sit there like a man at a funeral." She pulled him down by the hand, and she kissed him first, hard, like everything else she did, and the lamp went low over the two of them and the fourteen crates of whale gum stood around them like an audience that had known before they did.

They came up out of the cellar near dawn, and the plan was made over cold bread, because neither of them was good at mornings and both were good at work.

"North," he said. "To the cold sea. There is a whale-class leviathan out past where the ice ports stop, just past one hundred thousand years. It took its first heavenly tribulation in the spring and it is still paying for it, with a burn along its flank the width of a road, and that burn is the only reason two people can stand in front of it at all."

"A hundred thousand years." She said it slowly, tasting it. "For my ninth."

"For your ninth. It is red, it carries two skills, and it goes to whoever's working is in it when the heart stops, so the last blow is yours and the plan is built around that. And you will not stop at ninety." He watched her do the arithmetic he had given her eight nights ago. "A ring that old lifts three to six on its own, and behind your wall five or six are already waiting with their boots on. When the door opens you will go through ninety like a river through a broken dam, and where you land, we will count together, on the water, out loud."

"Ninety-five," she said. "Maybe ninety-six."

"Maybe," he said. "And the bone comes too. A hundred thousand always leaves one, for whatever part of you is still empty, and a bone that old carries two skills and rebuilds the body around them. Nine days, worse than the pill, and I will be holding you through it as I held you through the pill."

"Stay," she said, "will be the word, not sit."

"Stay," he said. "That was always the word. I was just slow."

# Chapter 09 — The Cold North Sea

They sailed north out of the ice ports on a trader that took their gold and asked nothing. On the ninth day the water went from green to the black grey of slate, and the captain said his ship went no further, and his grandfather's had not either.

"We are getting off here," he said, and paid the second half, and the trader ran south under every sail. Two people stood on the cold water with nothing under them, one on a floor of golden light and one on eight rings of black silk laid over the swell, and the wind came down out of the north and found them both.

She was not the woman who had left Spirit City. Eighteen days of whale gum had finished its slow work through her, and eighty-nine levels sat in her packed to the seam, and five levels' worth of medicine waited behind her wall with their boots on, and the grey devouring spider rode her left shoulder in the daylight now, like it had earned the place. When she looked at him across the water there was nothing guarded left in the looking.

"Three days," he said. "It circles slow. Hurt things always do."

They found it on the third morning, and the sea opened under them without hurry.

A back came up first, and the back kept going, a cathedral ridge of blue-black hide that shed the water in sheets. Then the head, blunt and barnacled and wider than the trader's beam. Then the eye, the size of a carriage wheel and the colour of winter milk, and it looked at them with the flat patience of something alive since before the current temple had a roof. And down the whole starboard flank of it ran the burn: a branching white scar the width of a road where the hide had been cooked and sloughed and was growing back wrong, slow and pale.

"The spring storms," he said. "Its first heavenly tribulation. Most of them die in it, and the ones that live carry the receipt for a decade. That scar is the door. I will hold its attention and I will hold the sea flat, because a whale-class leviathan fights with the ocean itself and I am going to take the ocean away from it. You will go in through the burn and put the death into the place where it is already dying. When the heart stops, the ring is yours, and it will be your ninth, and the wall will not last a breath after it."

"And while the wall is falling," she said, "I am going to be sitting in the water taking a hundred thousand years and five levels of waiting medicine through one body, and you are going to be holding me while I scream, and I want it said now that I asked for all of it."

"Said now," he agreed. "You asked for all of it."

He went in front, and the gold came up around him the instant the leviathan decided they were real.

Eleven rings, no year on any of them. The domain went out from him in a circle half a mile wide and lay down on the sea like a lid on a pot, and the water inside it went still and stayed still. The leviathan felt the theft at once, and the flat patience went out of the winter-milk eye.

The tail came over the water like a city wall falling sideways. He did not dodge. He took it on the domain with both hands up, and the domain rang like a struck bell and bucked and held. The shock went down through the light into the sea and came back up through his boots and sat in his teeth. The second blow took him under. Under the grey water in the cold dark the great body turned with a sound like grinding and the mouth came open, and he burned a road of holy light along the roof of that mouth and went up through the blowhole spray into the air again. The domain was still flat, and it was costing him, and he could feel the cost.

Out on the flank, she was already inside.

The devouring spider went first, grey and flat and hungry, laying itself against the pale wrong-growing flesh of the scar and drinking. A hundred thousand years of leviathan shuddered at the bite, and the shudder stood up out of the still water as a wave and broke over the domain and over her. Under the cover of it the death spider put eight legs into the wound and lowered her in, and she climbed inside the receipt the sky had written, into heat and stink and the deep red dark of a body that size. This was the thing the core had bought: two souls working one purpose. The devouring sister opened and drank, the death sister poured behind it, a venom that does not kill fast but convinces flesh to stop repairing itself, poured into the one place where all that century of strength was being spent on repair.

The leviathan screamed with a sound the weather makes and not a beast, and the great head came around to its own flank, and he would not let it arrive. He went down the long curve of the turn like a stone off a sling, the holy light sword in both hands, and he put it into the winter-milk eye and turned it. The scream changed register. The third blow of the tail caught him across the chest and put him through the air into the water a quarter mile off. The domain flickered. He came up under it spitting blood and laughing, because the sea was still flat, and her soul was still burning steady out on the flank, and the leviathan was turning slower every turn.

It took another hour. He kept the eye side, she kept the wound, and the devouring spider drank and the death spider poisoned. At the end the great body simply stopped mid-turn, and the flat patience came back into the good eye for a moment, almost like understanding, and then it went down out of the world without a wave.

Neither of them said anything for a while. There is no ceremony that fits a hundred thousand years going out of the world. The sea, held flat for an hour, began to move again in long grey swells, and the domain came down out of the sky in pieces, a held breath finally let go.

The ring rose out of the deep a minute later.

A hundred thousand years of it, red as a coal held in the dark, turning once over the place where the body had been, looking for the one whose venom was in the wound when the heart stopped. It found her. It came to her over the still water and stood waiting above her head like a question you answer with your whole life. Behind it a second thing rose, pale and heavy as a fallen column. A torso bone, longer than she was tall, the colour of old ivory, and the law of a hundred thousand years is that the body leaves its best piece with the killer for the part of them still empty, and there was nothing on that water to argue with the law.

"Say it again," she said, looking up at the red. "The arithmetic."

"Three to six from the ring. Five or six waiting behind your wall. You are the most loaded eighty-nine the continent has ever made, and in about a minute the door opens."

"Then hold me," said Bibi Dong, and sat down on the silk over the cold sea, and the ninth ring came down onto her.

The wall of ninety did not last a breath.

What failed first was her body, and it failed even through the whale gum. The door opened and the waiting medicine came through like water through a burst sluice, five levels of it at once, and a hundred thousand years of red tide came in behind it, and her channels, rebuilt by the gum and still only channels, were asked to be a riverbed for an ocean. They began to let go along their seams. Her blood found the seams. It came out of her nose and both her ears. Her back arched. The scar beside her spiritual sea, nine days old, opened again, and the newborn core spun up too fast for itself and began to heat like a bearing with no oil, and the second failure followed the first into the light.

He was inside the gold before she fell. What he could not repair he held, both hands in the burning map of her, holding the seams of her channels shut from the inside while the red river went through, holding the spinning grain still with his own two cores set against it like hands on a wheel. Somewhere in the second hour she screamed. Somewhere in the fourth she stopped. Somewhere in the grey of the third morning the light over the water went from red to gold to nothing, and it was done.

The phenomena had not been quiet. For three days a pillar of red light stood on the northern sea where no light had ever stood. The water within a mile of it steamed, and the sky kept a bruise in it long after the pillar went down. Sailors in the ice ports would tell their grandchildren about the summer the north caught fire. Under the pillar, for all three days, two spiders stood over the burning woman without moving. When she came up out of it at last, a ninth ring had joined her company: the eight she had hunted for, circling the death sister, and the red one among them now, enormous among them now, like a coal banked in a row of lamps.

"Count with me," he said.

"Ninety," she said. Her voice shook. "The wall is gone. Ninety-one. Ninety-two." She was counting her own soul power as a drowning woman counts steps to the shore. "The medicine is spending. It is all spending. Ninety-three. Ninety-four." A long breath. "Ninety-five. It stopped at ninety-five. I am ninety-five."

"Six levels," he said. "One door, two floods. You are a Titled Douluo, Bibi Dong. You are twenty years old, and there is no one in any record of this continent who was younger, and the ring gave you its skills while you were screaming, as its kind always does, and you have not even looked at them yet."

She closed her eyes and went looking, and her breath changed.

"Two," she said. "A hundred thousand always gives two. The first is a web, but not silk. Pressure. I lay it on the water or the air and everything inside it is at the bottom of the sea, slower, heavier, and I feel every thread with my own hands. The second is hunger. I put it in a thing through the web and I take its power out, a little at a time, and it does not stop until I stop." She opened her eyes. "There is no name in any book for those two."

"No," he said. "There is not."

"Web of the Abyss," she said. "And Deep Hunger." She tried the second name once more, heard it, and kept it. "They are mine, and they are ugly, and they fit."

The bone took nine days, in a rented room above a whaling inn in the ice port, and it was worse than the pill, and he was there for all nine, and *stay* was the word the whole way through.

It went into her back with a cold that made the red ring feel warm, and it did not seal itself away as old bones do. A hundred-thousand-year bone is a hundred thousand years of a leviathan's body remembering itself, and it rebuilt her around it, slow and total. Her breath became the breath of the deep, so that water and smoke and the thin air over high mountains became the same nothing to her lungs. Her flesh took on the grain of the abyss, and blows sank into her and died there as stones die in deep water. Two skills, as every bone that old carries two. The first she named Deep Water, because she was in a bad mood the day she named it. The second she named The Long Breath. She used it once in the courtyard, held her breath for the length of an afternoon while the innkeeper's children counted, and laughed herself sick at the top of her own lungs.

She tested the Web of the Abyss on the harbour the next day, laid it across the water from the end of the pier until the gulls flew into it and slid off like flies into a window. By evening the ice port had decided the southern woman was a sea goddess and left fish at her door. She found it hilarious. He found it instructive, because a goddess is a rumour, and a rumour is a wall.

"They eat soul power like a furnace," he told her, watching the children run. "The bone's two, the ring's two. In a long fight you will spend more holding the deep in your flesh than you spend hitting. There will come a day when you have to choose which is worth more. That is the cost, and it is the whole cost."

"And the scars."

"Yours for life. They ache in the cold, which is a joke the north will keep making at your expense." He shrugged. "Your core needs years. The red ring needs years. You are ninety-five at twenty with nine rings and four skills no book holds, and when we come home you will decide, every day, what a person does with all of that, because I will not decide it for you and nobody else alive can."

"How many are there," she said, "above ninety-four. On the whole continent. Tonight."

He thought about it honestly, because she had earned honest arithmetic and he had stopped keeping ledgers from her.

"Three," he said. "My father at ninety-nine, who has not left his hill in sixty years. Me at ninety-seven. And you, at twenty, with a red ring that is three months older than your daughter."

She came to his room that night without knocking, which was like everything else she did, and the lamp burned low for a long time over the two of them, and the cold sea went on being cold outside without anyone's help.

In the morning she lay against his shoulder and counted rings on her own fingers like a child counting money. Then she said the thing she had been carrying since the ice port.

"Our daughter."

He went very still.

"I said it," she said. "I have said it now. I am not taking it back and I am not saying it twice, so you had better not make a face."

"I am not making a face."

"You are making a face." She sat up and looked down at him, and her eyes were dry and bright and absolutely steady. "When she is old enough to be told, I will be the one who tells her. All of it. What you did and what you are and what she is. I will not make it pretty, because I am her mother, and I am apparently a person who can hold both things at once. And you will be there when I tell her."

"I will be there," he said.

"Good," said Bibi Dong, and went down to breakfast at ninety-five levels of soul power and twenty years of age, the youngest Titled Douluo the continent had ever made, and the north wind came down off the ice and could not find either of them.

# Chapter 05 — The Two Waters

The child was due when the cold broke, and the cold had six weeks left in it.

He laid the month out in his head. The guild's second branch could wait. The third volume of the codex could wait, and the Elders would argue over it whether he sat in the room or not.

One thing could not wait, because it was not his to schedule.

There was a place east of the city. A basin in a forest, with two waters in it, and herbs beside the waters that grew nowhere else on the continent. He had read about them in a book that had not been written yet.

There was medicine in that basin. Not the kind the Hall kept in its cabinets, ground and weighed and folded into paper. He knew what it did for a woman carrying a child. He knew what it did for a man who meant to take himself apart.

Six weeks was enough, if he wasted none of it.

---

He told one person.

Qian Daoliu stood in the hall of statues, where he always stood in the last hour before dawn. Ninety-nine years old and ninety-nine levels deep, with the stillness of a man who had stopped needing to move.

"I am going east."

His father did not turn around. "For how long."

"A month, less if the roads hold."

"And who goes with you."

"No one."

The old man turned then, and looked at his son as he looked at a wall he suspected of settling — not with alarm, but to find the crack.

"You are the Pope."

"I am a Titled Douluo, and there are three of us on this continent, and I am the youngest of the three by forty years. Nothing east of this city can hurt me."

"Something east of this city could take a month out of you."

"Yes."

The old man let that stand, because it was true. "What are you going to fetch."

"Medicine for the woman carrying my child."

The hall went quiet.

Qian Daoliu counted nine breaths before he answered.

"The blood is what matters," he said. "Whatever she is, the child is ours. Bring the medicine, and do not die in a forest, because I have spent fifty years on you and I am not beginning again."

He bowed, because the body remembered to, and went out into the yard.

---

He left through the east gate in the dark. A plain sword at his hip. A bag with gold in it, a jade cup, a knife, and clothes with no gold thread in them.

Nobody stopped him, because a tall man in a grey coat walking out at an hour when only farmers walk out is not a thing the eye holds onto.

The country east was flat and frozen, and he crossed it at a pace that would have killed a horse. Twice he passed soul masters on the road, and neither of them looked at him, because dust on a man's boots makes him nobody.

The forest began on the third day, and none of the beasts in it came near him.

On the fourth day the snow stopped.

Not thinned. Stopped, in a line, as if somebody had drawn it with a rule.

Then the steam, and then the smell: sulphur, and under it something green and heavy that he had no name for.

He stood at the top of the rise and looked down.

The basin was an inverted cone, old fire black on one side and old ice white on the other, and at the bottom of it lay a pool no larger than a courtyard.

Two waters in it. One the colour of milk, and one the colour of a wound, and the line between them did not move.

Ten thousand zhang below, two dragons lay buried in the same hole, and the world had never closed over them.

The rim was ringed with flowers, jade-scaled, seven to a stalk and low to the ground, and they were beautiful as a drawn blade is beautiful.

The wind came off them toward him. He tasted a faint sweetness at the back of his throat, and under the sweetness, patient as a debt collector, the thing that would stop a man's blood where it stood.

He let the domain out.

Golden radiance came off him and lay across the stone. The sweetness went out of the air. The blood comprehends the domain, and nothing else in the world can.

He walked down through the flowers. It cost him a steady draw the whole way, like holding a heavy door open with one hand while he worked with the other.

---

Twelve things of the highest grade grew in that hole.

He found the pale pink flower that cures no poison and defeats every one of them. He found a red lotus that pulled fire-poison out of a body. He found the snow silkworm, and a fungus with nine grades in its cap, and an orchid of eight petals that would not live a year outside jade.

The white herb stood at the centre of the cold spring, eight-cornered, its heart a cluster of points that caught the light as ice catches it, and ten metres from it the air bit.

The red one looked like a cabbage left in a forge, and the air above it bent.

The book said that neither could be taken alone, and that the two taken together in the same hour fight each other instead of you.

He tied the jade cup to a cord, and he went in fast and cut at the base and was out again before the cold had finished deciding what to do about it. He did not touch either of them with his skin, and he carried them downwind in separate folds of cloth.

He took six of the twelve and left six standing, and he wrote down where each of them grew. In twenty-three years a boy would come into this basin looking for exactly those. It seemed right to leave him something to find.

---

He made the pills on a flat stone, with an iron pot and a fire lit out of the wind.

The first kind was for a woman carrying a child. Red lotus, snow silkworm, and a ninth of the fungus, ground fine and bound with honey. Nine pills, each the size of a thumb joint. They would not make the birth safe, because nothing does, but they would put her body where it could do its own work.

He sent them west that evening with a courier who did not know what he carried, and with instructions that reached a servant and not the woman herself, because the woman herself would have thrown them into the fire.

---

Then he swallowed the two poisons and went into the cold water.

He had known it would be bad. He had not known it would be this.

They fought in his blood. The first thing that gave was a meridian along his left side. It opened as a dry channel opens, and the cold got in and stayed.

He bit down on his own tongue, and the domain went out.

Not because he let it. His body took the power back to hold itself together, as a house takes the beam out of the roof when the floor is going. The radiance thinned and died, the sweetness came back into the air, and the flowers at the rim had him.

The water saved him, because the cold of that spring was worse than the poison and the two went at each other through him instead of the poison finishing him on the stone.

He lay in the milk-white water with frost coming out along his arms. One meridian in his side ran hot and wrong. He understood that he had misjudged it. Not by much. Enough.

His heart slowed past the place where a heart should be. The warm core went on being warm, as a lamp does. It was not enough. He had never in his life needed it to be enough.

He dragged himself across the line that did not move and into the red water.

That one finished the argument. Heat went into cold and cold into heat. What came out was a body that neither of them could get into again. His own. Stitched. Running hot along one side.

He lay on the stone until he could stand. Then he stood, and steamed, and did the arithmetic.

A meridian. Not a vital one, and healed, and crooked for good.

That was the price of a body that could hold what came next, and he had paid it without meaning to, in a basin where a man who misjudged by a little would have been a corpse by morning with six wings and no help within a hundred li.

He wrote the misjudging down, and he did not excuse it.

---

He did the first ring that night.

He sat at the edge of the two waters with his back against the stone, so that if he went over he would go into the cold rather than onto the rock.

He had thought a long time about which one to begin with, and the thinking had not taken long. The first, and the yellow one. Four hundred and twenty years, out of a small bird with light for feathers that he had killed when he was a boy, and the smallest thing he owned.

He knew what the hammer does. There is a family on this continent that keeps one, and its deepest secret is that a man may break his own rings and pour the years out for one blow far above his weight. The rings come back over three days. Do it twice inside thirty-six and one of them may not come back at all.

He was not going to break anything. He was going to take a knot apart.

The ring was part of his body now, and had been for seven months, and it hung around him only because that was where a ring is supposed to hang. Under the light of it there was a fusion, and the fusion was a seam, and he had spent fifty years not looking at it and seven months learning that it was there.

He looked at it, and then he let go of it.

It did not open.

He had thought of a seam as a line, and a line comes apart when you stop holding it. This was not a line. It was fifty years of a body grown around a thing, and the body did not want the thing out, and when he pulled the seam tore, and the tear went up along his spine and took the breath with it.

Blood came out of his nose and his ears. Not much, and it did not need to be much.

The years came down into him anyway, raw years with no shape at all, and the core took them as a heart takes blood. Then the blood took them apart, and kept what it could hold, and let the rest go.

Something left him, and that part hurt. It was a hand going into him and taking a thing he did not have a second of, and what was leaving was not power but the substance that had been able to give power, and it was not coming back.

The core opened and drank. He felt it drink: the basin, the springs, the steam, the ground, the cold air over the flowers, all of it pulled sideways toward a man sitting against a stone. The pool dipped a finger's width and came back.

Inside the ring, small and furious and very old for what it was, the bird went through it.

Four hundred and twenty years of a small light bird, and a hundred thousand years poured through it. It could not hold a fraction of that and it did not try; it was carried as a feather is carried by a river it has no business being in. What came out the other side was still a sparrow's blessing, and it was not small any more.

The seam re-knit. It took most of the night and it did not close flush, and he would carry a ridge there under the first ring for the rest of his life, and know it in the dark with his fingers.

Then the ring came back.

It was not yellow. It was gold.

---

He had expected a change. He had not expected the ground to shake.

The light went up out of him in a pillar, and not out of one ring but out of all nine and the two centres and the blood under them. A column of gold stood over the basin and went up through the steam and lit the underside of the cloud, and the hollow turned day at midnight.

His wings came out, six of them, all the way, without his asking, and the feathers were white-gold to the tips and the light ran along them like water.

Every ring he carried shone at once, and the first of them, the small one, the sparrow, burned a pure gold that he had never seen on a living man. He had seen it once on a page, in a description of what a god wears.

The domain tore open on its own and rolled out over the forest, and the flowers at the rim went flat, and somewhere past the trees a beast that had been asleep stood up.

And the gold came out through him.

It came up along his arms and his neck as a rash comes up, only it was light. Where it settled, his skin went white-gold and lay over him in overlapping rows small as a sparrow's feathers, and every one of them gave off light. A halo stood out from the gold ring at his waist, wide as his arms, and turned slowly.

He looked at his own hands, and they belonged to a stranger.

He sat in the middle of it with blood drying on his face and one hand on the ridge under his first ring. He had exactly one thought, and it was not about the light.

The pillar could be seen for a hundred li in every direction.

Then the surge went into the wall.

It went up as water goes up a stair, ninety-six and then against the next step, and he felt the step as a man feels a door he has pushed against before. It did not move. The power piled up behind it.

He had written that rule into his own codex, in the second volume: power keeps accumulating behind a wall, and shows when the ring arrives. It was accumulating. He was not at ninety-seven.

He stayed there until the light went down, and the wings folded, and the golden layer sank back under his skin. Then he sat in the dark in a basin that smelled of ozone and sulphur and his own blood, and took stock.

---

The first thing he did was look for the year.

He had written the ladder himself, in the second volume: white at ten, yellow at a hundred, purple at a thousand, black at ten thousand, red at a hundred thousand, and above that one gold streak for every hundred thousand more, up to nine.

Every ring on the continent was a number, and the number was how long the animal had been alive.

The gold ring at his waist had no streaks, and it had no number at all.

He looked at it for a long time. He turned it over in his mind as a man turns over a coin he suspects is not money.

The years had not gone into the ring. They had gone into the blood, and the blood had kept what it could hold, and the ring was no longer a record of a dead bird. It was a record of him.

It carried no year because it was not made of years. It was made of the concentration of the Angel God's blood in a man's body, and it would be exactly as deep as that blood got, and it would go on deepening for as long as he did.

Two beings in everything he had ever read had carried a ring like that, and both of them were dragons, with blood old enough that the world had rules about it.

A sparrow's blessing out of a four-hundred-and-twenty-year bird had no business being gold. The only reason it was gold was that it had stopped being the bird's ring a long time ago, in the middle of the seclusion month of black sweat in a chamber under the Hall.

He was the third, and he was the first who was not a dragon.

He did not feel triumphant. He felt as a man feels who has been handed something expensive in a dark street, and has not yet been told what it costs.

---

The blessing was still the bird's. He let it out and looked at it.

Warm golden light came off his hands. The same gentle light he had put into a locked room in Spirit City six months ago, the light that had closed a woman's bruises and left her hatred entirely alone. Fifteen small particles, going slowly around him, exactly as they had always done.

It healed the torn meridian in his side in the time it took him to notice.

He sat with that for a while.

Then he checked the other thing, and checked it twice, because it was the kind of thing a man gets wrong when he is tired and bleeding.

The light was not running off his soul power.

His soul power was low and still climbing back from the night's work, and the light did not care. It ran off his blood, as a lamp runs off oil and not off the hand that holds it.

A man could strip his soul power out of him — seal it, drain it, break it — and the light would go on coming.

The same skill, and the same fifteen lights. The old thing with the handbrake off, running on a fuel that nobody on this continent knew was fuel. He understood, with a coldness that had nothing to do with the spring, that this light held to a man long enough would do things he had no name for.

He put it away, and then he counted what it had cost, because a man who does not count dies in the second round.

The blood he had given away was gone for good, and the blood was poorer by exactly that much, and it would not make it back. The seam under the first ring would never close flush. The meridian in his side was crooked, and his hands were not entirely steady.

And eight rings were left.

Each of them would turn gold in its turn, because none of them was a beast's ring any more, and each of them would want more blood than the last, because the blood ran deeper each time and deeper costs more. That was the whole reason to do any of it, and the whole reason to be careful.

He looked at the two waters, which had not moved in ten thousand years and did not move now, and the pool sat a finger's width higher than it had.

He put his coat on over a body that no longer noticed the cold.

Then he started walking west, into a continent that had watched a golden pillar stand up over the eastern forest at midnight, and was already asking the only question that mattered.

He had known it would be seen. That was why he had done it at night, in the one place on the continent where anyone sent to look would find a hole in the ground and nothing in it.

He had not counted on how long the light would stand.

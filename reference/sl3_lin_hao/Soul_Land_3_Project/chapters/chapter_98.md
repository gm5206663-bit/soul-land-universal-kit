## Part 1: The Tempest

Whatever else he was tonight, he was not a soul master. The boy at the first table had put the captain away somewhere with the trial armor and the seventy points, and what stood there in his place was a blacksmith of the fourth rank, three years consolidated, opening his work the way the profession had opened its works since before the Federation had a name: three light taps, crisp as a clock starting.

Then the storm.

Power gathered at his feet and climbed his spine like something hoisted; his whole body twisted into the swing, and both hammers came down together with a boom that rattled the room's glass and everyone's teeth in it. Zhuo Shi's eyes flew wide — his body had *resonated* with the strike, veteran of a hundred wars resonating with a thirteen-year-old's hammer, and he'd had to shed the feeling on purpose. The rebound came up through Wulin's arms and he rode it into the next strike, harder, and the next, and the booms and the smaller fragmented rumbles underneath them began to fuse into one fantastical rhythm — wild, deafening, and delicate as a waltz if you had the ears to hear the floor of it.

Through all of it, at the second station, Lin Hao worked — no booms, no rattled glass; the Yaluo bar ticking under taps placed like a man writing in a ledger while a thunderstorm read over his shoulder. The room's eyes went where the noise was. The room's best eyes did not.

"Stacked hammers," Elder Cai said quietly. "The rare effect. And he can *fully control* it. That's third rank at minimum — Zhou, the Blacksmith Association has been hiding a thirteen-year-old like this."

"Judging by how he carries himself, he had a good teacher." Zhuo Shi was leaning forward now, grey hair catching the furnace light. "I heard the Association's greatest genius is a girl named Mu Xi — fourth rank before twenty. I should pay the Association a visit. Find this kid a master of his own. Maybe he can reach even her level."

"At his table," Elder Cai said, without looking away from the second station, "sits the reason the Association's three pages keep asking questions, Zhou. The master you want to visit is already in the room's address book. The letter on my desk says the boy in Eastsea found two."

"Two," Zhuo Shi repeated. "The Association's greatest genius — fourth rank before twenty. And you're telling me—"

"I'm telling you her record is already dead, Zhou. It died in an auction yard in Eastsea, and it dies again at that table tonight — a full rank beyond hers, seven years younger. Watch both tables. However this night ends, some of our records end with it."

Hundred-refined in ten strikes. The metal came down under the tempest, shrinking, its vein lines standing out like weather. The booms climbed to rings, the rings to one long note — and on the forty-eighth strike the room went white with a pillar of silver light, and everyone in it heard, unmistakably, the heavy silver *cheer* — light flying up like a small roaring dragon.

"He thousand refined it with *spirit*," Elder Li said, breaking his long silence, shocked out of the elder's gravity. "First-grade. A fourth-rank need only second-grade — this is the *peak* of fourth." He shook his head slowly, solemnly. "I say this as a man who has watched the profession for sixty years: this child is the greatest blacksmithing genius in the history of the world. Thirteen. It is unheard of. He is a monster that surpasses monsters."

Behind the painted line, Xie Xie whispered to Xiaoyan: "We *know* him. How do we know him?"

"And the other one?" Zhuo Shi asked the room — dazed, because the verdict was still ringing and the room held one table too many.

"Ask the page," Elder Cai said, and did not turn it. Her eyes had stopped pretending to watch the first table at all.

## Part 2: The Bridge

Purple light stood in Wulin's eyes, and the forging changed species.

He stepped to the metal's side and began, of all things, to *circle* it — tapping it lightly as he went, each knock carrying a spark of white, soul power riding the hammers down into the bar like a slow transfusion. And then the room saw what the fourth rank could not do, and did:

The Bluesilver Grass came out of him and wrapped the scorching metal, strand over strand, and began, visibly, to die. The grass shriveled. The grass burned. He never once looked away, and his face never once wavered, and he kept circling, tapping, patient as a man walking a child into cold water.

"You two have made a mess," Elder Li said — and there was no longer any admiration in his voice. There was fear. "Spirit gets you first-grade thousand refined. That is not spirit refinement. Spirit refinement is *life*, and life goes in through only one door — the martial soul, as a bridge, carrying the smith's own life force into the metal. Soul power, life, metal, harmonized. The rule is four rings before you even *attempt* it, and grown masters fail it and bleed for the failing. He has three. If he fails now, the backlash may be a thing he never recovers from — and then, Zhou, then you and she will learn what FENG WUYU'S wrath looks like, and I will hold both your coats."

Zhuo Shi had gone grey around the mouth. "Can we stop him?"

"He connected his life force to the metal the moment the grass touched it." Elder Li's voice was final as a closing door. "Interrupt him now and the shock could take him — could *kill* him. There is nothing left that anyone in this room can do. Pray."

"This is your fault," Zhuo Shi hissed.

"Why didn't *you* stop me?" But for once Elder Cai's retort came out at half volume, because she was watching a boy burn his own soul's grass against a bar of silver, and even her page had stopped turning.

At the door, the man the color of winter did not move at all.

## Part 3: The Testimony

And in the roar of all of it, almost nobody had marked that the second hammer had never stopped — that through the whole roar, Lin Hao had kept working.

Lin Hao worked the way deep water moves. No booms; the Yaluo bar glowed under taps that did not ring so much as *arrive* — each one placed the way his strokes had been placed on a certain cave wall, the intent arriving before the hammer did. Sword consciousness rode his hands tonight, not as a weapon but as a grammar: *the intent that knows the cut before the cut* translated whole into the one art that had owned him since a back room in Glorybound — he was not striking the metal, he was *reading it aloud*, and the metal, hearing itself said correctly for the first time, began to listen.

The elders' eyes came to him one by one, the way heads turn at a second conversation in a quiet room. Where the captain bridged life in with blood and fire and dying grass, the fifth was doing something the profession had no rank-shape for: he fanned the spark the thousand refinements had left — fanned it with *order*. Lightning first, a whisper of it, keying the grain. Wind, finding the seams. Water, laying the temper true. Ice, last, closing what the others opened. The four, in the iron that was their house, one after another, each element a sentence in a language the bar had been born knowing and never once heard spoken.

Elder Li crossed the floor mid-prayer and stood at the second table, and after a long time the old man said, softly, to no one:

"He isn't *giving* it life. He's teaching it that it was alive all along."

The grass at the first table burned down to ash-wrapped silver, and Wulin, swaying, exhaustion in every line of him, kept tapping, kept circling, his heart the hammer, his soul the guide, his mind going down into the metal one ten-thousandth at a time — *their minds becoming one in ten thousand refinements of life.* That was Spirit Refinement. The boy was paying its whole price in a single night, on three rings, for one point.

The bar at the second table cooled from white to gold to a grey that held the light like a held breath. It did not cheer. It did something quieter and, in Elder Li's sixty years, rarer: when Lin Hao laid two fingers on it at the last, the metal turned toward the touch — slightly, certainly — as a sleeping thing turns toward a familiar hand.

The old man looked at the bar. Then at Lin Hao. Then at the bar again, as if one of them owed him an explanation.

"Fifth," he said.

One word. The same word, the room's older hands would later swear, that the forge-yard itself had somehow pronounced.

Elder Cai let out a breath she had been holding since the grass caught fire, and reached over and closed her page — not finished; *banked* — and looked at Zhuo Shi with an expression that said, clear as daylight: *the letter said find. The letter was right.*

For a moment nobody in authority spoke, and the room's arithmetic said it for them — the stack the academy had been assembling all exam without once pronouncing aloud.

Rank forty-five at thirteen: the youngest the academy's ten-thousand-year records held, and not by a little. Spiritual power that registered — *registered*, the way a river registers a gauge — at Soul Emperor tier: a height masters spend lifetimes failing to reach, and on him a baseline, not a summit. Elder Li, who had lived long enough to have watched the age of legends, said the name quietly, once, the way a man handles an old blade: "Not since Huo Yuhao has spiritual power like that worn a body this young." And even that reading was only where the instruments ended — not where the boy ended. Three years, nine trials, a legend's charge in a cave: nothing had ever forced him past what he was willing to show. He had never used full power. He had never needed to. The ceiling was not a number. It was a question nobody had earned the answer to.

A fourth soul ring the color of deep night — ten-thousand-year stock, an age top powerhouses count themselves lucky to wear once — set on a boy with three rings around it. A sword style with strokes in it no teacher ever wrote: self-made, at thirteen, the intent that knows the cut before the cut. A fifth-rank blacksmith, proven to the profession's own elder in one word. And the whole absurd stack carried on ONE martial soul — one — a variant hawk the Awakening halls had once filed under ordinary and would be filing corrections about for a generation.

"We spent this exam grading our scales," Zhuo Shi said at last, quietly. "And one of our examinees was never on any of them."

At the door, the man the color of winter watched the world finish catching up to his student, and his face did not move at all — which, from Wu Zhangkong, was a standing ovation.

Wulin's forty-ninth tap fell.

The whole room — elders, classmates, the winter man at the door, the fifth with his cooled and listening bar — leaned toward the silver, waiting for the metal to speak.

## Part 1: The Invitation

Gu Yue was not there, and Lin Hao found afterwards that he had checked, and that checking was its own small fact.

Wu Zhangkong asked in the hallway, which was not where he usually asked things.

"What plans do you have for the vacation?"

Wulin, who had a bag half-packed for a month at the workshop, said the truth. "Forging. And cultivating."

"Since you don't have any plans," Wu Zhangkong said, "come with me. I'm visiting another city on business. You'll learn more about the world outside of school, and you'll relax at the same time."

He said *relax* in exactly the same voice he said *run*, and Wulin noticed, and did not comment on it.

"Do you want time to think it over?"

"No," Wulin said. "I'll go."

He said it in the same half-second he had said *I want to join*, and Lin Hao — who was four metres away with his own bag and his own month, and who had stopped pretending he did not listen — understood that this was becoming a pattern, and that the pattern was that Wulin said yes to Wu Zhangkong before he had finished asking.

Lin Hao put his hand up.

Wu Zhangkong looked at him.

"Am I invited."

"You're standing there."

"That's not a yes."

"It's the only yes I have," Wu Zhangkong said, and walked off, and Lin Hao took it, because it was accurate.

---

## Part 2: The Train

Wu Zhangkong travelled in **white**, and he carried **nothing.**

That was the part that Xie Xie could not get past. He spent the first eleven minutes of the journey looking at Wu Zhangkong's empty hands from three different angles, the way you look at a trick you have decided to work out.

"You have a storage device," he said at last.

Wu Zhangkong did not look up. "Yes."

"Can I see it."

"No."

"Is it a ring."

"You'll find out when you have one."

The train went out of Eastsea City in the late afternoon, and the port went past on the left, and then the city went past, and then there was not a city any more.

Lin Hao sat by the window.

He had been on a train before — once, from Glorybound, with a free pamphlet and a rucksack and no idea that there were eighteen first-class cities in five regions. He had sat by that window and watched a town become a city and thought that was the whole of the world getting bigger.

This was different. This was a city becoming **not a city**, and it went on for hours.

---

## Part 3: What The Hawk Did

The forest started at about the third hour and did not stop.

Lin Hao had read about the **Great Star Dou Forest** in the same library book with the cracked spine, on a different page, and he had read the sentence about it *teeming with soul beasts* and understood it the way you understand a number in a book — as a fact that belonged to the page.

It did not belong to the page.

It went to the horizon in every direction the window allowed, and it was not one colour, it was a great many, and it moved — not the way a field moves in wind, but the way a *body* moves, slowly, in its sleep. And there were things in it that were large enough to see from a train at speed, and they were not doing anything in particular, and that was the worst part.

The hawk went still.

Lin Hao had learned what stillness meant. Stillness meant *deciding.* He had felt it twice — once at the thirteen-year-old with the purple ring, and once at the bear.

This was not that.

This was the stillness of a thing that had found something it recognised, and was not going to say what.

It stayed that way for **four hours.**

Lin Hao sat with his forehead against the glass and did not move, because moving seemed like the wrong thing to do, and when the forest finally thinned and the light changed and the train began to slow, the hawk came back into him the way it always did, without comment.

He did not write it down that night.

He wrote it down the next morning, in the column, and he wrote it in six words, because he could not get it to be accurate at any greater length.

**The hawk recognised the forest.**

---

## Part 4: The City With No Skyscrapers

Heaven Dou City had no tall buildings.

That was the first thing, and Lin Hao understood from the way Xie Xie kept looking up that it was not the first thing for everybody.

They came out of the station into a city of old-era buildings — stone and dark timber and roofs that had been repaired many times by many different people, so that no two were quite the same — and the tallest thing in Lin Hao's line of sight was a **tree.**

Not a building. A tree.

"There aren't any skyscrapers," Wulin said. He said it to Wu Zhangkong, quietly, the way you ask a thing you have already worked out and want confirmed.

"I've been waiting four hours for somebody to say that," Lin Hao said. "I said it at the second hour. To the window. The window did not respond."

"You talked to the window."

"I talked *at* the window. There's a difference, and it's the same difference as between me and Xie Xie, which is that one of us has been to sleep in the last two days."

"I have been to sleep," Xie Xie said, from behind them.

"Not recently."

"Recently enough."

"Xie Xie," Lin Hao said, kindly, "you asked a man about his storage device eleven minutes into a train journey and he said *you'll find out when you have one,* and you have not spoken a complete sentence since. That is not sleep. That is grief."

"Heaven Dou City is a historical city," Wu Zhangkong said. "To preserve the historical value, the city requires that no building exceed **fifty metres** or **ten storeys.**" He said it the way he said everything. "The regulations have held for a long time."

Xie Xie said, "That's the stupidest thing I've ever heard."

"It is the opposite."

"How is it the opposite."

Wu Zhangkong looked at him, and then — remarkably — at the street, and then back at Xie Xie.

"Eastsea City is second of five in an alliance," he said. "It has three million people and a port and buildings you can see from a train. **This city has the Tang Sect.**" He started walking. "Come along. You wanted to see the world outside of school. This is the part of it that does not need to be tall."

---

## Part 5: What Lin Hao Understood In The Street

He understood it about twenty minutes later, in a street that smelled of hot metal and old wood, and it was not a large understanding and it changed a great deal.

In Eastsea City he was **large.** Three rings, thirty-one ranks, a hawk at one thousand one hundred and eighty-six years, and he walked down a street and people looked, because he was ten years old and none of those things were supposed to be true of a ten-year-old. Being large was the interesting thing about him, and he had got used to it the way you get used to a sound in your own house.

In Heaven Dou City nobody looked at him.

Not because he was not large. Because **large was not the interesting thing here.**

The man at the forge on the corner had a soul ring and a hammer and forty years of something, and he was doing a thing with a bar that Lin Hao watched for six minutes from across the street and could not work out. The woman at the stall two doors down was selling something in a jar that hummed. An old man went past with **two** rings and a cane and did not hurry, and the street made room for him in a way that had nothing to do with his rings.

"What are you looking at," Wulin said.

"That man."

"For six minutes."

"I've been counting," Lin Hao said. "He's done the same thing forty-one times and every single one of them was slightly different, and I cannot work out what he's changing. It's not the angle. It's not the heat. It's something about how he's *standing*, and he's not doing it on purpose, and I would like to know how long it takes to not do something on purpose."

Wulin looked at the man for a while.

"Thirty years," he said.

"You don't know that."

"My father has been fixing machines for nineteen and he still stands wrong sometimes." Wulin shrugged. "So thirty."

"That is the most depressing thing anybody has said to me all week," Lin Hao said, "and Xie Xie has been talking about his shoulder."

Lin Hao stood in a city with no skyscrapers and understood that he had spent four months being the largest thing in a room, and that this was a place full of rooms where that did not matter at all.

It was the best thing that had happened to him since the crossing.

---

## Part 6: What Wu Zhangkong Said At The Grave

⚠️ *The Tang Sect's founder's grave is visited. No place beyond Heaven Dou City is named.*

It was on the fourth day, and it was not on the schedule, and Wu Zhangkong took the five of them up a hill behind the old quarter to a stone that did not have much on it.

He stood there for a while without speaking.

Then he said, "The Tang Sect was founded in this city."

Wulin said nothing. He was looking at the stone.

"Its headquarters is in **Shrek City** now," Wu Zhangkong said. "It has been for a long time. This is where it started." He put his hands behind his back. "The man who founded it had **Bluesilver Grass.**"

Wulin's breath did something.

"Everybody knows the story," Wu Zhangkong said. "That is not why I brought you here." He looked at the stone. "I brought you here because you are about to be in an organisation that is eight thousand years old, and you are ten, and you have three rings, and you lasted nine seconds against a legend — and the only thing that is going to keep you alive in a place like that is understanding that **you are not the interesting thing that has ever happened to it.**"

There was a silence.

"He had Bluesilver Grass," Wulin said. His voice was not steady. "And he founded it."

"He had Bluesilver Grass," Wu Zhangkong agreed. "And he was not the interesting thing that had ever happened to it either. He was just the one who was there, and kept going." He turned around. "Come along. There's a man I need to see about a set of vests, and he is going to try to overcharge me, and I would like witnesses."

---

He wrote it that night, in the room above the forge where Wu Zhangkong had put them, with the window open onto a street that had no tall buildings in it.

*Four hours of the hawk being still at a forest. I wrote six words about it and I cannot make it be accurate at seven.*

*This city has no skyscrapers and it has the Tang Sect, and nobody here looked at me, and I have never been anywhere that was better for me.*

*Wu Zhangkong took us to a grave to tell us we are not the interesting thing that has ever happened. I thought that was a small thing to say. It took me until the street to understand it, and now I cannot stop thinking about it, which is how I know it was not small.*

Then he looked at the page, and he added the line at the bottom, because he had learned that the line at the bottom was the one that mattered.

*I want to stay here. That is a problem, because we are not staying.*

*Zhou Zhangxi asked me today whether the Track was something you could learn without a hawk, and I said I did not know, and he said that was the fourth time somebody had said that to him this week, and Yun Xiao said from the bed that it was the fifth, and that they were keeping count.*

He closed the book.

And then he felt it, because he had stopped expecting to feel it and that was apparently the condition.

**Thirty-two.**

Not a wall. Not a coil. Just the next one, arriving the way the last one had not — quietly, in a room above a forge in a city with no tall buildings, at the end of a month in which he had been outlasted by a bear and walked out of a dead man's record with the Hall of Heroes out of files on him, and ignored by an entire street.

He had not been trying. That was the part he would think about longest. He had been **learning**, and the rank had simply come along with it, the way the hawk came along with everything else, without asking.

The **hawk-gold lock** at his temple lay across his cheekbone, and the **marks on his forearms** were faint under his sleeves, and the hawk turned over in him — **one thousand one hundred and eighty-six** — and did not lean toward anything, because the thing it had recognised was forty kilometres away and going further.

Lin Hao lay on his back in a city with no skyscrapers and did not sleep for a while.

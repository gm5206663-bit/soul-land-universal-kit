## Part 1: The Overturned Table

They went back for the beef.

It was Xie Xie's idea, of course — Xie Xie had decided, sometime around the fourth ladle of the first visit, that a single meal at Uncle Li's shop counted as an introduction and not a relationship, and that relationships required maintenance. Gu Yue had permitted one more meal in the tone she used for permitting weather. Wulin had agreed because Wulin agreed to meals the way water agreed to downhill. And Lin Hao had agreed because the lane shop had a forge-brazier at its cooking heart that he had spent the whole first visit watching, and because — though he would have put it less pretentiously — a place that fed twenty-two loud boys without ever once advertising had earned a second look from anyone who understood what quality did when nobody was watching.

They heard it before they saw it. Uncle Li's voice — wrong, thin, the voice of a man arguing from the ground.

The cooking table lay overturned across the lane's mouth. Earthen jars lay everywhere, shattered, the hundred little flames of the shop's famous beef hissing out in the spill, and the smell — the smell that had walked half the grade home with them after the first visit — went up in steam over the wreckage like something saying goodbye. Uncle Li was in the doorway, on his knees, blood at the corner of his mouth and one eye already swollen shut, and three men stood over him, and the lane's whole crowd stood around that at the careful distance crowds keep when none of them intends to be the first anything.

The largest of the three was bald and bare-chested in the cold, which he was clearly aware of, because the dragon tattoos down his arms had been given the cold to look at.

"Surnamed Li, a wise man submits to his circumstances," he was saying, in a voice built for lanes. "There isn't anyone on this street who dares to not hand over the protection fee money, yet you're always coming up with all sorts of excuses. Could it be that I, Guang Long, need to beg?"

"My wife is sick—" Uncle Li's voice came up off the stones. "The little I earn goes to the doctor. You've smashed my business — now she won't be able to live much longer. When her time comes, I won't live either."

"Threatening me with your life?" Guang Long laughed, the big easy laugh of a man who has won this argument so many times it has stopped being an argument. "Even if you die, I can substitute you with someone else and be paid all the same. Don't forget — this daddy is a Soul Master."

"So just because you're a Soul Master," said a voice from the mouth of the lane, "you have the right to bully people?"

The crowd opened. Tang Wulin was walking through it with large strides and a face nobody in class five had seen — not the ledger face, not the grinning face; something older had come up through both, the way bedrock comes up through soil. Beside him, without any decision anyone remembered making, the other three were walking too.

Guang Long looked them over — four children, one obviously expensive, one obviously fed — and laughed the way you laugh at weather. "Beat it. This daddy doesn't like to hit children."

Wulin did not beat it. Wulin hit him.

It was a plain punch, void of soul skills, without even a ripple of soul power in it — and Guang Long, mid-laugh, was staggered a full step back, and the laugh came off his face like paint. "You little—" His palm came out, flat, casual, the way a man swats a dog; and Wulin's second punch met it, and the sound that came back was not the sound a child's fist makes. It was the sound of a hammer meeting cast copper. Wulin slid back two steps. Guang Long's palm trembled, and the two of them looked at each other with the identical, offended surprise of two things that had expected to be the strongest in the lane.

The other two thugs moved then — around, not in, the way men who collect money for a living know to move — and that was the last mistake the lane's afternoon made that belonged to anyone but Lin Hao.

He did not draw. Not yet. The first thug got a foot — simply there, simply under him, and the man's own speed did the rest, delivering him face-first into the lane wall with a sound like a dropped crate. The second was smarter, swung a loaded fist at the back of the boy turning away from his friend — and the boy wasn't there anymore; there was only a pressure in the air where he'd been, and then the thug's own arm was folding the wrong direction against nothing he could see, and then he was on his knees in the spilled beef with his wrist held at an angle his voice could not stop describing. The Stormbringer came out sometime in there; afterward, nobody in the lane could have said when. It hung at the fourth boy's side now, point down, doing absolutely nothing, and both thugs looked at it and decided, with one accord, that the afternoon had somewhere else to be.

"Apologize," Lin Hao said, at walking volume, "and pay for the jars."

They apologized. They did not, it developed, have money on them — a circumstance Lin Hao accepted with a nod that promised a follow-up appointment, and they left the lane at a speed their profession would have found embarrassing.

Which left Guang Long. And Guang Long, rubbing a jaw that had stopped laughing, had begun to look at the four of them less like weather and more like an arithmetic problem.

## Part 2: Armored Dragon

Wulin's Bluesilver Grass took him first — a strand from the right hand, Bind, and the lane's stones went soft under Guang Long's feet the way the world goes soft in a bad dream; and in the same breath Xie Xie went up, off the overturned table, off the wall, the Light Dragon Dagger arriving at the thug's throat before the crowd had finished understanding that a child was in the air.

"Compensate for Uncle Li's losses and his medical fees," Xie Xie said, icy as a contract, "or I'll kill this baldy."

"Don't be impulsive, little brother," Guang Long said — and *laughed again,* which was the wrong sound for a man with a dagger at his throat, and Lin Hao's hawk-eyes caught why a half-second before the laugh finished: under the skin of the neck, where the tattoos gave way, something was lying in a different pattern than flesh. Metal-gray. Set in courses, like scale-roofing. "So you're a one-ring Soul Master. Do it, then. I want to see how you'll kill me."

"Xie Xie — the *neck—*"

The warning and the dagger arrived together, which is to say the warning arrived one heartbeat too late to be a warning and exactly on time to be an epitaph for the plan. The Light Dragon Dagger pressed down — and stopped. Xie Xie's arm pushed; the neck did not. It was, his arms learned, like pressing a dagger into a ship's anchor. And under the pressed steel, Guang Long's laugh finished becoming a snarl.

"Be careful!" Wulin's shout, Gu Yue's hand — a fireball, small and precise, the size of a bun and roughly as friendly, taking Guang Long's right hand at the knuckles. The explosion separated dagger from throat and Xie Xie from his landing, and Guang Long came out of the smoke with two rings turning under his feet — *one white, one yellow* — and two little spirit souls seated atop his shoulders like accountants: an earthen-yellow lizard, and a metal-gray one, larger, older, bored.

The lane went the particular silence of a crowd revising its afternoon.

"Armored Dragon," Lin Hao said quietly — land dragon; power and defense; the plate under the skin was the soul wearing the man — and the words were barely finished before Guang Long stomped and *launched,* not at Xie Xie, not at the girl: at Wulin, the way a decision goes at the thing it was about.

Gu Yue's earth rose to swamp him and the swamp broke like sugar glass. Wulin's Thousand Refined Tungsten Hammers came out and met the first fist and the collision rang down the lane like a smith's yard; and Wulin — who had taken a two-ring ape's backhand off a friend's blade and who hit like a falling wall himself — went back seven and eight steps, pale, his boots plowing two lines in the dirt. The heavy silver hammers replaced the tungsten; this wasn't the academy, and it wasn't training. He threw the right one, Bluesilver Grass knotted to the handle, and Guang Long swatted a Thousand Refined hammer — *swatted it* — out of the air with a bare palm that didn't so much as blush.

Lin Hao went in under the arc of the thrown hammer, because that was the line the fight had opened and the sword had already read it.

Plate has seams. Scales have grain. Everything forged — and everything *grown* toward armor — keeps the memory of the shape it grew over, and the Armored Dragon's plates had grown over a man: a street thug's thick, vain, human neck, and the soul had roofed it in courses, and courses have the lines where one course ends and the next begins. Xie Xie's dagger had failed because it had arrived *across* the grain, at the flat of the plate, the way a chisel fails on a weld. The Stormbringer did not arrive across anything. Lin Hao laid the Gale Talon along the edge — the wind folding into its ribbon, the sword's own voice given teeth — and cut *along* the seam under the jaw, one economical stroke, the way you open a bar along the line it already wanted to bend.

The lane heard it: a sound like a nail drawn out of green wood. And then it saw the thing nobody had seen all afternoon — a line of red, bright and absolutely mortal, opening down the metal-gray of Guang Long's throat.

First blood. The first blood of the afternoon, drawn by the smallest person in it.

Guang Long's hand came around — the second soul skill lighting along him as it came, the silver sheath rolling over the gray, eight centimeters of claw unfurling from each knuckle — and Lin Hao, who had seen it coming, who had already begun the Wind-Step that would have put him a lane-width away—

—learned what rank twenty-seven, double-skilled, innately god-strong, and *furious* actually meant.

The claw-hand caught the air where he had been and the air hit back. Something like a wall made of the thrown hammer's echo went through his chest; the stall behind him — a fruit stall, a good one, the lane's best persimmons — received him, folded, and kept most of his momentum for itself. He came to rest under a ruin of crates and crushed fruit with his right arm numb from the shoulder down, his ribs performing a small bright song, and the honest, unhandsome understanding that this one, this *particular* one, was not a match he could win today with the tools he'd brought.

Across the lane, Guang Long touched the blood on his own throat. Looked at it. And his face, which had been a lane-bully's face all afternoon, finished becoming something with no humor left in it at all.

"You brats," he said, "have no idea what you've done."

He went through them the way weather goes through a market. Gu Yue's light flashed — she was in front of Wulin, and behind her Wulin was still on one knee tasting blood, and she did not step aside, and Lin Hao, hauling himself out of the persimmons with his good arm, understood with a lurch exactly why she didn't. Earth rose in front of her. Earth broke. And then she was lifted, two plate-gray arms around her waist, and the lane's last polite distance evaporated into screaming—

—and Wulin's arms were around her too, from behind, pulling, both of them braced against a strength that did not care, and Tang Wulin — bleeding from the mouth and the nose, one hammer gone, rank fifteen against rank twenty-seven — planted his feet in his own blood-spotted dirt and said, in a voice with no tremor in it anywhere:

"Let me face him."

## Part 3: Gold

What followed, the lane would retell for a year, and never once correctly.

And the part the lane never got right — the part that would have been impossible in a lane without him — was that **the dagger did not draw the blood.**

Xie Xie's Light Dragon Dagger went across the scales, because scales are what they are and a dagger goes where it is pointed, and the scales did not open, because that is what scales do. **Lin Hao's blade went along the seam under the jaw instead, because plate has seams and scales have grain, and he had spent six years reading grain in metal that did not want to be read.** Same lane. Same dragon. Same scales. **Different line.**

That is the butterfly, and it is the whole of it. **In a lane without him, Xie Xie's dagger fails and Wulin's hammer does the work and the lane retells a story about a hammer.** In a lane with him in it, the dagger fails and the sword draws first blood, and the lane retells the story wrong for a year because nobody in it understood that the difference was not the weapon.

And his shout — *"the NECK—"* — arrived one heartbeat late, and he would think about that heartbeat for longer than he thought about anything else that week, because reading a thing and saying it in time are two different skills and he had only ever had the first.

Fist and hammer met. The sound was less a sound than a weather event; the crowd fell back with its hands over its ears; and Tang Wulin — the forge-boy, the boy the grade had learned to measure matches by — was thrown out of the collision like a stone from a sling, and Xie Xie, who dashed in to catch him, learned what catching him meant, and both of them went down in a heap at the wall of the beef shop. The Light Dragon Dagger, jarred out of control in the fall, scored a line across Wulin's back.

"So painful. Xie Xie, your Light Dragon Dagger is stabbing me."

"I'm sorry — that wasn't my—"

Guang Long shook out his shoulders and turned his back on them. On all of them. He faced the crowd instead, spreading his arms wide over the spilled beef and the broken jars, his voice finding its lane again now that nothing in the lane could interrupt it. "You see? If you dare defy me, this will be the outcome. These kids are Soul Masters — and they are in *no way* a match for this daddy. Whomever dares defy me, beware of this daddy's Armored Dragon claws tearing you into shreds—"

And behind him, in the boy on the ground, the cut on his back began to burn.

Lin Hao saw it start. From under his crate-ruin, half-blind with dust and the bright song in his ribs, his hawk-eyes — running, as they always ran, whether he asked them or not — saw the heat before he felt anything: a *shimmer* rising off Wulin's shoulders, the air above him going wavy the way air goes over summer stone, and then, along the back of Wulin's right hand, the small golden spots coming up like a fire coming up through ash.

Not the first time. Lin Hao knew. He had seen one scale, thumbnail-sized, in a gray bedroom months ago, with the air lying around it like water over a wreck.

This was the wreck surfacing.

He did not go toward the fight. That was the choice he made, and it was the hardest one he had made all afternoon, because every line of him wanted to be between the claw and the boy on the ground — but the sword in his shaking right hand could not, today, be between anything and this; and there was something in the lane that needed doing that only he was positioned to do. So Lin Hao got his feet, and walked — swayed, walked — to the mouth of the crowd, and stood in it, and spread his coat, and became, with his back to everything, a door that would not open.

"Show's over," he said, at walking volume, to forty faces. "Nobody's eyes past me."

A fish-wife two arms' length away looked at him — at the blood on his temple he hadn't inventoried yet, at the ruined arm he was holding like something borrowed, at the absolutely unreasonable calm of a boy standing in front of a fight like a gate in front of a garden — and she did not move past him. Neither, it turned out, did anyone. Years later, the lane would still agree on almost nothing about that afternoon, except the boy in the doorway, and that you did not go past him, and that — one persimmon-seller's wife would add, whenever the telling reached that far — he had the strangest eyes, gold-flecked, the color of a hawk's in strong light, and he had looked at them all like a *smith* looks at a crowd, as if deciding which of them might be trusted to hold something hot.

Behind the door, the gold was finishing its work.

Wulin rose. The burning had gone from his tailbone through his body and into his brain; his right arm was scales to the wrist and the scales were claws and his soul power was going down like a tide out of a harbor, and none of it mattered, because Guang Long was turning back around, and the lane-bully who had shrugged off hammers and swallowed a dagger and broken an earth-wall looked into the face of the smallest fighter in his afternoon—

—and the gold was in the boy's eyes. The pupils had gone to slits — cold, vertical, patient — the way a very old something looks out through a window it has not used in a long while. And Guang Long, whose Armored Dragon was a land dragon, whose blood was the thin diluted echo of some scaled and ancient thing, felt a shiver run through every plate on his body, and his arms — his god-strong, hammer-swatting, boy-throwing arms — went *limp.*

Bang.

One hammer. The Armored Dragon went out of the lane like a jar out of a window, and the crowd heard the bones of his arm fracture on the way, and his two returning thugs — arrived, fatefully, at exactly the wrong moment — tried to catch him, and failed, and the three of them finished the afternoon in a heap against the far wall, which is where the lane felt, poetically, they had belonged all along.

And then the gold was receding — the scales dimming into points of light and going back under, the heat cooling, the whole of it ten seconds long — and Tang Wulin stood in the middle of the wrecked lane with a right arm he could not lift and a soul power scraped to the bottom of its well, looking at his own hand like a man meeting a stranger who had his face.

"Your—" Xie Xie had seen. Xie Xie had seen all of it, from the heap, four meters away: the gold, the slits, the limp. His voice came out an octave wrong. "Wulin. *What was that?*"

"I don't know," Wulin said. And looked, over Xie Xie's shoulder, at the boy in the doorway — at Lin Hao, who was leaning against the lane wall with his coat coming off his numbed shoulder and his face saying nothing at all — and something passed between the two of them that had already passed once before, in a gray bedroom, months ago. The file. The keeping of it.

Gu Yue stood with her hands slowly lowering from a healing she no longer needed to give, and her expression was the one Lin Hao — watching her watch Wulin's hand — would have described, in the privacy of his own head, as a scholar being handed a book in a language she had been told did not exist. *What is that,* her stillness said, in the voice she did not use. And then, quieter under it, something else — not fear. *Recognition* of the shape of a secret, by someone who kept larger ones.

The street, released from its door, poured in to help. Uncle Li came up off his knees into forty hands; the tables rose; the jars were swept. "That bastard ran off without giving any compensation," Xie Xie snarled at the far wall's empty heap — the thugs having achieved, sometime during the retelling, a vertical and a vanishing — and Wulin, pale as paper, pressed a small heavy purse of federation coins into Uncle Li's hand instead: "For the medicine. Don't ask." And did not mention, because he did not need to, whose name the searching for that money had originally been for.

On the far water inside Lin Hao, something lifted its head.

Not toward the fight. Toward the gold. One slow turn of an old bird's attention toward a direction only it could see, and then — settled again, watchful, the way you watch a horizon that has finally, after years, shown you a sail.

Lin Hao filed it. The shelf was getting extremely crowded.

## Part 4: The Night Test

They went to Wu Zhangkong's room after dinner, all four of them this time, because the secret had stopped fitting inside smaller rooms.

The teacher opened in moon-white pajamas, ice-cold, one eyebrow already assembled for trouble; and Xie Xie, who had been vibrating since the lane, explained in one breathless run the gold, the slits, the limp, the ten seconds, the arm. And Wulin — who had thought about it the whole walk, the way he thought about metal — said the honest thing that undid Xie Xie's entire theory before it could sit down.

"Both times, Xie Xie's dagger had just cut me. Both times. But—" and here he shook his head, because the honesty was the point, "—the *first* time wasn't a fight. I woke up with a scale on my arm. No dagger. No cut. It was just *there,* and then it wasn't."

"Then it's not the dagger," Xie Xie said, deflated. "It's — I don't know, a hidden second martial soul? A *fusion skill?* Wulin, do you know what it means if we have compatible souls? There is no one in Eastsea City who can do a fusion skill. *No one.* If we—"

"Xie Xie." Wu Zhangkong's voice ended the fantasy the way his hand ended spars. And then, because the boy was not wrong to wonder, only early: "Test it. Xie Xie — cut him. Slowly. Let's see what your dagger's light does to that wound."

The cut was careful, almost gentle, three pairs of eyes on it — and Lin Hao's, from his corner, saw what the other three were too close to see: as the Light Dragon Dagger parted the skin, *light went in.* Not out — in. Faint rays leaving the blade and entering the wound the way water enters a lamp-wick, and under the skin the heat began its slow turn up from the tailbone, and on the back of Wulin's hand, one golden spot surfaced, hesitated—

and faded. The heat banked. The scale went back under, like a coal decided against.

"Strange," Wu Zhangkong said, after a long moment, in the voice of a man adding a file to a cabinet he did not like. "The light feeds it. But it did not rise. Not like this — not *asked for.*" He looked at Wulin for a long, considering second. "It comes when you cannot afford for it not to. Don't test it again. And none of you—" the smoky green eyes went around the room and included, with no particular gentleness, the fourth boy in the corner, "—will speak of this outside this door. The world is full of people who collect interesting things. Interesting children most of all."

They filed out into the dormitory hall. Xie Xie was already rebuilding the fusion-skill theory out of its own ruins, aloud; Gu Yue walked in her own silence, which had a new shelf in it; Wulin flexed a hand that still didn't entirely belong to him, thinking about light going *into* wounds.

And Lin Hao, last out, paused in the doorframe — because the smith's question had been walking circles in his head since the lane, and the door was the only place it would ever have, and Wu Zhangkong was the only person in the building it could be said to.

"Teacher. The light didn't come *from* the dagger." A pause; the finding of the words in the dark, the way you find a seam with a thumb. "The dagger's a *door.* Something behind it leaned close when the door opened. Today it looked. It didn't come through." He met the ice-cold eyes squarely. "In the forge, when you heat a bar and it moves on its own — the fire didn't move it. The fire just reminded the metal what the metal *was.*"

Wu Zhangkong said nothing for a long moment. The hall lamp made two shadows of him and none of anyone else.

---

Room 205 was not dark when they got back.

**Yun Xiao** was awake at the lower bunk with his book and a stub of candle, in the specific posture of a person who had decided an hour ago that he was not going to sleep and had since made peace with it. **Zhou Zhangxi** was awake on the bunk above him, flat on his back, staring at the ceiling, which was Zhou Zhangxi's version of the same decision.

Neither of them said anything for a moment. Then Zhou Zhangxi said, "You're bleeding."

"It's not mine," said Wulin.

"That's not better."

"No," said Wulin. "It isn't."

Yun Xiao closed his book. He did not ask what had happened. He had not asked what had happened on any of the nights, which was, Lin Hao thought, the most under-rated thing about him — he simply wrote things down and waited until somebody was ready.

"Four of you," he said instead. "Every night. Three months." He adjusted his glasses. "There are twenty students in this class. Sixteen of them are also going to be in the tournament, and none of them have been asked to stay after the bell once."

Nobody said anything, because there was nothing to say that was not an apology.

"I'm not complaining," said Yun Xiao. "I'm noting. There's a difference, and I want it noted."

"You're in the roster matches," said Wulin. "All of us are. That's the whole class."

"Yes," said Yun Xiao. "And you four are additionally the combat team, which is a thing that exists, and which is going to be on the board, and which people are going to say the names of." He put the book down. "I'd like to be good at something that gets said. That's all. I'd have thought that was obvious."

Zhou Zhangxi rolled over, and the bunk creaked like a ship.

"I can lift more than anyone in this grade except you," he said to Wulin, without any heat in it at all, which was worse. "And I can't do the *thing.* Whatever the thing is. The thing you four do where you go and come back different." A pause. "Is it teachable?"

The room was very quiet.

"No," said Lin Hao, because it was the only honest answer and because he had learned that Zhou Zhangxi would have hated a kind one. "It isn't teachable. But the other parts are. Running is running. Lifting is lifting. And you're better at both than anyone he's got."

"You're saying I'm the strongest person not on the team."

"I'm saying you're the strongest person not on the team," said Lin Hao, "and that in six months somebody is going to need somebody like that, and it isn't going to be a comfort to them that you were sad about it in the spring."

Zhou Zhangxi stared at the ceiling for a long time.

"That was a shit thing to say," he said at last.

"Yes."

"I'm going to think about it anyway."

"Good," said Lin Hao.

Yun Xiao, from the lower bunk, in the voice of a boy adding a line to a ledger: "Noted."


"Go to sleep, Lin Hao."

"Yes, teacher."

"And Lin Hao." He was half down the hall when the voice followed him, quieter, and — if such a temperature could be said to have degrees — less cold. "That was not a child's thought. Keep having them. *Quietly.*"

In room eleven, Xie Xie was asleep mid-sentence. In room twelve, the harbor did its nightly accounting. And somewhere under the city's noise, in the direction the far water kept looking, a man with a broken arm and a broken livelihood was climbing the stairs of a taller brother's patience — because Guang Long had not spent the afternoon learning nothing. He had learned that four children had cost him his lane, his fees, and the use of an arm; and he had learned, in the last gray minute before the heap, that children like that attended a school, and schools had gates, and gates — unlike lanes — had people willing to pay for what came through them.

The pictures of them would be ready by morning. All four.

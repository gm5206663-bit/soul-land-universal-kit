## Part 1: The Old Lady's Ledger

In a luxurious room, the Silver Moon Douluo sat with the report and was quiet several long moments.

"It looks like I won't have to secretly give them some bonus points," she muttered. "At this rate, they'll manage to get to sixty. Hmm. Maybe that brat's strength is related to his stomach." A pause, and then the question the whole academy would eventually ask in one form or another: "Where did Zhuo Shi even find these little monsters? Especially that Tang Wulin and Gu Yue..."

She did not mention the fifth one, because she was still reading that page, and pages had a way of needing the whole day.

## Part 2: The Seventh Room

The first trial had tested spiritual power; the second, combat; the third, adaptability and courage. The fourth, fifth, and sixth had taken talent, profession, and willpower. The seventh was a marathon of endurance, and it would push bodies to the limit.

The surprise of the room was that it was not empty. Other examinees were still present — hours into the examination, still grinding for the six points of a finished series, faces the color of the effort. It was the first time class zero had seen the field they were actually playing on: a room full of strangers, all paying the same price at different exchange rates.

The tests were read out. Running ten kilometers. Squatting fifty kilograms, one thousand times. One thousand chin-ups. Finish all three and six points were yours; finish inside an hour and it was ten; every ten minutes over cost a point, down to the floor of six. The same tests for every type of soul master — power-types held the advantage, which was not Shrek's problem, because the first trial had favored everybody *but* power-types. Fairness was not the design; breadth was. And this trial, at least, put no ban on soul power.

Wulin's glance at Gu Yue and Xiaoyan was brief and entirely anxious. Then the questions, in order: how many trials? — *ten.* Points to pass? — *sixty.* What do the remaining trials test? — *"I can't."* And the parts? — *two points each.*

He ran the numbers where he stood — eighty reachable, twenty of leeway, three trials unknown — and turned to the girls with the plan already load-bearing. "You two only need to run the ten kilometers. Xie Xie, we'll complete all three. Let's get started." Neither girl asked to see his arithmetic. He was stingy; stingy people are excellent at numbers; they trusted the arithmetic the way you trust a miser with a ledger.

They ran on soul treadmills. Wulin's pace was, mysteriously, fast — Shen Yi stood at the rail of his belt with wide eyes, an agility-type's trial being eaten by a power-type — and she asked the question under it: *"Why do you only want them to score two points? Aren't you worried they won't score enough later?"*

"Their bodies aren't that strong. Chin-ups and squats are basically impossible for them — if I pushed, they'd waste time *and* stamina we need for the final three trials. Conserve now, do our best there. We scored well where we scored. We still have a chance this way."

Shen Yi smiled despite the day she was having. "Excellent. What a thorough analysis."

Wulin and Xie Xie took ten minutes for the run. Wulin then did one thousand fifty-kilogram squats the way other men breathe, the Golden Dragon King's bloodline turning mathematics into a rumor — not even ten minutes — and went to the bar; all three of his tests were done before thirty minutes had passed. Xie Xie, sturdy under the agility, lagged and did not suffer: forty-five minutes, full marks anyway. The girls ran their ten kilometers in about twenty and sat out the rest, conserving, and soul power put the color back in them between sentences.

## Part 3: The Metronome

An examinee from some inland academy, four hours into his own six points, watched the fifth stranger finish his run.

The boy had stepped onto the belt out of turn, unhurried, and had run the ten kilometers the way a clock runs — not fast the way the gold-flashed one was fast, just *exactly*, the same length stride at the same point of every minute, breathing on a schedule, as if the treadmill were a bench and the bench owed him a rate. Then the squats: one thousand, unbroken, at a pace that never once consulted fatigue. Then the bar. The whole hour's work in well under half of it — and the boy stepped down looking exactly as he had stepped up, gathered the hour's ten points like a man accepting correct change, and went to stand with the others without one gram of display about any of it.

The inland examinee hated him a little, in the way you hate things that are quietly beyond you, and then went back to his own squats, and thought about the boy's pace for the rest of the day, and improved, later, enormously. Shrek's trials worked on everybody in the room. That has always been the design.

What the inland examinee could not see was the reading underneath. The sixth had spent the run the way he had spent the gates — on the seams. The talent — the oldest thing he owned, the one that had been taking the world apart and keeping what worked since before he had words for it — had turned inward for the hour and read its own instrument: this muscle's margin, that tendon's debt, where the lactic arithmetic turned, what the breath actually cost. He paced the hour to the minute not from discipline alone but from *knowledge*, the body read like any other metal — and when the reading said the ten points were banked at the lowest price, he stopped. Everything the arithmetic required, on time, and not one repetition past it.

## Part 4: The Form

They rested the fifteen minutes that cost nothing, and then Wulin asked for the numbers, and Shen Yi looked at the form she was holding.

"After the seventh trial — Tang Wulin, you have fifty points." Her eyes moved over the sheet and then back at him, because excluding the twenty that temper had burned, the boy had a full score: maximum points, every trial that had actually been scored. "Gu Yue, thirty-eight points. Xie Xie, forty-eight points. Xu Xiaoyan, forty points."

And one more line, in a hand the form had needed a second column for. "Lin Hao — fifty points."

The room did the arithmetic at different speeds. Fifty — with the two zeros standing in the middle of it like the temper they had been — was ten short of the line, with three trials and thirty points still on the table. And the fifth examinee had never yet lost a point he could see, so everybody in the room could finish that sum: the passing line was not a question of whether, only of which trial. He received the number the way he received correct change, and the class looked at him and then away, because there was nothing to say that the arithmetic had not said.

The others' situations were soberer. Wulin and Xie Xie were safe for the outer court and could not reach the inner by any arithmetic left on the table — *impossible to enter directly*, the numbers said, plain as a wall — so the minimum was the mission now. Xiaoyan's standing was not much better than precarious. And Gu Yue was in the tightest box of the five: eight points of leeway across three trials. She could lose, at most, two or three points per trial — she could not lose more than three points in any one of them, or she was eliminated.

Nobody said any of this aloud. Wulin's face did the captain's version — the plan already forming behind it — and Gu Yue looked at the wall with her jaw set at the angle the class had learned meant *the purse has seen the problem*.

Twilight was covering the horizon when Shen Yi gave them the eighth.

## Part 5: Ten Doors

"The eighth trial is a comprehensive battle. You will fight a current student of Shrek Academy. The available opponents are random, so your luck will be a factor. Last ten minutes against your opponent to pass. Defeat them, and you receive full points. Between — marks of six to ten, on performance."

A battle. Wulin let out a breath he had been holding since the buns — whatever else happened, Gu Yue would not drown here — and Xiaoyan's odds were left to the lottery.

"Together or one at a time?" he asked, heart thumping.

"You'll take turns."

"Then can we decide the order?"

"There will be ten opponents for you all to choose from," Shen Yi said. "So it isn't just your strength being tested — it's your judgement. There are strong and weak hidden among the ten. Some may naturally restrain you; some you may restrain. A large portion of your performance will be determined by your choice of opponent."

The room they entered for it was circular and much larger than any before — prepared while they had run, which meant the ten had been waiting all afternoon, listening to a wall, getting ready for children.

At the chairs, the class's eyes did the flick. Battle trials had an order, and the order was a decision, and the decision was judgement, and the judgement was about to be five people arguing sweetly in whispers over ten closed doors — three of which hid restraint, and one of which, if the hall that hears everything had any humor at all, was already smiling.

The margin goes last. The margin always goes last. But somebody had to go first, and somebody had to *choose*, and the choosing was the trial.

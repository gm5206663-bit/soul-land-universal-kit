## Part 1: What Wei Xiaofeng Was Doing at Six in the Morning

Lin Hao found him because he could not sleep, and he could not sleep because Wu Zhangkong had hit him eleven times the previous evening and his ribs had opinions, and the dormitory at six in the morning smelled of cold stone and Zhou Zhangxi's snoring, so he went outside to do the breathing that his father had taught him for exactly this situation.

Wei Xiaofeng was on the training ground. Alone. Doing his clone work.

Not sparring. Not training. *Drilling* — the same feint-and-split he had used in the gauntlet, the one Lin Hao had learned in three weeks and turned back on him in eleven seconds, over and over, from the top, with the mechanical patience of a boy who had been given an answer and had decided, correctly, that the answer was not good enough.

Lin Hao stood in the doorway for a full minute watching, because it was the most interesting thing he had seen all week, and because he understood immediately what he was looking at: Wei Xiaofeng was not practicing the move. He was practicing the *variant.* Every fourth repetition he changed something — the angle of the split, the depth of the feint, the beat between them — and then he went back to the original and started again.

"You're building a second one," Lin Hao said.

Wei Xiaofeng did not jump. That was the first thing. Wei Xiaofeng, who was loud about everything, did not jump, which meant he had known Lin Hao was there for some time and had chosen, deliberately, to keep working.

"Go away," Wei Xiaofeng said, without heat.

"You're building a second variant so the next time I answer the first one, there's a different one behind it." Lin Hao walked out onto the ground. "That's correct. That's the correct response. I want you to know that I recognize it as correct, because I'm the one who made it necessary, and I'm not unsympathetic."

"You beat me in eleven seconds."

"I did."

"In front of *everyone.*"

"I did," Lin Hao agreed, "and I've thought about that, actually. I've thought about it a lot, because I liked beating you and I didn't like *how* I beat you, and those are two different things, and I'd like to explain the difference if you'll let me, and if you won't let me I'll explain it anyway, because it's six in the morning and I have ribs and no self-control."

Wei Xiaofeng looked at him. It was a long look, and it was the first look he had given Lin Hao since the gauntlet that was not made of outrage.

"You learned my technique in three weeks," he said. "How."

"I have a thing," Lin Hao said. "It's not a secret, it's just not interesting to talk about, because the interesting part isn't the learning, it's the *keeping.* I keep them. Every fight I've ever had is still in me, working. Seventy-eight of them, this morning. Mo Si's short sword, Qin Lang's feed-line, a bear's honest momentum, your clone work, my brother's hammer, Wu Zhangkong's wrist." He sat down on the training-ground wall, which hurt, and did it anyway. "And here's the part I want you to hear, Wei Xiaofeng, and I want you to hear it while you're still angry, because it'll sound better while you're angry. You are the only person in class zero who has ever taught me the same thing *twice.* Everyone else gave me one answer. You gave me a variant, and a variant is worth four answers, because a variant means the technique is alive, and a live technique grows, and everything else I have is a dead one that I know perfectly."

Wei Xiaofeng was quiet for a long moment.

"So don't stop," Lin Hao said. "Build the second one. Build the third. Make me keep coming back, because every time I come back I have to *re-learn* you, and re-learning is the only kind of learning that costs me anything." He got up off the wall. "Also it's six in the morning and you have no shirt on and it's eleven degrees and I'm going back inside, and if you tell anyone about this conversation I'll deny it and then I'll beat you again in nine seconds, which is a full two seconds faster, and you'll never hear the end of it."

"I'm going to tell everyone," Wei Xiaofeng said.

"You're going to tell *Xie Xie,*" Lin Hao said, "because Xie Xie is the only one who'll enjoy it, and you like him, and you've never admitted that either." And he went inside, and the ribs complained, and behind him on the cold training ground Wei Xiaofeng put his shirt back on very slowly, and then went back to work, and built the second variant before breakfast.

## Part 2

He caught his reflection in a window and looked away, and then looked back, because he had stopped recognising himself at speed. Storm-gray at the temple, gold-flecked eyes, faint feather-marks on both forearms. He had been ten years old a long time and none of this had been in the plan, and there had never been a plan.
: The Accident

The platform sessions were announced on the fourth day, in the flat voice, at the end of the evening session, and the announcement was four sentences long and changed the shape of everything.

"The Eastsea Spirit Pagoda has granted class zero access to the elementary Spirit Ascension Platform," Wu Zhangkong said. "You enter in two weeks. Inside, you will fight soul beasts, and the soul beasts will not be adjusted for your age, and nothing in there will care what your name is. You will be alone — the platform separates you on entry. You will find each other or you will not." A pause. "Some of you will press the exit within a minute. I would prefer you didn't tell each other which."

"Can we die?" Zhang Yangzi asked, because Zhang Yangzi asked the questions.

"You cannot die," Wu Zhangkong said, which was true, and which was not the whole truth, and which Lin Hao noticed, and filed.

And then, on the eighth day, the accident happened, and the accident is the reason that anyone in class zero learned what Gu Yue actually was.

It was a falling beam. Class zero trained in the old hall at the north edge of the grounds, and the old hall had a roof that the academy had been promising to fix since before any of them were born, and the beam that came down was not dramatic — it was not a collapsing building, it was one beam, maybe forty kilograms, coming off a rotten bracket at four meters, directly above the place where Xie Xie had been standing a half-second before and was no longer, because Xie Xie had moved.

The problem was that Xie Xie had moved *into* Zhang Yangzi.

It was the sort of thing that happens in a room full of people who are all moving fast, and it would have been nothing — a shove, a stumble, an elbow — except that Zhang Yangzi had been mid-feint with his weight committed forward and his wings half-out. The shove put him down hard on his left wrist at the exact angle that wrists do not like, and the sound the wrist made was a sound that all seven of them heard, and then the beam landed, and the beam was not the emergency.

Lin Hao was already moving. He had been moving before the beam fell, which was a thing he would think about later — that his body had gone toward the *sound* and not the *danger,* because the sound was the sound of a person being hurt and his six years of deciding to be strong enough had wired him to it faster than to anything else. He got to Zhang Yangzi in two strides. He got his hands under the wrist. He had three seconds of actual competence, because he had set his father's dislocated thumb twice and he knew what a wrist looked like when it was broken and this wrist was broken.

And then Gu Yue was there, and Gu Yue did a thing.

She did not run. Lin Hao saw it happen from ninety centimetres away and he saw all of it, and he would keep all of it for the rest of his life: the air around her went *green,* a green so pale it was almost a thought. Then there was a flash of silver that was not light exactly, it was more like the place where she had been *stopped being a place,* and she was crouching beside Zhang Yangzi's wrist with her hand already on it, and the distance she had crossed was eleven meters, and she had not crossed it.

"Hold him still," Gu Yue said.

"You—," Lin Hao said.

"*Hold him still.*"

He held him still.

What she did to the wrist was not healing — Lin Hao knew what healing looked like, his mother had a neighbour who was a healer, and this was not that. This was *cold,* a cold that came out of her palm and went into the swelling and *argued* with it, and the swelling lost. Zhang Yangzi's face went white and then less white. The bone did not go back, because she was not that, but the pain left, and the pain leaving was the whole of the mercy available, and she gave all of it.

"Splint," she said. "Three weeks. Don't let him use the wings."

"Right," Lin Hao said. And then, because he was Lin Hao, because he had been handed the single most interesting thing that had happened to him since the sea took a girl, and because the only thing he knew how to do with an interesting thing was to say it out loud: "You teleported."

"No."

"You *teleported.* Eleven meters. I was watching you and you didn't move, you just — there was a green thing, and a silver thing, and then you were here."

"I moved quickly," Gu Yue said, and stood up, and wiped her hand on her trouser leg, and looked at him with an expression that was not a warning and was absolutely not nothing.

"That's the *worst* lie I have ever heard," Lin Hao said, delighted. "Xie Xie is fast. Xie Xie is the fastest thing in this building, and Xie Xie *moves,* you can see him move, he's a blur and a blur is still a *thing that travels.* You didn't travel. You were there and then you were here and the middle part didn't happen, and I know what the middle part is, Gu Yue, the middle part is *space,* and you did something to it."

The hall was silent. All six of them were watching, and Wu Zhangkong had appeared in the doorway at some point and was leaning against the frame with his arms folded and the ghost of something on his face that Lin Hao would have bet money on.

Gu Yue looked at him for a long moment.

"You have seventy-eight of them," she said.

Lin Hao stopped. "What?"

"Seventy-eight. You told Wei Xiaofeng." Her eyes were the colour of a filed report. "You keep them. Everyone you've ever fought. That's what you told him at six in the morning on the training ground, and I know because I was there before you were, and I left before you saw me, and I have not decided yet whether I meant to." A beat. "You have seventy-eight. And you have one page with nothing on it, and the page is me, and you have been carrying that for three weeks, and you have been *cheerful* about it, which is the part I do not understand."

Lin Hao, for the first time in the four months that anyone in class zero had known him, did not immediately have something to say.

"So here is the thing," Gu Yue said. "I can tell you what that was, and then you would have seventy-nine, and the page would not be blank, and you would stop being the only person in this building who has to *work* to see me." She tilted her head a fraction. "And I have decided that I do not want that. Not because I don't trust you. I trust you more than anyone here, and I want you to understand that I am aware of how strange that is to say out loud."

"Say it again."

"No."

"Gu Yue. *Say it again.*"

"I said I don't want you to have it," she said, "not that I don't want you to have *me,* and those are different, and you are smart enough to know the difference, and you are also going to spend a week pretending you're not." And she walked out of the hall, and the six of them stood there, and Zhang Yangzi — whose wrist was splinted, whose pain was gone, who had been carried out of his own catastrophe by a girl he had never once spoken to — said, in a small voice, "What just happened?"

"I have no idea," Xie Xie said, awed. "That was the best thing I've ever seen and I have seen this one *walk up a wall.*"

## Part 3: Seventy-Nine

He wrote the line anyway.

Not the technique — he did not have the technique, and he understood now that he was not going to get it, and he had made his peace with that faster than he had expected, because there was something in *I trust you more than anyone here* that outweighed the arithmetic, and he had not known that about himself.

The line was this: **Gu Yue — does not cross distance. Rearranges it. Green first, then silver. She chose to show me and chose not to explain, which means the showing was not an accident and the silence was not a refusal. She was on the training ground at six in the morning before I was. She has been watching me the way I watch everyone.**

And underneath it, in the different ink — the ink he had used once before, for Guang Long, for the fight he had lost — he wrote: **she is not the blank page. I was the blank page. She has had me written down for months and I never once noticed, and I notice everything.**

That was the seventy-ninth line, and it was the first line in the ledger that was not about how to win.

The growth came two days later, and it came the way it always came now, which was sideways and overnight and slightly rude. one hundred and ninety-one became one hundred and ninety-one — seven points, the largest single movement since the tournament — and he lay in the dark doing the arithmetic and understood, with a mild shock, that the fifteen points had not come from a fight at all. They had come from *being wrong.* From discovering, at ten years old, that he had misread a person for four months, and that the misreading had been comfortable, and that he had preferred the comfortable misreading to the work of checking it.

His soul had eaten the correction. That was the whole mechanism, and it was more honest than he wanted it to be: the talent did not reward him for winning. It rewarded him for *updating.*

"Hao," Wulin said, from below, in the voice of a boy who had stopped being surprised. "You're doing the noise again."

"Fifteen."

"In your sleep."

"In my sleep."

"You did that last time too." Wulin rolled over. "I've decided it's fine. I've decided you're a furnace and I live next to you and I get warm, and I've stopped asking why."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me."

"I'm going to hit you."

"You're going to *try,*" Lin Hao said, and the harbour did its accounting, and the ribs complained, and somewhere in the dark a hawk that had been watching a girl for four months turned its head, and did not ask its question, and did not need to.

## Part 4: Two Weeks

The last thing that happened before the platform was that Wang Jinxi asked to be paired with him.

Not demanded. Wang Jinxi did not demand anything anymore. He asked, in the flat manner of a boy who had learned from Wu Zhangkong that the flat manner was how you said things that mattered, at the end of the evening session, when the other five had gone in.

"Two weeks," Wang Jinxi said. "In the platform."

"Yes."

"I want to fight you before we go in."

Lin Hao looked at him. "You lost in the gauntlet in one exchange."

"I know what happened." Wang Jinxi's jaw did something. "I know *exactly* what happened. You used my own fold against me and you told me I'd taught you how, and I've thought about it every day, and the part I can't get past is not that I lost. It's that you were *right.* I did teach you. I was thorough. I showed you the whole thing because I wanted to be certain you understood what I was doing, and being certain was more important to me than winning." He looked at the ground. "That's a thing I do. I've done it my whole life. My father says it's rigour and I've started to think it's fear."

"That's a big thing to say out loud."

"You said something big out loud to Wei Xiaofeng at six in the morning," Wang Jinxi said, "and he built a second variant before breakfast, and he has told absolutely no one, which is how I know you didn't humiliate him, you *recruited* him. So. Fight me. Not to win. I want to find out what I'm made of when the person across from me already has me written down. Because that's what the platform is going to be, isn't it. Something in there is going to have me written down."

Lin Hao considered this. And then he drew the Stormbringer, because the answer was yes, and because the answer had been yes since the second sentence, and because there was a version of himself that he was slowly becoming — a version that answered requests like this one before the request was finished — and he was beginning to like that version.

They fought for nineteen minutes.

It was the longest fight of Lin Hao's life that was not with Wu Zhangkong, and it was long because Wang Jinxi did the thing he had never done, which was to stop being thorough. He fought ugly. He fought incomplete. He threw attacks he had not finished building and abandoned positions he had spent weeks perfecting, and Lin Hao — who had him written down, all of him, the whole careful architecture — found that the written-down version was useless against a boy who had decided to stop making sense.

Wang Jinxi did not win. He could not; the gap was what it was, and both of them knew it. But he landed four times, which was three more than the gauntlet, and on the nineteenth minute he got Lin Hao's sword outside his guard and put his palm flat against Lin Hao's chest, and held it there.

"There," Wang Jinxi said, breathing hard. "That's the thing I can't do. That's *reaching.* You're never where the ledger says."

"Neither are you," Lin Hao said, "anymore."

"No," Wang Jinxi agreed. He took his hand away. "Thank you." He started toward the dormitory, and then stopped, without turning around. "In the platform. If I find you."

"Yes."

"I'm going to want to be the one who finds you," Wang Jinxi said. "Not the other way around. So if you come looking for me, I'm going to be annoyed."

"Noted," Lin Hao said, and grinned at the back of the boy's head, and stood alone on the training ground with his ribs and his ledger and seventy-nine lines in it, and thought that in two weeks he was going to walk into a forest that did not know his name, and that this was the first time in four months he had no idea what was going to happen to him, and that he could not, entirely, wait.

## Part 5: The Steel

He made the sword on a Saturday, in the side forge, because there was a thing he had been carrying for eleven days and a forge was the only place he knew to put it down.

Mu Chen was there. Mu Chen was always there on a Saturday, and Mu Chen had asked, four months ago, to strike his edge himself, which was the highest thing a smith of the Eastsea Branch ever said to a boy, and which Lin Hao had allowed exactly once and then never mentioned again, because mentioning it would have made it a thing instead of a fact.

"Second rank," Mu Chen said, without looking up from his own work. It was not a question. It was the way the older man said everything, which was as though the words had already been agreed.

"Second rank," Lin Hao agreed.

"You passed at nine."

"I passed at nine."

"I passed at nineteen." Mu Chen turned the bar in his tongs, and looked at the colour, and did not take it out. "The Branch has four hundred years of records and there are eleven names under twenty in it. Wulin's is one now, and yours is one, and there are people in this building who have been fourth rank for thirty years and who will not look at either of you." He put the bar back in. "So whatever it is you're making, make it properly, because it's going to be looked at whether you like it or not."

Lin Hao made it properly.

He had a bar of spirit steel that his father had given him at eight and that he had been saving without being able to say why, and he had spent eleven days knowing why. The bar went into the fire and the hawk-eyes did the thing they did — not finding the flaws, which any competent smith could do by sound, but finding the *grain* first, the thousand-year weather of compression and direction inside the metal, the places where it wanted to move and the places where it would fight — and he struck only where it had already said yes.

one hundred and ninety-one strikes. A journeyman needs three hundred. A journeyman who cannot read needs five hundred and gets a worse bar.

**Thousand Refinements.** He had the number in his head the way other people have a tune: not a claim, a count. He had reached it at the Association a month ago and had not told anyone, because there had not been a reason, and because the reason had arrived eleven days ago in the shape of a stick and a stance and a stroke.

Because here was the thing nobody had told him, and which he had worked out alone at four in the morning above Zhou Zhangxi's snoring: **Thousand Refinements was a swordsmanship technique.** Not a metaphor. The refinement was the same act as the Answering Stroke — you did not impose a shape on the material, you waited for the material to commit and then you arrived at the place it had already promised. Wu Zhangkong's homework had been *ask yourself why a swordsman's whole curriculum is reading,* and the answer was standing in his hands in a side forge on a Saturday, because it had been standing in his hands since he was six years old and had never once been in a sword.

He had been a swordsman at the anvil for four years. He just hadn't had a name for it.

The blade came out of the oil at the colour of the harbour before rain, and Mu Chen looked at it for a long time, and then did the thing that Lin Hao would remember longer than the gauntlet, longer than the number, longer than almost anything: he picked it up, and turned it, and put it down again carefully, the way you put down something that belongs to someone else's future.

"That's not a second-rank blade," Mu Chen said.

"It's a second-rank smith's blade."

"No," Mu Chen said. "It's not." And he went back to his own work, and did not say anything else for an hour, and Lin Hao wrapped the sword in oilcloth and carried it back to the academy under his coat, and did not show it to anyone, not even Wulin, because there was going to be a forest in two weeks and he wanted the first thing it ever cut to matter.

Nine days later the growth came again, and it came the same way it always came — not from sitting, from being *corrected* — and by then he had stopped being surprised by it, and had started, instead, to notice the pattern, which was that his soul did not grow when he won. It grew when he found out he had been wrong.

The rank did not move. The rank could not move; there was a hawk in him at eight hundred and fifty-two years and a door at the top of the stairs that would not open for a bird, and he had made his peace with that in the fourth week. What moved instead was everything the rank could not hold — the spiritual power, the ledger, the reading, the sword. He was, he understood, filling up a vessel that had stopped getting bigger, and the pressure was starting to look for somewhere to go.

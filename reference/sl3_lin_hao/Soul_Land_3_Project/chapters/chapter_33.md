## Part 1: The Debrief

"Tell me about your experiences in the spirit ascension platform yesterday."

Wu Zhangkong stood at the lectern and said it without enthusiasm, to five students, two of whom — Wang Jinxi and Zhang Yangzi — had dark puffy eyes and had clearly not slept. Zhang Yangzi's was depression. Wang Jinxi's was nightmares: he could not get the chill of the Man-Faced Demon Spider out of him, the one that had reached, he said afterwards, to the depth of his soul. Xie Xie's complexion was no better; he had dreamed of wolves, one after another, and had fallen out of bed at some point in the night.

Gu Yue was exactly as usual. Tang Wulin was the only one in good spirits, because his spirit soul had evolved and he had not yet been told otherwise.

"We'll go in order of elimination," Wu Zhangkong said. "Zhang Yangzi. You first."

Nobody wanted to go first, so he had named one.

"My performance was horrible," Zhang Yangzi said, standing up. "Teacher Wu, I was wrong."

"I don't need you to be wrong." Wu Zhangkong waved it away. "It's your own life. What I need is for you to be *accurate.* Sit down and be accurate next time."

He went around the room. Each of them gave an account, and each account was, Wu Zhangkong let them discover, mostly about themselves. Then he reached the end of the list, and he did not move on.

"Tang Wulin."

Wulin stood up.

"If you had been able to kill the thousand-year Crystal Bear without being crystallised," Wu Zhangkong said, "then Xie Xie would not have had to throw himself into it, and Gu Yue would not have had to burn herself out holding the far side, and the four of you would have walked out of that wolf pack without a scratch." He let that sit. "Their sacrifices were because of you."

Wulin's face did the thing it did — not crumbling, exactly. Settling. He had been proud of the result all the way home.

"Yes, teacher."

"Don't agree with me yet, you haven't heard the rest." Wu Zhangkong's voice did not rise; it never rose. "In a real fight that is not a bad afternoon. That is a dead team. You were crystallised from the ankle up and you were still standing there deciding what to do about it, and by the time you decided, two people had already spent themselves buying you the time." He tapped the lectern once. "You need to strengthen your knowledge and understanding of soul beasts. Don't think that because soul beasts are rare now, studying them is useless. **Over ninety percent of the soul skills we Soul Masters possess belonged to a soul beast at some point.** Understanding soul beasts is understanding your competitors. It is also the only thing that will keep you alive on that platform."

Then he turned, and Lin Hao understood — one full second before it happened, which was not comfort — that the turn had been for him.

"And you."

Lin Hao stood up.

"You were there," Wu Zhangkong said. "You were the fourth. You are the one in that forest who reads a situation, so I am going to ask you a question and I want the accurate answer, not the loyal one." He folded his hands. "Whose fault was it?"

The room went very still. Xie Xie, who had been about to say something, decided against it.

Wulin opened his mouth.

"He'll answer," Wu Zhangkong said, "or you'll answer for him, and then it's your answer and not his."

Lin Hao thought about it. He thought about it honestly, which took longer than he would have liked, because the honest answer was not the one that made him feel like a good friend.

"Mine," he said.

Gu Yue's head came up.

"I read the bear," Lin Hao said. "I read it early — before the first strike, I knew it was doing something with the cold, I knew the cold was *staying* on people rather than hurting them, which is a different thing, and I said 'it's building' and I did not say 'get out.' I had the information and I gave them a description instead of an order. Wulin got crystallised because I told him something interesting instead of telling him something useful." He stopped. "That's the accurate answer. It isn't the whole of it — he still walked into it — but it's the part that was mine."

There was a silence of the kind Lin Hao had learned to file.

"Sit down," Wu Zhangkong said.

Lin Hao sat down.

"Two things," Wu Zhangkong said, to the room, in the flat voice he used when he was giving something away. "Wulin: you are not the only reason anyone got hurt, and I am not going to let you carry a whole team's blood because it's simpler than carrying your third of it. Lin Hao: you have just discovered that being right early is worth nothing if you say it in a shape nobody can act on. A reading is not a warning. **A warning is a sentence that tells somebody what to do.**" He picked up his book. "Longer you survive on that platform, greater the benefit. You all have a long way to go. Morning lesson today is the soul beasts you met yesterday. Every trait, every level. Bring something written."

## Part 2: Show Me

"Do it again," Wu Zhangkong said.

"I've told you—"

"I know what you've told me. Do it anyway."

So Lin Hao did it anyway, in the empty training ground at six in the morning, with the dew still on the boards and a straw dummy standing where a wolf had been eleven days ago. He drew. He settled. He waited for the thing that had happened in the forest — the one-handed backhand off the wrong shoulder at an angle no form contained, the stroke that had come out of him when the correct grip was a third of a second away and he had not had a third of a second.

He produced a backhand.

It was a fine backhand. It was in a form. It had a name, in the sense that a movement you have done before has a name. The straw dummy lost a corner, which was roughly what a good backhand should do to a straw dummy, and Wu Zhangkong watched the whole of it without moving, and then said, "That was not it."

"No."

"What was different."

"I was doing it."

Wu Zhangkong was quiet for a moment.

"Say that again."

"I was *doing* it," Lin Hao said, and he was annoyed, because he had said it out loud in order to hear it and hearing it had not helped. "In the forest I wasn't doing anything. I was too busy. There were twelve of them and Xie Xie was on the ground and I had a leg that was going and I did not at any point decide to swing my sword, and it swung. And now I've decided, and it won't."

"How many times have you tried."

"Since the forest? Forty-one."

"And."

"And I've got forty-one backhands and a sore shoulder." Lin Hao put the sword down, because holding it while saying this felt like lying. "I've tried it tired. I've tried it angry. I've tried it at four in the morning and after eating and before eating and once when Wulin was shouting at me, which I thought was the most promising condition. Nothing. It's not a technique. I've been treating it like a technique I forgot, and it isn't, and I don't know what it is."

Wu Zhangkong looked at the straw dummy for a while.

"Sit down," he said.

---

Wulin was in the yard when he came out, sitting on the low wall with a whetstone and a hammer head, which meant he had chosen the yard rather than the shed, and Lin Hao had learned by then that Wulin chose places the way other people chose sentences.

"You did the thing again," Wulin said, not looking up from the stone.

"I don't know what the thing is."

"I know." Wulin turned the hammer head over. "That's what I mean. You've done it three times and you still don't know what it is, and you're going to go and ask him, and he's going to give you half an answer, and you're going to spend a week on it." He looked up. "Come and eat afterwards. I'm not asking you to talk about it. I'm asking you to eat."

"Both."

"What?"

"Both. I'll eat and I'll talk, because I've been doing the not-talking thing for a fortnight and it turns out I'm bad at it." Lin Hao sat down on the wall beside him. "It reaches. That's all I know. It goes at things I can't get to."

Wulin was quiet for a while, running the stone along the edge.

"That's not frightening," he said, at last.

"It's extremely frightening."

"No." Wulin tested the edge with his thumb. "It's just the first thing you've ever had that you can't read. You've read everything else your whole life — me, the steel, the wind, every person who ever stood in front of you. This is the first thing that doesn't let you." He put the stone down. "That's not a monster. That's just the first thing you've met that's as stubborn as you are."

## Part 3: The Place He Did Not Name

They sat on the bench by the weapon rack, which was not a thing Wu Zhangkong did, and Lin Hao noticed it and did not say so, because noticing things and saying them had gotten him into enough trouble.

"I had a sword once," Wu Zhangkong said, "that I could not put down."

Lin Hao waited.

"I was nineteen and I was the best swordsman in a city full of people who had been training since they could walk, and I was told — by people who were correct, at the time, about everything — that I would be the best of my generation." He turned his hands over on his knees. They were enormous hands and completely still. "And I was. For about six years. And then I was not, because a boy came through who was seventeen and had been taught by somebody better than anybody I had ever met, and he beat me in eleven seconds, and I did something about it that I am not going to describe to a ten-year-old."

"Was that why—"

"That was one of the reasons." Wu Zhangkong said it flatly, the way he said everything, and it landed anyway. "There is a place I came from. I am not going to tell you where it is, and I want to be clear that this is not a test and not a lesson and not me being difficult. I am not going to tell you because I was thrown out of it, and because the version of the story I would tell you is the version where I was wronged, and I have had thirty years to notice that I keep reaching for that version, and I do not trust myself to tell it accurately."

Lin Hao thought about that for a while.

"That's the most honest thing anybody's ever said to me," he said, "and it's the second time this month, and both times it was you."

"I have had a great deal of practice at being honest about the wrong things." Wu Zhangkong stood up. "The reason I am telling you any of it is that you have a sword in your hand that you cannot put down either, and you are ten, and you are going the same direction I went, and the direction is not a mistake — it is where people like us go — but there is a turn in it, and I would like you to arrive at the turn with somebody standing next to you."

## Part 4: The Wrong Question

They went back to the dummy.

"You have been asking the wrong question for forty-one attempts," Wu Zhangkong said. "You have been asking how to do it. The stroke is not a thing you do."

"Then what is it."

"It is what is left when doing runs out." Wu Zhangkong picked up the pointing stick, and did not use it. "Watch my arm." He held it out, relaxed. "I can move this. I can decide to move it and move it, and you can see the decision in the shoulder a half-beat before the hand goes, and every swordsman alive can read that, because deciding is a *movement* and movements are visible. That is what a form is. A form is a decision you made earlier and stored."

"And the stroke has no decision in it."

"The stroke has no decision in it, which is why it cannot be read, which is why it worked on twelve animals that had spent a minute learning everything else you did." Wu Zhangkong lowered the stick. "So. You cannot summon it, because summoning is deciding, and the moment you decide it is not the stroke any more. Forty-one attempts. You were never going to get there that way. You were standing in a room trying to leave through the wall you came in through."

Lin Hao sat with it.

"That's not a solution," he said, at last. "That's a diagnosis. You've told me why I can't. You haven't told me how."

"No," Wu Zhangkong agreed. "Because there isn't a how. There is a *condition.*" He put the stick back in the rack. "You produced it once, under a specific set of circumstances: no time, no plan, no option, and somebody behind you. You have spent eleven days trying to reproduce it in a training ground with a straw dummy and a scheduled hour, which is the opposite of every one of those conditions, and you are surprised."

"So I need to be nearly killed."

"You need to not be in charge." Wu Zhangkong said it the way he said the weather. "Which is the hardest thing in the world for a person like you, and I want to be precise about which person I mean. Not a swordsman. A *reader.* You have spent four years being the person in the room who knows what is going to happen, and the stroke only comes when you do not. So the work is not sword work. The work is learning to be the one who does not know, on purpose, while staying alive."

"That's not a technique."

"I never said it was." Wu Zhangkong turned to go, and stopped. "Your homework was a style and a first form, and you gave me both. Here is the second one. **Find me a stroke you cannot name, and teach it to Xie Xie.**"

"...What?"

"Teach it to him. If you can put it in words, it is a technique, and you will be able to do it whenever you like. If you cannot put it in words, you will find that out in front of a witness, which is where you need to find it out." He left, and the dew was going off the boards, and Lin Hao stood in an empty training ground with a straw dummy that had lost a corner.

## Part 5: Teaching

Xie Xie came at seven, because Xie Xie came at seven, and listened to the whole explanation with his mouth open, and then said, "So you have to teach me a thing you can't do."

"Yes."

"That's the stupidest assignment I've ever heard and I've been taught by Ye Yingluo." He cracked his knuckles. "Right. Show me."

Lin Hao showed him the backhand.

"That's a backhand."

"That's the backhand."

"Do the other one."

"I can't."

"Do the other one *slowly.*"

Lin Hao did the other one slowly, and it was a backhand, and Xie Xie watched it four times and then said something that turned out to be the most useful thing anybody said to him all week.

"You keep looking at the dummy."

"What?"

"When you do the real one — in the forest, you said you weren't doing anything — were you looking at the wolf?"

Lin Hao opened his mouth, and closed it.

"No," he said. "I was looking at Xie Xie."

Xie Xie blinked. "Me?"

"You were on the ground. I was between you and the rest of them. I wasn't watching the wolf at all, I was watching whether you were going to get up, and the wolf was — it was somewhere near me, and I knew roughly where, and I did the thing." He stopped. "I've been trying to reproduce it facing a target for forty-one attempts. There was no target. There was never a target. There was a person behind me and a shape in front of me and I wasn't *looking* at the shape."

"That," Xie Xie said, "is either the most profound thing you've ever said or you hit a wolf by accident."

"Both, probably."

"Right. Well." Xie Xie stood up and walked round to the other side of the dummy, and sat down on the boards with his back against it, and looked up. "Then don't look at it. Look at me."

---

It took until the fourth day, and it did not work, and then it half-worked, and the half that worked was not a stroke.

What worked was this: Lin Hao stood with his back to the dummy and his eyes on Xie Xie, and talked to him — about nothing, about the list, about whether Mu Chen would take an apprentice who was rude — and let the sword hang, and did not decide anything at all. And somewhere in the middle of a sentence about the price of spirit steel, his arm moved, and the dummy lost its head, and neither of them had seen it happen.

Xie Xie made a noise.

"That was it," he said. "That was absolutely it. Do it again."

"I can't."

"I *know* you can't, that's the whole thing, but—" Xie Xie got up and looked at the headless dummy, and then at Lin Hao, with an expression that was not often on his face. "You weren't doing it. You were talking about steel. And your arm did it."

"Yes."

"So the sword knows something you don't."

Lin Hao looked at the sword.

"No," he said, slowly, because he was working it out as he said it and it was important not to say the wrong thing to a boy who would remember it. "The sword doesn't know anything. The sword is a bar. *I* know something I don't know — I've known how to do it for four years, the same way I know how to walk, and the knowing is under the part of me that decides things, and every time I go looking for it I send the deciding part, and the deciding part can't see it, the same way you can't see your own eye without a mirror." He put the sword away. "So I can't teach it to you. That's the answer to the homework. I can't teach it, because there's nothing to teach, because it isn't knowledge, it's the thing that happens when knowledge stops being consulted."

"You're going to tell Wu Zhangkong that."

"I'm going to tell Wu Zhangkong that."

"He's going to say he knew."

"He's absolutely going to say he knew," Lin Hao agreed, "and he's going to be wrong, because he thought it was about not being in charge, and it's about not being in charge *of the right thing* — you can stop deciding and still be looking at the target, and I did that for four days. The condition isn't no control. It's no *audience.*" He looked at Xie Xie. "You were the only person who could have got me there, and you got me there by sitting on the floor and being boring. I want that noted."

"I'm not boring."

"You're the least boring person in this city and you were boring on purpose for four days, which is the greatest thing anyone's ever done for me, and I want you to know I noticed, and I'm never going to say it again."

## Part 6: The City He Did Not Name

Wu Zhangkong listened to the whole of it with his arms folded, in the yard, at the end of the week.

"You cannot teach it," Lin Hao said. "Because there's nothing to teach. It isn't knowledge. It's what happens when knowledge stops being consulted, and the condition isn't lack of control, it's lack of an *audience* — I spent four days not deciding things and staring at a target, and nothing happened, and then Xie Xie sat on the floor and talked to me about steel and my arm took a straw dummy's head off in the middle of a sentence about spirit-steel prices."

Wu Zhangkong was quiet for a while.

"You're going to say you knew," Lin Hao said.

"I was going to." Wu Zhangkong unfolded his arms. "I won't, because I didn't. I told you it was about not being in charge. That was a swordsman's answer and it was about half right, and the half I was missing is that a swordsman is always performing, even alone, because a sword is a thing you hold in front of you." He looked at the sword at Lin Hao's hip. "A reader has a worse version of the problem. You do not perform with the sword. You perform with the *eyes.* You have been watched your entire life — by your father, by an association, by a director, by a man with a card in his pocket — and you learned very young that being watched was the same as being safe, so you cannot stop being watched even by yourself."

"That's—"

"It's not a criticism. It's the reason you're good." Wu Zhangkong said it flatly. "The eyes are why you can do everything else. And they are also the thing that will not let you do this one thing. That's not a flaw to fix. That's a trade you've already made, and it was a good trade, and you will pay for it in exactly one place, for the rest of your life."

Lin Hao thought about that for most of the walk back.

At the gate Wu Zhangkong stopped, and said the thing he had plainly been carrying since the bench.

"When you can do the stroke on purpose — and you will, in ten years or twenty, because you are the sort of person who eventually does — you will be better than I ever was. Better than the boy who beat me in eleven seconds. Better than most of the people who threw me out." He did not look at him. "There is a city I was thrown out of. I am not going to name it, and I have told you why. But I am going to tell you one thing about it, because you will hear the name eventually and I would rather you heard it from me with this attached."

"Yes."

"They are not wrong about most things," Wu Zhangkong said. "That's the part nobody tells you about places like that. They are not a cabal of villains. They are correct, and rigorous, and they produce the best fighters on the continent, and they are *ruthless about the correct thing* — and the day they are ruthless about you, you will understand every reason they gave and hate them anyway." He went through the gate. "And if you ever get the chance to go there, you should take it, and you should not let them make you into me."

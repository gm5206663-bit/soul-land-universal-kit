## Part 1: What They Found

Elder Duan sent a man, and the man spent two days, and the man came back with a folder, and the folder was thin.

That was the first thing.

Lin Hao heard about it afterwards, from Mu Xi, because Mu Xi had spent two days in a smithy with him and had decided that made her the person who told him things, and because she had resented him for the right reason and stopped resenting him at about the same time she started telling him things, and neither had been planned.

"They investigated you," she said.

"Who."

"The Skysea Blacksmith's Association. Elder Duan. He wants to know if you can be taken from Eastsea." Mu Xi said it flatly, the way she said everything now that she had stopped performing. "They sent a man for two days. He came back with a folder."

"How thin was it."

Mu Xi looked at him.

"How did you know it was thin."

"Because my father makes horseshoes," Lin Hao said.

## Part 2: What The Folder Said

Lin Wei made horseshoes.

That was the whole of it. He had a forge in Glorybound City with a sign two different people had repainted, and he made horseshoes, and he made them well, and he had made them for nineteen years, and he had a son with three purple rings who had hit a bar of coppertite eighty-one times in front of a full hall.

That was the folder.

Lin Hao had thought about this off and on for four months, and he had arrived at a position, and the position was that he did not mind. His father made horseshoes. His father had put a hammer in his hand at six and explained nothing, and had said *don't get ahead of yourself, martial souls are unpredictable*, and had been right, and had never once said anything about being proud.

That was the folder.

And Lin Hao stood in a smithy in Skysea City and understood that the thin folder was the most useful thing that could have happened to him, because a thin folder meant there was nothing to take, and nothing to take meant there was nothing to offer him, and nothing to offer him meant that whatever happened next would happen because of what he did and not because of what he was.

## Part 3: What Elder Duan Said

He came to the smithy himself, on the ninth day, which was not a thing a man with a thin folder usually did.

He was old. He had white hair and a smile that had been used a great many times, and he stood in the doorway where Mu Xi had stood, and he did not stand there for eleven seconds.

"Lin Hao," he said.

"Yes."

"You hit a bar of blue coppertite eighty-one times."

"Yes."

"I've been a fifth rank twenty-two years," Elder Duan said. "I'm not upset. I've been sitting with not being upset about six minutes, which is longer than the last man managed." He turned a bar on the anvil and did not look at him. "I want you to know I'm telling you that on purpose."

"That's three people now," Lin Hao said.

"A personal record for each of us." Elder Duan smiled the used smile. "I've looked into you. Your father makes horseshoes."

"Yes."

"In Glorybound City."

"Yes."

"That's a thin folder."

"Yes."

"I want to offer you a place in the Skysea Association," Elder Duan said. "Resources. A forge of your own. A teacher at fifth rank. Everything Eastsea has given you and a good deal more. I'm offering it rather than waiting for you to ask, because I've decided you work things out fast, and I'd rather say it first."

Lin Hao set the hammer down.

"No," he said.

Elder Duan's smile did not change, which was how Lin Hao knew it had been a used smile.

"You haven't heard what I'm offering."

"I know what you're offering," Lin Hao said. "You're offering me a thing before I can hold it. I've been offered three things before I could hold them in three weeks, and there's a pattern, and the pattern is that the people offering me things are the ones who've already decided what I am." He stopped. "You investigated me two days and came back with a thin folder, and you're offering me a fifth-rank teacher, and you haven't once asked me what I'm trying to do."

Elder Duan was quiet a moment.

"What are you trying to do."

"I don't know," Lin Hao said. "That's the fourth time I've said it, and it's the true answer. You're the first person who's asked. You asked after I said no. I'd like to have been asked before."

## Part 4: What Ye Xinglan Did

Her sword was called the Stargod Sword, and it came up with two yellow rings, and there were sparks of light along the yellow blade like stars in a night sky, and it was the most beautiful thing Lin Hao had seen in Skysea City, and he understood in about two seconds that it was going to be the hardest thing he had fought.

She saw his rings.

He watched her see them, because he noticed things, and because he had learned that watching a person recalculate was the most informative thing you could watch.

Three purple rings.

Her face did something, and then it did not do anything, because she was a Shrek student and her confidence was not a thing a set of rings could move, and she had decided that before the match and the decision did not change.

"That's a thousand-year configuration," she said.

"Yes."

"To support a thousand-year ring your body and spirit both have to be powerful." She said it the way you say a thing you have worked out and are not impressed by. "There's no other explanation. You're more talented than I gave you credit for."

"Thank you."

"That wasn't a compliment."

"I know," Lin Hao said. "I took it as one anyway."

## Part 5: What The Track Did

She was fast.

Not Xie Xie fast. Xie Xie was fast the way a thing is fast when it has decided where it is going. Ye Xinglan was fast the way a thing is fast when it has decided where it is going *and can change its mind*, and the Stargod Sword had a reach Lin Hao had not accounted for, and the Track put him four metres left of where she had been, and she was not there any more.

He used the Track four times.

She was not there four times.

Then he stopped using it, because Wu Zhangkong had taught him that a thing you use four times is a thing your opponent has now seen four times, and the fifth time she would be where he was going to be. The Track was a decision about where he was going to be, made before he moved, and the deciding was the part that mattered, and he had been using it as a movement, and it was not a movement.

The fifth time, he did not decide where to go.

He decided where *she* was going to be.

He could not afterwards give a good account of it, and that troubled him, because he was accurate about everything else. What happened was that he stopped deciding about himself and started deciding about her, and the Track happened anyway, and he was four metres left of where she was going to be rather than where she had been, and the Stargod Sword went into the place he had been, and the sword that had been the most beautiful thing in Skysea City was in the wrong place.

He won in thirty-one seconds.

Ye Xinglan stood in the ring with her sword down and her confidence entirely intact, which was the most impressive thing he had seen in Skysea City, and she said, "That's not a Track," and Lin Hao said, "No," and she said, "What is it," and he said, "I don't know," and she said, "That's the fourth time you've said that," and he said, "I know."

## Part 6: What Wulin Said

He found Wulin on the seawall again, because the seawall was where Wulin went, and because Lin Hao had started to understand that going to find somebody was itself a thing worth doing and he had not done it enough.

"They investigated you," Wulin said.

"They investigated me."

"I know. Mu Xi told me." Wulin was eating dried fish. "She's decided she's the person who tells people things now. She resented you four days and now she tells me things, and I haven't worked out how both of those are true."

"Neither have I."

"That's the fourth time somebody's said that to me this week." Wulin ate the dried fish. "I noticed that, too."

Lin Hao sat down.

"Thirty-five," he said.

"What."

"I'm thirty-five. It happened twenty minutes ago, in the ring, after the fifth time I used the Track and it wasn't a movement. I haven't told anybody." He stopped. "I'm telling you first. I didn't plan to."

Wulin was quiet a moment.

"That's the nicest thing anybody's said to me all week," he said. "You taught me that saying a thing is the same act as thinking it, and I've stopped noticing when I do it, and the not-noticing is the part that scares me a little, and I'm telling you that too."

Lin Hao looked at him.

"I'm glad you're here," he said.

"That's the eighth time."

"I know."

## Part 6-B: The Form

The Association had a form.

It was not a large form. It was one page, because the Eastsea Branch had used the same one for forty years, and it asked for a name and an age and a martial soul, and then — under a ruled heading that said **SYSTEM** — it offered six small empty squares in a row, each with a word printed underneath in the flat grey type the Federation used for things it did not expect anybody to read.

**Assault. Agility. Power. Control. Defense. Auxiliary.**

And under the sixth square there was a blank line, because forty years ago somebody had thought to leave one, and nobody had ever used it, and nobody had ever thought to take it away.

Lin Hao sat at the desk with a pen in his hand for a long time.

The clerk had been pleasant about it. The clerk had said *"most people know before they sit down,"* and had gone to fetch a stamp. And Lin Hao had looked at the six squares and done the thing he did, which was go through them one at a time, honestly.

**Assault.** Yes. He had ended a fight in nine seconds on a stage in front of four thousand people.
**Agility.** Yes. The Track.
**Power.** His fist was past the machine.
**Control.** He had held a man's blade with wind for four seconds and the man had not been able to move it.
**Defense.** He had stood in the path of twelve wolves and walked out.
**Auxiliary.** He had spent nine weeks teaching six people to be better at themselves.

Six squares. And he understood, sitting there, that the form was not asking which of these he was *good at*. It was asking which of these he was *for*. Which one he belonged to. Which one would go on the rest of the paperwork and decide who he was put in a team with, and be the thing a stranger said about him in one breath at a bar in a city he had never been to.

Xie Xie was **Agility**. You could say it in a word and it was true and it was *his*.
Gu Yue was—

And he stopped, because that was the thing that undid the whole of it.

**Gu Yue was an Elementalist.** That was what everybody said, and had said since the second week, and what was on her form, and what the roster said, and what Long Hengxu had written in his notes about the tournament. And **Elementalist was not one of the six.** It was not on the form. There was no square for it. Somebody had looked at a girl with six elements and no System and written a word in the blank line, and the word had gone into the roster, and from that day forward every adult in that academy had described her with a category **that did not exist.**

Nobody had argued about it. Nobody had checked. Nobody had ever once said *there is no such thing as an Elementalist* — because there was now, because it was on a form, and because forms are how a thing becomes real.

Lin Hao looked at the blank line under the sixth square.

And he thought: *well.*

He wrote in it.

He wrote it in the same hand he wrote everything in, small and even and slightly slanted, and he did not think very hard about the word, because the word was obvious, and because he was ten years old and tired and had a tournament in four days and had already spent four minutes on a piece of paper most people filled in standing up.

**Comprehensive.**

Then he handed it back.

The clerk read the name. Read the age, and looked up for a moment, and read the age again. Read the martial soul. And then came to the SYSTEM line, and read the word in the blank, and did not do anything at all.

"Right," the clerk said, and stamped it.

That was the whole of it.

Nobody checked. Nobody had ever checked anybody's. Gu Yue had written **Elementalist** and it had been true ever since, and Xie Xie had written **Agility** without reading the squares because he already knew, and somewhere in this building there was a forty-year-old form with a word on it that no book contained, and the word was still in use.

Lin Hao walked out of the Eastsea Branch of the Blacksmith's Association at about half past four on a Tuesday, having invented a category of soul master in the space of eleven seconds, and did not know he had done it, and would not know for a very long time, and nobody would tell him — because you cannot congratulate a person on founding something when the founding was a word on a form that nobody read.

## Part 7: What Lin Hao Wrote

That night, in the room that smelled of the sea, he took the book down and wrote the number first, because the number never asked anything of him.

*Two hundred and fifty-six.* It had been two hundred and forty-nine. Eight points, somewhere between saying no to a fifth-rank teacher and using the Track five times and stopping using it as a movement. He could not say which had done it. He had written that three times now, and the writing-it-three-times was the only honest part.

*One thousand three hundred and eighty-two.*

*There's a blank line under the six squares on the Association's form and I wrote a word in it and the man stamped it and that was the whole of it.*

*Gu Yue is an Elementalist. I've known that a year. I've said it myself. It's on her form and on the roster and in Long Hengxu's notes, and there's no square for it and there never was, and somebody wrote a word in a blank line and the word became true, and not one adult in that building has ever questioned it.* So I wrote a word in a blank line. *Comprehensive.* It seemed the honest thing. It took eleven seconds. The clerk said *right.* I've thought about it twice since.

*I'm not sorry. I'm also lonely, and the reason isn't that I have no category, because I've just been given one. It's that Xie Xie has a word and everybody knows what it means, and Gu Yue has a word and everybody knows what it means, and I have a word I made up four hours ago and nobody knows what it means, including me.*

*This is the first time I've ever made something that wasn't a stroke.*

*My father makes horseshoes. That's the folder. I've thought about it four months and the position I've arrived at is that I don't mind.*

*Elder Duan investigated me two days and came back with a thin folder and offered me a fifth-rank teacher, and he'd already decided what I am, and I said no, and he asked what I'm trying to do after I said no, and I'd like to have been asked before.*

*The Track is not a movement. I used it four times as a movement and she wasn't there four times, and the fifth time I stopped deciding where to go and started deciding where she was going to be, and it happened anyway, and I don't have an account of it.*

And then the line at the bottom, which he wrote last because it was the one he had been circling all week.

*I have said I don't know five times now. Wu Zhangkong has said it twice about me. Three men have told me they're not upset. A man with a thin folder offered me a thing before I could hold it and I said no.*

*Every person who's offered me a thing has already decided what I am. Every person who's asked me a question has asked after I said no. I've started to suspect the thing I am isn't a thing anybody can decide. This is the closest I've come to being frightened, and I'm not frightened, and I'd like to know why I'm not.*

He closed the book.

The lock at his temple lay gold across his cheekbone, and the marks on his forearms were raised and had been raised for thirty-four days, and the hawk turned over in him — *one thousand three hundred and eighty-two* — and did not pull toward anything.

Lin Hao lay on his back in a city that smelled of salt and hot metal, and thought about a father who made horseshoes, and about a thin folder, and about the fact that the thin folder was the most useful thing that had happened to him, and about the fact that he was not frightened.

He slept.

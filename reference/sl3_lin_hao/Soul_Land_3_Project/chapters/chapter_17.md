## Part 1: I Have a Dragon in My Body?

The question had been walking around inside Tang Wulin for nine days before he said it out loud, and when it finally came out, in Wu Zhangkong's office, with the winter light lying across the desk like a ruled page, it came out exactly the way a ten-year-old says the largest thing he owns:

"Teacher Wu. I think — I think I have a dragon in my body."

Wu Zhangkong did not look up from the paper he was writing. "What makes you say that."

Not *don't be absurd.* Not *what nonsense.* The flat grammar of the question was its own kind of answer, and Wulin — who had rehearsed this speech for nine days and lost the whole of it in the doorway — simply held out his right arm and let the gold answer for him. It came when he called it now, which was new: a single scale rising through the skin of his wrist like a coin through water, holding the lamplight the way coals hold heat. And beside him, because a brother does not stand outside a door like this one alone, Lin Hao watched their teacher's brush stop.

"The scales started small. Months ago. One, then gone." Wulin's voice had gone into the register he kept for forge-true things. "Then the lane. My eyes went — I don't know what they went. The man's arm broke like it wanted to break. And at the tournament, when I stopped my fist next to Wang Jinxi—" he flexed the hand, the one that had not entirely stopped singing, "—something in me *answered his soul,* Teacher. His dragon leaned at me like — like it knew me. Grass doesn't do that. Goldsong doesn't do that. So. I have a dragon in my body."

The brush stayed stopped for a long moment. Then Wu Zhangkong set it down, and looked at Xie Xie — who had come because Xie Xie went where the interesting went, and was for once in his life entirely silent — and at Lin Hao, and his smoky green eyes took the inventory of the room the way they took the inventory of a sparring circle: exits, threats, and students.

"What I am about to say leaves this room in none of you," he said. "Three facts. First: I do not know what it is. I have watched you since you were a Bluesilver Grass boy with a hammer, and I have seen it now with my own eyes — and heard the rest of it from a boy who does not lie to me — and I do not know what it is. Anyone who tells you they know is selling you something." He turned the paper he had been writing face down. "Second: it is powerful. The things that break men's arms at your age are not martial souls. Martial souls wait for your cultivation. This does not wait. It grows on its own schedule, it answers to feeling before it answers to you, and one day it will be stronger than you are — that is not a fear, it is arithmetic." A beat. "Third, and the only one that matters: power is not identity. A dragon in your body does not make you a dragon. A hammer in your hand does not make you a hammer." His eyes went to Linhao's sheathed sword, and back. "You are the one who decides what the thing inside you is *for.* Every day. That is the whole of cultivation, and the whole of being a man, and adults fail at it constantly. Control it — or be controlled. There is no third option that lasts."

"And if I can't control it?" Wulin asked, quiet. "If it comes out when I'm — when I'm not me?"

"Then you will owe someone an apology, and they will owe you a broken arm, and you will both be older." Wu Zhangkong picked up his brush again, which was, class five had learned, the interview over. And then, without looking up, in the coldest voice that was somehow also the opposite of cold: "But you have a brother who hears your spiral change pitch in a dark room. And a captain's sense. And — despite everything — a habit of stopping your fist one inch short." The brush moved. "Go and train the stopping. The rest of it is not yours yet."

Out in the corridor, Xie Xie exhaled like a kettle. "That was the most terrifying speech I have ever been included in."

"He told you how to live," Lin Hao said, "and then he made it homework." He was smiling a little, but his eyes were somewhere else — on the file, the shelf, the fourth nail of light where no martial soul grew, the reading his teacher had taken and never once spoken of again. *Three facts,* he thought. *He gave you three facts and kept four.*

He did not say that out loud either. The shelf was getting very good company.

## Part 2

The marks on his forearms were still coming and going at that point, faint in the morning and darker after work, and he had taken to wearing long sleeves at the Association without anyone asking him to.
: The Letter

It came four days later, in the evening post, in a hand like roots growing downhill.

Wulin read it twice in the lamplight of room eleven, and the second time his thumb went — Lin Hao saw it — to the underside of his wrist, where the gold lived.

"Old Tang?"

"Old Tang." Wulin folded the letter with the care he folded everything. "He wants me home. Glorybound, as soon as the academy releases me." He was quiet a moment. "He doesn't say why. Old Tang never says why. He says—" and here Wulin's voice did the thing it did when it was holding both a smile and a worry, "—he says, 'the water in your cup is rising, and you are drinking from it like a man who has never seen a well. Come home. There are things a spiritual guide must see with his own eyes, and things a boy must swallow that taste worse than they are.' That's all. That's the whole letter."

*A spiritual guide.* Lin Hao let the two words sit. Old Tang — the presence that had lived in Wulin's life the way weather lives in a valley, never named in any official anything, never explained, known to the family the way the family knew gravity — was asking for the boy. And Lin Hao, who had spent a year learning which silences in his brother were empty and which were full, had no trouble at all identifying this one. This silence was full. Something was happening in Wulin — the scales, the singing hand, the answer to the dragon's soul — and whatever Old Tang was, he had felt it from Glorybound City, the way you feel a storm change the well-water before the storm arrives.

"I'll go," Wulin said, to the room, to himself, deciding. "Teacher Wu will release me; I'll ask tomorrow. It's — I don't know how long. Weeks, he says. The Association keeps my Fridays; Mu Chen will curse me and keep them." He looked at Lin Hao, and the worry and the smile both came up into his face together, which was the face Lin Hao had known since they were six. "Hao. The class — the rematch talk, the platform sessions, whatever's coming — you hold it. All of it. You're the one who reads first."

"You'll miss my readings. You'll have to think for yourself."

"I've been thinking for myself since I was nine, you *barometer,* I just let you enjoy—" The argument went on for twenty minutes, which is what arguments between brothers sound like when both of them are, underneath, saying *come back whole.*

He left on the morning boat, and Lin Hao stood on the seawall and watched the ferry shrink, and gave him the thing he had made.

It was small. That was the point. A band for the wrist — forged over three Fridays from the offcuts of his own commissions, spirit steel folded eleven times, which was four more folds than the work needed and exactly the number his hands had wanted to give it. No ornament. No gem. Just the steel, dark as a harbor at night, with one hairline seam running its circuit that caught the light like a held breath — a deliberate seam, a *told* one, the way a smith signs a blade for the one person who will read it.

"It remembers its shape," Lin Hao had said, closing it around his brother's wrist — the left one; the right one belonged to the file. "Eleven folds. If you ever forget what you're made of — and everyone forgets, that's what the furnace weeks are for — you hold this and you remember that something folded you eleven times on purpose."

Wulin had not said anything clever. Wulin had hugged him like a vise and nearly cracked a rib, which is what Wulin did instead.

On the far water, the hawk watched the boat go south with an attention Lin Hao felt in his own sternum — not the stillness it kept for the gold, not the preening it kept for him; something older, the way a bird watches weather it has crossed before. He filed that too. He was running out of shelf, and beginning, without deciding to, to suspect that this was what his life *was.*

## Part 3: The Second Ring

Wulin had been gone eleven days when Xie Xie broke.

And the reason it broke in a yard instead of in a room, where nobody would have heard it, was that Lin Hao had been crossing that yard at that hour every day for eleven days, because Xie Xie trained at that hour and Lin Hao had started crossing the yard at that hour without ever deciding to.

**In a yard without him, Xie Xie breaks alone, in a room, at night, and comes back the next morning with the second ring and a face that does not match, and nobody ever knows what it cost.** That is what happens in a story where nobody is watching. Xie Xie is a person who performs, and a person who performs does not break in front of an audience, so in a yard without him the breaking goes underground and stays there.

**In a yard with him in it, Xie Xie breaks in front of the one person in the grade who has never once needed him to perform.** And that is the whole butterfly — not that Lin Hao did anything. He did not do anything. He was crossing a yard. **But he had been crossing it at that hour for eleven days without deciding to, and Xie Xie broke where somebody could hear it, and that is the only reason anybody ever knew.**

Lin Hao heard it before Xie Xie knew it — the way he had heard rank fourteen in a dark room months ago, because the ears that read spirals do not clock out. He was crossing the yard after the Association shift when the air over the training ground went *taut,* the particular tautness of a vessel a hand's width from its limit, and he was already walking faster, because he knew whose spiral that was, and he knew — the way the smith knows the color a bar throws the quarter-second before it cracks — that the dagger-boy was on the far side of rank twenty and climbing a wall with his bare hands.

He arrived as the second ring came up out of the ground.

It is not in the books, because almost no one is standing close enough to a soul when its rank-wall breaks. The yellow ring that had been under Xie Xie's feet since enrollment — the hundred-year soul his family's money had bought, the single ring that had capped him at rank twenty for a year and made him vicious about it — stayed exactly what it was. Rings do not change their color; that is the first law of them. The color is the soul's age on the day they met, and it stays that way forever. But *under* the old ring, new light was gathering, climbing — and then it set, the way poured steel sets, and the yard had a two-ring Soul Grandmaster in it, age ten, and the air let go of its tautness all at once, and Xie Xie stood in the middle of it all with his mouth open.

And the Light Dragon Dagger in his hand — released, singing, spinning its thin bright note — grew one circle smaller and one degree more *solid,* the lines along the blade sharpening into something that stopped looking like a weapon and started looking like a *fact.*

"My soul," he said. "It's *denser.* Father bought it at a hundred years — a hundred years, do you know what that cost? — and I've been swinging it at everything that moved for a year, and it got *denser.* The ring came thick. Do souls *do* that?"

"They do when they're fed," Lin Hao said. He was grinning; he had not decided to start. "Nine weeks of special training, three tournament matches, a fusion door, and a summer of you swinging it at everything that moved. Your father bought a hundred years, Xie Xie. You *earned* whatever's past it." And that was true — and it was also, he knew, the same law that had taken a ninety-year hawk to seven hundred and sixty: souls grew in tandem with the hands that used them. His brother's friend had simply been used *magnificently.*

"Test it," Xie Xie said immediately, because Xie Xie said immediately. "Now. You. Full kit. I want to know what two rings *is.*"

And Lin Hao — who had spent eleven days holding a class, answering a rematch letter's paperwork, watching a shelf, and missing his brother like a missing tooth — drew the Stormbringer, and felt the grin go from his face into his *hands,* and thought, *yes. This. This is the part I'm for.*

They went eleven minutes, and the yard collected.

Two rings was real. That was the first thing the sword learned: the dagger-boy's soul power had thickened past its rank the way the blade had thickened past its size — every meeting of steel came back up Lin Hao's arm with more behind it than rank twenty had ever carried, and the speed — the speed had always been the weapon — now had *weight* riding under it. Xie Xie took the center line twice. He turned a Gale Talon ribbon with the flat of the dagger the way a man turns a page, and once — once, and the yard would talk about this one for a term — he stepped *inside* a Wind-Step, not chasing it, *arriving,* and laid the flat of the Light Dragon Dagger across Lin Hao's ribs with a note like a struck glass.

It did not hurt much. It counted enormously.

"First blood," Xie Xie panted, circling, two rings burning under him like twin moons. "First. Blood."

"Noted," Lin Hao said, and stopped being polite.

He ended it the way he ended things now — with the homework. Not speed against speed; the sword against the *pattern:* three exchanges spent reading where the two new rings had changed the old rhythm (they had changed where Xie Xie *breathed,* which had changed where he committed, which was the whole of it), and then one stroke laid exactly where the dagger-boy's next step was already promised — the Stormbringer's flat resting against Xie Xie's throat a half-breath before Xie Xie arrived, the way a door waits for a man who has lived in the house his whole life.

"Yield," Lin Hao said, at walking volume, and the yard made its noise, and Xie Xie yielded — grinning like a struck bell, absolutely undefeated in his own opinion, which was the only opinion he had ever consulted.

Afterward, sitting on the yard wall with their shoulders touching, watching the winter come off the harbor, Xie Xie said, "When Wulin gets back, we're going to be *monsters.* You know that, right? A two-ring dagger and a whatever-rank-you and a forge that walks. We're going to walk into that rematch and they're going to wish they'd stayed class one."

Lin Hao watched the gulls. The rematch letter had come six days ago — on cream paper, in a hand like a drawn blade, with the class-one seal pressed into it hard enough to bruise the fiber: *Class two — formerly class one — requests the honor of a second meeting. The platform. The full roster. Let the teachers watch.* And under it, in the smaller script of the petition's sponsor, one line Long Hengxu had read to Wu Zhangkong with the expression of a man watching two weather systems introduce themselves: *—with the regards of Ye Yingluo.*

"They'll be there," Lin Hao said. "They've been sharpening since we beat them."

"Good," said Xie Xie, and spun the dagger — one ring's worth of habit, two rings' worth of light — and caught it. "So have I."

## Part 4: Tang Wulin Returns

He came back on the evening boat, twenty-three days after he had left, and Lin Hao was on the seawall before the gangplank was down, because the hawk had been restless all afternoon in a way that had nothing to do with weather.

The boy who came up the gangplank was the same height. That was the first wrongness, and Lin Hao's eyes went through the rest of the inventory before they shook hands: the same face, the same terrible coat, the steel band on the left wrist worn bright at the seam from a thumb that had clearly found it often. But he stood a half-degree differently, the way a furnace stands after it has been relined — the same footprint, more *inside.* His skin had gone the particular density Lin Hao had seen once before on hot metal that looked cold. And when Wulin laughed at something the boatman said — the same laugh, same key — the air over the seawall *warmed,* faintly, locally, the way it warms over a banked forge at three in the morning.

"You're different," Lin Hao said, instead of hello, because they had never once been the kind of brothers who started with hello.

"There was a storm." Wulin's smile did not entirely reach the new steadiness in his eyes. "Where I was — Old Tang's — there was a week of it, and I was inside the week, and then I came out the other side." He looked down at his right hand, and opened it, and closed it. The gold did not come. But Lin Hao's hawk-eyes saw it — *of course* they saw it: the veins under the skin of that hand had gone a shade deeper, finer, laid in a pattern that no anatomy had ever put in a boy, and behind the sternum where the first seal's furnace lived there was now — nested inside it, curled in it, asleep in it — a *second* heat. Not a seal. Something the seals were keeping. Something the storm week had *nested deeper* — the gold that had first surfaced in a gray bedroom and torn through a beef lane now folded down past the first seal, waiting, very patiently, to be needed.

Lin Hao said nothing about any of it. He took his brother's bag instead, which was heavier than bags used to be, and asked the only question the shelf allowed: "Old Tang?"

"Saw what he needed to see." Wulin's voice went careful, the way it did at file boundaries. "Gave me what he said I'd need to swallow. Tastes worse than it was, he was right about that." A pause, and then, because he was Wulin, and because a brother is not a shelf: "Hao. If — when — it comes out again. The gold. I think I can bring it *back* now. I think I can decide." His jaw set, eleven-folds set. "And if I ever can't — you'll know before I will. That's your whole job. You hear me coming."

"That's been my whole job since we were six," Lin Hao said. "You're just now making it official."

They walked up through the town in the blue dark, and Wulin ate four meat buns on the way, and described absolutely nothing about the storm week — but he described the harbor, because the harbor was the other thing he had done with his twenty-three days. "I walked the notice-line every morning. The fish-wife at the end of the pier keeps ours up — she oils the paper; she says the girl's face deserves better weather than the rest. Two are gone; the rain took them. And one boatman swore he saw a girl like the picture on a southbound freighter two years ago." He shrugged — the shrug of a boy who has learned what hope costs and pays anyway. "It's nothing. It's always nothing. But the line's still up, and the fund's still funded, and somebody down that coast is going to remember something on a day we can't guess. That's the whole plan. It's a slow plan." *That's the whole plan,* Lin Hao thought — *for both of us* — and said nothing, because the shelf had learned that some files travel better in company than in speech. And at the dormitory gate the notice board wore its new paper like a flag:

**FIRST GRADE — INTER-CLASS CHALLENGE, SANCTIONED. CLASS ONE vs CLASS TWO — formerly class one, demoted by the ladder they lost. Full roster. Third morning. Director Long Hengxu refereeing. Faculty in attendance.**

And under it, smaller, the kind of addition a posting-master makes when a teacher has leaned over his shoulder and dictated one line and meant every degree of its cold:

**Wu Zhangkong, attending.**

Wulin looked at the board for a long time. The steam went off his fourth bun in the winter air, and something behind his sternum — the nested heat, the thing the storm week had made — turned over once in its sleep, the way a dog turns when the house it guards is touched.

"They want the platform," Wulin said quietly. "And the teachers. And a second meeting." He finished the bun. "They must have missed us."

"They missed losing," Lin Hao said. He was looking at the small script of the petition's sponsor in his mind, the hand like a drawn blade, the two-year wound that had learned to wait. His sword-arm itched, which it had not done since the fusion's seam. "Xie Xie crossed a hundred years while you were gone. Two rings. He's been feeding on spite and protein."

"Good," said Tang Wulin, and the gold under his skin did not show, and did not need to. "Then we'll be monsters. Together."

Third morning. The platform. And somewhere across the city, in a cool office, a woman with a two-year patience folded a copy of the sanction notice into her desk, and waited for the morning she had been promised.


Rank twenty-seven arrived quietly, in the third week, after the second ring. Not from the ring — the ring was the gate, not the fuel. The fuel was eight weeks of a monster for a sparring partner and a man with a pointing stick who had begun, without saying so, to aim at him differently.

The hawk was eight hundred and fifteen years old that week. He noticed it the way he noticed the weather — because it had changed, and because the change had a direction.

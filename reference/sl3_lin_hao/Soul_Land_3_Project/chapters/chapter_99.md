## Part 1: The Bridge of Blood

The room understood the moment his hand closed on the ingot, and the room was wrong about what it understood.

Both hammers went into his left hand. His right — bare, unarmored, a boy's hand — took hold of the heavy silver directly, and the metal answered the way a fire answers flesh: white smoke rolled, the sizzles filled the room like frying rain, and every smith's nightmare played out in front of three elders who could do nothing at all.

Except the hand did not melt.

Gold scaled up the arm like a verdict — he had called the grass home and the golden ring forward in the same breath — and the scales walked across his shoulder, his torso, until the boy stood in a shell of himself, clothes burst away, hammering with his left while his right held on to a temperature that should have taken the hand off and kept hammering.

And what poured across that grip, into the silver, was not soul power anymore. Soul power had run its well dry ten minutes in. What fed the metal now came up red.

"Blood essence," Elder Li said, and for once the profession's encyclopedist sounded old. "He's bridging with blood essence. Dismayed doesn't cover it, Zhou. If that bridge breaks now—"

"Let him."

The voice came from the second table — the fifth examinee, still working, not looking up, his own taps never faltering. Lin Hao said it the way he said everything that mattered, quietly and at forge-pitch, which in that room carried like a bell.

"Blood feeds iron truer than soul does. Soul persuades the metal. Blood *obliges* it. He's not failing the refinement, Elders — he's found the older door. Let him through."

Zhuo Shi looked at Cai. Cai looked at the page in her hand, and then at the boy hanging onto a miracle by one scaled hand, and did not stop it either.

## Part 2: The Hammers Drink

What no ledger in the room could hold was what happened next, though one boy in it could have told them the shape of it in advance, because it was his own grammar happening in front of him at another man's table.

The silver began to go *into* the hammers.

Every strike, a portion of the melting metal stayed behind on the hammer-faces — soaked in, gold trailing up the handles like dawn climbing a well. The cloud-patterns on the twin hammers writhed, sharpened, and became something with teeth: a dragon, baring them, plain as a signature. The hammers had been blood-sacrificed years ago; fed their master's blood essence now, at the moment of a spirit refinement, they did what blood-fed iron does — the only thing it knows.

They grew. Toward him.

Lin Hao watched his own law perform at another man's table, and packed nothing on his face about it, and thought: *the iron is the house, and blood is the door, and he has just walked in by the oldest entrance there is. Mu Chen taught him the syllabus. The blood taught him the mother tongue.* The Yaluo bar slept in its wrap, cooled, listening, proven. His night's work was done; he stood now the way smiths have always stood at another smith's miracle — hat in hand, in whatever name the profession keeps its awe.

The silver drank faster than soul power had ever fed it. That was the arithmetic nobody wanted to say aloud: blood runs out too, and a boy has less of it than a master. Wulin's circling slowed. His strikes stayed true. The ounce of silver left on the table glowed like a caught breath.

He trembled. He coughed — red, onto the metal, and did not stop. The hammer rose one more time, higher than his head, and came down like weather.

Thunder. A pillar of gold one and a half meters tall — and through the light, unmistakable, *a dragon's roar*, as if something in the metal had taken the shape the hammers wore and decided to announce it. The light didn't shine; it *declared* — an aura of life, standing in the room like a new person.

It was born.

The heavy silver was spirit refined. And the hammers, dull gold now, the dragon plain on their faces, did the last thing hammers have ever done for a smith who made them family: they melted into his hands — not into any storage, into *him* — the way the old books said and almost nobody present had ever seen. Spirit-refined metal goes home to the one who fed it, drip by drip, blood by blood. It becomes the body.

*That*, the room realized in the same cold wave, *is how battle armor masters wear their armor like skin.*

## Part 3: Straight as a Steel Rod

He fell straight as a steel rod.

Xie Xie caught him — of course Xie Xie caught him; three years of positioning finally paid its dividend — and the captain lay in his arms snow-white, breath thin, soul and blood both spent to the last readable drop, asleep the way the drowned sleep, and smiling, faintly, at whatever it is smiths see.

"He succeeded," Zhuo Shi said. The words came out with the lid off. "He actually — Zhou Shi's boy spirit-refined at thirteen. Do you understand what is standing in this room? A fifth-rank *master* blacksmith at thirteen. There is one Divine Craftsman on this continent and Shrek's best armor is three words because of it, and *that*—" his hand moved at Wulin, at the gold that had been hammers, "—that is what one looks like at the beginning. Even Shrek scrambles for this. Any academy on Douluo scrambles for this."

He took Wulin out of Xie Xie's arms himself, gently, a grandfather collecting something breakable, and the scarlet came up around him — a red aura, corporeal, jade-bright, heavy as a season, and under it the class felt for the first time what a Title Douluo actually is when he stops being scenery.

The red light poured into Wulin, and Wulin's body — golden lines threading him like makeshift veins, dimmer than the forge had left them — drank it. Not received. *Drank.* Sucked the scarlet in, brighter and brighter, the treatment going somewhere no treatment had been invited.

"Woah," said Zhuo Shi, and did not stop.

"His body isn't receiving the treatment," Lin Hao said quietly, to the room, over the light. "It's auditing it. The line decides what stays. It always has — it's why the joining counts seconds instead of spending them. Elders, you are not healing him. You are *paying tribute*." He said it without fear and without awe, the way a man reads a gauge, because someone in that room had to say the true thing in a plain voice, and the smith who audits dragons was standing right there.

Elder Cai, for once, did not score the answer.

She walked instead to Gu Yue — fists balled, breath held, tunnel-eyed on the boy in the red light — and stood beside her, almost gently. "Don't think you're done with tests now that you've passed," she said. "Battle armor masters by twenty, or forget the inner court. That is the next sentence of your life, girl. Write it." And she left, boots even on the stone, and the word *passed* lay on the floor behind her like a dropped key.

Elder Li looked over the three standing students for a long moment — and his gaze stopped on Xu Xiaoyan for a hairbreadth of a second, and something in the old man smiled without moving his mouth — and he went out with his arms folded behind him, satisfied with the world for once.

## Part 4: What the Teacher Said

The scarlet light poured. The golden lines drank. The night went long, and nobody left, and somewhere in it Xie Xie sat down on the floor where the miracle had happened and stared at the place the ounce of silver had been until Xiaoyan sat down next to him, and Gu Yue stood where Cai had left her, holding the dropped key.

Then the man the color of winter came off the doorframe at last.

Wu Zhangkong crossed to the second table first — his path had been decided for three chapters of silence — and looked down at the rolled tool-wrap, the packed bar, the boy who audits dragons. Everyone expected frost. What came was quieter and heavier than frost.

"You gave him the law when it mattered," his teacher said. "'Rows breathe in threes.' He was somewhere it was, because you told him where it was." A pause the length of a winter. "Talent is a debt, Lin Hao. Tonight, both of you paid interest." And then — the gesture nobody in Class Zero ever forgot, though only four people were awake to see it — Wu Zhangkong picked up his student's tool-wrap, and rolled it properly, and set it on the boy's shoulder like a wing being folded.

"Tomorrow the continent starts learning your names," he said. "Tonight you eat, and you sleep. That is also the profession."

At the first table, the red light poured. The dragon drank. The city around them slept under stars that admitted to nothing, and in a metal room at the academy's root, a boy who was now part hammer dreamed of nothing at all, and smiled — and somewhere north of everything, in a forest made of the academy's memory, a mother's heartbeat kept time for a cub that would never know how close it had come to being a ring.

The night kept all of it. Morning would be loud.

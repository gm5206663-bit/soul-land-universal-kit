## Part 1: Ten

"You all scored ten points," Shen Yi said, and strained visibly to say it tranquilly, and led the way out of the yard while the number was still landing.

Behind her face she was doing sums she had not expected to do. Consecutive trials, no rest, children — she had worried. Instead they had scored well twice and then dominated the hardest test of the three, and the record book would have to invent a column for it. *That Tang Wulin really deserves to be class zero's captain,* she thought, and filed the rest under the heading she had opened three pages ago, the one that kept growing.

Wulin, for his part, was doing the rationing arithmetic and was pleased with it. Apart from him — blood essence and soul power, spent like a man paying for five — the others had come through the gates almost whole. That had been the whole plan: he took the front of the trials now, and the strongest of them stayed wrapped for the ones to come.

"You can get down now," Lin Hao said.

The arms around his neck did not move.

He waited a beat, at speed, the way he noted everything. "Did you get injured back there?"

Gu Yue shook her head against his back — he felt it more than saw it — and then, under her breath, in the voice she used for prices: "Just carry me a bit longer. I'm a bit scared."

Xie Xie and Xu Xiaoyan pulled faces at each other. *Scared.* Of the four of them, Gu Yue was the one the word had never once applied to; strangers got the cold eyes, teammates got the arithmetic, and fear had never gotten anything at all, because there had never been anything to get. And yet since the exams began she had been — warmer was not the word. Closer to a word. The juice box. The front seat. The ride, chosen out loud, in front of the hall. *Just what is going on here?* the two of them asked each other with their eyebrows, and got no answer, because there was no answer that did not cost somebody something.

Wulin, who was the only one of them with the whole of it, studied the ceiling with enormous attention. He had been trusted with a thread when they were eleven, in a forge-smell dark, with two words and no ceremony, and he had carried it since the way he carried everything of his brother's: level, both hands, forever. So he looked at the ceiling, and kept his face brother-shaped, and said nothing at all.

Gu Yue's heart was going like a smith's hammer on a deadline. Lin Hao's was not. His face did not do crimson; steady was a garment he had cut to his own measurements, and he had hemmed it twice this month. Her hair came loose and tickled his cheek, and he noted that too, and put it precisely nowhere, and carried her a bit longer, because she had asked for a bit longer, and the asking was the thing — the first spend she had made of the treaty in nine days, deniable as ever, small as ever, and real.

He set her down at the stairs without comment. The treaty held. The purse stayed shut. Both of them kept its customs, and walked up the stairs with the others, and if the class noticed the extra half-second, the class had the manners not to price it.

## Part 2: The Gold Lines

The second floor was white, and the white was written on.

"Those are... soul device blueprints?" Xu Xiaoyan said, tilting her head at the walls. She had chosen mecha designer for her second profession, and knew the grammar of these lines the way the others knew grass — circuit and channel, arrays like sentence-structure, the whole corridor a page of somebody's handwriting in gold.

Wulin squinted at them and got approximately nothing; the diagrams swam and folded and refused him, and he was honest enough about his own spiritual power to stop squinting.

Lin Hao read the walls once, top to bottom, the way he had read the sixth gate's spring. Blueprints were promises about metal — a curve was a pour, a channel was a quench, a tolerance was a smith's opinion of himself — and the walls of this academy were a library of other men's promises, fifty years deep. He walked the corridor a half-step behind the others and read four diagrams and the margins of two, and the reading cost nothing and paid eight points by the time they reached the door, and he did not mention it, because it had not been a trial. It had been a wall.

## Part 3: One Specialty

The room they entered was large and spare. "Wait here," Shen Yi said, pointing to chairs. "I'm going to go get this trial's administrators. This trial will test your personal strength. Only one of your specialties will be examined, so consider carefully what you will present. Your greatest specialty is directly related to your strength." And she was gone, about-faced, efficient.

"Our specialty?" Xie Xie said, at a loss.

Gu Yue sat down cross-legged first and closed her eyes, which was her version of *figure it out yourselves.*

"It's simple," Wulin said, taking the seat beside her. "Your specialty is your greatest strength as a soul master. Xie Xie — you're agility. Show them the limit of your speed; embody what *agility-type* means. You're not a normal one — twin martial souls — so show them both together. Speed and the twins. Same for everyone: show them what you're best at."

Xu Xiaoyan's brow folded. "Captain. What do I do? My full potential only comes up at night."

"Then you make do with words," Wulin said, wry. "You already used it in the second trial — the proof exists, three examiners watched it happen. Explain it thoroughly and honestly. As long as you're speaking the truth, I don't think they'll demand you raise the moon at noon."

"...I got it," she said, easier.

"Then rest while there's resting to be had," Wulin said, and closed his eyes mid-sentence, meditating the moment the words were out — because blood essence ran on soul power, and there was no food in this building, and his stomach had already filed its opinion of the morning's six catches with the entire examination hall. His only option was to recover by breath what he could not recover by breakfast. The class had begun keeping a private count of the captain's meals the way other families track weather.

And the sixth sat with his back to the wall and said nothing, which was his answer to a question he was still holding: *one specialty, the one that is directly related to your strength.* He had two lives and the hall owned three pages on him already, and the wrong one of his lives, shown here, would spend the other.

## Part 4: Elder Cai

Not five minutes later, Shen Yi came back with three people.

Two were middle-aged men with the stillness of examiners. The third was an elderly lady, walking stick in hand, gait unsteady, and the room rearranged its gravity around her: the two men seated themselves on either side of her only after she had sat, and Shen Yi stood, which said everything about who outranked whom in this building.

Lin Hao's eyes went to the stick, once, the way they went to everything. The ferrule was iron, worn to a shine at one facet — a right-handed lady, decades of the same lean — and above the ferrule a repair-ring had been forged onto the shaft, old work, neat, by somebody who had liked her: a smith's promise, keeping a stick honest. He put it away. It was not a trial either. It was a wall.

The old lady looked over the four meditating children who had not noticed the door, and a smile tugged at the corner of her mouth. "These kids sure know how to make the most of their time," she said, hoarse. "Alright then, Little Yiyi — tell them we're beginning."

*Yiyi,* the sixth did not say aloud.

"Yes, Elder Cai," Shen Yi said, with no discontent at all, deference like a worn coat, and crossed to the examinees. "The administrators are here. Which of you goes first?"

Wulin opened his eyes and did the order-arithmetic — first up leaves the deepest mark; first must go with a bang — and counted his people, and there were five of them, and one of the five was a question the whole class had been carrying since the stairs.

"Xie Xie," Wulin said. "You're first." And he leaned over and whispered a few words into Xie Xie's ear that were about the twins, and about joy, and Xie Xie nodded, stood, and walked to the middle of the room.

The class's eyes flicked — all four, one flick, a unit — to the sixth, who was deciding, in the silence he was good at, which of his lives to show a table of strangers. Whatever he answered, he would answer last. The margin goes last. It was becoming the custom of them.

## Part 5: Xie Xie, Three Times

"Hello, teachers," Xie Xie said, and bowed deeply.

The old lady chuckled. "Go on, kid. You only get once — show me what you're best at."

"Yes." Three soul rings rose beneath him — two yellow, one purple — and the purple one lit.

He blurred. One Xie Xie became three, side by side, identical to the last eyelash, and the three bowed again — politely, in unison, which was somehow the most alarming thing yet — and then went transparent and broke in three directions at once, steps like rumors, each figure trailing a Light Dragon Dagger into the air.

The daggers began to hiss. Faster, then blurred, then the whole center of the room was a small private hurricane with three ghosts in it, cutting at an opponent who existed only in Xie Xie's excellent opinion, and the wind of it reached the chairs.

The two middle-aged examiners sat forward at the same time. Soul power went out from them like a hand laid flat over the arena — reading, confirming — and what it confirmed made them look at each other.

The clones were not light. They were not tricks. They had weight, and breath, and mass — three of him, tangible, all real.

Elder Cai's stick settled against her palm, and the smile at the corner of her mouth stopped tugging and simply stayed.

## Part 1: The Mountain That Waited

They came together at speed, and neither released a soul — two boys reading each other on the approach, rings rising only when the gap had shrunk to ten meters. Identical configurations, two yellow and one purple, and then the difference arrived all at once.

Xie Xie took the Light Dragon Dagger. Student number two *swelled.*

The slender frame inflated like a bellows, clothes bursting to shreds, the famous absurdly-elastic undergarments surviving what the academy's tailoring could not; brown hair swept up four meters of body and two arms like bridge cables, and the room's air took on the rumor of a landslide. *A giant gorilla,* Xie Xie thought, awed in the professional way. *Maybe even the—* and then the ground came at him.

What followed was Xie Xie's whole grammar, spent honestly. The ground-smash met a golden blade of light that cut lines into the arena's walls; the counter-leap came down with the might of a mountain and met *three* Xie Xies, each kicking off the other two, scattering in three directions — a trick that made the line of ten go quiet, because this boy had chosen the strongest of them and was not dead yet. Three punches answered, one per clone, air hissing; the clones kicked off nothing itself and escaped by the margin of a haircut.

By the time both sides returned to the ground, the theory was written: the attacker spends more than the defender, ranks near equal, the agility-type banking on the Mysterious Heaven Method to outlast. And the mountain simply stood in the arena's center, patient as furniture, because he could not strike three directions at once and knew something worth knowing: sustaining three clones of a thousand-year skill was bleeding his opponent white. *He'll come,* number two thought, correctly. *Soon.*

He came. The clones were faster now, and Xie Xie with them, and the second ring lit — **Light Dragon Storm**, the three copies converging into one spiraling lance of light that he had every right to believe would injure anything it touched.

Number two did not dodge. His burly arms moved in a motion that was — to almost nobody in the room — legible.

## Part 2: The Green Light

**Controlling Crane, Capturing Dragon.**

An aura expanded outward like a slammed door, and the storm broke around it; the hands pressed flat, the second ring flared, and the air *rumbled* — thunder in a closed room — and all six clones, light and shadow, were flung backward to crash into one boy again mid-air. Number two stomped and rose like artillery, first ring lit, and punched the air itself.

The shockwave crossed the arena at a speed that ended conversations. Xie Xie's mind was chaos and his reserves were ash; he could not have dodged a falling leaf.

A green light flashed over and dragged him out of the wave's path and down to the ground, gentle as a mother cat. Number two halted mid-advance, bowed to Shen Yi, and walked back to the line.

Five minutes, start to finish. Suppressed the whole way; without the teacher's hand, gravely injured. This was the strength of a Shrek Academy student — in the face of it, twin martial souls and type matchups meant nothing at all.

In the chairs, Tang Wulin — roused out of his recovery by thunder — sat with a brand-new problem: *What if I had picked him? Could I have lasted? Could I have won?* He had no answer. He could not fathom the bottom of it.

## Part 3: Three Points and a Lecture

"Xie Xie — three points," Shen Yi said, and did not enjoy saying it. "Points will be deducted at the term's end for a failed trial. You chose him yourself."

Xie Xie came back with his shoulders down and his chin already returning to its natural altitude. Not disgraceful — he had shown everything he had, and it had simply not been enough of anything.

"Why didn't you just keep running?" Wulin demanded, solemn. "You *knew* how strong he is."

"My clones eat soul power, and without them his ranged game kills me anywhere in this box." Xie Xie's laugh was bitter and honest. "A bigger arena, maybe. This one — no."

"You! *Reflect!*" And the captain opened the ledger on him in front of the hall: his opponent had been that strong *because* of a thousand-year skill — his consumption couldn't be lower; twin souls and the Mysterious Heaven Method meant attrition was the one war Xie Xie could never afford to lose; three clones in three directions; control the distance; make the mountain walk. "Do you have anything to say for yourself?"

Xie Xie slapped his thigh. "You're right! I forgot he was bleeding as fast as I was — I've been fighting too many soul *beasts* lately. I treated him like a soul beast!"

This was announced at full volume to the entire arena. Number two, halfway back into his jacket, went rigid, turned, and scowled. Xie Xie met the scowl chin-first, haughty as a proclamation: *next time.*

Wulin chuckled, tired. The lecture had been for morale, and it had worked — the boy had been in ruins one minute and was scheduling rematches the next.

## Part 4: The Question Under the Floor

The fifth examinee had watched all five minutes the way a jeweler watches someone else's shop.

The mountain's patience had been genuine — the strongest of ten, and the most disciplined, holding a ring-skill in reserve for the exact moment the clones could no longer sustain. But that was not what had opened Lin Hao's eyes for the first time all evening.

*Controlling Crane, Capturing Dragon.* The winters' first coordination — the art you learned before your hands were worth coordinating, inner-sect, taught person to person down a lineage that was supposed to end at walls. He had watched a boy of Shrek shape it with academy-polished wrists, textbook timing, no lineage in it anywhere.

When number two had re-dressed and re-joined the line, the fifth came down the floor to him — unhurried, polite, hands loose — and said, at a volume for two people:

"Controlling Crane, Capturing Dragon. Who taught you?"

Everything about number two stopped for one full second. His eyes came up and went over the smaller boy with new attention — the soulless boy, the four exchanges, the concession they would be hearing about for a year. "Where," he said finally, "does a brat with no soul light learn that name?"

"Inside," Lin Hao said.

The answer plainly satisfied nobody, and both of them knew the shape of the moment: two holders of the same question, each declining to pay the answer. Number two's jaw set. The fifth nodded once — *thank you for the confirmation* — and walked back to the chairs, and along the wall the ten exchanged the look of people recalculating an entire evening. In her seat, Shen Yi's pen moved. Her file now had a section that was just questions, and the section was the fastest-growing one.

Xie Xie dropped into the chair beside the fifth, arms over the back of it, watching the line of ten with new professional interest. "Four exchanges."

"You counted?"

"The whole academy counted." He said it without resentment, which was its own kind of progress. Then, quieter, honestly: "He wasn't even angry. That's the part I keep replaying."

"Mountains don't get angry," the fifth said. "They just decide when."

Gu Yue was already standing.

## Part 5: The Forgettable One

"Number one has fought," Shen Yi said, neutral, and the room's attention flicked to the line. Canon law and house law agreed: selections were final, and taken was taken. "Choose from those remaining."

Gu Yue walked the line once, slower than Wulin had, reading it her way — and the purse's read was not the captain's read, nor the smith's. She stopped at a boy about whom nothing could be said at all: middle height, middle face, middle stillness, the one even the scoreboard's eye had slid past all evening. Fourth in the line. The forgettable one.

"Number four," Gu Yue said.

Xiaoyan made a small sound. Xie Xie, arms crossed, muttered, "She picked the *blank* one?"

"He's not blank," Wulin said quietly, watching the boy detach from the wall and walk out with the calm of furniture being moved. "None of them are blank. That's the whole academy." Lamplight stood in the round room's bowls now; true night was assembling outside, and one trial after this one, and a staff that wanted stars. The arithmetic of the evening was tightening exactly as designed.

For Gu Yue the arithmetic was simpler and worse. Thirty-eight points, two trials after this one, and a margin that could not absorb a stumble: scoring badly here would essentially mean not passing. The whole examination narrowed to the plainest sentence in it — *winning was her only option.*

"Begin!"

Soul rings rose beneath both of them — and the hall's breath went with them.

Three rings. **All three purple.**

Not the slender mountain's two-yellow-one-purple; not anything the evening had calibrated against. Three thousand-year rings stood over the forgettable boy like a sentence in a language nobody had spoken all day, and Gu Yue — whose reserves were the deepest secret in the room — let her hands rise into her working position, her expression unchanged, as the first spell left her fingers and the ranged war began.

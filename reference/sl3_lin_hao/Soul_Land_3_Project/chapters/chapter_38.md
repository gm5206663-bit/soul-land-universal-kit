## Part 1: Three Days

He had thirty-one lines in the ledger about what a soul beast would do.

He had none about what a person would do, because until three days ago there had not been any people in there, and he had built the ledger the way he built everything — from what he had actually seen, and not one line further.

So he started a new column, and he titled it, in the small careful hand he used for things he was not yet sure of, **WHAT THEY WANT.**

It was harder than the other column. A Horned Dragon wanted the same thing on Tuesday that it wanted on Friday. A thirteen-year-old with three rings and an unfinished spirit soul wanted something on Monday that he would not have recognised in himself by Thursday, and Lin Hao had no instrument for that except the one he had been carrying around since he was six, which was that he noticed things and could not switch it off.

He went to the dormitory and got Yun Xiao.

"You've got the numbers on the last four rebellions," he said.

"I have the numbers on the last **six.**"

"You've got six."

"Four of them are incomplete. Two are complete." Yun Xiao closed the book, which was how he said *I am now listening.* "What do you want?"

"Survival rate by team size."

Yun Xiao looked at him for a moment. "That's a strange thing to want."

"Is it."

"Everyone wants the kill rate. Or the energy taken per entrant. Nobody has ever asked me about survival by team size." He opened the book again, and turned pages, and stopped. "Larger teams survive longer."

"Obviously."

"Not obviously." Yun Xiao turned the book around. "Look. Teams of two survive **eleven minutes** on average. Teams of five survive **thirty-eight**. Teams of seven survive **fifty-one.** But teams of seven also **lose more of their members** — because in a team of seven, six people will let one person be the bait, and in a team of two there isn't anybody to be."

"And the exam needs an hour," Lin Hao said.

"The exam needs an hour."

"So the average team of seven **fails.**"

"Most of them," Yun Xiao said. "Yes."

Lin Hao looked at the numbers for a long time.

"That's the whole thing, isn't it," he said.

"That's the whole thing."

## Part 2: What He Brought

Wu Zhangkong said "show me" on the third morning, in the training ground, with the wooden sword still on its rack.

Lin Hao did not show him a diagram. He had tried a diagram on the first night and hated it, because a diagram of five people is five dots and five dots do not do anything.

Instead he made them walk it.

"Four shapes," he said. "**Wulin is the middle.** Always. Not because he's the strongest of the four of you — he is, but that's not why, and I want to be careful about that sentence because I am the fifth shape and I am not counting myself in it. It's because his grass owns ground, and ground is the only thing in there that stays put."

Wulin, who had been appointed captain for reasons he still did not entirely believe, nodded.

"**Gu Yue is the weather.** She doesn't hold a position, she holds a *condition* — she makes the air bad on one side so nobody comes from that side. That's not a wall, it's a slope. People slide off slopes without noticing they're sliding."

Gu Yue said nothing, which was agreement.

"**Xie Xie is the answer.** He doesn't go anywhere until something happens, and then he's already there. His job is to not be predictable, which is the one thing he's genuinely better at than anyone in this city."

"I'll take that," Xie Xie said, "and I'll take the second half as a compliment and ignore the first."

"**Zhang Yangzi and Wang Jinxi are the door.**" Lin Hao looked at them. "Not a wall. A *door.* You two open and you two close, and the difference between those is the whole fight."

Wang Jinxi, who did not speak much, said, "Our fusion isn't ready."

"I know. So don't use it."

Both of them looked at him.

"Your fusion is rudimentary — Wu Zhangkong's word, not mine, and he's right. So you don't use it as a weapon, you use it as a *shape.* Eagle goes high, dragon goes low, and there's a gap between you that nobody can come through without one of you seeing them. That doesn't need the fusion to work. That just needs you to both know where the other one is." He paused. "Which is the part the fusion was supposed to do for you, and which you can simply do yourselves."

Zhang Yangzi opened his mouth, and closed it, and then said, to nobody in particular, "That's insulting and it's also right."

"And me," Lin Hao said.

"You," Wu Zhangkong said, from where he had been standing without moving for eleven minutes, "have not said."

"I'm the one who goes and gets hit."

"That is not a shape."

"It is," Lin Hao said. "It's the shape that tells the other four where the people are. Somebody has to be the one who finds out, and it should be the one who can find out and come back." He shrugged. "I read things. I'm fast. And I've got a sword that has opinions."

Wu Zhangkong looked at him for a long moment.

"You have built," he said, "a formation whose centre is a boy with a plant, whose weather is a girl nobody has a file on, whose answer is a boy with two daggers and one secret, whose door is two boys who cannot yet do the thing they are supposed to do — and whose scout is you."

"Yes."

"And the reason."

"Because those are the five people we have."

There was a silence of the kind that Lin Hao had learned to file, in the column marked *things that were not answers.*

"Walk it again," Wu Zhangkong said.

## Part 3: The Room With The Window

They went in on the fourth morning, and they were not naive, and they released their martial souls the moment the light finished changing.

The **storm-gray lock at his temple** had grown again — long enough now to fall across his cheekbone, and he had stopped pushing it back, because pushing it back was something you did about a thing you were hiding, and he had stopped hiding it about four months ago. The **hair tips** of it had been **hawk-gold** at the ends since the spring, and Xie Xie had stopped making jokes about it, which was how Lin Hao knew it had become a fact rather than a curiosity.

Lin Hao had been in the platform eleven times and it still did the thing to him — the wrongness of a forest that had not been a forest a second ago, the smell of a place that had never had weather. This time the trees were thicker and the light came down in a colour he could not name, and everything in it was, he understood immediately, **angry.**

The rebellion. The beasts were excited. More visible. Closer.

He could feel the hawk go still in him, which was not calm. Nine hundred and fifty years of something deciding.

*Right,* he thought. *Me too.*

Two hundred metres in, Wulin's grass went out ahead of them and parted the undergrowth into a path, and they walked it in the shape Lin Hao had drawn, and for nineteen minutes nothing happened.

The twentieth minute was a boar.

The thirty-first was something with too many legs that Wang Jinxi's eagle saw from forty metres up and closed the door on.

The forty-second minute was a person.

Lin Hao knew it before the others did, because the forest had gone quiet in one specific direction, and forests do not go quiet for boars.

"**Stop,**" he said, quietly, and the shape closed around Wulin without anybody discussing it.

A boy came out of the trees about sixty metres off. Thirteen, maybe. Three rings — Lin Hao counted them the way he counted everything, and filed them, and did not like the third one, which was purple.

The boy was not looking at them. He was looking at a **thousand-year** something forty metres the other way, which had its back to both of them, and which was bleeding.

And Lin Hao understood, in the flat clear way he understood things when there was no time to be frightened, exactly what was about to happen.

The boy was going to kill it. And it was going to take a hundred seconds to take the energy. And the hundred seconds were going to start, and there were going to be two teams within a hundred seconds of each other, and the rule said the energy went to whoever was nearest.

"He's going to finish it," Xie Xie said, very quietly.

"Yes."

"And then we're nearest."

"Yes."

"So we kill him."

"No," Lin Hao said.

Xie Xie looked at him.

"**No?**"

"Watch."

---

The boy killed it.

He was good. It took him eleven seconds and it was not a clean eleven seconds, and the thing went down, and the light came up out of it the way Lin Hao had seen it come up out of things eleven times before.

And the hundred seconds started.

Lin Hao had been counting. He had started counting the moment the light appeared, because he could not help it, and because he had worked out on the second night, sitting on a floor with a book he had borrowed from a boy who made notes, that the hundred seconds were the only clock in the forest that everybody was reading.

At four seconds he said, "Gu Yue. Sixty metres, west. Make it bad."

At six seconds the air on the west side of the clearing went **wrong** — not cold, not hot, simply *unwelcome*, the way a room becomes unwelcome when somebody in it decides something.

At nine seconds the boy, who had been crouched over his kill with his eyes shut and his hands in the light, opened his eyes, because the west side of his world had stopped being a place he wanted to be.

At eleven seconds he looked east.

And he saw five children in a shape he did not recognise, standing very still, with a boy in the middle holding a plant and a boy at the front holding a sword and not doing anything at all with it.

At fourteen seconds he made a decision.

It was the wrong one, but Lin Hao had known it was going to be the wrong one, because he had spent three days in a dormitory learning what thirteen-year-olds with three rings want, and what they want is to not be interrupted, and the fastest way to not be interrupted is to remove the interruption.

He came at them.

"**Door,**" Lin Hao said.

Zhang Yangzi went up. Wang Jinxi went low. And the gap between them — the gap that had needed no fusion skill at all, only two boys who knew where each other was — closed on a thirteen-year-old with a purple third ring like a hand closing on a wrist.

He did not get through. He did not even get close. He got held, for four seconds, in a doorway made of an eagle and a dragon, and in those four seconds Gu Yue made the ground under him into something that was not ground, and Xie Xie put a dagger against a place that would have been a tendon, and did not cut it.

At ninety-one seconds Lin Hao said, "**Go.**"

They went. All five of them, out of the clearing at a run, in the shape, with Wulin's grass pulling shut behind them like a curtain.

At one hundred and four seconds, somewhere behind them, a thousand years of cultivation energy finished having nowhere to go.

## Part 4: What The Man In The Window Wrote

In the Spirit Pagoda's monitoring room, a staff member turned to Wu Zhangkong with the expression of a man who has been paid to be impressed and has found that he means it.

"These kids are pretty good," he said. "Teacher Wu really is very good at teaching."

Wu Zhangkong gave a humble nod, because that was the correct response and he had learned the correct responses a long time ago. "Let's see how they do first."

He did not say what he was thinking.

He was thinking about a motto that was twenty thousand years old, which had been in his head all morning and which he had not invited: *monsters, not ordinary people.* It was the kind of sentence that got carved into things, and it had been carved into him, and he had spent a long time finding out what it actually cost.

*Talent isn't the whole of it,* he thought, watching the five of them run. *Anybody can have talent. Talent is the part that happens to you.*

He watched the boy at the front of the shape — the one with the sword, the one whose numbers did not behave, the one whose name he had watched a colleague put on a card instead of a form — and he watched him do a thing that no amount of talent explained.

The boy had counted.

He had counted a hundred seconds that nobody else in the forest was counting, and he had spent four of them holding still while a thirteen-year-old with a purple ring came at him, because he was waiting for the shape to be right rather than for himself to be safe.

*Perseverance,* Wu Zhangkong thought. *And something else. Something I don't have a word for.*

He looked at the other four and made his private ranking, the way he made it every time, because it was a habit and because he did not entirely trust habits: the girl, first, and by so much that it was almost a different category — there was *no record of her martial soul in the Pagoda's history or the Academy's*, and he had looked, and looking had told him nothing at all. Xie Xie second, the twins a gift whether nature had made them or a laboratory had. The other two decent, compatible, unfinished. And Wulin, whom he had made captain, not for strength, though he was the strongest of them, but because he was *brave enough to shoulder it.*

And then Lin Hao, who did not fit in the ranking, because the ranking was a list of what people had and Lin Hao was a list of what people *did.*

*If I had to choose one of them to take the exam,* Wu Zhangkong thought, *Wulin is the right answer. He is the one who will still be standing in ten years.*

And then, uninvited, because the morning had been a strange one:

*And the other one is the one who will have made sure of it.*

## Part 5: Fifty-One

They came out at **seventy-one minutes**, all five of them, and the man at the door checked a board and nodded, because seventy-one was past the hour and the hour was the whole of what the exam asked for.

Yun Xiao was waiting outside with the book open, because he did not say things without the book.

"Seventy-one," he said. "The average for a team of **seven** is fifty-one."

"We were a team of five," Xie Xie said.

"I know what you were."

"So we beat the seven-minute average by twenty."

"By twenty," Yun Xiao said. "With two fewer people." He closed the book, which was how he said *I have finished being impressed and am now merely recording it.* "Most teams of seven don't make the hour at all."

Wulin sat down on the steps outside the Spirit Pagoda with his grass still unspooling slowly back into him and his face the colour of old paper, and he said, "Nobody died."

"Nobody died," Lin Hao agreed.

"In a rebellion."

"In a rebellion."

Wulin looked at his hands for a while. Then he said, "The thirteen-year-old."

"Is fine. Gu Yue made the ground soft, not sharp, and Xie Xie didn't cut."

"He'll come back."

"Probably."

"With friends."

"Probably."

Wulin was quiet for a moment. "You counted the hundred seconds."

"I count everything."

"I know." Wulin looked up at him. "You counted them and you stood still for four of them."

"Four was how long the door needed."

"You didn't know that."

"No," Lin Hao said. "I didn't."

There was a silence between them that had a great deal in it and that neither of them was going to say anything about, because they were ten years old and they had a language for this, and the language was that you did not.

Then Wulin said, "You're going to be unbearable about this."

"I'm already unbearable."

"Yes," Wulin said. "But more."

"More," Lin Hao agreed.

---

He did not tell them about the last thing.

He did not tell them that at ninety-one seconds, when he had said *go,* the hawk in him had done something he had not asked for — had leaned, the way a person leans toward a door they have heard open — and that the leaning had gone toward the energy coming up out of the dead thing, and not away from it.

He did not tell them, because he did not know what it meant, and because he had learned from Wu Zhangkong, of all people, that a reading is not a warning.

He wrote it in the ledger, in the new column, under **WHAT THEY WANT.**

It was the first line he had written in that column that was not about a person.

He looked at it for a long time, and then he closed the book, because fifty years was still fifty years, and he had a formation to walk again in the morning.

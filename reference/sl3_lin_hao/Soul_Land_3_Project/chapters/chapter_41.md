## Part 1: The Man With The Ice Staff

They found him at the bottom of a slope, backed against a rock, with four of them coming at him from the trees.

He was about fourteen. He had an ice staff, and a purple ring, and the specific posture of a boy who has worked out that he is going to lose and has decided to lose expensively.

Lin Hao had stopped the shape at the top of the slope, because that was what the shape did now — it stopped when he stopped, and nobody had discussed that either.

"**Do we?**" Xie Xie said.

"He's got four on him."

"So?"

"So there are four."

Wulin was already looking at the ground, which was how he worked out whether something was possible. "The lions aren't here."

"The lions are always here."

"The lions aren't *here.*" Wulin looked up. "Gu Yue. Can you make it cold on that slope?"

Gu Yue had been quiet for eleven minutes, which with her meant she was counting something. She said, "I can make it cold on that slope."

"Then do that, and I'll take the left two."

Nobody said *and Lin Hao will take the right two,* because it had stopped being a thing anybody said.

---

The boy with the ice staff never did work out what Gu Yue's martial soul was.

Lin Hao watched him try, from forty metres, while the two on his own side found out what a sword that had stopped being a tool could do.

A green tornado went up on the slope — not fast, not sudden, the way weather arrives — and **icicles** went into it, dozens of them, spinning in the middle of it like something in a bowl. The boy put up a wall of ice. The tornado did not care. The wall came apart in the direction it had come from.

His ring went **purple.**

His second soul skill came up and then **receded**, because whatever he had been about to do was not the biggest thing he had, and he understood in about one second that he needed the biggest thing he had.

**Fury of Ice.**

Two metres of spear came out of the top of his staff, and there were faint patterns running in the shaft of it that Lin Hao's eyes followed the way his eyes followed the grain of a bar, and he understood — with a lurch, because he had never felt anything like it — that **none of them could have stood in front of that.**

Not dodged. Not blocked. Not *survived.*

"A thousand-year soul skill," Wang Jinxi said, very quietly.

They had never seen one. They had been told about them the way you are told about weather on other continents. And Lin Hao stood at the top of a slope in a forest that did not exist and watched a fourteen-year-old throw one, and thought: **that is what a thousand years does.**

He looked down at his own three rings.

Two of them were purple. One of them was empty.

## Part 2: What Little Black Came Back As

Zhang Yangzi sat down against the rock afterwards with his eyes shut, and after about four minutes a small dark shape came up out of him and sat on his knee.

**Little Black.**

It was not right. It was the right shape and the right size and it had the right way of turning its head, but it was **thin** — not thin the way a bird is thin, thin the way a drawing is thin — and it did not look at anything except the direction Zhang Yangzi turned his face.

"It can come out," Zhang Yangzi said, without opening his eyes. "It can't fight. It can see, and it can give me what it sees, and that's all it can do. Full restoration is several more days."

Several more days. In a place where you could not spend several more days.

"That's fine," Wulin said.

"It's not fine, it's a third of my—"

"**Zhang Yangzi.**" Wulin sat down next to him, which was the second time in two days he had done that to somebody. "It can see. That's the part we needed. That was always the part we needed."

And Lin Hao, sitting four metres away with three purple rings going slowly dim around his boots, understood something that he had not understood in the dormitory three days ago when he had asked an eagle to go up into the sky.

He had built the shape around the nine seconds. The nine seconds had cost a boy a third of himself. And the nine seconds were still there — thinner, weaker, unable to do anything except look — and they were **still the most useful thing in the formation.**

He had got it wrong and he had got it right, and both were true, and he was not going to pretend otherwise.

He wrote it down later. He did not cross it out this time.

## Part 3: The Lions

They came at the thirty-ninth minute.

Lin Hao heard them before the hawk did, which was new, and he did not have time to think about what that meant. A roar behind them, and then numerous red figures in the trees, moving in the way that a *pride* moves — not together, but in the same direction, which is worse.

**Blazing Demon Lions.**

"Soul beasts absorb spirit energy too," Gu Yue said. She said it the way she said everything, flat, and it took Lin Hao two seconds to understand that she was not explaining and was instead **apologising**, because the four hundred years hanging off the six of them was the reason the lions had come.

They had killed a hundred-year spider and taken four hundred years out of the air, and now every beast within a kilometre could smell them.

"**Run,**" Wulin said.

They ran.

Lin Hao had run from things eleven times in the platform and it had always been the same arithmetic — how far, how fast, how long until the shape came apart. This time the arithmetic had a new term in it, and he found it while he was running, the way he found everything, which was too late to be useful and exactly on time.

**He had three rings.**

Two full and one empty. Thirty-one ranks. **Thirty-one ranks put him in a realm the other five were not in** — Wu Zhangkong had said it once, flatly, the way he said everything, that *the wall every ten ranks is not a bigger step, it is a different kind of thing*, and that the difference between rank twenty-one and rank thirty-one is not ten of anything. And the thing he understood, running third in a line of six children with a pride of the strongest beast race on the continent behind them, was that **he was the only one in the line who could have stood and fought.**

Not won. He was not stupid, and he had just watched a fourteen-year-old throw a two-metre spear that none of them could have survived, and he knew perfectly well what a pride of Blazing Demon Lions was.

But he could have **stood.** And the other five could not have.

*Perhaps if they all possessed three soul rings,* he thought, and did not finish the sentence, because the sentence ended in a place he was not going to look at while he was running.

---

## Part 4: What He Did Anyway

The shape came apart at the fifty-second minute, because shapes do.

Wang Jinxi went down over a root and did not get up fast enough, and the pride came around the left side of them in the way that prides do, and there was a lion between Wang Jinxi and the rest of them, and it was not a large one, and it was going to be on him in about two seconds.

Lin Hao stopped.

He did not decide to. He would think about that for a long time afterwards and never be entirely honest with himself about it, but the truth was that he did not decide — the shape had a hole in it, the hole was in the back, and he was the only one who could fill it.

He turned around and put the Stormbringer in front of him.

The wind went up.

Not the wind he had been making for two years, which had been a tool, which had been *weather he could point.* This was the wind from the crossing, and it came up out of the ground in a circle nine metres across, and it took the leaf litter and the small branches and the loose bark off the forest floor and put them in the air, and the lion that had been about to be on Wang Jinxi in two seconds ran into it and did not come out the other side.

Lin Hao stood in the middle of it with three rings around his feet — **two purple, one empty** — and the **marks on his forearms raised and glowing**, and the **hawk-gold lock** at his temple the colour of the sword, and he held the circle for eleven seconds, which was as long as he could hold it, and in those eleven seconds Wang Jinxi got up and the shape closed and the six of them got out.

He let the wind down.

His legs went. He caught himself on the sword, which was not a thing you were supposed to be able to do, and Xie Xie came back for him, because Xie Xie had stopped being the kind of person who ran first somewhere around the spider.

"You've got three rings," Xie Xie said, hauling him up.

"Yes."

"You've had three rings for forty minutes and you didn't **say.**"

"I said."

"You said it to *yourself.*" Xie Xie got his arm over his shoulder and started walking. "That's not saying. That's the most Lin Hao thing you have ever done and you have done a great many of them."

## Part 5: What Gu Yue Saw

They made the ejection at sixty-eight minutes, all six of them, and the light took them.

Outside, on the steps, in the ordinary grey afternoon of a city that was second of five in an alliance, Gu Yue sat down next to Lin Hao without being asked, which she had never done.

"You held it for eleven seconds," she said.

"Ten."

"Eleven. I was counting." She looked straight ahead, at nothing. "Your soul is not doing what a thousand-year soul does."

"I know."

"A thousand-year soul gives you more. It doesn't give you **weather.**" She turned her head a few degrees, which with her was the equivalent of turning around. "The boy with the ice staff threw a thousand-year soul skill and it was a spear. A *spear.* Two metres, patterns in the shaft, extremely good. That is what a thousand years is *supposed* to look like."

"And mine is wind."

"Yours is wind." Gu Yue considered it. "And you can see the tracks now, and your hair went gold to the root, and your marks came out of your skin, and I think—" she stopped, and Lin Hao understood that she was doing the thing she did not like doing, which was saying something she had not finished working out.

"I think you didn't cross a thousand years," she said. "I think something crossed a thousand years **and it was already more than that.**"

There was a silence.

"The hawk was nine hundred and sixty-four," Lin Hao said.

"Yes."

"And the spider gave me two hundred."

"Yes."

"So it was one thousand one hundred and sixty-four."

"Yes," Gu Yue said. "And I would like to know where the other part came from, because a hawk does not become weather, and yours did."

Linhao looked at his hands. The **marks on his forearms** were still raised, faintly, and they had not gone back under since the clearing, and he had stopped expecting them to.

"I don't know," he said.

"That's the first time you've said that to me," Gu Yue said, "and I believe you."

---

He did not tell her about the leaning.

He had not told anybody about the leaning — not the ninety-one seconds in the first hour, and not the thirty-ninth minute when the lions came and the hawk had turned toward the energy coming off them rather than away.

He wrote it in the ledger that night, in the column marked **WHAT THEY WANT**, and he did not cross it out, and he did not finish the line either, because he had learned two things in one day and the second one was that an unfinished line was sometimes the honest one.

**The hawk wants something. It is not the crossing. The crossing was forty minutes ago and it is still**

He stopped there.

Outside, the sun went down over Eastsea City, and a boy with **hawk-gold hair** and three rings sat at a window with a book on his knee, and forty-three metres away in a monitoring room that smelled of hot metal, a file was being written that would be read by somebody in a city in the centre region that held only two cities.

He did not know that either.

He was getting quite good at not knowing things.

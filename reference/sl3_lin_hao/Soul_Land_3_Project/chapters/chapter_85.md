## Part 1: The Bill

The Silver Moon Douluo had given them her second zero and her terms — *do well anyway, and the inner court despite it; if not, go back to where you came from* — and turned for the door, sleeve already flicking.

And the sixth spoke. First words of the day in her direction, in the voice he used for numbers, which the hall had now heard twice: once for a spring, once for a word.

"Well — I can't take it now."

Elder Cai stopped with her hand above the door.

"Your academy has shown nothing worthy enough." No heat in it at all; that was the terrible part. It was a reading, delivered the way he delivered iron. "The gate priced us by quota. The trials ran clean until the scoring got personal. And the arithmetic standing in front of you has been ignored since the wall." A pause exactly long enough. "So — I am not joking. Clearly."

The room did not breathe. Wulin's stomach dropped through the floor with the sure arithmetic of a boy watching his brother bill the continent's summit; Shen Yi stopped existing; Xie Xie forgot his own grievance the way you forget a splinter when the house catches fire. Gu Yue's head came up, and something in her face agreed with every word and would be admitting nothing.

The Silver Moon Douluo looked at him for three full seconds — the flint look, the one that had nine rings behind it and a red one on top.

"Zero was cheap, then," she said, and the door slammed behind her hard enough to move every paper in the room.

What nobody present saw, because the door was shut, was the smile forming on her lips out in the corridor.

*"Hehe. I bet that old fart Zhuo Shi is fuming right about now. Serves him right. Those brats are pretty good — tempering them a bit more should be fine."* She snorted at the thought of the girl. *"Since you dare to reject me, there will be a time when you come back begging."* Hands crossed behind her back, her steps were light as air as she departed.

The bill had been served to a woman already smiling. The ledger did not know that. Ledgers never do.

## Part 2: The Sixth Trial

Aggrieved, indignant, and down twenty points, class zero arrived at the site of the sixth trial — which was a room full of giant silver trays, and on the trays, mountains of **steamed buns.**

The sign explained everything and nothing. Fifteen buns: passing. Every additional five: one more point. Thirty minutes. **No soul power** — your own stomach or your honor.

They stood stupefied before the majesty of it. Then, as one, Xiaoyan's and Xie Xie's eyes slid to Tang Wulin.

Something flickered under the captain's calm mask — the flicker of a man seeing providence — and he turned to Shen Yi with a straight face. "Teacher Shen. Can we help each other eat for this trial?"

Shen Yi, still depressed, replied grumpily: "If you can eat enough to score full points, then you can help the others."

The trial made a strange kind of sense, for a monstrous academy. It tested willpower — eat past the body's limits, no shortcuts, medical staff on standby for the examinees who discovered that this innocent little trial was a devil in disguise, dragging people through the depths of hell and making them wish for the sweet release of death.

Today, an irregularity had appeared.

"Thank you, Teacher Shen," Tang Wulin said, sincere as a prayer. In his mind, this trial had been made for him — the morning's meager portion, the blood essence spent across four trials, the hunger that had been filing complaints since the guillotines. A mountain of buns was the most beautiful scene he had ever witnessed. He took two in each hand and began.

Xie Xie put his hands behind his head and leaned back, leisurely waiting. Xiaoyan sat down cross-legged to meditate. Gu Yue was the only one who picked up a bun, to accompany him.

"What are those two doing?" Shen Yi frowned. "Why aren't they starting?"

"Teacher Shen," Xie Xie said, smiling. "Just wait and see."

## Part 3: The Rice Tub

Eight buns vanished before she had finished the sentence.

"Eat slower—" Shen Yi began, maternal. "It doesn't matter how fast, as long as you eat enough; if you eat too quickly your digestion will—"

Tang Wulin snatched four more and put away two of them before the sentence found its end.

"Teacher Shen," Xie Xie said, smirking now, "you don't need to worry about our captain. His greatest strength isn't his combat ability — it's his stomach. He ate our dining hall into poverty. What are a few steamed buns to him?"

Shen Yi then witnessed what it truly meant to be called a rice bucket. No — a rice *tub.*

The captain calculated as he ate: fifteen buns was six points, twenty was seven, and thirty-five of the fifty on a tray was the full ten. At the eight-minute mark he had inhaled his own thirty-five, and sauntered to Xiaoyan's tray as if the record had been a warm-up: thirty-five more in seven minutes. Then Xie Xie's. By twenty-two minutes the total stood at a hundred and five, his belly had not bulged by a hair, and Shen Yi's expression had completed its journey — surprise, shock, dumbfoundedness, stupification — while the supervising examiner, who in all his long years at this trial had never seen such a glutton, quietly stopped pretending to supervise and simply watched.

No soul power. She was certain of it. That was the part that kept her staring.

At the twenty-eight minute mark, the captain finished Gu Yue's thirty-five for her and kept going, disappointed that the tray was running out of runway. "Time is up!" the stupefied examiner just barely remembered to call. Forty-eight of fifty. Two lonely buns survived.

"Teacher Shen." Tang Wulin patted his belly, wistful. "We got full marks, right?"

Shen Yi nodded and turned to leave, because her legs wanted to be anywhere else while her mind ran the phrase *was he always such a monster — is his stomach a bottomless pit* in circles.

"Teacher Shen, can I take the leftover steamed buns with me? I didn't eat breakfast this morning..."

She stumbled. She waved: do as you wish. And the boy swept every surviving bun into bags and into his storage ring, and followed her out wearing the most contented smile of the examination season, his blood essence nearly replenished on starch and joy.

At his own tray, the sixth worked through thirty-five buns — the full ten — unhurried, the last one half-cooled at twenty-nine of the thirty minutes: perfect, and not a minute wasted being perfect. The margin ate like the margin: everything the arithmetic required, on time, and not one bite past it.

## Part 4: The Report

In a luxurious room, Elder Cai listened to the middle-aged examiner's report with her face contorting into a strange expression.

"All four got full points for the sixth trial?"

"Yes, full marks. They even took the leftovers with them. It seems the majority was eaten by Tang Wulin."

She sat with that a moment. Somewhere behind the flint, a ledger of her own was adding a column to a bill a thirteen-year-old had served her an hour before — the polite one, with the three pages and the sword-line and the word she did not have.

*Nothing worthy enough,* he had said. *So I am not joking. Clearly.*

"Tempering," she said to the empty room, in the tone of a woman confirming a forecast. "A bit more should be fine."

The run, meanwhile, had turned simple again, which was the only way the class liked it: thirty points banked through three trials, twenty burned by temper, forty won back on starch. **Four trials left. The outer court asking sixty. The inner court asking eighty-five — reachable now through exactly one door, and the door had a name, and the name had already smiled on them twice without their knowing.**

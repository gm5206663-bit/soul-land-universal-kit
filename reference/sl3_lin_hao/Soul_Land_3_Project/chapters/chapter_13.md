## Part 1: The Slate Was Wrong

Class two came onto the platform three times.

That was the first thing the stands understood, and it took them a moment, because the three boys walking out were the same boy — the same jaw, the same sharp eyes, the same flat calm — copied twice, in the same uniform, with one white ring apiece waiting under their feet. Triplets. The grade had known it all week and had not, until this moment, understood what it *meant:* three faces that had spent ten years learning to think in the same direction, three bodies drilled by a teacher for months until, as the older students in the stands put it, they flew like one bird with three sets of wings.

Gu Tianri walked at the point. Gu Tianyue and Gu Tianming walked at his shoulders. None of them looked at the stands.

"Three-rings between them," Wulin murmured, echoing his own slate, and Xie Xie — who had written the other half of it, the half that said *speed line, they press early* — cracked his knuckles with the confidence of a boy who had watched class two lose to class one and drawn exactly the lesson a spectator draws.

"They press early," Xie Xie agreed. "I'll take the press."

"Begin," said Long Hengxu.

And class two went up.

Not a jump. Not a leap. The three of them simply left the platform the way three thoughts leave a mind — together, without discussion — and soul-light unfolded from their backs in three pairs of wings that were the same soul three times, and they rose until they were standing on the morning the way other people stand on floors, ten meters up, in a triangle, calm as a diagram.

The stands made the sound of a grade doing arithmetic and not liking the answer.

"They didn't press," Xie Xie said.

"No," Lin Hao said. His eyes were up, already walking the sky the way they walked a forge — finding the grain. "The slate watched them lose. Class one made them press, because pressing was the only thing left to try against a fusion. This—" his chin lifted a fraction at the patient triangle, "—is them not losing. They don't need to press. They own the sky. Every second we spend down here, we're standing in their room, and they're waiting for us to jump."

Because that was the read, and it had taken him four seconds: the triangle wasn't an attack. It was a *door,* left open on purpose. Fly at them — anyone, any way — and the fight became theirs at the exact moment your momentum died, because everything about a falling body is a confession. Their first soul skill didn't strike. It made them *fast,* and fast is only a weapon when the other person is slow — and nothing is slower than a boy at the top of a jump with nothing under his feet.

Up in the visiting corner of the stands, a woman in a class-two teacher's coat watched with her arms folded, and her name was Ye Yingrong, and she had trained that triangle for months, and she was, at this moment, entirely at peace. She had watched class five's previous matches. The forge-boy was strong and nothing more; the girl seemed to carry water in her hands; the dagger-boy was the only real threat, and the dagger-boy could not fly. The correct play against flyers was patience — stay down, stay defended, wait for the drain, because wings ate soul power like a furnace ate coal. She had drilled the waiting into her triplets herself. If class five stayed on the ground, the match would be long, and ugly, and hers. If class five charged the sky—

Their success was assured. It was that simple. She almost felt sorry for them.

Down on the platform, Lin Hao sheathed the read and opened his mouth, and three heads turned, because nine weeks and three matches had built a habit that no amount of being the fourth could erode: when the forge-boy said something at walking volume, the world was about to change shape.

"They're waiting for us to jump," he said. "So nobody jumps. Wulin — Xie Xie, when the sky opens, and *only* when. Gu Yue — stones for his feet, blue ones, the kind that hold. I'll go up first and give them something to look at."

"You'll go *up—*" Xie Xie began.

But Lin Hao had already drawn.

## Part 2

He came down out of the sky with his hair standing out behind him, storm-light at the tips, and the whole lane looked up, and afterwards nobody could agree on what they had seen except that the hair had not been that colour before the tournament.
: The Sword Makes a Road

The Stormbringer came out of the air the way it always did, and the stands — which had learned to count, which had learned the ten seconds and the twenty-one — went quiet in the particular way that meant they were about to have to learn something else.

Lin Hao did not cut at the triangle. He cut at the empty air two meters in front of himself — one clean, economical stroke, the way you'd open a seam in hot steel — and the wind along the blade's path did not disperse. It *stayed.* The Gale Talon's principle, laid not along an edge this time but along a line in space: compressed air, a shelf of it, hard as a plank for as long as he could pay for it. He stepped onto nothing, and nothing held.

The stands made no sound at all.

He cut again. Higher. Stepped again. The wind answered the sword the way it answered everything he held — because the sword asked in the hawk's own language, and the sky, it turned out, was fluent. Left foot, right foot, the blade sweeping a stair out of the morning, and Lin Hao climbed it the way he climbed the forge stairs at home: like it was there, like it had always been there, like the only remarkable thing was that anyone had ever used stairs for anything else.

It cost. That was the part the stands couldn't see, and the part he counted with his breath: every shelf a slice of soul power, every step a subtraction, the Spirit Connection's hundred-and-twenty going down like a waterline. By the fourth step his forearms were warm. By the sixth, sweat had found his collar. This was not the hawk's flight — the hawk gave nothing here, sat silent on its far water, watching. This was his. Steel and wind and arithmetic, a boy paying for the sky one cut at a time.

And the sky, as advertised, belonged to class two — and class two had months of drilling and one clear instruction for anything that came up: *answer it together.*

Gu Tianming folded his wings and came down at him.

The dive was the speed skill lit all at once — a white line out of the sun, angled to meet the climbing boy at the exact place a climbing boy would be — and the hawk-eyes saw it coming early enough to admire, and the sword was already moving, and it was still not early enough, because the air under Lin Hao's feet was his own work and the kick that arrived came through the shelf like a hammer through a window.

The shelf broke. Lin Hao fell.

Two meters of sky went past him — arms numb from the wrist up where the kick had landed on the Stormbringer's flat, the platform a long way down, the triangle re-forming above with the calm of something that had just confirmed a lesson. Ye Yingrong, in the stands, permitted herself the smallest nod. This was the moment. This was the confession.

The sword went into the empty air beneath him — not a slash, a *seam* — and the wind caught the edge the way it caught everything he asked of it, and **Wind-Step** fired on a shelf no wider than a boot, and the fall became a step, and the step became a climb, and Lin Hao went back up the sky like a sentence that had decided to continue.

The road was not under his feet. The road was in his hand.

And now the triangle had looked at him, and now the triangle had *answered* him, and now — this was the whole arithmetic, this was the four seconds' read coming home — the triangle was no longer a triangle. Gu Tianming had broken to meet him and was recovering low. Gu Tianyue had slid to cover his brother and was wide. And Gu Tianri, at the point, was alone at the top of a diagram that had stopped being a diagram, watching the sword-boy come up the air at him with a face like a locked gate.

Lin Hao did not go up at him. That was Xie Xie's door, and the sky was open now, and promises were promises.

He went up at the *sky.* The Stormbringer came around in a long, flat arc — Gale Talon running the full length of the steel, the sword's own voice given teeth — and the wind-edge did not seek a boy. It sought the lift under Gu Tianming's left wing, and it *cut,* and the air that had been holding the diving boy up simply stopped being there for half a meter. Tianming pitched. Wings hammered. The speed skill flared and flared and found nothing to be fast *in,* because you cannot fly through a hole. He wrenched sideways, out of the recovery, out of formation, out of the diagram — a bird thrown off a wire.

He didn't cut the flyers. He cut the flyers' sky.

"Xie Xie," said Lin Hao, at walking volume, standing on nothing, "now."

Below — and the stands would argue about the timing for a year, and the timing was perfect — Tang Wulin's hands closed on Xie Xie's waist, and the forge turned, and class two's fastest went into the air like a thrown knife.

What followed, class five would retell until the words wore smooth. A flash of blue light under the dagger-boy's feet — the girl's hands moving below, ice balls forming and freezing in the air like stones across a stream — and Xie Xie's foot tapping the blue light, and the borrowed force bending his fall into a *rise,* and Gu Tianri at the top of the sky discovering, too late, that the patient game only works while the other side is on the ground. The Light Dragon Dagger came out of the climb in a golden line that closed the escape, and Xie Xie was there — knife to the side, left hand pulling the flyer's leg, body turning over in the air like a mirage — and the flat of the dagger tapped Gu Tianri on the head with a small, polite note.

"Out, Gu Tianri," said Long Hengxu from the ground, and added, for the record, in the voice of a man filing something he intended to think about later, that a moment ago, if the dagger-boy had intended it, the eldest brother's throat would have been open.

Three bundles of blue light bloomed in the air. Xie Xie connected the three points, flipped three times between them — the stands' noise went thin and high, the sound of people watching something their vocabulary had no shelf for — and came out of the last flip beside Gu Tianyue with the dagger resting against his head, and Gu Tianyue, who was not a boy who liked to die, descended.

And Gu Tianming — wing still wobbling where the sword had taken his sky, formation long gone, months of drilled unison scattered — did the only thing left that was his: folded what lift he had and threw himself at the descending girl, because a flyer who comes down empty-handed has to answer for it, and she was right there, slow in her green light, and someone had to be defeated—

The sword was already there. It had been there since the pitch. One more cut, laid not under the wing this time but *along* the dive — the wind-edge turning the air into a chute, a slope, a suggestion the diving boy could not refuse — The desperate charge arrived at Tang Wulin's waiting grass two meters off its line and half its speed. The Bluesilver Grass took Gu Tianming around the waist the way a river takes a stone, and pulled, and the flyer came down out of his own element and onto the platform in a heap, and Wulin's other arm was already around Gu Yue, setting her on her feet.

"First grade's class five wins."

Long Hengxu's voice had a note in it. The stands, given a moment to find their feet, used them.

The four of them stood on the platform in the noise — the forge, the light, the flood learning to be a cup, and the fourth boy coming down his dissolving stair one cut at a time, sword last to sleep. The counting that had become the grade's religion ran one more time and came out wrong in the way it always did now. You could count the rings (four; two on one boy). You could count the seconds. But there was no arithmetic yet built for a swordsman who had *walked up* into a flying match and taken the sky away from people who were born in it.

"What do you mean, *flying soul masters?*" a second-year was already shouting, delighted, in the row behind class five's own twenty-two. "You saw him! He didn't fly! He *walked!*"

## Part 3: What Ye Yingrong Saw

Ye Yingrong did not hear him.

She was standing very still with her arms still folded, running the match back through herself at the speed of a woman who had spent months building something and two minutes watching it come apart. The dive had been correct. The waiting had been correct. The read of class five had been correct — *was* correct, she'd watched their matches, the forge-boy was strong and nothing more, the girl carried water, the dagger-boy couldn't fly—

The dagger-boy couldn't fly. The dagger-boy had never touched the ground after the first throw, and had ended the match in the air, on ice that hadn't existed until the girl's hands asked for it, three times, at the exact coordinates of a boy in motion. And the fourth one — the one the grade's gossip had wrapped in weather — had not flown either. He had *built.* She had watched him pay for every step, watched the kick break his footing, watched the fall that was supposed to be the end of the argument become a footnote, and she understood, with a teacher's precision, exactly what she had watched: they hadn't executed a plan. They had *improvised* one, mid-air, around a boy climbing a road that didn't exist until he cut it — and the coordination had held. Why did the coordination hold? Who were these children?

"Congratulations, Teacher Wu." She had crossed to him without noticing the decision. Her voice came out level; she was proud of it. "You will be in class two now."

"That is not my goal," Wu Zhangkong said mildly, and inclined his head a fraction — the smallest bow that could still be called one — and turned to follow his students off the platform, and gave them no words at all.

And Ye Yingrong went back up the teachers' wall and wrote her assessment, and the assessment was wrong, and she would not find out for two years, and the reason she was wrong is the whole of the butterfly.

She had scored the match. Three flyers, speed souls, Ye Yingrong's own training behind them, and class five had taken them apart in the air, which was not supposed to be possible, and she had scored it correctly: **the dagger-boy is fast, the grass-boy owns ground, the girl owns weather, and the boy with the sword is the one who decided where the other three would be.**

That is a complete assessment. It is also the assessment of a class that does not contain him — because what she had actually watched was not four students coordinating. It was **one student reading three flyers faster than three flyers could fly,** and the other three arriving at the places he had already put them. She had scored the arrival and missed the reading, because the reading does not look like anything from the wall.

**In a class without him, that match is a loss and Ye Yingrong's assessment is correct.** In a class with him in it, the match is a win and her assessment is a description of the furniture.

"Is Teacher Wu pleased with us or not?" Xie Xie demanded of the world at large, suspiciously, as the four of them came down the steps.

"How would I know?" said Wulin. "I'm guessing we'll need to summarize the fight, though."

"We shouldn't have gone up first." Gu Yue delivered the verdict while the stands were still shouting, in the tone of someone reading a ledger aloud. "If we'd held the ground, they'd have had to come to us, and wings eat soul power — we could have waited them out. Instead we put our fastest and our heaviest problem into their element on an improvised plan with *three seconds'* notice. It worked. It shouldn't have worked. Next time, we wait."

"Next time," Xie Xie said, "you'll be throwing me at a *dragon,* and I'd like to practice."

Up in the emptying stands, Ye Yingrong took out a soul communicator and stood for a moment with it in her hand, watching the four below — the girl already arguing tactics, the dagger-boy already boasting, the forge-boy apologizing sincerely for his own impulsiveness in the middle of being *right,* and the fourth one walking at their edge with a sheathed sword and a face that, she noted with the detached irritation of a woman cataloguing facts, was going to be a problem for someone's concentration in about four years.

She punched in a number.

"What is it, little sister?" The voice that answered was pure and cold and two years patient.

"Big sister," Ye Yingrong said. "We lost."

A pause. "You lost? Don't tell me the opponents were also Flying Soul Masters?"

"Not at all. It's just that their coordination is exceptional, and their team is comprised of outstanding individuals." She watched the swordsman below say something at walking volume that made the dagger-boy sputter. "Big sister, you need to be careful. Your class one might run into worthy opponents this time. The students of that Icily Arrogant Prince Charming aren't easy to handle. You definitely need to be prepared."

The communicator was silent for a moment. When the cold voice came back, the patience in it had developed an edge.

"I've been waiting a long time for this day. That time, he humiliated me — but this time, I'll return the favor."

"Big sister, you—"

But her sister had already hung up, and Ye Yingrong let out a long sigh to no one, and to two years of a grudge she had watched from this exact side of the argument. "You really are too competitive. He wasn't in the wrong when he rejected you. Yet you've held it for two years already."

## Part 4: The Chair

They celebrated at the first window, because Gu Yue had said *one meal* in the voice that made it law, and because Xie Xie had immediately bet her the meal that class one would last longer than class two had, and because the first window's auntie had started giving class five extra ladle without being asked and without, so far, admitting it.

"Walked," Xie Xie said, around a mouthful, pointing his chopsticks at Lin Hao with the air of a man presenting evidence at a trial. "*Walked.* Up. Into a *flying match.* Do you know what the stands are calling you now? Not even a name yet. Just — *he walked.* Like it's a place. Like the sky's a neighborhood."

"Someone has to open the door," Lin Hao said, "before someone else can show off through it."

"I did *not—* the ice was *load-bearing—*"

"The ice was beautiful," Lin Hao conceded, and Wulin's laugh and Gu Yue's almost-smile arrived at the same instant, and Xie Xie sat back, deeply satisfied, having—as he saw it—won the meal without paying for it.

Later, on the roof, the harbor did its nightly accounting of lights, and Wulin's slate came out (class one: *the dragon goes spectral a quarter at a time — physical blows pass. The eagle folds over him. The fusion made a sound. Do not let them touch.*), and the argument about the top of the ladder went on until the lamps on the far seawall blinked their midnight shift, and none of it — none of the tactics, the boasting, the honest slow searching for words — was the thing Lin Hao carried back down the dormitory stairs and into the dark of room twelve and set down, carefully, like a coal still lit.

Except that on the way past room eleven, he stopped.

Because he heard it again — the settling. The note. Three tournament matches in four days, a bear's momentum turned through his arms, a flyer pulled out of his own sky, a dragon's bell humming in the next room of his bones, nine weeks of a monster for a sparring partner and a hammer and a grass that had finally learned to be one tool — and the sum of it, at last, came through the wall in the deep quiet spiral of a boy who sat down into meditation the way he sat down into everything: fully. Soul Master rank fifteen, arrived without a sound, the way forged things arrive.

Lin Hao knocked once on the doorframe with his knuckle. "Fifteen."

From inside, in the dark, a grin you could hear: "Your ears are *unnatural.*"

"Dinner's on you tomorrow, rank fifteen." And then, quieter, because it was true and therefore worth saying exactly once: "You know you reached it in half the time the grade thinks is possible. Half. Keep the ledger honest."

"The ledger's *always* honest—" But the forge-boy was laughing, and the Goldsong under his sleeve was stirring warm hellos at a rank it had been climbing toward since it was a ten-year scrap of a soul in a shop window, and the second seal behind his sternum pressed once, hard, like a shoulder against a door, and was told — not unkindly — *not yet.*

And in room twelve, in the dark, the thing Lin Hao had actually been carrying all day finally found its shelf.

He had spent the morning in the hawk's element.

He had climbed the sky without it. Taken a kick that broke his footing and stood — no: *stepped* — back up. Cut the lift out from under a flyer with the sword's own voice. And through all of it, the seven-hundred-and-sixty-year silence on its far water had not moved, had not answered, had not once said *careful*—

It had *watched.* Not the watching of the last year — the leading, the lending, the older eyes doing the seeing. This had been the other kind. The kind a hawk does from a high branch while something young and foolish and promising learns the wind on its own wings. The chair in the room of his sleep had not moved, and the question under it — *can you see, without me?* — had not been asked again, and had not, he understood now, needed to be.

Two days ago the answer would have been an argument. Today it was a morning's worth of footprints, dissolving behind him down the sky.

Lin Hao lay back on the narrow bed and looked at nothing in particular, and somewhere in the spiritual sea under it all, a very old bird folded one wing, and the silence — for the first time since the question — felt less like a held breath than like a nod.

Not an answer. Not yet.

But the top of the ladder was a dragon. Tomorrow the grade would attend class one the way you attend weather. And somewhere across the city a cold voice, two years patient, had decided to return a favor.

The fourth boy of class five closed his eyes with the sword's road still singing in his arms, and slept the way he had started sleeping lately: like a room where someone had pulled up a chair, and was, tonight, watching him sleep with something that a kinder vocabulary than his might have called *pride.*


Rank twenty-five arrived that night, in the deep quiet after the third match, the way forged things arrive — not with a sound, with a settling. Three tournament matches in four days, a bear's momentum turned through his arms, a flyer pulled out of his own sky: the soul had taken all of it and made a rank out of it.

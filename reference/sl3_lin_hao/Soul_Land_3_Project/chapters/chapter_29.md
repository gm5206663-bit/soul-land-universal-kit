## Part 1: The Triangle

They had it. That was the thing Lin Hao would remember afterwards, and the thing nobody who watched it ever quite believed.

Three children who had been alive for ten years had a thousand-year Crystal Bear in a triangle, and the bear did not know which of them to kill.

It stomped in the centre of its own shredded clearing and turned, and every time it turned one of them moved, and the moving was not panic — it was *agreed.* Wulin was calling it out loud, because he had stopped being able to hold it in his head: belly, eyes, rear. Xie Xie had come out of the shadows entirely, both daggers in his hands, the light one catching the green and the dark one not being there at all, and he put two blades into the bear's belly and the bear decided about him.

It came at him in strides that ate ground.

And Gu Yue filled its eyes with fire.

Not the one great fireball she had used on the Horned Dragon. Dozens, and small, and everywhere at once, and the bear threw a paw up and ducked its head, and ducking its head was the whole of what Wulin had needed, because he had spent the last nine seconds climbing a tree with a strand of grass and the entire rest of his soul power.

Lin Hao watched him come down.

He watched the right hammer stay back and the left come up in front of the chest, and he watched the gold run to the wrist, and he understood — with the flat, arithmetic-free certainty of a boy who has stood at an anvil long enough to know what a committed strike sounds like — that there was nothing behind it. Wulin had emptied himself into one blow, and if the blow did not land, there would be no second one, and there would not be time to be sorry.

A green wind blade went past with a whistle that hurt the teeth.

Gu Yue had made it to hide the sound. Lin Hao would think about that for a week: that she had spent something she did not have on a *noise,* because the noise was worth more than the damage.

Then the hammer came down on the neck, and the neck made a sound like a door being broken.

## Part 2

He could see the tracks now. Not clearly, and not always, but when he was tired and his eyes went soft there were faint lines in the air where soul power had moved, the way a boat leaves a wake. He had never mentioned it, because there had been nobody to mention it to who would not have asked him to prove it.
: What Came Out of the Bear

The bear fell.

It fell head-first, which is how big things fall, and Xie Xie made a noise that was not a word, and for about two seconds the clearing was the happiest place in the forest.

Then the light came out of it.

Yellow, and one metre across, and it did not spread and it did not fade — it simply *chose,* and what it chose was Wulin, and Lin Hao was forty metres away and running and knowing perfectly well that he was going to be too late, because the light was faster than any of them and had already made up its mind.

"No—"

That was Gu Yue. She had nothing left. Lin Hao had watched her spend it — the teleports, the tornado of leaves, the fireballs, the wind blade that was only a noise — and she had nothing left, and she flashed silver anyway and got there anyway, and put her arms under a boy who had become a statue and weighed as much as a statue, and the two Heavy Silver hammers were still in his hands.

They went down together.

Lin Hao heard the thud from forty metres and it did not sound like a person landing.

The crystal is frail. That was the whole horror of it and he understood it while he was still running: crystal does not bend, so crystal breaks, and a boy made of crystal falling onto forest floor becomes a great many pieces of boy, and this was a training platform, and a training platform was supposed to be safe, and the part of his mind that kept files had already found the entry — *residual effects. Possible permanent injury.* — and had underlined it.

Gu Yue was underneath him.

She had her arms locked and her face turned sideways and her eyes shut, and her face had gone the colour of old paper, and she was holding a thousand kilograms of frozen boy off the ground with her shoulders and her spine and nothing else.

## Part 3: The Harsher Path

Xie Xie got Wulin off her.

That was the first thing he did and it was the right first thing, and Lin Hao saw him do it and thought, *good,* because he had been afraid — genuinely afraid, for the first time in the forest — that Xie Xie would do the dramatic thing instead of the correct thing. He did not. He lifted, he dragged, he rolled the crystal boy onto his own side, and then he pulled Gu Yue out from under, and then he looked at her face and made a decision in the space of one breath.

He slapped the button on the back of her hand.

She went into light and was gone.

"You—" Lin Hao began, arriving.

"She was dying," Xie Xie said, without turning around. "Say it if you want, say it fast, I don't have long."

Lin Hao did not say it. He said the other thing. "Wulin's button."

"Can't reach it." Xie Xie's voice was flat and much too steady. "He's *crystal,* Hao. I'd have to break him to get to his wrist. I checked. I checked three times." He was breathing hard and he was not looking at anything in particular. "So. That's the arithmetic of it. One of them gets out and one of them doesn't, and I'm the one who gets to stand here and be useless next to the one who doesn't."

The thicket started to rustle.

Lin Hao heard it and Xie Xie heard it, and neither of them said anything for a moment, because saying it would have made it real, and then it was real anyway.

They came out of the green one at a time, which was worse than all at once. Dark fur along the spine. Amber eyes with nothing behind them. Ten of them, maybe twelve, and half of them peeled off toward the Horned Dragon's corpse with the professional indifference of things that had eaten their whole lives off other people's fights, and the other half did not.

The other half came toward the boy made of glass and the boy with two daggers.

**Green Wolves.** Ten-year soul beasts. Scavengers. Lin Hao's mind offered the entry without being asked — *pack, violent, will reach a hundred years on carrion if you let them* — and he pushed it aside, because the entry was not useful and the next ninety seconds were.

Xie Xie knelt.

He knelt down beside Wulin in the shredded grass and put the two daggers up and started breathing on purpose, in and out, slow, the way a person does when they have decided something and are getting their body to agree.

"Xie Xie," Lin Hao said.

"Don't."

"You've got nothing left. You spent it on the belly."

"I know what I've got." Xie Xie's jaw was set and his eyes were bright and wet at the same time, which was an expression Lin Hao had never once seen on him. "My father told me a true man can hold up heaven and earth. And men are loyal, and they face it, and they don't leave their comrades. Even for the price of the life." He laughed, once, sharply. "I'm ten. I've never done anything like this. I'm a newborn calf, he'd say, I'm not even a *calf,* I'm the idea of a calf. But I'm not going anywhere."

Lin Hao looked at him.

And the thing is — and this is the part he thought about for a year afterwards — he had arrived *after.* He had been forty metres away and running for a long time, and he had watched the button get slapped and the girl go into light, and he had stood there while Xie Xie knelt down and made his decision, and he had not said a single word to stop it.

He could have. He was faster. He could have shouted from the treeline, *run, you idiot, he's crystal and you're empty,* and Xie Xie would have listened, because Xie Xie always listened, and it would have been correct.

He had not shouted.

Because some things are a person's to decide, and a friend who takes that away from you has taken something more expensive than your life.

So Lin Hao drew the Stormbringer with his right hand and the forge blade with his left, and stepped past Xie Xie, and put himself between the kneeling boy and the amber eyes, and said, over his shoulder, "For the record. I think you're wrong."

"I know you think I'm wrong."

"You've got no soul power, no plan, and you're kneeling next to a statue."

"I know."

"And you're staying."

"I'm staying," Xie Xie said.

"Right," Lin Hao said. "Good. Then I'm staying too, and one of us should probably mention that this is the worst idea we've ever had, and I want it said that I said so out loud, in front of a witness, before it happened."

## Part 4: What the Sword Did

The first wolf came in on his left, and he killed it, and the second came in on his right, and he killed it, and the third did something he had not seen a wolf do.

It stopped.

Six feet out, in the open, with its head low, and it *stopped,* and the two behind it fanned wider instead of following, and Lin Hao — who had fought a pack that learned, in this same forest, ninety minutes ago — understood instantly and coldly that these were not the clever kind. These were simply the kind that had eaten well and were not in a hurry.

Which made them worse. A pack that learns can be lied to. A pack that is patient cannot.

"Xie Xie," he said. "How long until he thaws."

"I don't *know.*"

"Guess."

"Five minutes. Maybe ten. Maybe he doesn't and I'm talking to a lamp."

"Then we need five minutes," Lin Hao said, and the fourth wolf came, and he did a thing.

He did not decide to do it. That was the strange part, and the part he could not afterwards put in any order. The wolf came at his sword-side, and the forge blade was in his left hand and the left hand was *busy,* and the correct move — the move in the ledger, the move with a name — required the right hand to change grip, and there was not time to change grip, and so his right hand did not.

The Stormbringer went out one-handed and low and *backwards,* across his own body, from the wrong shoulder, at an angle that no form he had ever learned contained, and it went into the wolf's throat on the way past, and the wolf went down behind him rather than in front of him.

He had not aimed it. He had not chosen it. He had, for approximately one third of a second, not been doing the thing at all.

"WHAT—" Xie Xie said, from the ground.

"Shut up," Lin Hao said, and the fifth wolf came.

He got five minutes. That is the honest account of it: five minutes, twelve wolves, one statue, one empty boy, and a sword that at some point stopped being held and started being *used,* and by the end he was not counting them any more because counting required a part of him that was busy, and the part that was busy was not thinking, and the not-thinking was, he discovered, extremely good at this.

The last wolf left. They always leave. Carrion eaters do not pay for meat.

Lin Hao stood in the clearing with both arms hanging uselessly at his sides and blood on him that was perhaps a third his own, and behind him there was a sound like a window being unmade, and Wulin came out of the crystal the way a man comes out of deep water — all at once, and gasping, and furious.

"—TOLD YOU TO RETREAT!" Wulin shouted, at nobody, at the sky, still holding both hammers.

"We did not retreat," Xie Xie said, from the ground, where he had lain down at some point without mentioning it.

"I can see that," Wulin said.

## Part 5: Comrades

Wu Zhangkong was in the forest.

That was not supposed to be possible, and Lin Hao said so, and Wu Zhangkong said, "It isn't," and did not elaborate, and the two of them understood each other perfectly.

He looked at the crystal-broken ground, and the twelve wolves, and the girl-shaped absence where Gu Yue had gone into light, and he looked at Wulin, who was sitting down hard and holding his right wrist, and at Xie Xie, who could not currently be moved, and at Lin Hao, who was standing because sitting down would have involved a decision about his legs.

"Whose idea was the triangle," Wu Zhangkong said.

"Mine," Wulin said. "It was — I don't know. I could see where they needed to be."

"And the neck."

"It was the only place."

"And you bet everything you had on one strike." Wu Zhangkong's voice did not change, which was how you knew. "With two people depending on you being able to fight afterwards."

Wulin looked at the ground. "It worked."

"It worked," Wu Zhangkong agreed. "And if her fireball had been a half-second late you would have been a statue in front of a bear with its neck half-broken and two children with nothing left. Do you understand that I am not angry about the strike. I am angry that you did not *count* it first." He turned away, because that was how he ended things. "Both of you are foolish. Both of you are also the reason I have not slept properly in four months. Get your comrade."

Lin Hao and Wulin carried Xie Xie between them, which was undignified and necessary, and Xie Xie complained the entire way, which was how they knew he would live.

They pressed the button at the edge of the sector, all three of them at once, and the forest went away.

---

Xie Xie was on a bench with his feet up and a blanket he had not asked for.

A man in white came along first with a plate, because that was the procedure and the procedure did not care what anyone had just been through. Lin Hao put his hand on it without looking, and the dial went round and settled, and the man wrote the number on his board and moved on to Wulin without comment.

**two hundred and five.**

Lin Hao looked at it. He had been at two hundred and eight when he went in, and he had not sat down once since, and he had not cultivated, and he had not meditated, and there had not been a single moment in the last hour that felt like growth.

"Seven," he said, to nobody.

"That's all?" Xie Xie said.

"That's all."

"You fought twelve wolves and carried two people and I think you killed something with your *face* at one point, and you got *seven.*"

"It's a good seven," Lin Hao said. "It happened while I wasn't looking."

Wu Zhangkong was waiting in the corridor outside the box room, and he did not let them go.

"You did a thing in that clearing," he said, to Lin Hao.

"I did several things."

"You did one." Wu Zhangkong put his hands behind his back. "A backhand. One-handed. Off the wrong shoulder, through your own centre, at an angle that is not in any school I have ever heard of, and you did it because the correct grip was a third of a second away and you did not have a third of a second." He looked at Lin Hao for a long moment. "Do you know what you did."

"No," Lin Hao said. "I've been meaning to mention that. I don't know what I did, I can't do it again — I tried, in the corridor, I looked like a man swatting a bee — and I'd like to know whether I should be worried."

"You should not be able to do it again." Wu Zhangkong said it the way he said everything, flatly, and it landed anyway. "That is the point of it. A form is a sentence you have said before. What you did was not a form. You were past the forms, because you had no time for them, and the sword said the only thing left to say." He turned to go, and stopped. "Your homework was the name of a style and the first form of a style. You gave me both. And then you went into a forest and did the thing the style is *for,* without knowing you were doing it."

"When do I get it back," Lin Hao said. "The stroke. When do I get it back."

"When you stop trying to," Wu Zhangkong said, and left, and Lin Hao stood in the corridor with his arms shaking and a bandaged thigh and a great deal to think about.

Xie Xie was on a bench with his feet up and a blanket he had not asked for.

"Hao."

"Yes."

"You came back."

"I was there the whole time."

"No." Xie Xie looked at him. "After. You carried Wulin out. I watched you. And then you *came back in,* for me, and you didn't have to, and I want you to know that I noticed, because I was on the ground pretending to be unconscious and I noticed." He pulled the blanket up. "You could have gone out. You'd already done the useful part."

"I know."

"So why."

Lin Hao sat down on the bench. His legs had, at last, made their decision, and it was that they were finished.

"Because I've thought about this," he said. "I gave a whole speech about it, actually, I gave it to you, in the forge, and you were extremely rude about it. A friend supports you and tells you the truth, and does both, and those aren't in order — they're at the same time. So: I told you it was the worst idea we'd ever had. That was the truth part. And then I stood in front of the wolves." He leaned his head back against the wall. "The supporting part doesn't come with a condition where you have to be right. That's the whole thing. That's the *entire* thing. If I only stood in front of the wolves when you had a good plan, I'd be a colleague."

Xie Xie was quiet for a while.

"Smithy School," he said, at last.

"I will end you."

"*Smithy School.*" He was grinning, and his eyes were wet, and the corridor was cold, and down at the end of it a man in Spirit Pagoda grey was watching the three of them from a doorway and writing something on a card he did not put in the ledger.


Eight hundred and eighty-five years of condensed wind turned over in him, and for the first time he thought he could smell rain in it that was not there.

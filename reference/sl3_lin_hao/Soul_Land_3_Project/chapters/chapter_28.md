## Part 1: The Letter

Xie Xie came to the forge at half past ten at night, which was not a thing he did.

He came because the side forge had a light on, and because he had walked past the dormitory twice already without going in, and because a boy who has been carrying a folded piece of paper in his pocket for six hours eventually goes to the one person he does not have to perform for.

Lin Hao was at the anvil. Not working — the fire was banked and the bar was cold — but standing at the anvil the way other people stand at a window, with his hands flat on it, thinking.

"You're doing the thing," Xie Xie said.

"What thing."

"The standing-at-it-without-hitting-it thing. My father does that. It means you've got a problem and you'd rather look at metal than at the problem."

"That's not what it means."

"That's *exactly* what it means." Xie Xie sat down on the coal sack, which was the only seat, and which he had claimed by right of arrival. "I've got a letter."

"From your father."

"How did you—"

"Because you've got your father's folding on it. He folds things four times, you fold things twice and badly, and there's a square in your pocket with four creases in it that you have taken out and put back at least nine times since you got here." Lin Hao turned round. "Sit properly. You're going to read it to me or you're going to make me guess, and if you make me guess I'll get it right and you'll be annoyed for a week."

Xie Xie sat properly.

"He wants me to sit the Association's mecha-engineering entrance in the spring," he said. "He's got me a place at the prep course. It's in Glorybound. It's four months." He looked at the letter, which he had not unfolded. "He says it's what I've wanted since I was six."

"Is it."

"Yes."

"Say it again."

"*Yes,*" Xie Xie said, louder, and then — because he was Xie Xie, and because the loud one is the one who is not sure — "obviously it is. It's the only thing I've ever wanted. Mecha Master. I've said it every day of my life. I said it on a bench when I was nine in front of half the class and Yun Xiao told me to shut up until I could beat Gu Yue, and I've carried that around for a year, and it's *mine.*"

"I didn't ask whether it was yours."

Xie Xie's mouth opened, and then closed.

"You asked whether it *is,*" he said.

"I asked whether it is. Present tense. Today."

## Part 2: What a Friend Does

Lin Hao pulled the second coal sack over and sat down on it, which took some effort, because the sacks were low and he had a bruise on his hip from a Horned Dragon and a crystal mark on his forearm that had gone the colour of a healing thumbprint.

"I'm going to tell you something," he said, "and I want you to understand that I know exactly how it's going to land, and I'm telling you anyway, and that's the whole point of me."

"Here we go."

"Six weeks ago you told me I'd been fighting the wrong half of you for nine weeks." Lin Hao held the look. "You were right. I had been. And the reason you were the only person who could tell me is that you'd spent nine weeks getting hit by the wrong half, and you were *bored* of it, and bored is honest. Gu Yue could have told me and didn't, because Gu Yue doesn't. Wulin couldn't have, because Wulin loves me and love is a terrible instrument for this, it's like trying to measure a seam with your thumb." He shrugged. "You told me. It cost you nothing and it changed me, and I've thought about it every day since, and I want to say the next part properly because it's the only thing I actually understand about people."

He put his hands flat on his knees, the way his father did when a bar had come out wrong and the explaining was going to take a minute.

"Every relationship has to be built on friendship, because friendship is the one you *make.* You don't get given it. Nobody hands you a friend the way they hand you a brother or a mother or a girl who looks at you a certain way — you build it, out of mornings, out of nine weeks of getting hit, out of choosing to come back the tenth week. And because you built it, it's the only one where both people know what it's made of.

"A friend is the best support there is. He's on your side. But a true friend is also your enemy, and that's not a contradiction, that's the *structure* — he supports you and he opposes you at the same time, and the balance between those two is the whole thing. He stands with you when you're wrong. He really does. If you go and do something stupid tomorrow I'll be standing next to you in it, and I'll help you carry whatever it costs, because that's the support half. And then I'll tell you it was stupid. Not instead. *And.*"

"That sounds exhausting."

"It's the only one that isn't. That's the joke." Lin Hao grinned, briefly. "Everything else — love, blood — the moment it gets hard it turns into choosing a side. And when you choose a side you stop seeing the person. You see the good and the bad goes invisible, and other people can see it and you can't, and they stop telling you because you've made it expensive to tell you. So it grows. And eventually the whole thing is held together by nobody saying anything." He tapped his own temple. "There's a name for it. People call it love sickness, like it's a weather. It's not a weather. It's a *blindness,* and it's the expensive kind, because it costs you the person you're blind about."

Xie Xie was quiet.

"And it's simple," Lin Hao said. "That's the part nobody can stand. It's genuinely simple — you support them and you tell them the truth, and you do both, and that's the whole of it. And people make it complicated on purpose. They make it complicated because they *want* something, and wanting makes you run, and when you're running you start arguing with yourself instead of with them, and then you're trying to prove you're right to *yourself,* in your own head, where nobody can check. That's not a relationship. That's a man alone in a room with a mirror, and he's been in there so long he thinks the mirror is talking back."

He stopped, and heard himself, and winced.

"That came out better than I'd planned and I want it said that I'm ten."

## Part 3: The Objection

Xie Xie unfolded the letter. He did not read it. He looked at the paper the way you look at a thing you have already read eleven times.

"So," he said. "Tell me."

"Tell you what."

"You just gave a speech about how a friend tells you. So tell me. Is it my dream or is it my father's."

Lin Hao opened his mouth and shut it.

"I'll tell you what I know," he said. "Your father spent a thousand years' worth of money on a hundred-year soul for you. I know that because you told me in the forest, when you thought you were going to die, which is when people say the true things. He has spent your entire life on you, and he is not a bad man, and he wants the mecha engineering because he has decided — correctly, probably, from where he's standing — that a noble family's second son with average spiritual power needs a trade that doesn't depend on the soul. That's not a father crushing a son. That's a frightened man building a wall."

"And?"

"And I don't know whether it's your dream, Xie Xie. I genuinely don't. Here's what I do know: you have said 'Mecha Master' every day of your life, and I have never once heard you talk about *building* one. Not once. You talk about *being* one. You talk about the coat, and the bench, and Yun Xiao shutting up, and the symbol of a true noble. You never talk about the circuits. You never ask Mu Chen about tolerances. You've been in a forge eleven times and you've never once picked up a caliper." He let that sit. "Whereas when you talk about the dagger — the *dagger,* not the dream — you talk for an hour and you don't notice."

Xie Xie did not say anything for a long time. The banked fire ticked.

"That's not fair," he said, at last. "That's not a fair thing to say to me."

"No."

"You don't know what it's like. You've got a *sword.* Yours came out of your hand when you were six years old, and your father is a smith, and your whole life has been one long permission. My father spent the family's money on a dagger and then spent eleven years being disappointed that it wasn't enough, and every single morning I wake up owing somebody." His voice had gone flat, which was worse than loud. "You get to have a dream because nobody had to pay for yours."

"That's true," Lin Hao said.

Xie Xie looked up.

"That's completely true and I'm not going to argue with it, because it's the sort of thing that's true whether I like it or not, and I've made a policy of not arguing with those." He turned the coal sack so he was facing his friend properly. "I got a sword. My father got a son who liked the same thing he liked. That's luck, and I'd be lying if I dressed it up as virtue. But here's the part I want you to hear, because I think it's the only useful thing I've ever had to say to anybody: **the owing is the problem, not the dream.** If you go to Glorybound because you want to build mechas, your father's money is a gift and you'll never think about it again. If you go because you owe him, you'll be in that prep course for four months and you'll be excellent at it, and you'll be miserable every day, and you'll be so busy being grateful that you won't notice you've stopped wanting anything at all. And then you'll be twenty, and you'll be a very good mecha engineer, and you'll meet somebody who asks you what you actually like, and you won't have an answer. That's the expensive version of the blindness. It's not romantic. It's just slow."

## Part 4: What Xie Xie Did With It

"So what do I do," Xie Xie said.

"I don't know."

"You never say that."

"I say it when it's true." Lin Hao got up and went to the rack, and came back with two things — a length of cold-rolled stock about the size of his forearm, and a scriber. He put them on the coal sack between them. "Here's what you do. You don't decide tonight, and you don't decide by asking yourself, because you've been asking yourself for six hours and you've come up with nothing, and that's because asking yourself is the mirror thing, and you're alone in the room with it."

"Then what."

"You find out what the work is like. Not the title. The *work.*" Lin Hao sat back down. "You come here on Saturdays. Not to watch me — I'm sick of you watching me. You learn the bench. Calipers, files, layout, the boring part, the part where nothing happens for three hours and then something is true. Six Saturdays. And separately, you get your father's prep-course syllabus and you read it, actually read it, the circuits and the tolerances and the boring parts, and you notice whether you're bored. And at the end of six weeks you'll know. Not because you decided. Because you'll have *evidence,* and evidence is the only thing that gets a man out of a room with a mirror."

Xie Xie looked at the scriber.

"And if I'm bored."

"Then you're bored, and you tell your father, and it's going to be the worst month of your life, and I'll be standing next to you in it — that's the support half — and I'll also tell you that you were right to do it, which is the other half, and you will find the combination extremely annoying."

"And if I'm not bored."

"Then it was always yours and your father was right, and you'll never have to think about it again, and I'll be wrong, and you'll bring that up constantly for years."

Xie Xie picked up the scriber. He turned it over once, and then he did something with it — a small, absent, entirely unconscious thing — scribing a line along the stock and then a second line parallel to it, checking the gap with his thumbnail, and then correcting the second line by a hair.

Lin Hao watched him do it and said nothing at all.

"What," Xie Xie said.

"Nothing."

"You're doing a face."

"I'm not doing a face."

"You're *absolutely* doing a face." Xie Xie put the scriber down. "What."

"Your second line was out by about the width of a hair and you found it with your thumb and fixed it without looking." Lin Hao's voice was carefully neutral, which was the voice he used when something interesting had happened. "Most grown men in this building can't do that. Mu Chen can. My father can. I can, but I had to be taught, and I had a *hawk,* and I've been complaining about the unfairness of it for four years." He picked the scriber back up and set it down in Xie Xie's hand. "Six Saturdays. Start now."

## Part 5: What the Style Is Called

Xie Xie stayed until two in the morning, which was the record, and most of it was not about the letter.

Most of it was Xie Xie at the bench with a file, being terrible at it, and complaining continuously, and getting marginally better, and at some point around midnight he said, without looking up, "So what's the name."

"What name."

"Your style. Wu Zhangkong wants it by the end of the month and you've got two weeks and you've told absolutely nobody, and I know you've got one because you've been standing at that anvil like a man at a funeral for a week." Xie Xie blew the filings off the stock. "Tell me."

"Reading the Grain."

Xie Xie's file stopped.

"That's *blacksmithing.*"

"Yes."

"Your sword style is called a blacksmithing thing."

"My sword style *is* a blacksmithing thing. That's the entire discovery, that's what I've been standing here about. Wu Zhangkong told me to make something that wasn't a quotation, and everything I have that isn't a quotation came out of that forge, so — Reading the Grain. You don't cut metal where you want it to open. You cut it where it's already willing." He shrugged. "And it turns out people have grain too."

"That's the most boring name I have ever heard."

"Good."

"It's *terrible.* 'Reading the Grain.' Nobody's going to fear that. Nobody's going to hear 'Reading the Grain' and think 'I should probably yield.' They're going to think you do *fences.*"

"I don't need them to fear the name," Lin Hao said. "I need them to not see it coming, and 'Reading the Grain' is a name nobody looks at twice, which is a feature. Besides — the first form is called Open Hand, and it's a form where I don't move at all, and it beats people who are much better than me, so the whole thing has a consistent reputation problem that I've made my peace with."

Xie Xie went back to filing.

"I'm going to call it the Smithy School," he said. "In front of Wu Zhangkong. In the formal setting. When there are people there."

"You will not."

"I absolutely will. I'll say 'and Lin Hao will now demonstrate a technique from the renowned Smithy School,' and I'll do the voice, and you'll have to stand there and take it, because that's the *support* half, and I'll be telling the truth about where it came from, which is the other half, and you will find the combination extremely annoying."

Lin Hao opened his mouth, and shut it, and then laughed, because it was exactly right — it was his own argument handed back at him with the sharp end first, and he had no defence at all that did not destroy it.

"That's the best thing you've ever done to me," he said.

"I've been learning from the best," Xie Xie said, and did not look up, and filed, and was, for the first time in six hours, not holding a folded piece of paper.

## Part 6: Two Weeks

He walked Xie Xie back at two, and the harbour did its accounting, and neither of them said the other thing, which was that the letter was still folded in Xie Xie's pocket and still not answered, and that six Saturdays was not an answer either, and that both of them knew it.

At the dormitory door Xie Xie stopped.

"Hao."

"Yes."

"If I go to Glorybound." Four months. Two hundred and forty days of mornings that would not happen. "You'd still tell me."

"That's not how distance works."

"Answer the question."

Lin Hao looked at him — at the boy who had been the first line in the ledger, who had drawn blood off him in a training ground and grinned about it, who had spent nine weeks being bored in exactly the right way — and understood that the question was not about Glorybound at all, and never had been.

"Yes," he said. "If you go, and you become exactly what he wants, and you're miserable, and you're excellent at it, and you never once complain because complaining would be ungrateful — I'll write to you and I'll say it, and it'll cost me the friendship to say it, and I'll pay that, because the alternative is watching you disappear by degrees and calling it support." A beat. "And if it costs the friendship, then it wasn't the thing I said it was, and I'd rather find that out than keep it."

Xie Xie was quiet for a moment.

"That's worse than a nice answer," he said.

"It's the only answer I've got."

"Yeah." He went in. Then he came back out, because he was Xie Xie. "Smithy School."

"I will end you."

"*Smithy School.*" And the door closed, and Lin Hao stood in the corridor with his bruised hip and his thumbprint of a scar and a two-week deadline and a friend who was going to spend six Saturdays finding out whether he wanted his own life, and he thought — not for the first time, and not for the last — that he would rather have this than the number.

Then he went to bed, because he was ten, and the anvil would still be there in the morning, and the grain would still be in the steel, waiting to be read.


The hawk was eight hundred and seventy-eight years old and had no opinion about the forge, or about anything, because it was a soul and not a person.

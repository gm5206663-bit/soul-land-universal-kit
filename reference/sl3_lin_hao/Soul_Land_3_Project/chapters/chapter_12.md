## Part 1: Bone and Iron

The stands were fuller on the second day, which the Academy's staff had begun to discuss in the tones usually reserved for plumbing failures.

Class three took the platform the way a unit takes a hill — together, drilled, and built around its center. The center was a boy named Qin Lang, and the first thing the stands understood about Qin Lang was that the word *big* had been sitting unused in the first grade's vocabulary for exactly one day, and now it belonged to him again. His martial soul had the shoulders of a gate and the hide of a grudge: an Iron-Back Ape, dark-furred and knuckle-walked, with two yellow rings turning slowly underfoot — a hundred years apiece, everyone had heard, bone and iron, a proper Soul Grandmaster's pair.

Class three had watched the first match. Everyone had. Class three had also, it was plain from the way they arrayed, *learned* from it: Qin Lang planted at the center like a driven pile, two support-types wide and deep behind him, no agility line at all. They had watched a ten-second match and drawn the only conclusion that mattered —

*Do not let it be ten seconds.*

"Begin," said Long Hengxu.

Class five crossed the platform at a walk. They had agreed, the night before, on what each of them was for; what they had not agreed on — because Lin Hao had said *wait* and the nine weeks had taught them what that word cost him — was a plan.

Qin Lang met them at the center line and did what the whole class three drill existed for: he took the first punch.

Wulin's fist landed on the crossed forearms of the Iron-Back Ape with the sound of a hammer meeting an anvil that did not care — and Qin Lang slid perhaps half a step, planted, and *stayed.* The ape-soul's hide drank the shock the way the ward drank sound. Behind him, the two supports fed: a yellow glow at the ape's shoulders, a second at its legs, amplification stacking on endurance stacking on two hundred combined years of ring.

Xie Xie went in while the echo was still ringing — one flash, dagger-light tracing the ape's flank at the speed that had ended class four — and the dagger *skated.* Iron-Back hide turned the edge the way rain turns off a roof. Xie Xie flashed again, higher, lower, behind; four lines of light, four skates, and Qin Lang turning in place with the bored patience of a gate on hinges.

"He's not chasing you," Lin Hao said — and walked in.

He had not released his sword because his sword was not what this was. He released it now. The Stormbringer came out of the air the way it always did — silver-blue, wind-etched, the lightning along its edge waking like a held breath let go — and the stands, which had learned to count, forgot to.

Xie Xie took the second support — one flash, dagger-light at a throat, held there — and then did something he had not done in nine weeks of sparring: he stopped fighting, and watched.

The hawk-eyes were there. They were always there; they were a tool in the kit, the way his father's forge-hands were a tool, the way the nine weeks were a tool. But the sword was the voice. He met the Iron-Back Ape's first backhand not with evasion but with the flat of the blade — and *took it.*

The blow landed like a falling wall. An ordinary first-grader would have folded — Lin Hao did not fold. He *skidded.* His boots scored two furrows in the stone, half a step, then a full step, and the shock rang down the steel into his arms, into the dense quiet architecture the Adaptation Talent had been building since the womb. His arms went numb to the elbow. His breath stopped. The stands saw the swordsman take the ape's blow and not go down — and saw, for the first time, that not going down was *work.* The two supports fed again; the ape's second swing was already coming, heavier than the first, the amplification stacking.

Beside the platform, Tang Wulin — who knew better than anyone in the grade what it cost to stand under that much force — went very still, and something in his chest that was not the seal hummed its approval.

Lin Hao did not take the second one. **Wind-Step** — one step, and he was inside the ape's guard again, before the second swing had finished starting, the stands' noise cutting off because they had not followed him across.

And the sword spoke.

"His breath stops between the pours," Lin Hao said. Walking volume. One sentence. The sword was already moving — not after the wind, not because of the wind, but because the sword had read the gap and the sword was the voice that answered it.

It cut the *door.* Not the wall — the feed-line between the ape and its left support. Lin Hao did not merely lay the blade there; he *used* the soul. The wind around the Stormbringer compressed, folding into a ribbon of moving pressure along the edge — the Gale Talon, laid not at range but along the steel itself, the sword's own voice given teeth — a rending seam of air that cut the amplification-line the way a smith cuts a bar along its grain. The support's glow arrived at Qin Lang's shoulders a half-beat late and uneven. Qin Lang's breath stopped—

—and under his planted feet the stone went suddenly, silently *slick.* A cup of water. No more. Not a wave, not a flood — a cup, laid exactly where four gripping toes needed purchase, poured by a girl who had raised one finger and lowered it again, and whose expression had not changed at any point in the proceedings.

The wall slipped. The wall's breath was still stopped. And into the half-beat where the Iron-Back Ape was a wall that had to hold still inside its own skin, the sword arrived — not the fist, not the hammer, the *sword* — the Stormbringer through the gap the late feed had left, under the crossed guard, with everything the nine weeks and the thousand refinements and the boy who had been a monster in every quiet area of his life had stored behind one edge.

The sword spoke before the world finished its sentence.

The gate came off its hinges.

Qin Lang went back — not seven meters, not ten, but *over,* the great ape-soul folding around him like a shuttered window — and the ward caught him, and Long Hengxu's voice rang, and the two supports stood alone in the middle of a very quiet platform with their feeds dying in their hands.

Twenty-one seconds. The stands counted, because the stands had learned to count. And at the platform's edge, Wu Zhangkong's hands came out from behind his back — the first time in nine weeks anyone had seen them there — and stayed out, at his sides, the way they stood when something on the field had said something worth hearing.

Class five released their rings for the record. The white ring in the gold grass — ten years old, still the cheapest thing on the platform. Two yellows. The Light Dragon Dagger's yellow — a hundred years of a noble family's money, still the only ring its owner had, still outranking half the grade's opinions. And the fourth boy's two, stacked like a promise and its proof — four rings again, and this time the counting had felt like *work*, and the grade understood, watching Qin Lang sit up dazed in his own folded soul, that the ten seconds of yesterday had not been the demonstration. *This* had been the demonstration. The ten seconds had been the mercy. And the swordsman — the one they had called the barometer, the Listener, the weather — had drawn his sword, and the weather had turned out to have an edge.

"Class five wins," said Long Hengxu, and consulted his slate, and the stands leaned, having learned the arithmetic yesterday. "As of this moment, you are first grade's class three." The stands did the ripple again — *class five, class four, class three, three days* — and the gospel held its breath. "Tomorrow: class two."

## Part 2

His hair had gone the colour of the underside of a wave at the tips, and in the yard light his eyes caught gold, and the second-years had started calling it the weather without ever explaining it to anybody who asked.
: The Story of the Stewed Beef

There is a shop in Eastsea City that has never once advertised, because it has never once needed to.

It stood on the lane behind the harbor road, small as a boxed lunch, with its cooking table out on the street where the whole lane could watch: a hundred little clay pots on a hundred little flames, each one bubbling its own private weather under its own lid. The beef went in cleaned, with its slices of onion and ginger; the pot kept the moisture in and the story sealed; ten-odd spices went on when the meat changed color; and then the long, slow hour in which the spices and the beef stopped being neighbors and became a single argument that had resolved.

The shop was famous in the way that survives guidebooks: every dock hand in the harbor could tell you which pot was his.

Class five took the long table under the lane's one tree, all twenty-two of them, because Zhou Zhangxi had made it a class matter within four minutes of the ward coming down and nobody had developed the heart to overrule him. The pots arrived in waves. The first wave went the way of all things near Zhou Zhangxi and the two furnace-boys. Xie Xie, who had appointed himself treasurer of the celebration and was auditing the second wave with the grief of a man watching a siege, set down his ledger-slate.

"I want it entered into the record," he said, "that I budgeted for a *victory meal* and what I am funding is a *migration.*"

"You budgeted four pots," Yun Xiao said.

"I budgeted four pots for *people,*" Xie Xie said. "Tang Wulin is not a person, he is a *destination.*"

Wulin, three pots deep and unrepentant, raised his fourth in a toast. Across the table, Gu Yue had taken the corner seat — the one with the tree between it and the lane, the one nobody had to be told was hers — and a single pot in front of her, and she was eating it with the precise, unhurried economy of someone for whom food, like everything else, was a cup and not a flood. When Xie Xie, rallying, tried to interest her in the second wave, she looked up, considered him for exactly as long as the question deserved, and said:

"No."

"That's the most word she's said all week and it's still a *loss,*" Xie Xie mourned, and the table went down, and Lin Hao laughed so hard the hawk in him stirred — not with leading, not with marking, just the old ordinary warmth of a thing that liked, it turned out, to hear him laugh — and he caught himself noticing that, and filed it, and did not examine it, because some files you open on rooftops and not at beef.

And then Xie Xie — because the treasurer's grief was, like all his griefs, a door — slid down the bench to the seat beside Lin Hao, and for once did not perform.

"The breath thing," he said. Quietly, so the bear song couldn't hear. "Between the pours. How long have you been able to do that?"

Lin Hao considered the question the way he considered everything that mattered — honestly. "Since before I could walk, probably. I didn't know it was a *thing* until Chen Long started hitting me for flinching before the hit."

Xie Xie was quiet for a beat, turning his slate over and over in his hands like a man checking both sides of a coin he'd already spent. "You know the worst part?" he said at last. "Today. I watched the *sword.* Not the eyes. Everyone watches the eyes — I've watched the eyes for nine weeks. But it was the sword that cut the door, and the sword that took the ape's hit, and the sword that—" he stopped, and did something Lin Hao had never seen him do, which was to say a true thing without dressing it first. "I've been fighting the wrong half of you for nine weeks."

"You've been fighting the loud half," Lin Hao agreed. "That's the half everyone fights."

It was, Xie Xie decided, the most unsettling thing anyone had said to him at beef — and he filed it, not in the ledger, but somewhere he kept things that mattered — and reached, without comment, for a third pot. He set it down in front of Lin Hao. The treasurer had closed his books for the night.

The lane smelled of spice and low tide. Twenty-two voices argued the match back into being — the slip, the cup, the half-beat — and at the head of the table Zhou Zhangxi was teaching the first wave's survivors a song about a bear that nobody finished. And Lin Hao, fourth pot cooling in front of him, looked down the table at all of it — the treasurer's grief, the corner seat, the song, the forge-boy's fourth pot — and thought, with the particular surprise of a boy who had spent his life being the one who watched:

*I'm not reading this. I'm just in it.*

It was, he decided, picking up his spoon, the best thing that had happened to him all week, and the hawk on its far water agreed in the only language it had been using lately, which was none.

## Part 3: The Ladder's Top

They went to watch class one on the third morning, all four of them, because Xie Xie had said *let's go and take a look* in the voice that meant the decision had already been made inside him, and because none of the four was the kind of person who watches a ladder without looking up.

The stands were different for class one. Quieter. The grade did not cheer for class one the way it cheered for class five; it *attended,* the way you attend weather. Class two was already on the platform — good students, three-rings between them, drilled — and class one walked out the way class one walked, and the noise thinned to nothing, and the match began.

It did not last long. But it lasted longer than ten seconds, and the four of them understood, watching, exactly why.

Wang Jinxi fought at the center, and the first thing Lin Hao's eyes told him — his eyes, running on himself, the hawk silent and watchful at the back of him like a held breath — was that the light *leaned away* from the boy. The Bone Dragon King walked under Wang Jinxi's skin like a thing under dark water: a dragon-soul the color of a sealed room, top-tier and dark-attribute, with two yellow rings turning under it, and every strike class two landed on him arrived at a quarter of him that was suddenly *not there* — shoulder, flank, knee going spectral for a heartbeat, physical blows passing through the ghost-quarter of him like breath through a gate. Bone Soul Conversion, the stands' older students murmured; a quarter of himself at a time, at two rings, and never a heartbeat longer.

He did not fight alone. When class two's best committed — a committed, good, three-ring combination that deserved better — Wang Jinxi and Zhang Yangzi closed, and the Shadow Phantasm Eagle folded over the Bone Dragon King like a wing over a bone, and the two souls *met*—

Martial Soul Fusion Skill. The word went through the stands the way wind goes through a field. Lin Hao had read about fusion skills in the Association's public library, in the dry careful prose of reference books: two souls, one shape, the rarest arithmetic in the profession. The reference books had not mentioned the *sound.* The Dark Eagle Dragon went through class two's combination the way a decision goes through an argument, and the platform's ward hummed a note Lin Hao felt in his hollow bones, and beside him—

And the four of them walked back to room eleven, and three of them were quiet in the way people are quiet when they have watched something they cannot do, and the fourth was quiet in a completely different way, and none of them knew the difference yet.

Xie Xie was doing arithmetic: *their rings agree and ours do not, and that is the whole of the problem.* Wulin was doing arithmetic: *the grass owns ground and the ground does not fuse.* Gu Yue was doing arithmetic: *weather does not fuse, weather simply is.*

**Lin Hao was not doing arithmetic. He was looking at the weld.**

Because a fusion is not a thing two people do. It is a **seam** — two voices agreeing, mid-stride, to be one voice, and the seam runs the length of the thing, and a seam is a shape, and Lin Hao had spent six years reading shapes in metal that did not want to be read. The stands saw a monster. **He saw a weld, and a weld has a place where it is thin, and he had already found it, walking back, without deciding to.**

That is the whole butterfly, and nobody in the stands could have seen it, because the stands were watching a fusion and he was watching a **seam.** In a class without him, class five watches that match and learns that they are going to lose. **In a class with him in it, they learn where the weld is thin — and the next morning the weld breaks.**

Beside him, Tang Wulin had gone very still.

Not frightened. Not awed. *Still* — the particular stillness Lin Hao had learned to sort in nine weeks of friendship: the stillness of the forge when the metal says something. Wulin's eyes were on the Bone Dragon King — on the dark water of it, the sealed-room color, the dragon-shape under the skin — and his right hand, hanging at his side, had closed once, slowly, and opened again.

"Your arm asleep?" Lin Hao asked, quietly, as the ward came down and the stands began their thin polite noise.

"No," Wulin said. And then, after a beat, in the voice he kept for the truest things: "Something in me *answered* it. Like — like hearing a bell in the next room and your own bones humming along." He shook his head, hard, the way he shook off things that wanted too much. "It's a dragon soul. I have a dragon's *appetite.* Old Tang says the blood in me is— it doesn't matter. It's nothing."

It was not nothing. Lin Hao filed it where the other files lived — deep, private, marked *later* — beside the wreck-water and the obedient wind and the question. The shelf was getting crowded. He was beginning to understand that this was simply what his life was now: the boy who kept the grade's strangest files, on his own eyes, without the bird, until the day—

*Until the day what?*

The hawk did not answer. It was getting extremely good at that.

## Part 4: Class Two, Tomorrow

The roof. The harbor wind. The city doing its nightly accounting of lights.

"Class two's fast," Wulin said. He had the slate out — the forge-temperature hand had been busy all evening: *class two, three-rings, speed line, they press early. The eagle-boy reads presses. Do not press early.* "Xie Xie says he can take their speed line alone."

"Xie Xie says a lot of things at beef."

"Xie Xie took class four's *both* of them alone," Wulin pointed out, fairly. "He's not wrong. He's just—" he paused, looking for the word with the honest slowness that Lin Hao had come to love like a hometown. "He's louder than his own evidence. That's all."

"That's not all," Lin Hao said. "That's *Xie Xie.*" He lay back on the tiles. Above them the stars did their slow tournament of their own, and out past the harbor the dark water kept whatever it kept, and in the spiritual sea under it all, a seven-hundred-and-sixty-year silence sat on its far water and watched the boy who had stopped asking it for the answers — and had not, Wulin's stillness aside, once in two full days been *wrong.*

The question lay under everything like ballast. *Can you see — without me?* Two days of seeing without it. A wall taken apart in twenty-one seconds on a reading that was purely, entirely, *his.* And the silence had not once said *careful,* because the silence had stopped needing to.

*You're waiting for something,* Lin Hao thought at it, not unkindly, the way you talk to a door you've stopped knocking on. *And I'm going to keep answering the way I know how — with matches, and beef, and my brother's ledger, and my own two eyes — until you decide I've said enough.* He closed his own eyes. *Tomorrow's class two. After that—* he did not finish the thought out loud, even inside; the ladder's top had a dragon on it, and his brother's bones had hummed, and some thoughts you kept until the shelf could hold them —*—after that, we'll see what the top of the ladder is made of.*

The hawk, on its far water, did not answer.

But when the sleep came, it came the way it had come the night before — like a room where someone had quietly pulled up a chair — and this time, Lin Hao noticed, the chair was a little closer.

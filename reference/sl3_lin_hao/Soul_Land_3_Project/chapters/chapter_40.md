## Part 1: The Arithmetic Of The Air

The webs began to crack about twenty minutes after the spider died.

Gu Yue noticed first, because Gu Yue noticed everything and then said only the part that mattered. "The toxicity has a limit. When the webs go, this stops being the safest place in the forest and starts being the most obvious one."

Wulin sat down cross-legged in the middle of them with his scaled hand going slowly back to being a hand, and he said the thing he had been turning over since the spider came apart.

"How much did we take?"

Gu Yue closed her eyes for a moment, the way she did when she was counting something that was not numbers.

"Four to five hundred years' worth, each of us." She opened them. "Wulin and I have it all in one ring, because we only have one. The rest of you have two, so it splits — **about two hundred years to each ring.** There's still a hundred or two hanging in the air here, but it would take too long to pull it out, and we don't have too long."

She looked at the trees.

"And after we leave, **other Soul Masters are a greater threat to us than anything with legs.** There's no true death in here. Everybody knows that. And everybody can smell four hundred years walking around."

Xie Xie, who had been lying on his back with his daggers on his chest, said, "That's the most cheerful thing anyone's said all day."

"It's the most useful."

Lin Hao had not heard any of it.

He had heard all of it — every word, in order, filed — but he had not *listened*, because something was happening in his chest that had started about ninety seconds ago and had not stopped, and it had the specific quality of a thing that was going to continue whether or not he paid attention to it.

**Two hundred years to each ring.**

He had nine hundred and sixty-four.

He did the sum, because that was what he did, and he got an answer, and then he did the sum again, because he had learned from Wu Zhangkong of all people that the first arithmetic is the one you want and the second is the one that's true.

**One thousand one hundred and sixty-four.**

He looked up.

"Wulin," he said.

Wulin turned. He looked at Lin Hao's face for perhaps one second, and then he was on his feet, because he had learned what that face meant too.

"How long," Wulin said.

"I don't know. It started ninety seconds ago."

"*Ninety—*"

"It's not hurting." Lin Hao said it carefully, because it was true and because he could tell it was not the whole truth. "It's not hurting. It's **crowded.**"

## Part 2: What A Thousand Years Sounds Like

It was not light.

He had expected light, because that was what happened in the stories and because he had watched Wulin's ring go gold in a dormitory in front of four people, and light was the shape the thing had taken then.

This was not light. This was **weight**, arriving.

The hawk had been in him for two years and it had never once been a *presence* — it had been a direction, a stillness, a thing that leaned. Now it was becoming an animal, and the becoming was not gentle, and it was not cruel either, and Lin Hao understood with a clarity that surprised him that this was simply what nine hundred and sixty-four years looked like when it finished adding itself up.

He went down onto one knee in the leaf litter, because his legs made a decision without him.

"**Gu Yue,**" Wulin said.

She was already there. She put one hand flat between his shoulder blades — not to steady him, Lin Hao understood later; to *read* him — and her face went through something he had never seen on it.

"Oh," she said.

"*Oh?*" Xie Xie said. "What's *oh?* Nobody says oh, you don't even have a face for—"

"**His soul is crossing a thousand years.**"

The clearing went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with the forest.

Wang Jinxi said, "Is that— is that good?"

"It's—" Gu Yue took her hand away. "It's **visible.**"

## Part 3: Purple

The rings came up.

Lin Hao had seen his own rings eleven times and they had been yellow both times he had bothered to look, because yellow was what a hundred-to-nine-hundred-ninety-nine-year soul made, and he had never had a reason to think about the colour the way other people thought about the weather.

They went purple.

Not one at a time. Both, together, the way two lamps go when the same hand turns them — and the purple was not a shade, it was a *depth*, and looking at it was like looking down something, and Lin Hao knelt in the leaf litter with his own light going on around him and understood for the first time in his life that a soul ring was not a mark on a person.

It was a **window.**

And through it, something that had been folded up very small for two years began to unfold.

The hawk came.

It did not come out. It was never going to come out; he understood that with total certainty, the way you understand a fact about your own body. It came **up** — through him, into his arms, into the hand that was holding a sword that had spent two years having opinions — and the Stormbringer went from being a good blade to being something that made the air in a ten-metre circle stop moving.

Wind. Not a gust. **Wind**, the way the forest had it before anybody was in it.

And lightning, which had been reaching for four months, stopped reaching and simply **was**, running the length of the steel in a line so steady it looked drawn.

The **storm-gray lock** at his temple went **hawk-gold** to the root.

Not the tips. The whole of it, all at once, the colour running down it the way ink runs down a thread, and Lin Hao felt it happen — a small warm distinct thing at the side of his skull, like a hand laid flat.

And the **marks on his forearms stood up** out of his skin. They had been fine lines under it for two years, **storm-light** and **gold-flecked**, the kind of thing you saw only when he was loaded or angry or in the dark. Now they were **on** him — raised, feather-fine, following the bone, glowing with a steady light that had no flicker in it at all.

His **hair** had a **sheen to it** that had not been there in the morning, and there was a **breeze in it** that was not coming from anywhere.

He could **see the tracks.**

That was the part he understood last and would think about longest. The spirit energy hanging in the clearing — the four hundred years Gu Yue had counted, the hundred or two still in the air — he could *see* it. Not as light. As **tracks**, the way you see a path through grass after somebody has walked on it; and he could see where it was thick and where it was thin and where it had been disturbed. He understood without being told that this was what Gu Yue had been doing with her eyes closed for two years.

Lin Hao looked at his own hand.

"That's new," he said.

"Your hair," Gu Yue said. She was looking at him with an expression he had finally learned to read, which was that she was working something out and had not finished. "It went all the way down. Yours only ever did the tips."

"It feels like a hand on the side of my head."

"That isn't a metaphor," Gu Yue said. "That's your soul being large enough to have edges."

Then he did the thing he had done four hundred times, because his hand did it before he decided to, and the reason he would remember this for the rest of his life is that **he did not do it any differently and it came out wrong.**

He laid a Gale Talon along the Stormbringer and let it go at a deadfall across the clearing.

He had laid four hundred of those. He knew what it cost — a particular small draw at the base of the ribs, like a mouthful of cold air — and he knew what it did: the wind shoved the edge forward and the lightning got there first, and the wood opened along the grain he had already read, and the whole thing took about as much attention as walking.

The deadfall did not open.

The deadfall **came apart**, thirty metres of it, quietly and all at once, and the sound arrived afterwards the way the sound of a large tree arrives afterwards, and the leaf litter on the far side of the clearing was still moving when the noise stopped.

Lin Hao stood there with his sword in his hand.

"That was not a Gale Talon," he said.

"I know," Gu Yue said.

"I didn't do anything different. I did the same thing." He turned the sword over, slowly, looking at it as though it were somebody else's. "I did exactly the same thing and it was **more.**"

"Your rings changed."

"Yes."

"So did the things they gave you." She said it the way she said everything, which was as though she were reading it off something. "A soul ring isn't a container. It's a **permission.** You were permitted a hundred years of wind and now you are permitted a thousand, and the wind did not get bigger, the *permission* did. Your skill didn't improve. It got **older.**"

Lin Hao looked at the deadfall.

"And the cost."

"What about it."

"It cost more." He said it carefully, because he had been counting without meaning to, the way he counted everything. "About three times. I did not decide to spend three times. It just took it."

Two kilometres away, in a room that smelled of hot metal and cold tea, the staff member had both hands flat on the plate.

"That was a soul skill," he said.

"I saw."

"That was a **thousand-year** soul skill." He said the words the way a man says them when he has read about a thing and never expected to be in the room. "Teacher Wu, I have been in this building eleven years and I have seen four of those. Three of them belonged to people over sixty." He looked up. "One of them belonged to **you.**"

Wu Zhangkong did not answer that.

He was looking at the boy in the leaf litter, who was standing very still in the middle of three purple rings and doing the thing Wu Zhangkong had begun to recognise over four months, which was counting.

"He's working out what it costs," Wu Zhangkong said.

"Sir?"

"Everybody else would be looking at the tree."

---

There was one more thing, and he did not tell anybody about it for a while.

The **Union** had changed too. He could feel it the way you feel a room you have not walked into — folded at the back of him where it had always been folded, exactly where it had always been, and **larger.** Not louder. Not straining. Larger in the way the clearing was larger now that he could see the tracks in it: the same amount of thing, permitted to take up more space.

He did not open it.

He had decided a long time ago that the Union was a decision and not a demonstration, and he was not going to change that in a forest with a woman watching and a plate in the sky two kilometres up, and so he closed his hand around it the way you close a hand around a rail, and he wrote down instead what he had noticed:

*That it got bigger without being asked. That it did not ask to be used. That the cost of it will not be the cost I planned for, and that I planned for a cost, and that the plan is now wrong.*

## Part 4: The Third

The third ring came eleven seconds after the second went purple, and it was the part nobody was ready for, including him.

It came up out of him like something surfacing, and it was **purple**, and it was **empty**, and Lin Hao looked at it and understood immediately what it was, because he had read the rule in a book at the age of eight and had not believed it applied to anybody he knew.

**A thousand-year spirit soul bestows up to three rings.**

He had been carrying two because two was what a hundred-year soul made. He had been at rank thirty for eleven chapters because rank thirty was where two rings stopped, and he had called that a wall, and then he had let Wu Zhangkong talk him into calling it a coil, and both of them had been right and neither of them had been enough.

The coil opened.

He felt the rank go. It was not dramatic. It was the sensation of a door that had been stuck for four months simply not being stuck any more, and the number behind it being **thirty-one**, and thirty-one being a bracket he had not been in since he was eight years old.

**Soul Elder.**

Wulin sat down in the leaf litter next to him, quite deliberately, the way you sit down next to somebody so that they are not alone.

"Say something," Wulin said.

"I'm thirty-one."

"Say something else."

Lin Hao looked at the sword, which was still doing the thing to the air, and at the three rings, which were purple and two of them were full and one of them was not, and at Gu Yue, who was watching him with an expression he had never seen on her and could not read at all.

"I've been wrong about the wall," he said.

"We've both been wrong about the wall."

"Yes," Lin Hao said. "But I'm the one who kept a ledger about it."

## Part 5: Who Was Watching

Two kilometres away and four hundred metres up, in a monitoring room that smelled of hot metal and cold tea, a staff member put down his cup.

He did not say anything for about six seconds. Then he said:

"Teacher Wu."

Wu Zhangkong had been standing at the back with his arms folded, in the posture he had held for the whole of the rebellion, which was the posture of a man who had decided in advance exactly how much he was going to react.

He unfolded his arms.

On the plate, in the middle of a forest that did not exist, a boy was kneeling in the leaf litter inside three purple rings and a column of wind that was bending the trees.

"That's a thousand years," the staff member said. "That's a *thousand-year* spirit soul. On a ten-year-old with a ten-year hawk." He turned around, and his face had the expression of a man who has just realised that his job is going to involve paperwork. "That's going to register."

"Yes."

"That registers at the **branch.** That registers at the **headquarters.** That's the kind of thing that gets a name put on a card."

Wu Zhangkong looked at the plate for a long moment.

"His name is already on a card," he said.

---

Lin Hao did not know any of that, and if he had known it, it would not have changed what he did next, which was to stand up in a forest that did not exist with three rings around his feet and a sword that had stopped being a tool, and to say to the five people who were the only five people in the world who had seen it:

"Nobody says anything about this."

Xie Xie opened his mouth.

"I mean it."

"You don't have to—"

"**Xie Xie.**" Lin Hao looked at him. "There is a man who has had my name on a card since the spring. He did not write it on the form. I have spent four months deciding what that meant, and I have just found out, and the answer is that it meant he was waiting for this." He let the sword down, and the wind in the circle went with it, slowly, like a person sitting down. "So. Nobody says anything."

Gu Yue, who had not spoken since the clearing, said:

"They already know."

Lin Hao looked at her.

"The platform **records**," Gu Yue said. "It has always recorded. You didn't do this in a forest, you did it in a machine that somebody is looking at." She said it without any particular expression, which with her was the closest thing to kindness. "So don't waste the next four months being quiet. It won't work. **Be somewhere with more people in it instead.**"

Lin Hao stood in the leaf litter with three purple rings going slowly dim around his boots, and thought about a pamphlet he had taken off a train because it was free, and a city that was second of five in an alliance, and a centre region that held only two cities, and a boy with a sword who had been told, four months ago, that in three years he was going to have to be a monster in front of a gate.

"Right," he said.

"Right," Wulin said.

And the webs cracked, and the air still hung thick with four hundred years of something that would make them the most interesting thing in the forest to anybody who could smell it, and they got up and walked out of the clearing in the shape that Lin Hao had built, because the shape still worked, and because the shape was the only thing he had that he had made himself.

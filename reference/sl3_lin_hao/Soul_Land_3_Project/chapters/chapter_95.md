## Part 1: The Answer

The door answered first.

The red eye had not blinked, and it did not roar. The maw simply opened at the end of the tunnel, and the dark turned orange — a torrent of flame down the passage, wide as the walls, hungry as a season. It crossed the space where the class had been frozen an hour ago and struck the wall of ice full on.

The ice didn't melt. It *rang.*

The lightning script flared white down its face, drinking the heat into itself, and the whole seam shed steam and light and held — one stroke's worth of Sword Intent standing off a legend's first breath. The wall groaned, wept, lost an inch, and held.

"Move!" Wulin had the four of them by voice and hand both, north along the gems, and the tunnel's last bend swallowed the light of the burning door behind them—

—and the world went white at the edges.

Not fire. Cleaner than fire. The valley's stars smeared like wet ink, the forest's dark folding at the corners the way a page lifts, and the last thing the five of them saw was each other, running, lit from everywhere at once.

Somewhere under the academy, an old woman took her hand off a switch.

## Part 2: The Room, and the Screen

Light through darkness. The hiss of seals. Five metal capsules slid out of the wall in a row, and five children sat up in them at the same time, breathing hard, in the same metal room where the exam had begun.

The big screen was live.

The cave. The tunnel. The wall of ice, streaming now, half-melted from a fire that had kept its promise even if the door had kept its — and beneath the wall, crouched in the gems, the adult. The great golden head bent low over the cub, and golden light rose around them both like water finding its level. On the screen, plainly, gently: the wounds closing. The smashed crown-scales knitting. The small body stirring, and the third eyelid — the whole exam had turned on that eyelid — flickering open, ordinary, unremarkable, a child waking up cranky in a treasure pile.

The mother pulled the cub into her chest and wrapped herself around it, and the light settled over them both like a blanket drawn up by hand.

Nobody in the metal room said anything for a while. The weight the five had been carrying out of that valley — the frozen four, the door, the bill of it — set itself down somewhere and did not come back.

"No regrets," Wulin said at last, grinning, wet-eyed, not fighting any of it. "I want that said. All of it. I'd do every part again."

"A man doesn't second-guess," Xie Xie said, which from him was a whole speech.

"I made my choice before the hammer was down." Xiaoyan was looking at the screen. "And she's happy. Look at her. That was the whole prize."

"It was the easiest choice I've ever made," Gu Yue said. "I never wanted it. I want what's mine, and that wasn't mine." 

They looked at the fifth.

Lin Hao looked back at them, at the four of them pooled out of their capsules, at the mother on the screen, and shrugged with one shoulder, the way he did at the forge when the quench came out right and there was nothing left to add. "I closed a door," he said. "I'd close it again."

Wulin put his right hand out, palm down. Gu Yue's came down on top of it. Xie Xie's on hers, Xiaoyan's on his — and Lin Hao put his hand on top of all of them, last, the closing stroke.

Five hands. It was the shape of the whole three years, and everybody knew it, and nobody had to say so.

## Part 3: The Clapping

Steady applause, from the dark of the room's far end. Shen Yi stepped into the light, and behind her, unhurried, came Elder Cai and the old man from the screen room, and Shen Yi's eyes were bright in a way proctors' eyes are not supposed to be.

"I have overseen this exam for many years," she said. "I still remember taking it. And I will remember watching you take it. You drew the hardest level of the ninth trial we possess. It tested your patience, your ability to survive — and, most importantly, your character. Which brings me to an apology: I lied to you. The fusion I promised in that world was never possible. Your character was the true test from the first step."

The five tests, laid out plain: the patience to find the valley — worth nothing in points, worth everything in time. The eyes to know a Three-eyed Golden Lion and a Duskgold Dreadclaw Bear for what they were. The courage to enter the cave — *that* one the gate itself; fail it and the whole trial scored zero. The cub: lose, and take nothing. And then the last test, the one whole teams have drowned in — the spoils.

"I have watched teams fall apart at that decision," Shen Yi said. "Turning their backs on their companions over a bone. I have watched children become wicked in that cave in under a minute." She paused. "The Auspicious Emperor has appeared once in twenty thousand years. Two of them, in one forest, for one class? They were never real. They were the best imitations this academy could build. Had you killed the cub and divided it calmly, you would have scored six points and I would have passed you and forgotten you by dinner. You chose not to kill. You'll understand if we didn't forget that."

"They were just data," Xie Xie said slowly, working it out loud, looking at the screen where the mother lay curled around her child. "The whole forest. The bear. Her." A pause. "It doesn't feel like just data."

"It isn't, quite," said Elder Cai. "The world is built, but what happens in it happens. Ask your fifth."

Every head turned.

"The stroke you put on that door," Cai said to Lin Hao, and her voice was all business again, an examiner reading off a page that had gotten longer all exam. "Four elements in a single attack, fused spiritual power behind it, Sword Intent holding the seam — I could name the techniques in it, and one day I will make a student of you and do exactly that. It cost you. One hundred points of true cultivation, spent in one breath, on a wall against a thing that was never real. The power does not come back because the beast wasn't real. It was spent true, so it stays spent." She let that sit. "I have watched Soul Emperors spend less on less. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That the door didn't know it was a door," Lin Hao said.

Somewhere behind her, Zhuo Shi made a sound that in a less dignified man would have been a laugh.

"I'm telling you," Cai said, "that when the world showed you a false thing, you answered with a true one. That is the entire academy in a sentence, and we will be arguing for a week about whether you passed the trial or the trial passed you. Six points was the price for calm butchery. You'll get the ten." Her page turned. "The gems, by the way, before anyone asks again —"

"The gems were the floor," Lin Hao said. "The floor belonged to the house."

This time Zhuo Shi did laugh.

## Part 4: The Ledger Read Aloud

"The ninth trial is what we call the overruling trial," Shen Yi said. "Fail it, and you are rejected whatever your points — eighty, ninety, it avails you nothing. We do not put monsters in armor. But you should hear the rest of the law while you stand here: Shrek has never advocated the slaughter of soul beasts. The Great Star Dou Forest has declined with every generation of our strength, and greed did most of the declining. If you become students of this academy, you will not hunt in the Great Star Dou Forest. Not once. Not ever. That is the price of the armor we will teach you to build."

Wulin looked at the screen — the mother, the cub, the light like a drawn blanket — and something in him settled into place with an audible click, or the click was Xie Xie's question finally finding its first answer: *if we are the ones who come in.* They had just spent nine trials being the ones who came in, and the academy had built the world entire to watch how they came.

"The scores," Shen Yi said, and the room stilled. "Trial nine, full value. Tang Wulin: seventy points. Xu Xiaoyan: sixty. Gu Yue: fifty-eight."

Fifty-eight.

Gu Yue did not move. Xie Xie's head snapped toward the number as if it had been thrown at her.

"And Lin Hao: seventy." Shen Yi's eyes moved to him, and there was the faintest weight on the name, an old woman setting a full cup down carefully. "Xie Xie: sixty-one."

"The deduction stands," Xie Xie said, not a question.

"The deduction stands," Shen Yi agreed, gently enough. "Sixty-one is a fine score. The minimum to pass this exam is sixty. Three of you have already passed it outright. Gu Yue is two points short — and there is exactly one trial left."

"I don't want luck," Gu Yue said, to the room, evenly, her chin level. "I want the two points earned. Which trial?"

## Part 5: The Roof

"Follow me," was all Shen Yi said.

She led them down the hall and up a flight of stairs none of them had known was there, and out onto the roof, where the night was the real night, stars admitting to nothing.

Three people stood waiting for them.

In the center, an elderly grey-haired man with stillness in him like deep water — a stillness two of the five had met before, in a cave, in a wall. On his left, Elder Cai, who had followed them up after all, or had never not been there. On his right, the elder who had administered the first trial, back at the very beginning, in another life.

The class of five stopped in a row, and the expressions on four and a half of their faces turned carefully sour.

*Her,* said every face, in perfect unison, without a sound. *What does she have to do with the last trial?*

Elder Cai looked back at them with the eyes of a woman about to enjoy her job.

# Chapter 1 — The Defective Shelf

## Part One — Row Nine

The glass was cold where his breath touched it.

Row Nine of the Eastsea Spirit Pagoda's soul vault was a quiet country. It held the ones the tall shelves did not want. A serpent with one eye gone cloudy. A lark whose wing-beat doubled back on itself. A fist of fog that never did decide on a shape. Their orbs sat dim on the rack, row on row. Above them, on the clean lit levels, the good souls glowed like small moons.

Disposal day came every thirty days. The clerks called it rotation. The orbs on Row Nine had other words for it, but souls in glass cannot speak. So they only went a little darker on the twenty-ninth day, one by one, like lamps being turned down.

He had stopped counting the days.

That was not pride. It was arithmetic. A soul that spent its strength dreading the thirtieth day arrived at it weaker than a soul that slept. So he slept, and he mended, and he listened to the guard's boots go past at morning bell and dusk bell, and he learned the vault the way a wolf learns a den he did not choose.

The little serpent beside him had forgotten how to fear the chute. He had watched it happen. Things broke here in their own time, on their own shelf, and none of it touched the law he lived by: outlast the time.

Then, on an ordinary afternoon between the two bells, something walked into the vault that made the whole room lean.

It did not come as sound. The step on the stair was light, a girl's step, though he had no way to know that yet. It came as weight. The light over the tall shelves seemed to bend a fraction toward the door. The air grew heavier by the weight of one held breath, and the breath was not his.

And his blood stood up inside him.

All six cold lights of it, the ones that slept in turns and never woke together, stood up at once like grass turning toward a sun. Beneath them, lower, the other thing in him lay down flat and tried to vanish. The gold in his bones — the soldier that feared nothing bred under the sky — folded itself small against the floor of him, and did not want to be noticed by whatever had come in.

*Royal,* something said, without asking him.

The word had no picture. It had only the pull. The way a river knows the sea long before it can smell salt. The way metal knows the mountain it was cut from, long after it was cut.

He did not understand royalty. He understood one thing: the girl was walking the length of the vault, and none of the dim orbs on Row Nine so much as breathed.

She stopped at the defective shelf. Most people never did.

## Part Two — What the Reader Read

"Defective stock is free to look at," the clerk said. "Expensive to love."

He was a round man with a registry ink stain on his cuff and the cheer of a man whose quota had just been met by somebody else. His tag read PANG. He looked at her student uniform first, then at the Pagoda card she had laid on the counter, and decided to be kind about it.

"The third floor has hundred-year souls at academy rates," he offered. "Very decent wind types. For a class like yours, honestly—"

"I saw the third floor," Gu Yue said. "I want the defective row."

The clerk's eyebrows did their arithmetic. A student ranked high enough for the elevators, asking to browse the chute. "May I ask why?"

"Because I watched one evolve."

That stopped him. It was a sentence out of the branch's own gossip. Three days ago, in the elementary spirit ascension platform, a boy in her class had carried in a ten-year soul that Pagoda stock had written off as junk, and walked out with it standing a hundred years higher. The whole hall had heard about it before the echo died.

"The boy's was a fluke," the clerk said carefully. "A lottery. Lottery luck doesn't repeat, miss. Junk is junk."

"His was weak junk," Gu Yue said. "Weak souls evolve easier. That is the doctrine in your own manuals, clerk Pang. Page one of the cheap shelf's apology."

He opened his mouth and closed it again. She had read the manual. Worse, she had read it *accurately*.

"A fusion costs spiritual power," he tried. "Even for a bargain. It isn't nothing."

"I know my realm," she said. "Tell the vault I am coming down."

The elevator had no numbers. It sank for a long minute, past floors whose doors never opened for students, and let them out into a hall of cold air and standing staffs. Row Nine waited at the hall's far end, behind glass.

The clerk carried the hand reader out of courtesy more than protocol. The defective row rarely got read; readings belonged to souls with prices. Gu Yue walked the row slowly. Her eyes moved down the dim orbs the way a hand moves across writing in the dark — not reading with the eyes at all — and stopped.

"That one," she said.

It was the dimmest light on the rack. It was so quiet the glass around it seemed bored. The clerk squinted at the tag, and what little cheer he had left went out of his face.

"Ah. That one, no." He palmed the reader against the glass anyway, because hall rules were hall rules. The little screen woke, counted, and blinked.

It read *one element.*

It read *six.*

It read *one.*

"Readers," the clerk said with relief, as if reassuring a piece of furniture. "Three months it's been sitting there doing that. Contradictory profile, unstable essence. Junk with delusions." He tapped the screen to settle it. "Disposal's written in for tonight's rotation, actually—"

"And what did the metal-body density say?" Gu Yue asked.

The clerk looked at her, then at the tag, then at the reader, and for the first time dropped the salesman's habit of steering. He waved down the senior assay man, a thin staff chief named Kuang, who came over drying his hands on a cloth.

Kuang ran the reader again. Watched it run one, six, one. Watched the density needle stand up at a level no soul-state should carry. His face, which had been mild, shut like a door.

"Purge flag," he said to the clerk. "Same-day. Annotate it as unstable-don't-sell." He started to turn away. "Something that reads like that and sits quiet for three months is not a bargain, miss. It is a mistake someone hasn't paid for yet."

Down in the glass, pressed against the cold, the wolf heard the word *purge* the way a fishhook hears the line go tight.

So the wolf did the one thing he had never done on the shelf. He spent himself.

He gathered the gold out of his blood — the soldier, the metal-body truth of him, the half of him that made needles stand up — and he drank it down deep, past where any reader could reach. It was like swallowing a lit coal and holding it. It cost him a week of strength in one breath. The old arithmetic again: a soul that looks harmless survives the day; a soul that looks true does not.

The reader's needle sank. The screen steadied, and wrote, in its small obedient hand: *weak anomaly, harmless.*

"There," the clerk breathed.

Kuang frowned at the screen and did not say *there.* He looked at the tag for a long moment, then filed the reading anyway.

And the wolf, who had learned patience on a shelf and intended to keep every fur of it, ruined all of it with what he did next.

He reached.

Not at the glass. Not at the men. At *her* — across the little distance between his cell and the girl's stillness, along a road that wasn't supposed to exist until a contract opened it. The hand reader's second needle, the one that measured contact, jumped without a power anywhere near it, and hung shaking at the far edge of its scale.

Gu Yue went very, very quiet.

The clerk saw a gauge twitch. The chief saw nothing, already walking. Only the girl with the recommendation letter and the high card looked at the dim orb and felt something reach her that had no business reaching anyone.

"Write the transfer," she said.

"Miss." The clerk actually touched her sleeve. "You don't even know what it is."

Gu Yue looked at the orb for one more breath, and then at him, and gave him the same flat voice she used on people who had wasted her time.

"Neither do you," she said. "That is why it is cheap."

## Part Three — Six Rooms

The fusion suite was one elevator stop below the hall, a small round room of quiet metal where the engraving of the contract array had worn smooth at the center from a century of bare feet. A Pagoda master read her the warnings twice, because for a second fusion the manual said twice. Spiritual power would be spent. The foreign essence would have to be guided. If the essence resisted, the attempt was to be abandoned, no exceptions.

It will not resist, she thought. But she signed.

She stood in the center of the array with the orb resting in her joined hands, and she paid.

Spiritual power went out of her the way warmth goes out of a limb in deep water — steady, unstoppable, priced. The clerk at the desk winced to watch the gauge and she did not so much as shift her weight. The light of the array climbed the glass, and the glass forgot it had ever been a wall.

For the first time in three months, nothing stood between the wolf and the outside.

He had thought, in the dim of the shelf, that stepping across would be escape. Leaving a cell. It was not leaving. It was *entering.* Where the chute or the shelf had been, there opened a country he had no map of — her country, soul-space stretching away around him wider than any den, quiet as deep night. He stood small at its edge and was afraid, honestly and for the first time in a long time, the way a cub is afraid of weather.

The country's attention found him.

Up close, the pull he had felt through glass was a hundredfold. *Royal,* the old pull said, and now it had a shape. It stood over him the way a mountain stands: not threatening, not kind, simply so much *more* that his gold blood pressed itself to the ground of him again and refused to rise.

But the hands of the attention were gentle. They did not seize. They waited for him to come the rest of the way in, and so, badly frightened and entirely decided, the wolf came.

She walked him — that was how it felt afterward — through the rooms of himself.

Here was a fire room, banked and aware of its heat, and her presence ran one finger along its door and the fire leaned to the touch. Here was a water room, deep and dark-bottomed. A room of earth that held weight without complaint; a room of wind that had paced its walls for three months. A room of clear light, low-burning but true.

Six rooms, she counted. Six.

And behind the last door — a room with no lamp and no need of one, thick and patient and black.

Her stillness came home to roost there. *Darkness.* Of the elements she carried, five had been shown to a teacher with wide eyes, and a sixth had been named for him with care. But this one she had never shown anyone, because this one did not belong on a student's report. This one belonged to the truth under the floor.

It stood in a wolf-cub in the defective row of the Eastsea Spirit Pagoda, wearing three months of patience and a price tag of junk.

Carefully, as if the door might bruise, she closed it exactly as she had found it.

The link settled between them then, thread by thread, the way a knot settles when the pulling is done and the rope rests. What came through it first was not thought and not speech. It was relief, large and shapeless, bigger than his whole small body — three months of held breath let go at once.

And then, through the same new road, unplanned, came one word. It arrived in a voice like paws finding snow.

*Moon.*

It caught in her like a hook.

She closed her hands around the quiet of it and waited for the next word, her heart striking hard and evenly against the small warm weight now curled at the edge of her soul. But there was no next word. Whatever he had spent on the glass, on the gold, on the reach, had run out on that one moon.

She did not push. Some doors you close as you found them.

Upstairs, the clerk at the registry desk stamped the transfer without looking up.

*Defective stock, shelf 9, disposed by student fusion. No flags.*

Across the hall, in an assay office that smelled of hot crystal, chief Kuang pulled the morning's reading file and looked at it for a long time. Then he clipped a second note to the back of the file, unsigned, and sent it up the pipe toward the research floors, where unsigned notes went when they were not curious enough to spend a signature on yet.

On the landing outside the vault, a white-clad back and a colder-than-steel voice were waiting for her.

"You are late, Gu Yue."

"Apologies, Teacher Wu." She dipped her head, exactly as late as she sounded, which was to say not very. "The elevator loses minutes."

"It loses none." He looked at her one beat longer than he looked at late students. Then the look let go of her, and the corridor took them both, toward the noise of the class just dismissed and the platform trip waiting in the morning.

Behind her ribs, in the new small country of the soul-link, something slept the sleep of the totally spent, paws twitching over ground he could not yet name.

"The defective shelf is free to look at," Gu Yue said under her breath, to nobody, and went to class.

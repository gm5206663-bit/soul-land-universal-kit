# Chapter 1 — The Choosing

## Part One — Both Courts Said No

The rotbirds had started counting him on the second day.

He knew how the counting went. It began high, in the dead pines, and came lower by one circle an hour. By dusk of the fourth day the circles were low enough that he could see their eyes. The eyes were the color of old ash, and they were patient, the way everything patient is, out at the forest's edge, where the smell of men had already died out of the wind and nothing human ever walked.

Six days since the silver pack had left him at the stream. The metal kennels had never come at all.

He was — the word was the same in both bloods; it was the one thing the two courts had ever agreed on — *defective*. The silver saw tarnish in him, a royal line drained thin. The metal smelled softness, a soldier's gold gone slack as river water. Neither was wrong. He had spent six days lying in the proof of it.

What he had left lived in six rooms behind his eyes. Five held lights that guttered: water, fire, earth, wind, light. They answered, when anything answered, like lamps low on oil. The sixth room was dark. He kept it dark himself, one paw against the door, because nobody had ever taught him what its tongue spoke and every instinct he owned said it spoke with its teeth.

A wolf does not beg a sky that has already refused him. He lay under the pines and counted his own breaths, and did not waste them.

Then, on the sixth dusk, the rotbirds all went silent at once.

Not fled. Silent. A hundred ash-colored shapes sitting still in the trees like dead men's hats, and beneath them the whole forest leaning, tipping by some fraction too small to point at, the way light leans toward a well. The stream's noise changed key. The cold air grew heavier by the weight of one held breath.

And his blood stood up inside him.

All five guttering lamps stood up at once, like grass turning toward a sun it had given up on. Beneath them the other thing in him — the gold, the soldier that feared nothing bred under any sky — folded itself small against the floor of his bones, and begged not to be noticed.

*Royal,* something said in him, without asking.

She came between the pines at a child's pace, in a plain grey dress, years too young to bend the weather. The weather bent anyway. The dusk leaned after her one soft step at a time, the way tall grass leans past someone walking, and settles, like no one allows it to do otherwise.

She stopped with the stream between them and looked at him the way the sky looks at the ground. No pity in it. That, out of everything, was what kept his eyes open.

Then she crossed the stream without wetting her dress, knelt in the mud where the silver pack would not kneel, and set one fingertip between his eyes.

The rooms opened.

He could not have stayed her. The door he kept shut with a paw, she opened with a thought; and she stood inside the dark sixth room longer than in the other five put together, saying nothing at all, and the dark kept her reckoning, and she kept the dark's. Then she closed the door on it exactly as she had found it, and stepped out through the lit five without touching a thing.

*Choosing,* the link said. The thought was hers; it did not arrive as words. It sat down in his skull with all the weight of weather: *I am choosing this one.*

And then, thinner, a second certainty laid beside the first: *but he must come across.*

Consent. She was not a door that gets walked through. She was a bridge that exists in both directions, and she would build her half, and his half was his to build or not.

Every court that had ever been asked had refused him. He dragged his forelegs under him — bone, and mud, and the five guttering lamps all burning low to buy the motion — and crawled the length of himself to where her open palm waited, and put himself in it, whole, rooms and dark and tarnish and all.

The bind took like heat to dry wood.

Afterwards she did not stand up. She sat down, abruptly, in the mud beside him, and the rotbirds watched the breath go out of her like a lamp turned down at dusk. Fusion costs the bearer; he did not know that law yet, he only saw it levied. She breathed three slow breaths of somebody very small who has just spent something enormous, and did not spend one word complaining of the price.

Over her head, a circle of pale yellow light gathered itself out of nothing.

No beast had died for it. No machine had minted it. The fog of her own breath rose and braided itself into a ring the way smoke learns a shape, and the ring settled down around her as quiet as dust, and the forest watched her wear it, and the watching held no judgment at all — only report.

"Mine are not taken," she said. It was the first sentence she ever gave him. "Remember that. The humans will ask one day."

She slept there until moonrise, in the pine needles, a hand-span away, spent; and because nobody in the world now knew they were two things instead of one, nobody mistook, and nobody hunted, and the rotbirds gave up the count and went to find easier dying.

## Part Two — Four Years in Change

The town on the river had a table, and the table had a book, and the book wrote down everything the road carried.

"Name?" said the clerk, without goodwill.

"Gu Yue."

The pen scratched. The wolf was hidden inside the girl's soul-sea the way a wintering thing hides in the lintel of a house, and he had no name at all yet, and it was the first time he heard hers spoken aloud to a human. Somewhere inside the link, before he could stop it, one word spent itself into the air behind her ribs:

*Moon.*

Her hand paused — half a breath, a stranger would have seen nothing. *Not your name,* she answered inside, dry as frost on stone. *Mine.* And the clerk wrote her in the book, and the book became her past, which was real paper and real ink and lied only about how it had begun.

Names were for later. She had said it once, asleep; he had never spent breath on it again.

---

The school was the third one, and the first that handed her to teachers with clean ledgers. She arrived the way she arrived everywhere: dressed plainly, registered late, underestimated by lunch.

He learned the first human lesson through her ears, in a bunkhouse, in the dark: that creatures who cannot feel the weather lie about it constantly, in writing, and believe their own paper.

Her rule, given to him once and never repeated: grow, and be counted for nothing. So the one yellow ring went public and stayed public for years, polite as a button, while behind it her soul-sea deepened in ways no auditor could ever hold still long enough to summon. Growth that cannot be counted cannot be audited. It also cannot be proved. She was content to be difficult about both.

She paid his six tongues their due without ever naming the debt. Once each season she put a coin of moon-pale metal on his tongue, silver-white, bought from a smith who knew better than to ask a child twice. The cost of it was the length of her own winter sleeves, and the sleeves got shorter each year, and she never once mentioned it aloud, and counted it fair trade for a warm room with five lit lamps.

The wound on his flanks had closed in the first month. What grew instead was stranger: the barely-adequate machinery of him, meeting new weather, quietly changing its fittings. Hoof-rot country made him rotproof. Smoke weeks taught his lungs sieves. Nothing about this ever got so much as a sentence between them. The silver pack had taught him already what happens to things that look unusual.

And the fourth winter, the letter came. A recommendation, sealed, to an academy two thousand li east — engineered the way seals are engineered, which is to say honestly, which is to say false only where the truth would have started fires. She sat on the bunk with it unopened across her knees, and there was a moment, freighted, in the link: *Go?*

He was not a politician. He was an animal that outlasts time. The world tilted east, and eastward the ground was well-trampled, and he knew nothing of it except that a wolf with a pack ledger walks farther than a wolf with a reputation.

*East,* he thought. One word. His second ever.

She opened the letter.

## Part Three — The Gates

Eastsea Academy rose out of sea mist like the idea of a fortress somebody had made rich. Wall, watchtower, banners of blue. He tasted it all through her nose on a morning thick with salt: oiled metal, discipline, five kinds of city weather laid down in terraces of paper and rank.

*Men build order,* she thought along the link, not to him exactly, *and then find it surprising that the weather doesn't apply.*

Dress: white. Regulation plainness the wardrobe had researched for a month. Her route to the grounds looped, deliberately, past the dirt of the athletics field. Watched gates are worse than watched doors, and she had a taste for arriving at edges.

Inside, a first-year class was eating a teacher alive. No — the reverse. A teacher with a face like winter was coming through a line of finishers as if exhaustion were a character flaw, and the food in this country's new body was beginning, slowly, to show itself for what it was. Running laps. Metal shirts. Sweat like rain promised to a soil with no say.

Two boys came past them in close order, both wearing the shirts, the younger one leaning into the other and the taller one carrying the lean without breaking stride at all. Cheerful labor. The girl, who had never once been carried, followed that pairing with a gaze that was four things at once.

The wolf felt the thing underneath first.

There was something on the tall boy — something that was not a shirt, older than a shirt — that made the gold in the wolf's marrow want to lie very, very flat. No word for it. Not danger; the danger was not pointed at anybody. Rank, but rank of a kind he had no country to measure it by. He flattened, politely, like a soldier in the presence of arms. She noticed the flattening, and filed it, and did not ask.

"What class are you in?"

The winter face had come over. The girl looked up, mild, and blinked as if being addressed were still unusual to her, after four careful years of being exactly that unusual to everyone.

"I'm here to attend school," she said. "However, I was told that the registration period has already passed."

A shape passed over the face of Wu Zhangkong. *Not a student and not permitted,* it said, with resentment to spare for people who minded. But somewhere ahead of it he also saw the white, and the gaze, and the unconcern of her; and somewhere past that, the part of him that was actually Shrek's made one quiet note downwards: *eyes.*

"This is a Soul Master's academy. If you want to attend here, then you must have the ability."

"I can take a test," said the girl, and smiled.

---

"Still undecided?" said Wu Zhangkong, once the last round of the runners came past. "Then watch this and make your mind up."

A slim boy named Xie Xie ran up and stood at attention. "Teacher Wu." Twin-soul line, rank eighteen, tidiest blade his age had. Wu Zhangkong looked from his wind-chilled face to Gu Yue's and delivered the weight. "Defeat her, and you won't have to run in the afternoon. Give yourself twenty minutes of rest; she'll wait."

"No problem." Xie Xie sat down to meditate, holding his breathing like it was money.

Twenty minutes later Wu Zhangkong lifted his eyes from the track. "Stand thirty meters apart from one another and prepare." They did. "Begin!"

The wolf felt him move when the call came — felt it through her, felt *everything* through her. And the first thing he understood, in the green-white rush of her raising one hand, was this: the girl had spent four winters sitting upon more power than the whole field's screaming could have emptied. She knew exactly, without looking, which droplets of it were allowed off the cloth.

Wind met the dagger at a stop. The cartwheeling blade was a question about opposites and the air answered with pressure from both sides at once, pinching the thing to a halt mid-thrust. And then the green turned red and the first fist of fire went out.

Xie Xie threw his waist ten degrees sideways in midair and the fire burned hair instead of a face.

*Damn,* he thought, landing hard, scrambling, open-mouthed. *What was that?*

He recovered, worked the flanks, picked his speed in earnest. Watching through the girl's senses as the feints stacked, the wolf read more patience in her stillness than threat in his mobility. The boy was good. He was the best the field made. None of it entered the part of her where the rooms of the world answer.

The dagger's hundred-year skill came out singing, a blade of yellow light with a dragon's roar riding on it, and one meter out from the girl the guiding wind simply made a suggestion, and it went past her, tragic and glorious, on rails no eye had built.

The earth swallowed Xie Xie halfway up the shin before his dignity did. Ice locked the bog. The wolf catalogued the moves: earth as trap, ice as discipline, wind as verdict — attendance everywhere, thrice and counted. And the link asked quietly, of her: *Light?* and she did not answer.

White light fisted out instead of fire on the last pass. Xie Xie understood, with all four limbs at once, that the difference between the mouth of the furnace and what was coming instead was safety, and still he lost the argument. Ice, fire, earth, wind, light — one after the other, without ceremony — and he collapsed in defeat.

Wu Zhangkong's eyes reflected colors. "Enough."

---

"Tell me what is your background," said the winter face. "And what was that martial soul of yours."

The letter came from her chest the way answers come from the confident. Sealed above, recomposed below — all four winters of her in real ink, and real names where real names had been written to the page by clerks with no stake in the truth of them. Near-perfect camouflage, privately evaluated. "This is a recommendation letter from my elementary academy," Gu Yue said. "And as of my martial soul — it is Elementalist."

*Elementalist.* The face did not change. The standing-in-front-of-it kind of seeing, the kind he used on soul masters the way other men used lab instruments, turned all its lenses on the word, which he had never heard once, and came back with a list of numbers and no ruling.

Below the ring of the girl the wolf waited, coiled, invisible. *Concealing her spirit soul,* the thought laid itself over the scene's grain, a tongue of weather in the year behind her that only the two of them knew the shape of. The yellow circle at her feet twinkled like a well-dispositioned jewel, and no one on the dirt had ever needed, or guessed, to look past it.

"I am able to control all six elements," Gu Yue said, without drama. "But I am unable to attach my soul ring onto any of these. Thus, although I can control the change in elements, it is hard to strengthen them. My first soul skill is Elemental Tide. It allows my soul power to hold on for a long period of time — and at the same time, lets me control the strength of the elements better."

Six, said his ears. The rooms behind her ribs numbered seven, and the dark one — his twin at the other end of the link, the private silver nobody here had ever seen — did not so much as listen. She had spent the morning deciding, again, not to show it. The morning was her custom. He had learned, four years in, to keep track of the customs like fences.

"I see," said the cold man at last, and the cold in his voice carried, through it, something infinitely small that smelled like revelation being put away carefully and privately. Four elements shown direct, two by nature's grace — a superior martial soul, exactly the sort schools burn credence to find. "Good. I've understood. You are now a part of our class — 1st Year Class 5."

"*Thank you*, teacher," said Gu Yue, and dipped her head, very slightly, one beat later than a grateful pupil does.

On the track, the tall boy with the two metal shirts hauled Xie Xie upright out of the bog-ice and had some trouble keeping down the smile. Gu Yue's gaze moved across all of them at once. It settled briefly on the boy again, resting there just long enough for the wolf to feel the same old acquaintance of *rank without a registry*. In the space of it the tall boy looked up. Their eyes met. The look she gave him meant something, and it was perfectly plain that he had no idea what.

Four years the secret had been walking, on paper and in silence, toward this ground. Today it stood on the track, breathing through a pair of black eyes, wearing a white dress and one harmless yellow ring, and nobody in the first grade's Class 5 had the instruments with which to keep the world's account.

# Chapter 5: The Weight Between Them

The underground field pretended to be an open sky.

Yan Shuo did not trust it.

The blue above the running track was too clean. Clouds drifted across the electronic ceiling in slow, perfect lines, and the artificial sunlight fell with the politeness of a teacher who had never shouted. Ventilation ports kept the air fresh. The ground had enough spring that a fall would hurt pride before bone. Everything in Heaven Luo Elementary Spirit Master Academy was designed, checked, and recorded.

That was why Yan Shuo did not trust it.

Real wind changed its mind. Real sunlight made shadows that children could chase. Real ground had dust. This place had none of those things, and yet the teachers stood at the side with recording devices as if a false sky could still reveal the truth of a child.

"Three laps," the physical teacher called. "Keep to the lanes. Do not use dangerous martial-soul output. Run properly."

Three laps meant one thousand two hundred meters. To adults it was a small test. To first graders, it was far enough for the loud ones to become quiet by the end.

Yan Shuo rolled his shoulders once.

The Fire Phoenix inside his chest answered the movement with a pleased pulse of heat. It liked running more than standing. It liked the idea of leaving everyone behind even more.

No flame, Yan Shuo reminded it.

His body began answering before his pride finished arguing. Warmth that wanted to jump into his palms thinned and slid lower, gathering around calves, ankles, and the small muscles along his ribs. His breath shortened on the inhale and lengthened on the exhale without asking permission from his thoughts. The field was artificial, the air was guided by vents, and his body was already learning the difference between this place and the courtyard outside.

His mother had said the same thing before he left that morning. Fire could help movement one day. Today it would only waste breath and make teachers write unnecessary notes. Attack-type did not mean foolish-type, even if half the attack class acted determined to prove otherwise.

Across the field, the control-type class was lining up too.

Lan Xuanyu stood somewhere in the middle of his row, neat and calm, looking less like a child about to be tested and more like someone politely waiting for lunch. He caught Yan Shuo's stare and smiled.

Yan Shuo lifted two fingers in a quick greeting.

Lan Xuanyu copied it one breath too late.

Yan Shuo almost laughed.

Then Ye Lingtong stepped to the front of the attack-type line, and the air changed in a way that had nothing to do with spirit power.

Some children wanted attention. Ye Lingtong expected it to arrive.

Her curly brown hair was tied back for running, and her chin carried the same proud angle it had worn since registration day. When the teacher raised a hand, she leaned forward before anyone else, already halfway into the race in her heart.

"Begin!"

Ye Lingtong shot out first.

For two breaths, Yan Shuo forgot his own plan.

She was fast.

Not merely fast for a proud child. Fast in a way that made the teachers' eyes sharpen and the front row stumble into chasing her rhythm. Her feet hit the track with trained certainty. Her arms did not swing wildly. She cut through the false sunlight as if the field had been built to prove her point.

Yan Shuo's chest heated.

The Fire Phoenix wanted to answer.

He nearly let it.

Then his breath caught on the edge of a lesson: fire that runs ahead of the body leaves the body behind.

He exhaled through his nose and settled into a pace he could keep. Not slow. Not safe enough to be boring. He entered the front cluster, a few steps behind the fastest children, and kept his warmth inside his ribs where it belonged. His legs found a rhythm. His shoulders loosened. When his right foot landed too hard on the first bend, the next step came softer. When his lungs tightened under the clean, recycled air, his breathing changed again, smaller but steadier. The track came and went beneath him, and every small discomfort became something his body quietly filed away.

Ye Lingtong disappeared around the bend.

Lan Xuanyu did not chase at all.

Yan Shuo noticed on the second lap, when the running lines stretched and the field grew full of small panting sounds. Lan Xuanyu stayed in the middle of the control class pack. He was not struggling. That was the strange part. He ran as if he could run longer, perhaps faster, but had been told not to prove it.

Yan Shuo knew that face. It was the face Lan Xuanyu wore when an adult instruction sat in his head like a cup he must not spill.

Do not stand out, then, Yan Shuo guessed.

He did not know why.

He did not ask across the field.

Ye Lingtong passed Lan Xuanyu from behind as if he were a post placed on the track for her convenience. She brushed close enough to make him hop aside. Yan Shuo's pace broke for half a step.

Lan Xuanyu said something.

Ye Lingtong turned her head too sharply and nearly ran into another child.

Yan Shuo did laugh then, but only once, and only because no one fell.

By the time the run ended, Ye Lingtong was first by a distance large enough to become a rumor before lunch. Yan Shuo finished well behind her, near the front but not close enough to be mistaken for her rival. He bent with his hands on his knees and breathed until the heat in his chest stopped complaining.

Lan Xuanyu finished much later, steady and barely red in the face.

"You could have gone faster," Yan Shuo said when the classes mixed around the water station.

Lan Xuanyu accepted a paper cup and considered the accusation seriously. "I finished."

"That is not the same."

"Teacher said three laps. I ran three laps."

There were times when arguing with Lan Xuanyu felt like punching water.

Yan Shuo drank his water in defeat.

Ye Lingtong did not look defeated by anything. She came toward them with her cheeks flushed from running and her eyes bright with unfinished anger.

"Lan Xuanyu," she said.

He turned. "Yes?"

"Spar with me."

Lan Xuanyu blinked as if she had asked whether he wanted to jump into a wall. "I don't want to."

Children nearby stopped pretending not to listen.

Ye Lingtong's face tightened. "Why not?"

"Mother said I should not bully girls."

Several children made small noises. One of the attack-class boys snorted and tried to hide it badly.

Yan Shuo closed his eyes for one instant.

Xuanyu, he thought, that was not the sentence.

Ye Lingtong looked as if Lan Xuanyu had slapped her with a towel in front of the whole field.

"Bully me?" she repeated. "You?"

"I just don't want to fight," Lan Xuanyu said, still honest enough to make everything worse.

Ye Lingtong moved.

The teachers were watching. That should have made the field safe. It did, in an adult way: no knives, no hard killing intent, no real disaster expected between first graders under supervision. But Ye Lingtong had trained before school. Her body already knew how to close distance, catch a wrist, kick a leg, and turn a child's balance into a fall.

Lan Xuanyu did not know any of that.

Yan Shuo saw his friend's eyes widen.

He took one step forward.

"Yan Shuo," his class teacher warned from the side.

The warning was sharp enough to stop his feet.

His heat did not stop.

Ye Lingtong caught Lan Xuanyu's right wrist and turned her shoulder beneath him. For a heartbeat the movement was beautiful in the way dangerous things could be beautiful: clean, trained, inevitable. Lan Xuanyu's feet left the ground. His free hand flailed with no skill at all.

Then something changed.

Yan Shuo felt it before he understood it.

The false sky seemed to press down.

Not on everyone. Not loudly. It was only a weight inside the breath, a brief deepening of the air, as if a mountain had remembered that it belonged above smaller things.

The Fire Phoenix in Yan Shuo's chest crouched.

His palms warmed.

Then the warmth pulled back by itself, away from the fingers that wanted to flare and into the center of his chest where the young phoenix shape could hold together. His shoulders dropped half an inch. His tongue tasted metal. His body treated the pressure like weather it had met before: dangerous, familiar, something to survive without becoming it.

He dragged one breath in, hard.

Hold shape.

Lan Xuanyu dropped.

Ye Lingtong's knees folded beneath the sudden weight. The clean throw collapsed into a heavy, ugly fall. The sound that followed was not the sound of a sparring lesson. It was the sound of children discovering that bodies were not toys.

Silence struck the field.

Lan Xuanyu lay across Ye Lingtong's back, stunned. Ye Lingtong did not get up.

The teachers moved at once.

"Back! Everyone back!"

Yan Shuo did not move forward this time. He stood with his hands clenched at his sides while adults lifted Lan Xuanyu away and checked Ye Lingtong. Lan Xuanyu kept saying something too soft to hear. His face had gone white.

The attack-class boy who had laughed earlier whispered, "She got crushed."

Yan Shuo turned his head.

The boy stopped smiling.

"She is hurt," Yan Shuo said.

It came out flatter than he expected. Hotter too.

The boy looked away.

Ye Lingtong was carried to the infirmary. Lan Xuanyu was kept behind for questions, then later measured and checked until the teachers could only frown at ordinary numbers. He was the height and weight a child should be. Nothing in the record explained why a trained throw had failed beneath a mountain's weight.

Adults liked records because records pretended the world was obedient.

That day, the record did not know what to do.

Yan Shuo found Lan Xuanyu after class by the courtyard fountain.

Lan Xuanyu sat with his hands folded in his lap, looking at the water without arranging toy cars or humming under his breath. That worried Yan Shuo more than tears would have.

"Are you hurt?" Yan Shuo asked.

Lan Xuanyu shook his head.

"She attacked you."

"I know."

"Then why do you look like you stole something?"

Lan Xuanyu looked down at his hands. "I didn't want her to get hurt."

Yan Shuo sat beside him.

For a while, neither spoke. The fountain changed from blue to green to a soft gold that made Yan Shuo's fingers itch with the memory of fire. Near Lan Xuanyu, the air had that familiar hidden heaviness again, but thinner than on the field, like an animal asleep behind a closed door.

Yan Shuo breathed slowly until the warmth inside him stopped tightening.

"I know you didn't," he said at last.

Lan Xuanyu's shoulders loosened a little.

"Next time," Yan Shuo added, because loyalty did not mean pretending problems disappeared, "if someone runs at you like that, shout earlier. Or fall sideways. Or do something that is not being lifted like a sack."

Lan Xuanyu thought about this with deep seriousness. "How do I fall sideways?"

Yan Shuo opened his mouth.

He did not actually know.

"We can ask a teacher," he decided.

Lan Xuanyu nodded, comforted by the existence of teachers.

Three days later, Ye Lingtong returned to physical training.

She did not run at the front.

That was how Yan Shuo knew the injury had been real. Her face was still pale, and her steps were careful in a way her pride hated. She jogged near the back with her jaw locked, pretending not to hear the whispers that followed her. Some children were cruel because they meant to be. More were cruel because they had never learned the weight of a sentence.

Yan Shuo watched her pass.

Ye Lingtong caught him watching and glared.

He did not glare back.

He also did not smile.

That seemed to anger her in a different way.

"When I reach rank ten," she snapped at Lan Xuanyu on another day, voice low but sharp enough to cut through the jogging line, "I will challenge you again. With a real soul skill."

Lan Xuanyu looked honestly troubled. "Do you have to?"

Ye Lingtong ran past before answering.

Yan Shuo, three steps behind, nearly stumbled from the effort of not saying anything.

Half a year passed like that: not quietly, because schools were never quiet, but steadily enough that the strange day became one knot in a long rope.

Lan Xuanyu became the child teachers praised when they wanted other children to study properly. Culture class, basic soul beast knowledge, written answers, history fragments, safety rules, anything that could be learned from a book or explained by an adult seemed to settle into him with patient ease. If the academy had been only desks and questions, he would have been impossible to catch.

Combat was different.

He learned. He tried. He listened. But he did not carry the natural hunger to win that drove Ye Lingtong, and he did not enjoy bruises the way some attack-type children pretended to. He was stable, polite, and oddly gentle for someone who had already caused two events teachers could not properly explain.

Yan Shuo learned different lessons.

They did not arrive as sudden revelations. They arrived as small corrections that never stopped. A blister changed how his foot met the floor the next day. A burned practice glove taught his palm to release heat wider instead of sharper. A failed dodge stayed in his knees until, during the next drill, they bent before he consciously remembered why. Even anger left marks: if his flame jumped when someone laughed at Lan Xuanyu, the next breath tried to pull it back sooner.

Attack-type training was less glorious than he had imagined.

It involved footwork until his calves ached. Breathing until boredom became its own enemy. Holding a flame at the edge of his palm without letting it leap. Striking pads without burning them through. Running after running. Stopping before contact when every part of him wanted the teacher to see that he could go harder.

"Again," his teacher said often.

"I did it right."

"Then do it right tired."

That sentence became a personal enemy.

At home, Lin Mei approved of the academy's cruelty.

"Good," she said when Yan Shuo complained. "A flame that behaves only when fresh is a festival lantern, not a martial soul."

Yan Ke, who had been checking school reports, looked up. "Festival lanterns are useful."

"They do not pass combat exams."

"Most paperwork also does not pass combat exams, but society depends on it."

Lin Mei gave him a look.

Yan Ke returned to the reports.

By the middle of the term, Yan Shuo's soul power had not taken one polite step. It had climbed like a child finding stairs built exactly for his feet.

Rank seven came first, almost quietly, after the first academy drills and his mother's breath corrections settled into the same rhythm. Yan Ke checked the home device twice because the number looked too easy. Lin Mei did not celebrate loudly. She only made Yan Shuo repeat the circulation until the new level stopped wobbling.

Rank eight came after sweat, but not after long waiting.

It came through bruised shins, burned practice gloves, meals that vanished faster than Yan Ke's budget expected, and sleep so hot and deep that Lin Mei began leaving an extra cooling charm near his pillow. His body was not merely storing soul power. It was learning where heat should sit, where it should not, how breath opened a path through the ribs, how the Fire Phoenix could rise without tearing his control apart. Every drill changed him a little. Every mistake left a smaller scar in the next attempt.

Rank nine arrived while the term still had weeks left.

That one made the teachers look twice.

It was still not a ring. It was still not Ye Lingtong's yellow hundred-year confidence. It was not Lan Xuanyu's impossible mystery. But for a boy who had awakened at innate level six, three ranks before the term was even finished was not ordinary. The academy wrote it as excellent compatibility, high martial-soul quality, strong family training, and unusually efficient early cultivation. All of those explanations were true.

They were just not the whole truth.

Then, before the final exam week, Yan Shuo touched the wall.

It happened in the family practice room, without audience or music or heroic light. He was holding a palm-sized flame steady through a breathing cycle when his soul power circled beneath his ribs, rose cleanly, and stopped against something thin and firm. Not pain. Not failure. A door.

Lin Mei's eyes changed before Yan Shuo spoke.

"Again," she said.

He tried again.

The same wall answered.

Yan Ke forgot the report tablet in his hands. "Is that..."

"Rank ten," Lin Mei said.

Yan Shuo stared at his palm. He had imagined rank ten would feel like winning a race. It felt more like reaching the edge of a roof and realizing the next step needed wings.

"So I get a soul spirit?" he asked.

"Not tonight," Lin Mei said at once.

He opened his mouth.

She lifted one finger.

He closed it.

"This is not a prize counter," she said. "The first soul spirit becomes part of you. We choose carefully, we check compatibility carefully, and you stabilize before anyone lets excitement make decisions."

Yan Ke found his voice. "Also, I need to check what our savings can survive."

That sounded more like him.

From that night onward, Yan Shuo's training changed. He could still polish control, strengthen his body, and deepen the road behind the wall, but he could not simply walk past it without the first ring. The fast climb had become a waiting place, and waiting was harder than running.

Yan Shuo's smile dimmed only a little when Ye Lingtong's ring became the school rumor. "She already has hers."

"She began higher. She trains hard. Her family has different resources." Lin Mei released his wrist after another check. "Do you want her path or yours?"

"Mine."

"Then count your steps from your own door. Yours moved quickly enough that now you must learn caution before pride makes you grab the wrong key."

Yan Shuo made a face. "You always say things that sound like homework."

"That is because you need homework."

Yan Ke nodded solemnly. "Your mother also says things that sound like warning labels. I married her anyway."

Lin Mei's mouth twitched.

Yan Shuo laughed, and the warmth in his chest settled brighter for it.

In the Lan household, the same half year carried a quieter worry.

Lan Xuanyu had done everything a good child should. He studied. He practiced. He listened to his parents. He tried to control water through the silver-patterned Blue Silver Grass on his left hand even when it answered him weakly and left him frustrated. He did not show his right hand. He did not speak carelessly about the parts of himself his father told him to hide.

His soul power stayed at rank eleven.

For another child, slow progress could be ordinary. For Lan Xuanyu, with the result his parents knew and the mysteries they had already hidden, ordinary explanations never stayed whole for long.

Nan Cheng worried with her hands: smoothing his collar, making extra food, checking his forehead, asking whether practice tired him.

Lan Xiao worried with silence.

"He is happy," he told Nan Cheng the night before the final exam.

"That is not an answer."

"It is the most important part of one."

Nan Cheng looked toward the hallway where Lan Xuanyu had gone to wash up. "He should have advanced more. Even normal children often move a rank in this time."

Lan Xiao knew. He also knew that too much attention could be more dangerous than slow progress. "If he does well tomorrow, we take the trip. We let him see space. He has been waiting for months."

"And after?"

"After, we keep watching."

It was not enough.

It was all they had.

Final exam day made the academy louder than usual.

Children who claimed not to be nervous arrived too early. Parents who claimed not to pressure their children gave too many reminders. Teachers carried lists, devices, and expressions that said they had already survived many generations of panic and expected to survive this one too.

Yan Shuo saw Lan Xuanyu near the entrance and waved.

Lan Xuanyu waved back, then was immediately captured by Ye Lingtong's approach.

She looked different.

Not taller by much. Not calmer. But there was a new confidence under her anger, a steadier pressure in the way she stood. Yan Shuo saw the way some attack-class students glanced at her and understood before she said it.

She had reached rank ten.

She had a soul ring.

The Fire Phoenix stirred, not with fear, but with the sting of comparison.

Yan Shuo was already at the rank ten wall by then, close enough to the next stage that his palms sometimes warmed when he looked at Ye Lingtong's yellow ring. Reaching the wall was not the same as opening it. A soul ring was not a decoration one grabbed because another child had one.

He breathed the sting down.

Count from your own door.

Ye Lingtong leaned toward Lan Xuanyu with a smile that did not belong to kindness. She told him about the combat exam in the afternoon, about the offense and control classes sparring together, about the rule that certain students could choose opponents. Lan Xuanyu's face changed slowly as he understood the trap.

Yan Shuo was close enough to hear only pieces.

He was close enough to see Ye Lingtong skip away satisfied.

"What did she say?" he asked.

Lan Xuanyu looked at him with great seriousness. "I think I am in trouble later."

"You think?"

"Maybe definitely."

Yan Shuo sighed. "After the written exam, stay near the teachers."

"The teachers are the ones arranging it."

That was unfortunate.

The morning papers went well for Lan Xuanyu. They went acceptably for Yan Shuo, which he considered a betrayal by certain questions about soul beast classification that had no respect for attack-type feelings. Lunch tasted like nervousness. The rest hour vanished too quickly.

Then the combat assessment began.

The academy kept first-grade combat simple. No one expected refined battle tactics from children just starting the path. The teachers wanted to see courage, control, movement, response, and whether a child understood the difference between sparring and hurting someone.

Those with active rings were separated first. Children who had reached the first bottleneck but had not obtained a ring yet were marked for observation rather than thrown into full spirit-skill exchanges.

Yan Shuo stood in the no-ring observation bracket and watched Ye Lingtong join the ringed group as if she had been born there. A yellow soul ring rose briefly at her feet during the preliminary check, bright enough that several children gasped.

Yellow.

A real hundred-year ring.

Yan Shuo's palms warmed before he could stop them.

He had reached the threshold too, but hers had already become a skill. His was still a locked door.

His own assessment came first.

His opponent was a sturdy boy with a Rock Arm martial soul, no ring, and the sincere belief that being heavier made him unbeatable. Because Yan Shuo's soul power was already at the bottleneck, the teacher added limits before they began: no flame burst, no direct burn, stop at control. Three months earlier, Yan Shuo would have answered with flame and pride and perhaps a burned uniform sleeve.

Today he stepped aside.

The first rush missed. His body remembered the scrape of earlier footwork drills and kept his heel from crossing too far.

The second nearly caught his shoulder. Heat gathered there at once, not enough to burn, only enough to remind him where contact would come.

The third met a small red-gold glow at Yan Shuo's forearm, not a blast, not a plume, only a controlled heat that made the boy pull back by instinct. Yan Shuo used the pause to shift his foot behind the boy's stance and push with his shoulder.

The boy sat down hard.

Yan Shuo froze with both hands up. "I stopped the fire."

His teacher looked at the recording device, then at the faint smoke curling from the edge of Yan Shuo's practice glove.

"Mostly," the teacher said. "Good movement. Acceptable control. Weak finish if the opponent is trained, but this is first grade, so I will be merciful."

Yan Shuo accepted this complicated praise with dignity.

Then Ye Lingtong chose Lan Xuanyu.

The field's attention shifted so completely that Yan Shuo felt his own small victory fold itself away.

Lan Xuanyu walked out looking as if he would rather be taking another written exam. His classmates cheered for him because they liked him, and because children liked to cheer before they understood what winning cost. Ye Lingtong stood opposite him with her chin high and her yellow ring ready beneath her feet.

"Use your martial soul," she demanded.

Lan Xuanyu raised his left hand.

Silver-patterned Blue Silver Grass unfurled from his palm. A white ring circled softly around it. Compared to Ye Lingtong's yellow ring and scaled transformation, it looked gentle enough to be misplaced.

Ye Lingtong released her spirit.

Scales surfaced over her hands, pale and angular. Her pupils sharpened. A low dragon sound passed through the field, not loud enough to frighten adults, but enough to make the younger children lean back. Light gathered around her small body as the yellow ring brightened.

Yan Shuo's Fire Phoenix reacted before he did.

Not to Ye Lingtong.

To the space between her and Lan Xuanyu.

Something old and hidden seemed to lift its head.

Yan Shuo's breath caught.

He gripped his own wrist with one hand, thumb pressing into the pulse point the way Lin Mei did when checking his circulation. Warmth tightened across his shoulders. For one strange instant he felt both too hot and too small.

Hold shape.

Lan Xuanyu panicked as Ye Lingtong charged.

The white ring around his Blue Silver Grass lit. Dew-bright water gathered, pulling into a turning shield before him. It was pretty, almost delicate, and Yan Shuo understood why Ye Lingtong did not slow. With a yellow ring and a dragon-line body, she should have broken through it.

She did not.

The light around Ye Lingtong vanished at the instant of contact.

It did not explode. It did not clash. It simply stopped being there.

Her charge carried her into the whirlpool without the protection she expected. Water took her, spun her, and flung her out onto the grass. She landed wet, shocked, and unharmed enough to rise with fury in her eyes.

The teachers stared.

The children shouted.

Lan Xuanyu looked at his own hand as if it had answered a question he had not asked.

Yan Shuo did not shout.

His stomach felt hollow.

The pressure had passed, but it left behind a heat under his skin and a tiredness that belonged to after-training, not watching. His Fire Phoenix had not flared. It had not helped. It had not won. It had only stayed itself while something higher moved nearby and ignored it.

That should have been nothing.

It felt like a lesson.

"Victory to Lan Xuanyu," Teacher Qiu announced, though her voice carried more confusion than certainty.

Class two erupted.

Ye Lingtong shouted Lan Xuanyu's name.

Lan Xuanyu turned with innocent eyes, and somehow that made her angrier.

Yan Shuo looked from one to the other and understood only that none of them understood enough.

After school, Lan Xuanyu went home on the school bus with a victory he did not know how to hold.

He should have been happy. The combat score mattered. His father had promised a trip if he did well. His classmates had cheered. Teacher Qiu had patted his shoulder and told him he had done well, even if she still looked puzzled afterward.

But when he reached home, he went straight to the cultivation room.

Water answered him, but weakly.

A droplet rolled near his fingers, faintly silver in the light of the Blue Silver Grass. He tried to make it sharper. Tried to make it colder. Tried to understand why Ye Lingtong's powerful skill vanished while his own control still felt like trying to hold a fish with wet hands.

"Why did you work then?" he asked the grass.

The grass did not answer.

The communication device rang.

Ye Lingtong's voice came through impatient and unhappy, and a moment later Lan Xuanyu opened the door to find her standing outside with Ye Feng behind her.

The apartment felt smaller with Ye Feng in it.

He was polite. He was also the kind of polite that belonged to soldiers and dangerous people: controlled, direct, and too heavy for a child's doorway. He asked to see Lan Xuanyu's martial soul because his daughter's skill had failed twice in ways he could not accept as coincidence.

Lan Xuanyu's parents were not home.

Lan Xuanyu still let them in.

In the cultivation room, Ye Lingtong repeated the charge.

Lan Xuanyu formed the water whirlpool again.

Ye Feng watched closely, his eyes carrying a faint purple ripple as if the world had slowed for him alone.

The result repeated.

Ye Lingtong's protective light melted away before contact. She was swept out, but this time Ye Feng caught her before she hit the wall. His expression changed from concern to shock.

This was not a normal collision between soul skills.

He stepped toward Lan Xuanyu.

"Let me see your martial soul," he said.

Lan Xuanyu held out the silver-patterned Blue Silver Grass.

Ye Feng touched the silver line.

The Iron Dragon inside him answered.

Eight rings rose in the small room: purple, purple, purple, purple, black, black, black, black. The pressure that burst from him was not an attack chosen by thought. It was instinct, reflex, a dragon-line martial soul reacting to something it believed stood above it.

Lan Xuanyu's mind went blank.

His right arm heated.

Warmth rushed through him as if a hidden door had opened. The silver-patterned grass curled down and wrapped his left arm from fingers to forearm, turning brighter and clearer. The water before him froze into a shield in the same breath.

The shield lasted only a moment.

Then Lan Xuanyu struck the wall and fell unconscious.

In the dark behind his eyes, gold and silver pushed against each other, neither willing to yield. A small nine-colored light appeared between them, broke, scattered, and joined one side before gathering again. The struggle quieted, not solved, only separated enough that the child could wake.

When Lan Xuanyu opened his eyes, there were too many frightened adults above him.

Nan Cheng held his hand as if she could anchor him to the world by grip alone. Lan Xiao's face was calm in the dangerous way ice could be calm. Ye Lingtong stood near her mother, pale and scared. Ye Feng carried guilt with a soldier's straight back.

A healing-type expert checked Lan Xuanyu and found nothing wrong.

That did not make the adults less afraid.

On the balcony, Ye Feng apologized formally and took responsibility. He explained what he believed had happened: not a simple skill failure, but suppression. Ye Lingtong's Celestial Dragon had been pressed down by something superior within Lan Xuanyu's mutation. When Ye Feng touched the silver pattern, his own Iron Dragon had reacted before he could stop it.

Lan Xiao listened.

He knew enough to be frightened by the same conclusion.

He also knew that fear must never look like permission.

"Do not come again without warning," he said coldly. "Especially not when only my child is home."

Ye Feng accepted it.

That night, Lan Xuanyu woke hungry.

Not snack hungry. Not growing-child hungry. Hungry as if some hidden furnace had begun to burn inside him and found the house unprepared.

Nan Cheng cooked until surprise became worry. Lan Xiao watched their son eat portion after portion and did not stop him until even a parent's love had to admit the kitchen had limits. Lan Xuanyu's stomach remained flat. His eyes remained bright. His confusion remained perfectly sincere.

After a short rest, Lan Xiao showed him the cultivation-room recording.

Lan Xuanyu stared at the image of his own arm wrapped in silver-patterned grass and the ice shield forming before it shattered.

"I can make ice?" he asked, wonder replacing fear.

This time, when he released the Blue Silver Grass and asked the water to freeze, it did.

Droplets turned to ice like pearls caught in leaves. Ice returned to water. Water gathered and scattered. When the grass wrapped his arm, the world of water elements opened more clearly than it ever had before, tiny blue lights answering him with joy.

Nan Cheng, whose own martial soul belonged to ice, watched in stunned silence.

Lan Xiao saw the good thing and the danger inside it at the same time.

He fetched the spiritual-power testing device.

The number climbed past the old result. Past fifty. It slowed only at sixty-two.

Spirit Connection realm.

For a child who had tested at eighteen not long ago, the leap was too large to be ordinary.

Lan Xiao and Nan Cheng did not tell Lan Xuanyu the number.

They praised him. They warned him. They reminded him not to speak carelessly at school.

Then they let him be a child again, because childhood was the one secret they were most desperate to protect.

The next day, Yan Shuo knew only pieces.

He knew Lan Xuanyu had not come to the gate in the morning with the same sleepy expression. He knew Lan Xuanyu ate enough at lunch to make two classmates count dumplings aloud. He knew Ye Lingtong looked guilty and angry at the same time, which should have been impossible but somehow suited her. He knew Lan Xuanyu seemed fine.

He did not know about eight rings.

He did not know about silver grass wrapping an arm.

He did not know about an ice shield, a dream of colors, or the number sixty-two.

What he knew was that when Lan Xuanyu sat beside him under the academy's artificial sunlight, the hidden heaviness around him felt clearer than before.

Yan Shuo's breath stopped for half a beat.

The Fire Phoenix inside him tightened, not afraid enough to hide and not foolish enough to challenge. It held itself like a young bird gripping a branch in sudden wind. Months of standing near Lan Xuanyu, of laughing beside him after strange days, of feeling a pressure no one named and still choosing to stay, had worn a narrow path through that fear. Yan Shuo did not understand the path. His body used it anyway.

Yan Shuo breathed out slowly.

"You are eating a lot," he said.

Lan Xuanyu looked down at his tray. "I am hungry."

"I can see that. Your noodles look scared."

Lan Xuanyu considered the noodles. "They should not be. I will eat them quickly."

Yan Shuo laughed despite the tiredness in his shoulders.

The laugh made breathing easier.

Exam results came without ceremony. Lan Xuanyu ranked near the top as expected, and because the combat record listed a victory over Ye Lingtong, his final combat score rose higher than even he seemed to understand. The teachers looked uncertain about it, but records were records once written.

Yan Shuo's own result was good enough to make his attack-class teacher read it twice: strong physical performance, improving culture score, rank ten bottleneck without a ring, excellent restraint for a young fire martial soul, needs more finishing discipline and careful first-ring planning.

He chose to ignore the last line until Lin Mei saw it.

School holiday began.

Ye Lingtong caught Lan Xuanyu before he left.

Yan Shuo was close enough to see but not close enough to own the moment.

For once, Ye Lingtong did not begin with a challenge. She pinched the hem of her shirt, looked away, then asked how he was. Her apology came out awkwardly, wrapped in an explanation about her father not meaning to hurt him and martial souls reacting by instinct.

Lan Xuanyu listened.

He accepted because he was Lan Xuanyu, and because being angry required holding onto a shape he had never liked.

But he did not become close to her in one breath.

That seemed fair to Yan Shuo.

Afterward, Lan Xuanyu found him at the gate.

"We are going on the trip," he said, eyes bright again. "Father bought tickets. We will go through Heaven Luo City and then take a spacecraft to Heaven Dou Planet."

Yan Shuo forgot restraint for a moment. "A real spacecraft?"

Lan Xuanyu nodded so hard his hair moved. "A real one."

The Fire Phoenix inside Yan Shuo rose in envy, wonder, and excitement all at once.

"Bring back a picture," Yan Shuo said.

"I will."

"Not only of your face."

Lan Xuanyu looked offended. "Why would I take only my face?"

"Because everyone likes looking at it."

Lan Xuanyu thought about this, then nodded gravely, accepting the burden of beauty with more dignity than it deserved.

Yan Shuo pushed his shoulder.

The two boys laughed beneath the academy gate while parents arrived, teachers organized holiday notices, and Ye Lingtong walked away with her chin not quite as high as before.

That evening, Yan Shuo stood in the family practice room and held a candle-sized flame around his palm for twelve breaths.

Seven had come like a door opening.

Eight had come like work.

Nine had come like a warning bell hidden inside praise.

Ten had come before the final exam and had refused to become eleven no matter how many times his pride pushed at it.

On the twelfth breath, the flame did not flare higher. It folded inward and brushed the same clear wall beneath his ribs. This time Yan Shuo did not shove. He let his soul power circle the boundary and return, cleaner than before.

Lin Mei nodded once. "Better."

"Still a wall," Yan Shuo muttered.

"Yes. That means it is doing its job."

Yan Ke looked up from the exam report. "Your bottleneck also appears to be doing a second job as a food expense multiplier."

Yan Shuo's stomach growled afterward with embarrassing force.

"That is evidence," Yan Ke said.

"That is not a real diagnosis."

"It is a father diagnosis."

Lin Mei handed Yan Shuo a towel. "Eat. Then sleep. Growth that does not rest becomes damage. The next choice will not be made while you are hungry enough to bargain with a kitchen cabinet."

Yan Shuo closed his hand and let the last warmth vanish.

Outside, Zi Luo City's metal towers held the evening light. Somewhere far beyond them, spacecraft waited to climb out of the world. Lan Xuanyu would see stars through real glass soon. Ye Lingtong would train through a holiday that pride would not let her waste. Teachers would file reports that explained only what they could measure.

Yan Shuo had reached the first bottleneck before the term ended.

He had no ring.

No soul spirit.

No soul skill.

Only a Fire Phoenix that had learned a little more about waiting, and a body that had spent six years quietly learning how to become ready before anyone knew what readiness meant.

He ate three bowls of dinner, fell asleep too quickly, and dreamed of running under a false sky without letting his fire leave him behind. In the dream, each wrong step warmed, softened, and tried again; each breath found the wall again and waited for the right wings.

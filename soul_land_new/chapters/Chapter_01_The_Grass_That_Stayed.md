# Chapter 1: The Grass That Stayed

The boy woke before his mother did, the way he always woke — all at once, and quiet, as if some part of him had already been up for a while watching the dark go grey.

There was no sound in the house yet except the roof beams settling with the cold. Lan Shen lay still for a few breaths and counted what he could hear: the last of the wind in the eaves, the mule two walls away shifting its weight in the straw, his father's slow breathing through the thin partition. No dogs barking in the lanes. Today was the day, and the village had slept badly because of it, and even the dogs seemed to know that and keep their mouths shut.

He was six years old, and only just — the age of the ceremony, which is the only age that matters in a village.

His grandmother had died in the first hard frost of winter, and the house was still shaped around her absence the way a field is shaped around a stone that has been pulled out — everything growing a little differently where she used to be. She had been the one who told him the story everyone in Qingni knew and nobody could prove: that long ago, in a village not far down the road, a child of these same fields had grown up to be someone the whole continent bowed to. She told it on winter evenings with her feet toward the stove, and she always ended the same way.

*A soul is like seed, little one. You cannot tell by the husk.*

He did not understand it then. He dressed now in the dark, by feel, in the order his mother had laid out on the stool the night before, and his fingers found every button without fumbling.

---

Breakfast was the best bowl. That was how he knew the day had weight.

His mother had skimmed the thick part of the porridge into his bowl and given his father the thin, which she would never have done on an ordinary morning, and neither of his parents mentioned it, which was how they did things. His father ate standing up, broad-shouldered under the roof beams, his eyes going now and then to the window where the sky was turning the color of creek water.

"Eat," his father said, once, which meant *you don't have to be afraid, but I can't think of what else to say.*

"Yes, father."

His mother stood with her hands folded in her apron, watching him eat with the particular stillness she had when she was counting something — grain sacks, copper, odds. Lan Shen had heard the women at the creek call his mother sharp. They said she could look at a man and tell you what he had in his pocket. She had looked at her son for six years now, and what she had found, and kept to herself, was this: the boy did not get sick the way other children got sick. He did not startle. When he fell, which was rare, he came up slowly and unhurried, checking himself like a farmer checks a tool after a drop, and went on. She had decided, in the private way she decided things, that he had a quiet belly, and that a quiet belly was worth more than most of what the world paid for.

Today her eyes did not count. They only stayed on him, and when he finished the bowl she wiped his mouth with her thumb the way she had when he was small, and said, "Whatever comes out of that hall, you come home and eat supper."

"Yes, mother."

"Say it like you mean it."

"Whatever comes out of that hall," said Lan Shen, "I come home and eat supper."

She gave him the road money for the fair then, which he did not need, and which he took with both hands.

---

Outside, Qingni was moving toward the square the way water moves toward a low place, by every lane at once.

It was early in the year, the fields still the color of last year, the creek running high and brown under the little bridge. The fair had come with the ceremony, as it always came, because the village had long ago learned to make one journey do two errands: cloth, salt, needles, the mender, the man with the honey cakes. Lanterns from the night before still hung on the elder's gate, unlit now, smelling of burnt oil.

Old Tao stood at the gate in his clean robe, which was so old it had gone soft as cloth worn by water, receiving each family with a nod. He was the elder of Qingni and had been the elder for as long as the creek had run, lanky and neat, and he took the ceremony the way some men take religion — personally.

"Children together," he said, as he said every year. "Mothers behind the line, fathers behind the mothers, and nobody speaks to the lord attendant unless spoken to. He comes all the way from Nuoding on our account."

"How many this year, elder?" someone asked.

"All of them this year," said Old Tao. "Every child of the age, and not one of them late, or I'll know the reason."

Lan Shen found himself at the edge of the children's line without meaning to, the way he found the edges of most things. Tao Wu found him a moment later, because Tao Wu found everything.

"My mother says I'll awaken a tiger," Tao Wu announced.

"You don't have a tiger in your family," Lan Shen said.

"Maybe not in the family. Maybe in the soul. The attendant comes from the city. He's seen everything." Tao Wu bounced on his heels. "What do you think you'll get?"

Lan Shen considered it honestly. He had been six for only a few days, and spent most of his life before that watching soil, weather, hens, and his father's hands. "A hoe," he said.

His grandmother had once told him that soul masters could fly. He did not expect to fly. He expected, if he expected anything, the warm flat feeling of not minding, which was what he usually felt about most things, and which his mother mistook, on her better days, for wisdom.

The cart came up the road while the fair was still setting out its wares.

---

It was an ordinary cart with an extraordinary passenger, which is the way of such things in villages.

The attendant from Nuoding was a dry man in Spirit Hall grey, neither young nor old, with the patient look of someone who had unpacked the same black stones in village after village. He greeted Old Tao with two fingers at his brow, accepted a cup of hot water and no tea, and set up on the raised boards of the fair platform, because the hall had learned long ago that ceremonies ran better when children had to climb up to them.

The stones were the color of night and round as fists, and he laid them out in a pattern without measuring, the way a woman lays out dumplings. Then, from a cloth, he took the crystal ball, and a sound went through the square that was not quite a whisper — because the crystal ball was blue the way the sky is blue on the one good week of the year, and it was the most beautiful thing most of the children had ever seen that did not belong to them.

"Children of Qingni," said Old Tao, in his ceremony voice. "Today the hall opens your souls. Whatever comes, you bow. Whatever comes, you remember your manners."

The attendant stood up. He did not raise his voice, and the square went quiet anyway, the way squares do around people who carry power the way other people carry tools.

"My name is Wen," he said. "I am of the Spirit Hall sub-hall at Nuoding. When your turn comes, stand in the stones and do not move until I tell you. It does not hurt." A pause, and the faintest ghost of something like kindness crossed his face and left. "Usually."

He opened his working, because a soul will not answer a man who keeps his closed.

Two rings rose from under his feet and hung in the morning air, turning slowly, the color of ripe grain. Yellow — a beast's years, caught and carried in a man. A farmer two rows back took off his hat without noticing he was doing it. Old Tao's chin came up, because this was what he had wanted the children to see with their own eyes — not the rank, which was a number, but the fact of it, that a person could hold a beast's years inside them and stand there drinking hot water.

"A grand soul master," Old Tao said, low, to no one, the way other men say *the harvest is good.*

The first child went up.

---

Lan Shen watched every one of them, because watching was what he did, and because the thing being done was going to be done to him, and he wanted to know its shape.

The boy with the blacksmith's nephew's face stood in the stones and shut his eyes hard, and a little hammer came out of his hand, the size of a sparrow, and hung there grey and trembling while the attendant watched it, and then the light behind the attendant's palm went out and the hammer vanished and the attendant said, in the voice he used for most of them, "No soul power. Cannot cultivate." The boy came down red-faced, and his father put a hand on his head, and that was the shape of most of them.

A bowl. No soul power. A length of rope, thin as a worm. No soul power. A girl whose soul turned out to be her own two hands, doubled and shining, which the attendant studied for a long moment before he said no, and she came down crying with pride anyway.

Few of the children awakened anything at all. Fewer still changed the attendant's face. The square had gone from festival to something older — a weighing, Lan Shen thought, though he did not have the word for it yet. A weighing that happened to everyone once, on one morning, at six years old, in front of the fair and the lanterns and the honey-cake man.

"Eleventh," said Old Tao, reading his list, and then stopped, because he had reached the end of the list and there was still one child. "Tenth. Tao Wu."

Tao Wu went up the boards like he was going up a hill he meant to own.

He stood in the stones. He did not shut his eyes — he glared at the air, daring it. The attendant's hand rose.

A dog came out of Tao Wu's chest.

It was brown and stubby-tailed and about the size of a hen, a field dog of no particular breed, and it sat down in the air, looked at the attendant, and sneezed.

The square made a sound. The attendant raised an eyebrow, and then his other eyebrow, and then he did something he had not done all morning: he brought the crystal ball forward without being asked.

"Hand on the ball."

Tao Wu put his hand on the ball, and the ball lit.

It was not a great light. Later, in the stories, it would become a great light, the way such things do, but standing there it was a small honest glow, like a lamp seen through a sleeve. The attendant looked at it for a long moment, and something that might have been memory moved behind his face — perhaps his own morning, in his own village, a lifetime ago.

"There is soul power," he said. "Faint, but true. The soul is cultivable." He looked at Tao Wu, who had gone the color of the lanterns. "Take care of it, boy. It's more than most get."

Tao Wu came down the boards into a village that had already decided about him. Old Tao was shaking his hand with both of his own. Someone was shouting about the quota. Tao Wu's mother had hold of the elder's sleeve and was saying the same thing four different ways. The fair had stopped being a fair.

And it was in that noise, with the village's back turned to him, that Lan Shen heard his own name called.

---

He climbed the boards one step at a time, and did not hurry, and did not slow down.

The attendant was repacking. The stones were already half in their cloth. It was the end of the morning, and the man from Nuoding had a second village before noon, and his kindness had been spent where kindness mattered, on the boy with the dog.

"Stand in the stones," the attendant said, without looking up. "It will be quick."

Lan Shen stepped in among the black stones and stood still.

He was aware, all at once and in no particular order, of: the boards under his feet, still warm from the children before him. His mother behind the line, with her hands folded. His father, whose eyes he did not look at, because looking at them would be a weight. The honey-cake man counting his morning's copper. Tao Wu on someone's shoulders. The blue ball on its cloth, like a piece of sky somebody had put down and forgotten.

The attendant's hand rose, and the warmth came.

It was not painful. It was like stepping into water that was a little warmer than you expected — it came up around him from the stones, through the boards, through the soles of his shoes, and found the place in him where the day had been waiting since before he was born, and knocked.

Something answered.

It came up out of his palm, and it was grass.

One blade, then a small tuft, blue-silver in the morning light, thin as thread, bending slightly in a wind that was not blowing. It was the grass of field edges and creek banks and broken walls. It was the grass that grows up through the place where a house used to be. It hung above his open hand, ordinary in a way that had weight, the way the last thing in a silent room has weight.

Somewhere behind him, someone laughed. It was not a cruel laugh. It was the laugh of a day letting its breath out.

The attendant looked at the grass. He had worn the hall's grey most of his life, and he had seen this soul in every province of the empire, because it is the soul the world has most of and prizes least, the way the world does with most things. His face did the courteous thing. His hand did not go to the crystal ball.

"Blue Silver Grass," he said, pleasantly, the way one names the weather. "I'm afraid it isn't a soul that can—"

The grass did not fade.

That was the small thing, and it was the only thing about the morning that was not like the other mornings of the circuit. The other children's souls had gone out the instant his light left them, like candles pinched. This one stood above the boy's palm, swaying a little, as if it had not been told the ceremony was over, or had been told and had decided to stay anyway. The attendant looked at it a second time, and frowned, and then, because he was a thorough man and it was cheaper than an unanswered question, he picked up the crystal ball.

"Hand," he said.

Lan Shen put his hand on the ball.

The ball lit the way a coal lights when the fire has mostly gone out — not dark, not bright. A small, steady, honest ember, no bigger than the hollow of his hand, and steady was the word for it, because it did not flicker, and the attendant watched it for a moment longer than the light deserved, and felt something he would not have been able to name: the sense of having weighed a thing and found the scale wrong, though he could not say which side of it was wrong.

Then he set the ball down, and did his duty, which was the truth.

"Soul power, third level," he said. "The soul is cultivable — technically. But a Blue Silver Grass at third level…" He did not finish, because finishing would have been unkind, and he had been at this a long time. He looked at the boy, who had not moved, whose hand was not shaking, and who was looking at the grass in his palm the way the grass might have looked back. "You may go down, child."

Lan Shen bowed, because Old Tao had raised him to bow, and came down the boards.

The square was already talking about Tao Wu's dog and the quota and the road to Nuoding, and the talk parted around him and closed again behind him, the way water closes around a stone, without malice, without interest, completely.

His father was at the bottom of the boards. His father's face was doing a hard thing badly, which was hiding what it felt. A big hand came down and rested on Lan Shen's head, heavy and warm, and stayed there longer than the head needed it.

"Come on," his father said. "Your mother will have supper early."

And whatever had come out of that hall, he came home.

---

That night, after the lamps were out and the house had settled into its sleeping shape, Lan Shen stood at the edge of the field behind the house, where the creek road bends, in the habit that had been his grandmother's before it was his — watching the treeline before sleep, the way she had taught him to do, though she had never told him why.

It was too dark to see the grass. He did not need to see it. It was there in every direction, along the field edges, down the creek banks, up through the broken wall of the old shrine — the thing the world had most of and prized least, the same everywhere, going quietly about its business of holding the soil together, and nobody thanked it, and it held the soil together anyway.

He opened his hand in the dark.

He could not see what was in it, but he could feel where the morning had been — a small warm place in the middle of his palm, like a seed that has decided something.

He stood there a long time, breathing in and out the way he always breathed, slow and all the way down, and he did not cry, and he did not make a promise, because he did not yet know what there was to promise against. He only stood with his hand open at the edge of the dark field, where the grass grows, and was, for the first time in his life, the same as everything around him.

The treeline stood quiet, and kept its counsel.

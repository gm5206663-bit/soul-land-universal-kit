# Chapter 4: The Road Came Home

The year that followed was not empty. That is the truest thing that can be said about it, and the thing that most stories would leave out, because empty years are easier to skip over, and because the year that followed was full the way a furrow is full — with what is put into it, one handful at a time, and with nothing else.

The boy was seven now, in the way the village counted, which was the only way the village counted. He had been six at the rite. He was seven at the ploughing, seven at the weeding, seven at the first cutting of the summer grass, and he was seven at the dusk field edge every evening, where the habit he had made in the winter kept itself, the way true habits do, without needing to be remembered.

His mother's teaching had gone the way of all her teaching, which was to say it had gone into him without announcement. By the time the year had turned once, he could hold a pot through a whole meal without being told twice, and he knew fire the way a good farmer knows weather — not in theory, but in the hand. He knew which greens went with which grain, and which roots forgave a slow fire and which punished it, and he knew the difference between feeding a family and filling them, which is a difference the poor understand before the rich ever hear of it. His grandmother's line ran in his mother's teaching too, in the plant way: what grew at the field edge that was good for a cough, what was good for a cut, what was good only for the compost, and what must not be touched at all. He learned these things the way he learned everything, which was completely, and with his whole body, and before the lesson was done it had become simply the way things were.

His father's teaching was slower and had more dirt in it. Lan He taught the way he did everything — by doing, and by letting the doing teach, and by asking a question only when the boy was ready to carry the answer. How to tell rain from the hill by the way the light lay on the far trees. How to choose seed by the weight of it in a closed hand. How to hold a hoe so the work lasted the day instead of the morning, which is the difference between a farmer and a boy with a hoe, and which the boy learned in his shoulders before he learned it in his head.

And there was the watching, which belonged to neither parent, and which the boy did alone.

The grass-report had grown. That was what he called it in himself, because his father had asked to be told when the grass did something, and a report needs a keeper. By the deep of that year he knew the grasses of Qingni's fields the way his mother knew her grains — the tall one the oxen loved, the creeping one that strangled young millet, the soft one that meant the soil there was tired. And the blue-silver, which was his. He kept its record honestly. It grew where it grew. It did not grow faster than its kind. It did not grow taller, nor thicker, nor stranger. In the spring it came up like any grass, and in the autumn it went down like any grass, and the only entry its ledger carried was the one from the hard winter, which was that it stood when the cold came, where the others lay, and that he had seen this, and did not know what it meant, and did not need to yet.

He told his father about it, each thing as it came, in the few words the report required. His father heard each one, and nodded, and asked nothing beyond it, which was the truest gift a father could give a son who was learning to see: to be believed without being examined.

And beneath all of this, patient as the water table, ran the thing that had no name in the village because the village had no word for it.

What grew in him that year was not nothing, and the record says so plain, because it is the kind of thing stories skip when they are in a hurry to the method.

The sense deepened the way everything deepened in him — by use, and honestly. By the spring he could hold the faintness at the field edge without losing it, the way you hold a bird in the hand without squeezing. By the summer he felt it in more than stillness: in the creek water going, in the sun on the soil, in the ox's flank beside him, in his mother's hands at the fire — everywhere and always, the world full of it the way the world is full of air, and everyone in the world breathing it without knowing. And the current in him that had no name grew of itself across the year, the way he had watched the grass grow: not commanded, because he had no commands; not given, because nothing gave it; grown by attention and season and what was already in him. By the late autumn he could move it, faintly, the way a stream moves through a meadow that has no bed — here and there, where the looking led, thin and wandering, and too much of it soaking away into nothing.

That was the true shape of the bottleneck, once there was something to bottleneck. It was not that he had nothing. He had something, and it was alive, and it was growing, and it was his. It was that he had no groove. Water without a channel goes everywhere and arrives nowhere; a year of it makes a marsh, not a field. Some evenings he could feel the slow soaking-away, and feel too that it was better than nothing, which it was, and not enough, which it also was. The warm place behind his ribs was no longer patient the way a seed is patient; it was patient the way a river is patient, upstream of a place where the land has not yet decided to open.

No one came. That was the plain fact of the year, and it is recorded plainly. The hall's man came once, at the season of the rite, with his six black stones and his crystal, and the village brought forward its six-year-olds, and this year the crystal gave back nothing worth the ink, and the man wrote what he wrote, and ate what he was given, and went back south to Nuoding with the weather behind him. No one came for the registered name that had no teacher. No one was coming, and no one owed the coming. The hall measured the light in children. It did not keep the children lit. That was what the boy understood, in the unexamined way he understood the seasons, and what he carried without complaint, because complaint had never once in the life of the village been observed to change the weather.

The road stayed silent too. Aunt Zhou carried news from the city the way she carried everything, at cart speed and half accuracy — a boy from a village down the belt had run away from the academy, or a merchant had paid a soul master more than a merchant paid for grain, or the city wall needed mending again. None of it was Tao Wu. None of it was a letter. The distance did what distance does, which was to be a distance, and the promise wire stayed strung across it, carrying no weight yet, and holding anyway.

And on a shelf in Nuoding, in a book that did not hope, a line of ink kept its place, patient as paper.

---

The road dried, and the carts began to go, and the year turned itself over the way it always did, by work. Then one morning in the walking season, when the belt was firm and the air had begun to think about cold, the elder's grandson came home.

Aunt Zhou saw him first, because Aunt Zhou saw everything first, and within the hour the village had assembled the news into its proper shape: Tao Wu, from the academy, walking the last stretch of the road alone, carrying a cloth bundle over one shoulder, and changed. That was the word the village used, and it was the right word, and the village was wrong about it anyway, because when the boy saw him coming up the lane between the houses, with the light of the afternoon behind him, the first thing he knew was that Tao Wu was not changed. He was Tao Wu. He was taller, and he walked differently, and he wore city shoes, and his clothes had been mended with a thread that was not the village's thread, but the loudness was in him, and the quickness, and the wanting to be something, and those had come home intact.

He went to the elder's house first, because he was the elder's grandson, and the village let him. It was the next day before he crossed to the Lan place, and the boy was at the field edge by then, at his dusk, and Tao Wu came there, which was the right place, and which he knew without being told, and which was how the boy knew the city had not gotten into the middle of him.

Tao Wu stood beside him and looked at the field, in the way of someone being shown a thing they had once known.

"It's smaller," Tao Wu said.

"It was always this size."

"No, I mean—" Tao Wu made a gesture that took in the field, the lane, the roofs of the houses along it, the hills beyond. "All of it. The city is big, and then you come back, and everything's been shrunk."

The boy considered this. "The field's the same," he said.

"That's what I'm telling you." Tao Wu laughed, and the laugh was the old laugh, the one that arrived ahead of him. "You're the same too. Still standing here looking at the grass."

"You said you'd tell me everything."

"I did say that." Tao Wu shifted his bundle on his shoulder, and his sleeve rode up, and there on his wrist, thin as thread now and going the way of all grass toward brown, was a ring that had been braided. The boy looked at it for a moment, and Tao Wu looked down at it too, and pulled his sleeve back down, not in shame and not in pride, just the way you pull a sleeve down. "Come on," he said. "Let's go to the creek. I've got things to say."

They went to the creek, to the bend where the road came in, where they had stood the day of the verdict, which neither of them named. The water was low this season, moving over the stones without hurry, and they sat on the bank the way boys sit, and Tao Wu talked.

The academy was hard, he said, and he said it with pride, because he was enduring it. The work-study place was a roomful of boys sharing cramped rooms, and the food was measured, and the city children knew things he had not known there were things to know, and the teachers were not cruel and were not kind and were busy, which was worse than either, until you learned to bring them questions worth their being interrupted for. He had learned to read some. He had learned to do sums in a book. He had learned to bow the way the city bowed, which he demonstrated with a mockery so accurate that the boy laughed, and Tao Wu laughed with him, pleased.

And then he came to the thing he had come to say, and he came at it the way he came at everything, ahead of himself.

"They teach us how to move it," he said. "The soul power. There's a way. Every day, first thing, all the students — a breathing way, and a sitting way, and you put your mind here." He touched the place below his ribs, close to the place where the boy kept his own small living warmth, and the boy felt himself go still in a way he had no practice being, which was the practice of everything else. "It took me ages to feel anything," Tao Wu said. "Months. The teacher said that was normal, that most people take ages. And then one morning it moved, and it's — I can't say. Like when your foot's been asleep and it comes back, except you can make it happen. You move it, and it goes, and when it goes you're more." He stopped, and for a moment the pride went out of his voice and left something truer in its place. "I haven't made a ring. Nobody at my place has made a ring. Rings need hunts, and hunts need masters and money, and the academy says it's all done proper when we're ready. So I'm just moving it, every day. But it's real. It's the real thing."

The boy sat with this, and let it settle the way he let everything settle. Then he said the only thing the moment had in it, which was the thing he always said: nothing. He looked at the water. He waited.

Tao Wu did not wait with him. Tao Wu was not made for waiting, which was why the city suited him, and why the promise had ever been made in the shape it was made.

"Sit up," Tao Wu said. "Straight like this. Now the breathing — listen. It's slow. You breathe in, and you think about drawing it down, like water coming down to a field. And you breathe out, and you let it go where it wants. And you don't push. That's the part that took me ages. You don't push. The teacher says pushing is how people hurt themselves. You attend to it, and it comes, or it doesn't, and if it doesn't you do it again tomorrow." He paused, and looked sideways at the boy, and did not manage to keep the next part casual, because he was Tao Wu. "I could teach it to you, if you want. The way we do it at the academy. Nobody'd mind. It's not a secret thing. It's the everyone thing. I asked about it, actually, I asked the teacher whether the registered ones who stayed home ever got taught, and he said the hall's got ways, registers and visits and such, but he said mostly it depends on the family, whether somebody comes by." He shrugged the whole city and its systems onto one shoulder. "I could just show you."

The boy looked at him for a long moment. Later, walking home in the dark, something his father had said at a gate would come to him — pay it where it grows — and he would understand then that Tao Wu had done, without ever hearing it, the thing those words meant. But at the creek, in the moment, there was no understanding, only the fact: here was the road, which had been silent, and here was what it had brought home, held out in the shape of a friend's impatience and a friend's goodness, which were the same thing in Tao Wu the way they were the same thing in all the loud ones.

"Show me," the boy said.

So Tao Wu showed him, and the teaching was exactly like Tao Wu — impatient, and generous, and better than it knew it was. He corrected the boy's shoulders. He corrected the boy's breathing twice, and then stopped correcting it, because the boy's breathing had stopped being wrong in a way Tao Wu could see but not explain, the way water stops being wrong when it finds its level. Then he watched the boy for a little while, the way you watch someone you are teaching, to see what wants fixing next, and what he saw made him stop in the middle of his next instruction.

"You're moving it," he said. "Already. You're actually moving it."

"It moves," the boy said, because that was the honest size of it, and it had been moving all year.

"Without anyone teaching you?" Tao Wu's voice did the thing it did when the world turned out to contain more than he had been told. He crouched in and looked at the boy the way the attendant had looked at the crystal. "The teacher said most people take months just to feel it, and that's with the academy doing it proper. How long have you—" He stopped, and did the sum out loud, the village way. "Since the winter. You've had it since the winter." His face went through the breaking-open and the closing-better, quick, the creek-road thing, and came out the other side proud, the way he was proud of everything that belonged to people he had decided belonged to him. "Then this will be fast for you," he said. "The method's only the groove. You've already got the water."

The light went out of the afternoon while they sat there, and when Tao Wu finally stood up to go back to the elder's house, he was carrying the ease of a debt paid, and the boy was carrying something he had no name for, and the creek went on doing what it had done all along, which was to go.

"It's every day," Tao Wu said, over his shoulder. "That's the whole law of it. Every day, or it's nothing."

"Every day," the boy said.

He kept it that evening, and every evening after.

---

He sat at the field edge when the dusk came, because the field edge was his, and because the watching and the breathing had become, without any decision, one thing. He sat the way Tao Wu had shown him, except for the parts of it that were not for Tao Wu's body, which his body had quietly set aside the way his body did everything — before he got there. He breathed the way the creek moved, low and without hurry. He attended to the place below his ribs, the way he attended to everything, without demanding anything of it.

And the warmth came — not for the first time, and that is the thing no academy teacher was there to tell him, and no one needed to tell him, because he had watched water all his life.

It had come before, all year, thin and wandering, soaking away into nothing the way water does where there is no bed for it. What was new was the groove. Not because he called it — he had understood what Tao Wu's teacher meant about pushing before Tao Wu had finished saying it, because pushing was what you did with what you owned from outside, and this was not outside. The shape Tao Wu had carried home out of the city took the year's wild water into itself the way a furrow takes rain — all of it, at once, and without effort, because that is what water does when the direction it has always wanted finally arrives. It moved. Not thin this time — gathered. Where it went he was more, exactly as Tao Wu had said, and nothing like Tao Wu had said, because it was his, in the way his hands were his, and a year of his own attention was waiting in it, and he did not think about any of this while it was happening, because he had been taught, by the whole first year of his life, to let what was happening happen.

It did not make him something else, and the record says so, because the record lies about nothing: one night of groove does not pay for a year of field, and growth still costs what growth costs, and no door opens in a night. But the marsh began that evening to become a field. The water knew where it was going.

When it had gone where it was going, and the dusk was nearly done, he opened his hands on his knees, the way he always did at the end of the watching, palms up, empty, which was his own law without ever having been spoken.

And there, in his palms, faint as breath on cold glass, stood the blue-silver.

He looked at it the way he looked at everything. It was not much. It was nothing, by the measure of the world, by any measure the city kept. It was the grass, his grass, common as water, answering the first shaped call it had ever been given, with the only answer it had, which was to be itself. And it was not only to be seen. For the first time he could feel it — the faintness gathered in those blades, close and clear, thicker than it had ever been at any field edge of the year, and he understood without being told that the groove had done that too.

Tell me when it does something, his father had said.

The boy closed his hands gently, the way you close your hands around a thing that is not yours yet, and stood up, and went in to the fire and his supper, because his mother kept both, and because tomorrow he would walk out to the fields with his father, and the report would have its first true entry, and because every road that ever reached anywhere began as a groove no wider than a breath.

And on a shelf in Nuoding, in the dark, in a book that did not hope, a line of ink kept its place between the years — patient still, and still correct, which is all that ink can be.

# Chapter 5: The Door in the Wall

The method was not a gift, though it had come as one, and that was the first lesson of the year that followed — the year of his age eight — because a gift sits still, but a method works, and what works must be worked, every day, or it stops being a method and becomes a memory.

Every day, then. First thing in the dark before the farm wanted him, and last thing at the field edge when the farm let him go. He sat the way Tao Wu had shown him, except for the parts of it that were Tao Wu's body's and not his, which by now he had set aside so completely that he could not have remembered they had ever been taught. He breathed the way the creek moved. He attended to the place below his ribs, the way he attended to everything, and the groove that had been cut on the first night went deeper the way grooves do — not by being cut again and again in the same furious place, but by being used, honestly, in season and out.

The winter of that year was when the stream became a river, and he felt it happen the way you feel a thaw — not in a moment, but in the moment when you realized the moment had already happened. What had been thin and wandering in him, soaking away into nothing for a year and more, gathered itself into the channel and began to go, and to keep going, and to come back fuller each time it came back. His body took the measure of it in its own way: he was hungrier than he had ever been, a deep clean hunger that arrived with the dark and sat at the table with him, and his mother looked at the pot and then at him and said nothing and added to the pot, because mothers can tell the difference between a boy who is wasting food and a boy who is being spent, and she had been keeping his accounts all his life. He slept deeper. He grew longer in the limbs, the way eight-year-olds do, and ached in the evenings in the places that were growing, and did not complain about it, because everyone ached somewhere and the aching was not the interesting part.

And his sense went on widening, because that was what it did, and nothing about it had ever been asked to stop.

By the spring he no longer needed the stillness of the field edge to feel the faintness. It had become like hearing, which does not wait for you to be quiet. He felt it in the soil when he broke it, a damp cold presence that was not only water. He felt it in the ox's flank, slow and deep, like a second heart. He felt it rise off the south slope in the noon sun the way heat rises, and lie in the creek hollow in the mornings the way mist lies. The world went about its business full of it, and he went about his full of it too, and between the two of them there was no longer any wall at all, only a habit of noticing that had become so fixed it had stopped being noticing and had become simply the way the world was arranged.

The grass came closer too, in its way.

Not out of the ground — nothing in the fields changed, and the record says so, because the record lies about nothing. The bank held, the ruts healed, the blue-silver stood and bent and came back, common as water, doing what it had always done. What changed was between his hands. On the evenings when the groove was full and the dusk was still, he could open his palms and the grass would come — faint, blue-silver, thin as breath on glass, but come: standing where a moment before there had been nothing, and standing for a breath, and then going again when he let it go. He could hold it longer than the first night. He could feel it, which was the part that had no comparison in the village, because there was nothing in the village to compare it to: the faintness gathered in those blades close and clear, the way it gathered nowhere else, and when he let the blades braid loosely in the air between his hands, the way his own hands had braided a ring of it for a boy leaving on a cart, the braiding held for a breath before it fell, and the falling was not failure, because the holding had been real.

It cost him. Everything about it cost him — the sitting, the hunger, the sleep spent on it, the attention that could not be spent on anything else while it was being paid — and the cost was the honest part, and he paid it the way his family paid everything, without drama, and without once mistaking the paying for the having.

---

He told his father in the early summer, at the south field edge, because the report had been standing between them since the winter before last, and because a report kept too long stops being a report and becomes a weight.

They were resting the way field-workers rest, standing, and the light was going the way it went in the long days, slowly, and Lan Shen looked at the grass at his feet and then at his father, and gave the report the way he had been taught to give it — each thing as it came, in the few words it required.

"The grass did something," he said.

His father looked at him the way he had looked the first time, the look that did not dismiss, and waited.

"It stood in the frost, when the others lay. I told you that. It stands in other places now." He paused, because what came next had no field words, and he had decided, walking out here, that he would not dress it in words that were not its own. "It comes to my hands. When I sit at the edge, the way you know. It comes, and it stands, and I can feel it. And there is something in everything, like water, that it drinks."

He did not show it. He had thought about showing it, and decided against it, because the report was the thing agreed between them, and the showing would have been for the village's eyes, and the village had no part in this. His father was quiet for a while, the way his father was quiet, which was not the quiet of a man thinking of what to say, but of a man weighing what he had heard.

"Show me," his father said.

So he showed him. It was a thin thing, and it was dusk, and the faint blue-silver stood in the boy's open palms for a breath and went, and the field went on around it the way the field went on around everything, and Lan He watched it go the way he watched weather, without surprise, because surprise is for people who believe the world has told them everything, and the fields had taught him better than that.

"Does it cost you?" his father asked.

"Work," Lan Shen said. "And supper."

His father considered this. Then he said the thing Lan Shen would carry the rest of his life, though neither of them knew that then, in the plain voice he used for weather and for seed.

"Water's everywhere," he said, "and we still dig wells."

It was the truest thing anyone had said about the faintness, and it needed no more than that, and it got no more than that. His father looked once more at the place where the grass had stood, and then at the far trees where the light lay the way it lay before rain, and turned toward home, and Lan Shen went with him, and the report between them was paid, and the report between them did not end, because reports never end; they only wait for the next entry.

---

The fullness came in the late summer, and with it the wall.

He felt it arrive the way you feel a river change under a boat. The groove was deep now, and the water in it moved strong and even, day after day, morning and evening, and then one sitting the moving stopped being a moving-toward. It went, and came back, and he was no more for it than he had been the day before. He did not believe it at first, because not believing is what fullness does to people, and he sat with it again in the morning, and again at the edge, and the river went, and the river came, and the level of him stood still the way water stands behind a dam.

He did not push. That was the part of it he was proudest of afterward, though he did not call it pride, because pushing was what you did with what you owned from outside, and Tao Wu's teacher had said it, and his own year of marsh-water had taught it, and his whole life of watching things grow had taught it: nothing is hurried into being a little more alive. He attended. He breathed. He let the water go where the groove took it and not a finger's width further, and he waited on the wall the way the village waited on winter — grumbling inwardly, and working meanwhile, and not once admitting he was waiting.

The weeks went. The harvest came and he worked it, properly, as a pair of hands that the farm could spend without counting the cost, and the harvest did not care about his wall, and the honesty of that was good for him. The wall stayed. The river stayed behind it. And in the evenings, when the grass came to his palms and stood, it stood the way it had stood all along — faithful, common, and no more able than he was to say what the wall was, or what lay on the far side of it, or what in the world could possibly be the key to it.

He kept the record of it honestly: *the river is full, and the river stands still, and I do not know why, and I am not afraid, and I do not know why that is either.*

---

The road carried Tao Wu home again when the air began to think about cold, because the road had learned the habit of him now, and habits keep themselves.

He was taller, and he had been taller the year before too, and would be the year after, and the city was doing that to him the way cities do, and there was a new ease in him, a way of standing in the lane as if the lane had been paved, and he had a belt that had been bought and not made, and none of it reached the middle of him, where the loudness lived, and the quickness, and the wanting to be something. He went to the elder's house. He crossed to the Lan place the next day. He found Lan Shen at the field edge, because he knew where to look, which was how Lan Shen knew the city had not gotten there.

They stood together a while, the way they did now, without needing to fill the first of it.

"I'm at the wall," Tao Wu said then, without preamble, because he was Tao Wu, and because the wall was the thing that was happening to him, and he had come home full of it. "Before the summer. The teacher said that's the bottleneck, and it's real, and nobody goes past it." He said it with awe, and with the pride of having been tested by something real. "You have to get a ring."

He had learned the city's way of saying things, and he used it, and it sat on him like the bought belt.

"A ring is what a soul beast leaves when it dies," he said. "Its years — the teacher said it's its years, like light — and it comes out over the body and hangs there, and you sit with it and you pull it in, at the bottleneck, and it opens the next ten, and you get one skill out of it. The beast's own. One. And the color of it is the years — white is ten years, yellow is a hundred, and up from there, and the higher the years the more it can bear you up, and the more it can kill you taking it in. The teacher says the first one wants to be small. White or yellow. He says a body that's been worked can take more, and a body that hasn't can be broken by less." He stopped for breath, and the awe came back up through the city phrasing. "You have to hunt it. That's the part. The beast has to die by a hunt, proper, and then you have to sit with it before it fades, and hunts need people who know the ground, and they need money, or they need the academy."

The light was going. Lan Shen looked at the field, and felt the river in him stand at its wall, and understood, the way you understand weather coming in from the far trees, what he had been standing behind all autumn.

"There's a list," Tao Wu said. "At the academy, for the work-study ones. You work extra — duties, errands, whatever they've got — and it counts, and when there's a place on a supervised hunt, the ones who've counted go out with masters, proper, safe as they can make it. I'm on the list. My grandfather's saving too, because the list is slow and the saving is sure, and he says one road or the other will get there and he doesn't care which." He paused, and looked sideways at Lan Shen, and did the sum, the village way, out loud. "You're at the wall too. Aren't you."

It was not a question by the end. Tao Wu looked at him, and looked at the field, and the face did the creek-road thing, the breaking-open and the closing-better, quick, because he was a boy who felt things at full size, and the size of this was that his friend was at the same wall he was, on the other side of every door that was open to him.

"There's no list for you," he said. "You're not on any book but the hall's."

"That's where I am," Lan Shen said, because it was true, and because the truth was the only ground either of them had ever been given to stand on.

Tao Wu was quiet for a while, which from Tao Wu was a weather event. Then he straightened, the way he did when he had decided something, and the deciding sat on him like the oath voice.

"I'll find out," he said. "There's got to be how the registered ones do it. The teacher said the hall keeps registers, and families hire masters when they can, and hunters take coin for guiding, and there's markets and there's talk — I'll ask, and I'll keep asking, and I'll bring what I find." He looked at Lan Shen the way he had looked at him over the honey-cake, and over the grass-ring, in the two dawns of their lives. "You don't get left at the wall," he said. "Not while I'm on the road."

The next morning the road took him back, and the village watched its one road go for the second time, and Old Tao's back was straight again, and the dust of the cart settled the way dust settles, and Lan Shen went to the field edge that evening because the field edge was his, and because the report of the day was that nothing had changed, which was not quite the truth of it.

What had changed was the shape of the door.

He sat with it while the light went, and the grass came to his palms and stood, faithful and common, and he felt the river at the top of its falls, full and held, and he thought about rings, and years like light, and beasts, and forests, and the distance between a village that had never had a soul master and the places of the world where such things were hunted, and he did not flinch from the size of it, because he had been taught by the whole of his life to look at the size of things the way you look at weather — honestly, and without arguing, and while getting on with the work that could be done today.

The wall was real. The door in the wall was real. And he had learned, in the year of his age eight, the thing that every farmer's son learns first, which is the last thing anyone else learns: that knowing the shape of the work is not the doing of it, but it is where the doing begins, and no one has ever done any work at all who did not first stand still long enough to learn its shape.

The grass stood in his hands, and the river held at the top of its falls, and the evening came down over Qingni the way it always had, and the next thing to learn was not how to grow.

It was where the world kept what he had to hunt.

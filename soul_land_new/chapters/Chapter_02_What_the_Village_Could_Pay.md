# Chapter 2: What the Village Could Pay

The village had decided about Tao Wu by the time the sun was properly up, and it decided about everything else by the way it decided about Tao Wu — all at once, in doorways, with the bread.

Lan Shen heard it from the field lane, where he had gone because his hands wanted work and the house wanted quiet. His mother had given him the seed basket, which was not a real errand, because it was the season's end and there was nothing left to seed, and they both knew it, and she had said *take your time* in the voice that meant *let your father have the morning.*

So he walked the lane with the empty basket, and listened to his village become a different village around him.

"The dog soul," said the honey-cake man's wife, at her gate, to anyone and everyone. "A beast soul, in Old Tao's line. Did you see it sneeze?"

"It'll be the academy for him," said somebody else, with the certainty of a person who had been certain about something different the day before. "City learning. The hall stipend, maybe, later on. A family can rise on a thing like that."

"And the Lan boy?"

A pause. The kind of pause that has weight.

"The attendant said what he said. Grass is grass."

"Third level," said the first voice, lower, the way people say a thing they don't want to be overheard saying but do want repeated. "They say he held his hand out and didn't shake."

"Some don't shake because they don't understand yet."

"Some don't shake," said the honey-cake man's wife, and left the rest of it hanging in the morning air, where it belonged.

Lan Shen walked past the gate with the empty basket, and neither of them noticed him, which was how he heard it all. He found, in himself, the same thing he had found on the boards yesterday: the fear came up, was looked at, and settled down again, like a bird deciding a branch was safe. He did not think this was brave. He did not think anything about it at all. It was simply what his body did, the way it had always done things, quietly, before he got there.

He set the basket down at the field edge and stood a while with the creek sound, and then, because there was nothing else he wanted, he squatted down where the bank broke and looked at the grass.

---

It was not the same looking as yesterday.

Yesterday he had looked at grass the way he looked at soil or weather, the way everyone in Qingni looked at everything — for what it could tell him about the day. Today he looked at it the way you look at a thing that has been said to be you.

Blue Silver Grass grew here the way it grew everywhere: in the broken places and the margins, thin blades with a blue-silver cast when the light caught them, tougher than they had any right to be. He watched how it came up through the path where the carts pressed it down, bent flat and coming back. He watched how the creek edge held together where the grass roots drank the water and gripped. He pulled one blade, gently, the way you pull a thing you are asking a question of, and felt it hold, and hold, and then let go at the root with a small clean sound.

He did not know what he was looking for. If anyone had asked — nobody asked; nobody would have thought to ask — he would have said he was looking at grass.

What was true, underneath that, was this: the warm place in his palm from yesterday was still there, small and steady, and when he looked at the grass the place seemed, in some wordless way he could not have spoken and did not try to speak, to lean toward the looking.

He stayed there a long time. The branch of himself that watched from inside, the one that noticed weather and treelines and his father's silences, made note of it without words, the way it made note of everything, and put it away where it kept the other things that mattered.

Then he picked up the empty basket and went home, because the morning was over and there would be talk at noon, and he had learned young that the place to be for talk was inside it, not outside.

---

The meeting was not a meeting. That was the first thing about it.

Qingni did not hold councils. What it held was the elder's gate, which was where things got decided when enough people stood around them long enough, and on this evening the gate had more people standing around it than it had seen since the year the creek flooded. Lanterns. The men with their arms folded and their best judgment on. The women nearer the wall, where you could hear and be seen to be only listening. The children underfoot until they were sent to the edges, where they stayed, because children sent to the edges always stay.

Old Tao stood in his own doorway, which was how he presided over things — on his own ground, with the lantern light catching the clean old robe.

"You know why we're here," he said. "The village holds one road to the city academy. It has held it since before my father's father, and it pays for it out of the common chest, and it is the biggest thing this village owns that you cannot eat. Two children awakened with soul power this year. That has not happened in Qingni in living memory. So we will say what we mean, out loud, and then we will decide."

Tao Wu stood beside his mother with his hair newly combed, looking as if he had been told to hold still forever. He found Lan Shen in the shadows at the edge, where the children stood, and looked away quickly, and then, because he was Tao Wu, looked back again.

"Speak," said Old Tao.

And the village spoke. It was not cruel. That was the second thing about it, and it would matter later, the way such things matter.

"The beast soul is worth the road," said Tao Wu's uncle, who had a voice like a market bell. "You can build on a dog soul. My wife's cousin's husband saw a man in Nuoding once who started with a mule soul and finished with a stipend. Grass — no offense to the Lan house — grass is what you plant when you can't plant anything else."

"The attendant said cultivable," said a voice from the wall. It was Aunt Zhou's voice, and it carried the particular weight of a person delivering news they had carried all day. "Both of them. He said it on the boards. *The soul is cultivable.* Those were the words. My sister's boy heard them same as me."

"He said it of the grass boy the way you say a ditch is a road," said the market-bell uncle. "Technically. I stood there too."

A sound went around the gate, low, because it was true, and because everyone had stood there.

"The chest pays for one," said Old Tao, when the sound had spent itself. He did not raise his voice; the gate quieted for it anyway. "One seat, one road, one child fed in the city while his family loses his hands at home. That is what we can pay. I'll hear if the Lan house wants to say anything to it, because it's their boy named, and because this gate does things proper."

Every head turned, in the dark, toward where Lan He stood with his hat in his hands.

Lan Shen watched his father, because there was nothing else at the gate worth watching.

His father looked at the elder, and at the lanterns, and at the folded arms of the men, and at his own hat. He was a man who had never once in his life spoken where work would do, and the whole village knew it, and the whole village waited anyway, because the gate does things proper.

"The chest pays for one," said Lan He, at last, in the voice he used for weather. "Pay it where it grows."

He put his hat back on. That was the speech.

And something moved in the dark beside the wall where the women stood, small and quick, as Zhou Shi turned her face away; and Lan Shen, watching from the edge with the other children, felt the warm place in his palm go very still, and understood — in the way of that evening, without words, the way everything that mattered that year came to him without words — that his father had not given him up. His father had looked at what the village could pay, and what the family could not, and had spent his own pride like a man paying a debt, so that the debt would not come to his son.

"Then it's Tao Wu," said Old Tao, gently, the way you close a book. "The road goes to Tao Wu, and the village will carry it, and the boy will make us proud, or we'll know the reason." He looked at Tao Wu, and his face did the thing that faces do when they are proud and frightened at once. "You hear me, boy? You go for all of us."

The gate came apart into talk and congratulations and the first of the planning, and nobody looked at the Lan house, because looking would have been a weight, and the village was not cruel.

Lan Shen walked home in the dark between his parents, and none of them spoke, and it was all right.

---

Tao Wu caught him the next morning at the place where the creek road bends.

He had been waiting, which was unlike him, and he had something in his hands behind his back, which was also unlike him, and his face was doing a difficult thing, which was the most unlike him of all.

"My mother says we go to the city after the harvest," he said, very fast. "There's a letter coming from the academy, she says, and a man from the sub-hall comes to take me, and I'll be — I'll be in the city, Lan Shen. In the city."

"I know," said Lan Shen.

"I wanted you to know I didn't — I didn't ask for the seat. I didn't say anything at the gate. My uncle said the things."

"I know," said Lan Shen, again, and meant it this time the second way, the way that matters.

Tao Wu's hands came out from behind his back. In them was a honey cake, the good kind from the fair, sticky and golden. He held it out the way you hold out a thing when you have rehearsed the holding but not the rest.

"Here," he said. "Take it. It's — here."

Lan Shen took it. It was warm from Tao Wu's hands.

They stood a moment with the creek running brown under the little bridge, the two of them at the bend of the road, one going to the city after the harvest and neither of them old enough to know what that sentence meant yet.

"Tell me what it's like," said Lan Shen.

"What?"

"The academy. The city. All of it." He bit the honey cake, because it was there, and because refusing it would have been a weight Tao Wu could not have carried. "When you come back. Tell me what it's like."

Something broke open in Tao Wu's face, and then closed again in a better shape. "When I come back," he said. "I'll tell you everything. I'll learn everything there is and I'll come back and tell you." He straightened up, because he was Tao Wu, and the world had given him a direction. "You wait here, then. You wait, and I'll come back and tell you."

"I'm not going anywhere," said Lan Shen, and ate his honey cake at the bend of the creek road, and watched his friend go whooping back toward the village to tell someone about the promise, the way Tao Wu told everything to someone, immediately.

The warm place in his palm was quiet. The grass along the bank stood in the early light, holding the earth together, the way it did everywhere, the way it always had, and Lan Shen finished his cake and brushed the honey from his fingers and went home to his work.

---

Far away, in a stone-floored room of the Spirit Hall sub-hall at Nuoding, the attendant Wen was doing the last part of his circuit, which was the part nobody ever saw.

The ledger was the hall's memory, and the hall's memory was long, and it was kept the way all true memories are kept — without interest, without malice, one line at a time. Wen sat with the brush and the lamp and the day's villages before him, and wrote them into the memory, because that was the duty, and because he had worn the grey most of his life and the duty was what the grey was for.

The day had been ordinary. He had ridden village to village since dawn, and the harvest of the day was the harvest of all such days: a handful of awakened souls, one or two that might amount to something, the rest into the world's great quiet majority. He wrote the names and the souls and the readings, in the hall's even hand, and the ledger took them all without opinion.

He came, at last, to the last village of the circuit.

The first line went quickly. The boy with the dog soul; the faint true light; the village's one road given to him; the quota letter already drafted for the autumn. A good entry. The kind that occasionally, over a lifetime of entries, became something.

The second line was where his brush slowed.

He wrote the name, and the soul, and the reading the crystal had shown him, because those were the facts, and the ledger took facts. Then he sat a moment with the brush held above the paper, in the lamplight, remembering the boards in the morning sun and the small steady light like an ember that would not go out, and the grass that had stayed.

A lesser attendant would have left it out, because it was nothing, and because nothing is what most of the day was. A fanciful one would have made it more than it was. Wen was neither; he was thorough, which is what he had been all morning when he had measured twice, and thoroughness in a servant of the hall is a kind of honesty.

He added a note in the margin, in the smaller hand the hall reserves for observations that are not measurements.

*Manifestation lingered after the rite closed. Subject stood without distress. Recommend nothing; recorded for completeness.*

He looked at it, and almost smiled, and did not, because he had worn the grey most of his life and had learned what such notes were worth — which was nothing, usually, and which was precisely why they were written. The ledger did not hope. The ledger remembered.

He sanded the ink, closed the book, and set it with the others on the shelf of years, where it stood shoulder to shoulder with circuits beyond counting of the same countryside, holding inside it the names and souls and small readings of everyone the hall had ever touched in that corner of the empire.

The lamp burned on. The ledger kept what it had been given.

And in the dark of the shelf, patient as grass, the memory of the village called Qingni waited — the way all such memories wait — for the day, if it ever came, when somebody would open the book and want to know.

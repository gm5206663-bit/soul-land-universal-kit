# Chapter 5: The Proof

The village found its new shape in about three weeks, the way villages
do. The well took the same rope. The hill took the same two — then
three, then two and a half on Sundays. The forge door stood open in
the mornings, which nobody commented on anymore, and in the evenings
the smoke over the west-side house went down at the same hour, because
the blacksmith's fire had acquired a second keeper who did not let it
drift.

Chen Xing's days made a simple pattern. Water before the well opened.
His mother's errands, done and then two more she had not set, done
anyway. The bench when his father was home — he was home more than he
had been, the Farfield beams being finished and the next job not yet
spoken for — and the roof of the spirit hall would have to wait for
the thaw anyway, his father said, the way a man says a thing he is not
going to do something about this week. Then the west side: bellows,
sweep, the quiet half-hour when Tang Hao ate and the boy did not, and
the rope coiled for the night.

"Door in the morning," Tang Hao said most evenings, which had stopped
meaning come back and started meaning the same thing said plainly.

The next job had not been spoken for, and the village did the
arithmetic on that the way it did all arithmetic: out loud, sideways,
in eights and halves. A father at home every night was a father
between wages. Nobody said it to the north-end house. The baker's
wife said it to the well, and then started sending bread on the days
the bench was running late, which nobody mentioned either.

His father was at the table most nights now, measuring nothing, and
his mother put food in front of him the way she put food in front of
everything she was keeping alive, and the lamp went to its low mark,
and outside the window stayed open.

---

The storm came in the second week of the thaw, late, out of the west,
with more noise than rain.

In the morning the lane was full of people standing around looking up
at the spirit hall, which had lost half its roof tiles and one of its
long beams had shifted in its seat and was lying at an angle against
the sky like a finger pointing at the wrong thing.

The hall with its head open was not a hall. It was a room with the
black stones showing and the weather in them.

Old Jack walked the length of the building twice before he said
anything, because walking was the office and talking was only what he
did while walking.

"The festival," he said finally. "The hall has to be sound for the
festival, or we eat outside and the stones sit in the rain another
year, and I am too old to eat outside in the rain on purpose."

Nobody said who would mend it, because nobody had to say it. Nobody
in Holy Spirit Village mended roofs. Roofs were the north-end house's
trade the way iron was the west side's, and the trade was standing
in the yard with his arm already held the way a man holds a thing he
has stopped arguing with.

"The beam's sitting on nothing," said the baker's wife. "It slipped
its seat. Who can put a roof-beam back, Jack?"

Everyone looked at the north-end boy, and then looked at where his
father was already climbing the ladder with his rule in his teeth.

---

Chen Jian walked the ridge the way he walked a floor, except slower,
and the boy came up behind him because the boy had always come up
behind him, and the cold up there did not take anybody's fingers the
way it took other people's, which the boy had never once mentioned.

From the ridge the whole village was a model of itself: the well a
black coin, the hill a shoulder, the road south leaving between the
fields the way roads leave when they are not asking anyone to follow
them today. The boy's breath went once, deep, and came back even —
the way it had come back past the burning since he was old enough to
run — and his boots had stopped asking the ridge for permission.

"It sat in its notch for twenty years," his father said, down on his
knees at the beam. "Storm shook the notch. The notch is wrong now, not
the beam."

"Can you cut the notch?"

"I can cut the notch." He put his hand flat on the wood. "Help me
walk it back first. Then I cut."

They got their fingers under the beam where it had slipped, and the
men below stood where the men below stand, ready to catch something
nobody had asked them to catch, and the two of them lifted.

The beam moved the way dead timber moves — not all at once, not
gracefully — and then the bad notch let go of what was left of its
hold, and the whole length of it went sideways down the slope of the
broken roof, and the boy's hands went with it, and what the wood did
not do was get away.

He held it. Not wisely. His arms took the weight the way a wall takes
weather, and his boots found the ridge, and for three or four breaths
nobody in the yard below said anything at all — the women with their
hands up as if hands that far down could do anything, the men at the
foot of the ladder already moving, Old Jack not saying once, for
once, that he had seen this before — and then six men were under each
end of it and the beam was on the ground with a sound the whole lane
felt.

His father came down the ridge after, faster than he had gone up, and
stood in the yard with one arm across his chest and his wrist already
swelling inside his sleeve, looking at the beam, then at the boy.

"Your hands," he said.

"They're all right."

"They are. That's what I'm looking at." His father flexed his own
wrist once, winced at it, and stopped flexing it. "I can point. I
cannot lift. There's a difference and today it's going to matter."

---

The work needed iron. The old straps that held the beam seats had
gone with the beam, rusted through at the pin-holes, and new ones
would have to be forged, and there was only one forge in Holy Spirit
Village.

The boy walked down with the broken strap in his hands, because he
was the only person in the village who moved freely between the house
with the rule and the house with the hammer, and he had been that
person for years without anybody naming the job.

Tang Hao looked at the strap for a while. He turned it over. He held
it the way he held everything, at arm's length, as if it might still
be trying something.

"Pinned hole's gone soft," he said. "I'd rather cut new. Same shape?"

"The same shape, but honest," the boy said. "My father says the hall
has settled since he hung it. Straight is what you draw. The road
isn't straight."

Tang Hao looked at him for a moment with no expression at all.

"Your father talks too much for a man who measures," he said, and
went back to the forge, and that was how the braces got made.

The first brace came over on the blacksmith's shoulder that evening
and was laid on the bench for the father's string. Chen Jian ran the
line across it, once, and again, and did not look up.

"Half a grain proud at the pin."

"It'll hold," Tang Hao said.

"I didn't say it wouldn't hold." Chen Jian tilted the brace so the
lamplight went down its length. "I said it's half a grain proud."

"Then it's honest," said Tang Hao, "and it'll hold," and both of
them stood there agreeing completely and not at all, until the boy
took the brace up the ladder, and neither of them watched him go,
which was how they watched everything of his that mattered.

Below, in the yard, the two makers did not consult each other so much
as occupy the same air: Chen Jian on the ground with his rule and his
string and his ruined wrist, calling the numbers up; Tang Hao at the
portable anvil they had carried over from the west side, answering
with hammer-strikes; and the boy in between, up the ladder and down
it, carrying marks up and answers down, the only moving part.

"Cut it honest," his father said when the boy brought the last
measurement down from the ridge, and the boy went back up with the
sentence in his pocket next to nothing else.

---

The spirit master came on the fourth day of the repair.

He came up the road in his cart with the two horses the village
recognized, and the baker's wife saw him first, which meant the yard
knew before the ladder knew.

Su Yuntao had not been back since the star and the crystal and the
morning that had sent the whole village to bed with its opinions
hanging out. He stopped at the yard the whole village was already
standing in, climbed down with a satchel, and looked for a moment at
the roof of the spirit hall, at the man with his arm across his chest
calling numbers, and at the boy on the ridge with a bundle of new
straps on his shoulder.

"I was told the fire lizard was keeping the roof up," he said, to
nobody.

The yard had its own arithmetic for a spirit master standing in it:
thirty people rearranged into an audience without anyone deciding to.
Jack came forward to do the elder part of it — the welcome, the
weather, the road — and Su Yuntao answered the welcome and skipped
the rest, because he had not come for the weather. He asked where the
boy's people were, and somebody pointed at the ladder, and the father
came down it one-handed, with the rule still in his teeth, and took
it out to speak.

"Chen Jian. The carpenter." He held out his good hand, then the
other one, thought about it, and held out the good one again. "Your
boy is on my roof."

"Your hall's roof," said the father. "It was built straight. The
storm has an argument with that, not me."

"Twenty years and no leak," Su Yuntao said, looking up at the
seated beam and the new straps. "The hall noted that in the year the
roof stopped leaking. It notes things." He looked at the wrist,
swollen and strapped to the chest. "Can you come down for a quarter
of an hour, or do you argue with weather from up there?"

"I come down when the beam does," said the father, which the village
took for wit, and then, louder: "Chen Xing. Come down. The man has
papers."

The boy came down. The yard made the room it always made for him.

"I wrote my report the week after the ceremony," Su Yuntao said. "The
Nuoding hall reads a report like other people read their breakfast —
and then it read it again, and then it wrote back, and told me to
bring you these. Not by post." He said the not by post the way a man
says something that still amuses him.

He opened the satchel and took out two papers.

The first was the awakening proof, stiff, official, signed at the
bottom in a hand that had signed a thousand of them.

"Proof of what you are, issued under my hand and the Nuoding branch's
seal. The blacksmith's boy took his the same morning. Yours has been
in my bag since then, waiting on the road back — I do not make house
calls." He held it out and did not let go of it until the boy's hands
were on it. "If a gatekeeper anywhere gives you trouble, name the
deacon who signed it. It is a useful name to a man with a stiff door."

The second paper was longer, and he did not hand it over. He read it
aloud, because Su Yuntao read his own writing the way he read the
bestiary plates — flat, practiced, for the room.

"Ground Fire Dragon Lizard. Fire attribute. Power attack. Innate
level six, mid band. Tested at Holy Spirit Village, witnessed by the
village elder and the branch's own record." He lowered the paper.
"The hall does not write this for children it intends to forget. When
the boy is old enough to walk it, there is a road to Nuoding that ends
at our door — schooling, the way a spirit master is schooled, not the
working kind. He presents this letter. It will be read."

"And the school?" Old Jack said. He asked it the way he asked about
taxes. "Nuoding schooling costs a family. The hall doesn't write
letters that cost families."

"The hall writes letters about what it wants," Su Yuntao said. "When
he presents it, the hall's account is the hall's business. I was not
asked to negotiate. I was asked to deliver, and I do not make house
calls, so here we all are."

"And if he's not old enough?" his mother said. She had come up the
lane without the boy seeing her, and she asked it flat, the way she
asked everything that mattered.

"Then it stays a letter until he is. Roads are patient, mistress. I'm
not asking for him today. I'm telling you the day will."

His father had come under the ladder with his rule in his good hand.
He looked at the paper a long time the way he looked at a joint that
had come out right.

"It goes in the bench," he said. "Where the true things go."

"Your son could put it in his own drawer," said Su Yuntao.

"He could," said his father. "He knows where the bench is."

The boy folded the letter the way his father folded a string — once,
square, without fuss — and put it in the slot in the workbench where
the plumb-line lived, next to the plumb-line, and neither of them
said anything about it, which in that house meant the matter was
settled but not finished.

Behind the yard the village did what villages do: fast, all at once,
with opinions. A dragon. The spirit master himself, twice now. A
letter with a seal on it, in the north-end house, where the flour
wasn't. The baker's wife said the boy had his mother's nerve, and
the woman beside her said the paper had better manners than most
relatives, and everyone agreed, and nobody meant it, and the morning
went on.

Tang Hao, at the anvil he had carried over, said nothing at all,
which in the west-side language was a whole paragraph of knowing.

---

The roof went back the way roofs go back: badly at first, then
properly, then all at once.

The boy was up top most of two days. The baker's wife sent bread up
in a basket on a rope, because bread, like weather, found the high
places first, and the blue-grass boy held the rope and the history at
the same time. The new straps went in true.
The cut his arm took from a lath that afternoon was not worth
mentioning to him, and he did not mention it, and at supper his
mother's two fingers found the back of his neck instead of his arm,
and stayed there a moment longer than the weather required.

In the morning the dark-crystal girl was at the well when he came for
the first buckets, and she looked at his rolled sleeve and then very
carefully not at it.

"It was open last night," she said.

"It was a scratch."

"It was open," she said again, in the voice of a girl who had
watched her own crystal come up dark and had not cried, and who
therefore noticed things.

"My mother puts a leaf on it when I cut myself," she said. "Under
the leaf, it closes by itself. Yours didn't have a leaf."

"I didn't need a leaf."

"No," she agreed, and went back to her bucket, and carried it away
without spilling, and the matter went into the well with the rest of
the talk, which is where her matters always went, and stayed there.

The blue-grass boy, who had appointed himself historian of the
repair, counted two braces, five days, one storm, and one time the
ladder had moved under the boy and the boy had not. He delivered the
count to Old Jack on the Sunday, standing up, out loud, in the order
it had happened; Jack listened to all of it, thanked him, and asked
what the boy had said when the ladder moved, and the historian said
he had said nothing at all, and that that was the part he had been
trying to write down, and could not.

Old Jack paid the hall's wages on the fifth evening, from the hall's
own tin that nobody asked about: to the father for the design, to the
blacksmith for the iron, and to the hands that carried both.

"There's no wage for standing on your father's roof," his father
said.

"There's a wage for the hands," Jack said, and put the coin in the
boy's own palm, and closed the boy's fingers on it like a man
starting a small argument he intended to lose. "First wage. Keep it
somewhere it can learn something."

The boy kept it in his boot.

---

The hall was sound for the festival. The last tile went on at
evening, and the black stones lay under their roof again with the
weather kept out of them, and the village stood in the yard a while
and looked at the building the way you look at something you had
started to forget the shape of. Inside, it smelled of new timber and
old stone, and the women began hanging things from the beams that
were going to hold for another year, and nobody looked up to check.

Old women said the north-end boy had his father's hands. Nobody
remembered when it had started being said about him instead of about
the boy who had gone south.

The hill was the same hill. The boy sat in his place and did the
thing he could do for an empty place, which was to keep it, and the
blue-grass boy did his own nothing beside him badly, and the river
went on with its work, and none of them mentioned anything. The
festival was coming the way festivals come: sideways, in smells and
ropes and arguments about the arch, and the village was already
setting the table for a boy who might not come to it.

That night the lamp went to its low mark and the window stood open
because it had always stood open, and his mother cleared the bowls
and looked at the bench, and at the stove, and at the boy, in that
order, and said the only thing on her list.

"Window stays open."

"Yes."

"Your father says the notch was wrong, not the beam. He's saying it
like it's the same thing."

"It is the same thing."

She considered that the way she considered weather.

"Eat," she said, and he ate, and behind the stove the tin stayed
shut, and in the bench the letter stayed folded, and the coin stayed
in the boot, and the night outside went on being the night —
ordinary, enormous, unspent, and now with a roof over its head.

Somewhere south a boy was writing his name in letters badly, learning
the way you learn anything, by doing it wrong first. Somewhere at the
north end a boy was asleep with his hands out flat on the blanket,
healing over in the dark the way he had always healed over in the
dark, and the village slept around its two, whole, keeping its shape
around its gaps and its papers.

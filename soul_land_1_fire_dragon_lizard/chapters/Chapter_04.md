# Chapter 4: The Road Out

The date came down the lane on a Monday, carried by Old Jack himself,
in the hat he wore for his own business, which by now the village read
the way sailors read weather.

"Tomorrow week," he said at the forge-yard gate, to the blacksmith's
house and to nobody and to everybody. "Two days' walking after that.
Get the boy ready."

The village had known for three months and still jumped, because
knowing a thing is not the same as hearing it with a hat on. Tomorrow
week. The words went from the gate to the square to the well in under
a minute, and by noon the baker's wife had already started a list of
what a city boy needs, most of which the city, in her opinion, would
not have the sense to provide.

"Socks," she said, reading from the list to nobody. "A second shirt.
Needle and thread — do they have needle and thread in a city? Of
course they don't, cities are famous for it." She looked up. "Somebody
should tell his father he's going. The man will forget to feed
himself on the last night."

"The boy won't let him," said the woman beside her, and it settled
the matter the way facts settled in Holy Spirit Village — not with
agreement, but with everyone quietly going back to what they were
doing.

At the north-end house the boy heard the news the way everyone did —
late and all at once — and then went on carrying water, because the
water did not know about the city and the buckets did not care, and
the body, when the head is too full, is glad of work that asks
nothing of it.

That afternoon at the hill he found San already there, sitting in the
exact middle of his usual place as if he could wear it in.

At the hill that afternoon the two boys sat in their usual place and
did their usual nothing for a while, which this time felt like
somebody had put a frame around it.

"I have to pack," San said eventually.

"Yes."

"Father says no forging tomorrow. He says Jack will talk the ears off
the cart-horse and I should eat a big breakfast."

The boy laughed, and then was embarrassed to have laughed, and then
laughed again, because that was the shape of it: a sentence that was
only fear wearing a work-shirt. He knew the shirt. He had watched his
own hands shake at a shut door all winter.

"Who cooks for him?" San asked, not looking up. "While I'm gone."

"You?"

"I'm leaving two full bowls and a pot of something that won't betray
him, and I'm telling the baker's wife where the bread is, and I'm
telling her he won't say thank you so she shouldn't wait for it."

"That's a lot of telling."

"Somebody has to," San said, with the particular flatness of a boy who
has been the grown one in a house since he could reach the table, and
the river went on going under the mountain or around it, and neither
of them asked, because for the first time since they had planned it,
one of them would not be there to find out.

They sat until the light went orange, which the boy watched the way
he watched all light now — for the color in it, for what the yellow
meant and the white meant and the quiet orange meant, having learned
the whole language at an anvil that was about to belong to one person
instead of two.

"Take clean socks," the boy said, at the last, standing up.

"I know how to pack."

"Clean socks and no opinions." He said it flat, in the exact shape
his mother used it, and San stared at him for a full second and then
laughed — the real one, the one that got away from him — and said,
"Your mother can come with us and mine can't," which was a strange
sentence about two mothers, one of whom the village never named and
one of whom it had stopped asking about, and they both let it stand
the way they let all their strange sentences stand: without turning
to look at it.

"I'll come to the gate," the boy said.

"I know you will."

Then San was going down the hill with his bundle's errand in his head
and his shoulders doing their careful best even without the weight,
and the boy stayed where they sat — his place, and now also a little
his place alone — until the light lost its color and the first cold
came down off the fields the way it always did, invited or not.

That evening he went to the forge, because the evening was the forge,
and found the west-side house in the private business of becoming
smaller. San was folding a shirt on the table with the precision of a
man packing a weapon. In the corner, his father was rolling a coat —
once, tight, the way he rolled anything that had to hold — and saying
nothing about it, because saying nothing about it was the whole of
what he had to say.

The boy fed the fire and worked the bellows and did not speak either,
and the three of them went on that way in the one room: two packers
and a fire, each doing the small exact thing in front of him, until
the man said, without looking up, "That's enough for tonight," and
the boy eased the bellows to rest, and the fire settled, and out in
the lane the night was already the kind that does not care about
cities.

---

The evening before, at the north-end house, the road came to supper
and did not sit down.

His mother listened to the date the way she listened to everything —
sideways, while her hands kept working — and at the end she only said,
"Well. Then you have one more good day of seeing him off."

"That's all?"

"What would you have me say?"

"I don't know," he said. "Something."

She set down the bowl. "You asked for a road. He got one. That is
what happened today, and it is a true thing, and there is nothing in
it to be sorry about and nothing in it yet for you." She picked the
bowl back up. "Eat. When there is something to earn, you will be ready
to earn it. That is the whole of the difference."

Behind the stove the tin sat where it had sat since the night it came
out — shut. It did not come out again. There was nothing to pay for.

He ate. Outside, the village was doing its evening business: gates,
voices, smoke. Somewhere on the west side a boy was packing a bundle
and fussing over pots, and an old man was letting himself be fussed
over without appearing to, and the next day the square would fill up
with hats.

"Two days," he said to nobody.

"Two days if he stops at everything," his mother said, "which he
will." And then, so quietly he almost missed it: "And you will be at
the gate before they are out of sight. Go to bed."

---

They were out of sight sooner than he had counted on.

Jack walked like a man who had been appointed by weather — one pace,
no hurry, all destination — and San matched him with his bundle on his
back and his shoulders doing their careful best, and the square did
what squares do at departures: it talked loudly to cover the part that
mattered.

Old women said the boy had his father's hands. The baker's wife said
he had his mother's sense, whoever she had been, and then blew her
nose into her apron without apology. The blue-grass boy, historian of
the expedition, whispered that the arch was really four characters and
ten meters and that he had counted them himself in Jack's telling.
The girl who had not cried at her crystal stood at the back and did
not cry at this either, and watched the warm boy watch his friend go.

The two of them had one minute at the edge of the square, in the
middle of all of it, which is where the important things are always
allowed to happen, because nobody can hear you over a square anyway.

"Broth at the crossroads," the boy said.

"Two days if I stop at everything."

"You'll stop at everything."

"Probably," San said. He adjusted the bundle strap the way he
adjusted everything — exactly, once. Then he looked at his friend
with the clear, level eyes that had been steady since before either
of them could remember, and said the thing he had clearly come up
with during the night and carried here in his mouth like a stone:
"When you get there, don't stop at everything."

The boy did not say *when*. The word was in the air, having been
neither thrown nor caught, and they both let it stay there, because
boys that age are honest about what they can promise and what they
cannot, and this one was the second kind.

"I'll write the name of the broth down," San said.

"You can't write."

"I'll learn," San said, "and then I'll write it, and then you'll come
get it spelled right." He stuck out his hand — not because anyone had
taught him to, but because the square was watching and handshakes are
what the square deserves — and the boy shook it, and both of them did
it far too seriously, and then Jack said, "Boys," and that was the
whole of it.

The north-end house came to the gate with San — the mother with her
rolls and the cheese, and behind her the father, Chen Jian, who had
come back a day early from the Farfield beam job because a boy walking
to a city is a thing a village measures only once. He did not make a speech. He shook the blacksmith's son's hand with
both of his own, the way one maker checks another's work, and said,
"Mind the hands. They're the part of you the city will believe first."
"Yes, sir," said San — more than he said to most grown men — and
adjusted the strap once.

The mother gave him the rolls she had put in his pocket without
announcing it, and the rest straight, without any softness in it that
he would have to carry:

"Walk fast. Eat first. And when you get there, find out who empties
the ash and ask them how it's done — because the boy who does the
lowest work well is the one they keep."

It was the longest speech she had ever made in a doorway, and she
followed it with nothing, and went back inside, and the gate post
kept its opinion to itself.

At the square the blue-grass boy was doing a count. "One bundle," he
announced to anyone who would look. "Two boots. One Jack. Six
carried, counting the cheese —" this last to the north-end boy, who
did not confirm or deny.

Old Jack made his own speech, because departures were half his office.
"Two days," he said. "I've walked it more times than either of you
have teeth. We stop at the crossroads, we eat what's carried, we do
not pick up anything we find on the road, no matter how interesting."
He looked at the historian. "Especially then."

They went south. The hats did not wave so much as rearrange
themselves. At the bend where the road first met the fields, San
turned around once — not a wave, just a look, checking the village
was still there — and then the two of them were a smaller shape, and
then a pair of decisions, and then the road alone.

The square emptied. The boy stood where he had stood on the day of
the speech, on the same patch of trampled ground, with the two words
of that day and the one word of this day taking their places next to
each other behind his ribs. The south road ran out of the village the
way it always had — past the last house, past the well's field, on
into the part of the world that had a city in it now and a boy in it,
and the boy watched it until there was nothing left to watch, which
took longer than it should have, because a road with somebody on it
is slower to disappear than a road with nobody on it.

His mother's hand found the back of his neck.

"Come," she said. "The day doesn't stop."

They walked home the long way, neither of them saying the thing out
loud — that this was the first walk in six years that did not end at
the hill with two boys on it — and at their gate the boy did what he
had done at every departure and every arrival and every ordinary
evening of his life, which was look south, where the road went, and
then go in, where the lamp was.

The village, behind him, closed its doors one at a time around the
fact of it: one boy gone, one boy in, and the well not caring
either way.

---

The hill did not change, which was the worst part.

It was exactly the same hill — the same dead grass, the same rock
that sat like a cat, the same view of the river doing its old work —
and there was one place on it that kept not being filled, and the boy
sat in his own place and kept glancing at the not-place, and then
stopped glancing, and then couldn't stop.

On the Sunday the boy with the sprig of blue grass — older now, taller
by a thumb, still appointed historian of everything — came up the
track and asked if he could come and sit, and was told, kindly, that
they were going to sit and not do anything, and he came anyway, and
after a while there were two boys on the hill doing nothing properly
and one doing it badly, and none of them mentioned the empty place,
because mentioning it would have been one more thing the empty place
had taken.

He ran that evening. Down the lane, past the well, along the river
road where the mud took your shoes and gave back twice as much, and
he ran until his lungs burned and then past the burning, to the place
where the breath evened out, and he did it three times, because the
body is a simple animal and will take simple orders on a day when the
head will not.

The women at the well the next morning had only one new thing to say,
and they said it in the old way: "The blacksmith's boy is gone to the
city." And then, to the north-end boy, passing with his water:
"You'll see him at festival."

"Yes," he said, because it was what the well expected, and went on
with his water, and the water was heavy, and the heaviness was good.

"And you," said the baker's wife, who had followed the bread
situation into being a daily visitor now, "are carrying that like you
were born to it."

"He was born to water carrying," said the woman beside her, and
everyone agreed, and nobody meant it, and the boy filled the second
bucket anyway, and the morning went on the way mornings go after
somebody leaves: exactly the same, and visibly different, like a room
with one chair taken out that nobody will admit to counting.

On the third day the west-side door was standing open in the morning —
not open for daylight, open — and Tang Hao was at the anvil with the
fire already up, doing the work of a house that had one less mouth in
it, and when the boy came up the lane the man did not look around and
did not say *not today*.

He said, "It's been up an hour."

The boy went in and worked the bellows, and the fire came up the way
it came up under his hands, from banked red to working orange in one
long breath, and outside in the lane the village passed and did not
look too hard, because this was the blacksmith's house and the
blacksmith's grief, if it was grief, was his own business, and the
boy at the bellows was there the way he had always been there — early,
exact, and not asked for twice.

He worked until his arms shook, then eased to the resting stroke
without being told.

"Good," Tang Hao said, not turning around. "It's not a horse."

Later — the fire down, the shop swept, the lane already dark — the boy
was coiling the bellows rope for the night when he heard himself ask,
the way you ask about weather: "Is the orange right?"

"It's been right for ten minutes," Tang Hao said. "You were watching
the lane."

"I was watching the rope."

"Mm," said Tang Hao, which in the west-side language was a whole
paragraph of knowing, and he took the coiled rope from the boy's
hands, checked the coil the way he checked everything — a long time,
no expression — and hung it where rope went, and said, "Door in the
morning," and went in, and the boy stood in the dark shop with his
heart hammering at nothing and everything, because *door in the
morning* was three words that meant: come back.

A week. Two. The forge took the boy the way the forge had taken the
three months — as a fact. The village, which had one boy in a city
book now and one boy at the well, went on being the village. The
baker's wife sent bread to the west-side house and made the north-end
boy carry it, and neither of them mentioned that she had started
sending it on the days the blacksmith's door stood open.

---

On the eighth night his mother took the tin down from behind the
stove.

She did not open it. She set it on the table between the bowls, the
way she had the first time, and looked at it for a moment, and then
at him, and he looked back, and it was the same look as the bucket
night and the lamp night — the one that meant the arithmetic had been
done and the answer held.

"When you know what it's for," she said, "you tell me, and we'll open
it."

"Suppose I never know?"

"Then it's a tin," she said, "and you're a boy, and there's soup.
Eat."

Chen Xing ate. The tin went back behind the stove, shut, patient — a road
being a road, by not being spent. Outside the window stood open
because it had always stood open, and the night came in the way the
cold came in: without an invitation and without any harm, and the
house did what the house did, which was to stay warm around the two
of them and the thing between them that was not yet a plan.

His mother cleared the bowls and then stood a moment at the door,
looking at nothing — at the lane, at the smoke standing over the
west-side house, at the sky where the road was even though the road
wasn't in it — and the boy watched her from the table and understood,
the way you understand weather, that she was doing the same count he
was doing: one at the well, one at the forge, one somewhere past the
crossroads with bread in his pocket, and the village whole anyway,
the way villages are whole, keeping their shape around their gaps.

"Window stays open," she said, not turning around.

"Yes."

"Good. It's cold."

"Yes."

She came back and set the lamp to its low mark, and the house
dimmed to the color it had always been at the end of days, and
outside the night went on being the night — ordinary, enormous,
unspent.

Somewhere south a boy was walking into the city with his name about
to go into a book, and somewhere north of the well a boy was sitting
at an open window, not sleeping, doing the one thing he could still
do for it — waiting, in his own way, the way a body waits: breathing,
evening out, ready.

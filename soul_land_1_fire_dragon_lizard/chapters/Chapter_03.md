# Chapter 3: Three Months

The news took four days to walk the whole village, which in Holy Spirit
Village counted as a sprint.

It started at the well, as most things did. The baker's wife said it
first, to a woman who was not listening, and by the time the sentence
had crossed the square it had grown the way village sentences grow —
one true thing, one admiring thing, and one thing somebody wished they
had thought of themselves. The north-end boy had lit the black stones.
The blacksmith's boy had stood in the golden light like he had been
waiting for it. And the spirit master, the one from Nuoding who never
came back for nobody, had looked at the fire lizard and stepped back
with his own two feet.

"Stepped back," the women said, at the well, at the door, in the
lane — and looked north, where the north-end house stood with its open
window and its smoke.

The two boys did not know they were news. Boys that age never do; the
news is for the mothers. They went back to the hill and the river the
next day the way they had gone back every day before the ceremony, and
the only thing that had changed was that now the other children
watched them do it. The girl who had not cried at her own dark crystal
followed them halfway up the hill and turned back. The boy with the
sprig of blue grass asked if he could come and was told, kindly, that
they were going to sit and not do anything, and he came anyway, and
after a while they all four sat without doing anything, which is how
the village saw the whole thing through: together, in silence, on a
hill.

The mothers talked. The mothers always talked. But there was a new
thread in it now, and everybody could hear the shape of it without
pulling it: two boys, one road, one village, and a spirit master who
had said *straight road* out loud in front of thirty witnesses and
then got into his cart like a man who has delivered a package.

The mothers did the arithmetic out loud, because that is what mothers
are for. A sickle child was a sickle future. A grass child was a field
future. And the two who had stood in the light — the quiet one with the
trash grass and the full power nobody could look at, and the warm one
with the fire and the mid power nobody could explain — those two were
a different ledger entirely, and the ledger had no column for them yet,
so the women made one on the spot and called it *we'll see*.

"Mine's going to be a working student," one of them said, to no one.

"Yours will be carrying water like the rest of us," said the baker's
wife, "and yours too, and the only difference between this village and
the next is that ours got looked at this year."

That was the whole of it, and it was enough. The village had been
looked at.

---

Old Jack came to the square on the fifth day, in the hat he wore for
his own business, which meant the village gathered without being told.

"Three months," he said. "Then I take the blacksmith's boy down to
Nuoding."

The square did what squares do — it went quiet, then loud, then quiet
again in pieces. Nuoding. The city. The academy with the stone arch
that Jack had seen twice in his life and described forty times.

"Working student," Jack went on, holding up one finger the way he
held up anything official. "Tuition waived. Lodging waived. He works
for his food — that is the whole of the arrangement, and it is a good
arrangement, because a soul master who cannot be bothered to feed
himself at six has no business being a soul master at all."

He had seen the place twice, he said, and he would describe it a third
time for those who hadn't: an arch of stone as wide as the well road,
iron gates, four characters carved so deep the rain couldn't take
them. Classrooms. A roof that didn't leak. And at the end of the week,
if your hands had done their work, food — because that was the
arrangement, hands for bread, and there was no shame in it, there was
shame in eating with hands that wouldn't work.

"What about the other one?" somebody asked — not loudly, because
villages never ask loudly when they are asking the thing they most
want answered.

Jack looked at the north-end house over the tops of the hats. "The
spirit master said *straight road* to that boy too," he said. "I
remember what I hear. I am an old man; it is the only thing I am good
for." He put his hands behind his back. "Three months. Then we see."

Then we see. It was not a promise. The village knew it was not a
promise, and it filed the two words exactly where it filed everything
that might become one later — under *wait* — and went home to supper.

The boy stayed. The square emptied around him the way squares do, hats
and elbows and the baker's wife's voice receding toward the ovens, and
he stood in the trampled ring where the speech had been with the two
words still warm in front of him. Not a promise. But a man who
remembers what he hears has just been heard remembering out loud, and
the boy did what he had always done with sentences he liked: he kept
it, next to the one about bending and quenching, and he stood there
until his mother's hand found the back of his neck the way it found
everything, without looking.

"Chen Xing. You heard," she said.

"I heard."

"Good. Then you know what three months is."

"Three months is a long time."

"Three months is exactly as long as it is," she said. "Come. The day
doesn't stop."

---

The west-side door shut on a Tuesday.

The boy noticed because he came on Wednesday. He came the way he had
come all winter — not early, not late, at the hour when the fire
needed the second feeding and the doorway was warmest — and the door
was shut, not slammed, not barred, just shut, in the way that a door
is shut when the people behind it have something they are doing.

He knocked on the Friday.

He meant to knock on the Wednesday, and on the Thursday he meant to,
and each time the meaning ran out somewhere around his knuckles and he
went home instead, having done nothing, which upset him more than the
cold did. On Friday he knocked because the cold had got into his
temper and temper is easier than courage.

The hammering stopped. The door stayed shut against his fist.

"Not today," Tang Hao said, through the door, without opening it, in
the tone he used for men who came to complain about prices.

"All right," the boy said.

He stood there long enough for it not to be nothing — long enough for
the man on the other side to know a boy had stood there and taken the
answer standing up — and then he went.

After that it stayed shut. Thursday, shut. Friday's answer already
given. On Saturday there was sound behind it — a ring of iron, irregular, followed by a pause that
was a man correcting somebody — and then quiet again. On Sunday the
door opened for daylight only, and the boy, passing with his eyes
already warm for it, saw Tang Hao alone at the anvil doing the work of
two and did not go in, because Tang Hao's face on Sundays was the face
of a man who had decided the week would be shorter if he simply didn't
talk.

The village, of course, had theories. Theories were the village's
native crop. But the blacksmith's house had always run on the policy
of *don't ask*, and even now — especially now — nobody knocked.

The boy knocked. Or nearly: he got as far as putting his palm flat on
the cold boards and then took it away again, because inside he could
hear the two hammers — the father's steady, the son's slow — and he
went away, because nobody likes to watch a boy learn while he is
still bad at it.

Instead he took the bellows' other job — the one nobody had ever named
— which was to make sure the yard did not stop being a yard. He split
what could be split. He stacked the iron stock against the rain that
was coming the way rain comes in the third month of the cold season,
with opinions. He mended the lean of the woodpile that everybody saw
and nobody fixed. He did it before his own work at home and after, in
the grey hours, and he never once did it while someone was watching, because he was
not doing it to be seen.

When Tang Hao came out on Monday for the water bucket and saw the
woodpile standing at true for the first time in two years, he looked
at it the way he looked at everything — a long time, no expression —
and then at the boy, who was not there, because the boy had learned
where to stand.

He did not say thank you. Blacksmiths did not say thank you; the
village had that from their side for a generation. But on Wednesday
the door was open when the boy passed, and the fire was up, and Tang
Hao said, without turning around, "It wants feeding," and the boy went
in and worked the bellows, and neither of them mentioned that three
days had gone missing.

The fire came up the way it always came up under his hands — not
raging, not sulking, just arriving, the whole bed of it turning from
banked red to working orange in one long breath, and the boy watched
the color find its place the way he had taught himself to watch it,
from this exact spot, a hundred mornings before the door had ever shut.

"More," Tang Hao said.

He gave more. The sparks came up off the hammer in the old rhythm —
three strokes, turn, quench — and the man worked in the middle of the
noise with his shoulders down and his mouth shut, and if there was
anything in him that wanted to explain three days of silence, it never
came near the surface, because Tang Hao did not run his life on the
surface.

When the boy's arms began to shake — the honest point, where the easy
work stops — he eased the bellows to the resting stroke without being
told,
and Tang Hao, still not turning, said, "Good. It's not a horse; you
don't have to kill it." And that, in the west-side language, was a
paragraph.

---

The hill evenings were quieter than they used to be.

Not worse — quieter. Xiao San came out at the same time, sat in the
same place, looked at the same river; but some of him was still back
there behind the shut door, working out what his hands had been told,
and the boy had the sense — born in him, or beaten into the world
around him — to let the quiet be quiet.

They talked about food, mostly. And dogs. And whether the river went
under the mountain or around it, which neither of them knew and both
of them intended to find out someday, and which was, without either
saying it, the first thing they had ever planned together.

"The broth at the crossroads," Xiao San said one evening, out of
nowhere, already somewhere else. "Jack made it sound like a city
secret."

"City secrets are just village secrets with better shoes."

"Say that again when we're there."

"*When?*" the boy said, and let the word sit in the grass between
them, and his friend did not take it back or explain it, and the river
went on not answering whether it went under or around.

Once, the boy asked, "Does it hurt?"

Tang San considered it the way he considered everything. "No," he
said. "It's just new."

"That's what the fire says too," the boy said — meaning the forge-fire,
the color-reading, the sentence about bending and quenching he still
carried like a warm coal — and Tang San looked at him, and something
passed between them that was not a secret and did not touch one: the
understanding that each of them had something happening behind a door,
and that neither of them was going to stand in front of the other's.

"You'll go to the city," the boy said. It was not a question.

"Father says Jack talks too much."

"Jack talks exactly enough."

Tang San almost smiled. "Then yes. I'll go."

A month later, in the dark of the north-end house, his mother asked
him what he wanted — not straight out, the way other adults ask, but
the way she asked everything, sideways, while he was eating.

"The road," he said.

"To school?"

"To work. Like Xiao San. They take working students. Jack said so."

She did not look up from her own bowl. "Jack said they take one."

"Jack said *then we see*."

Now she looked up. The lamp was low and her face had the expression he
had seen the night of the bucket — the one that meant she had put
something down and picked something else up and was not going to drop
it.

"Say the whole thing," she said. "Working students work. Say what the
work is."

"Whatever they're given."

"Hands," she said. "Beds. Floors. Firewood. Somebody else's mud. The
city doesn't know you from any other boy with two arms — and that is
the good part, do you hear me? In this village you are the warm one,
the window one, the lizard one. There your name is *hands*, until you
make it something else."

"Is that what you'd do?"

"I would do what my mother did, which is send you with clean socks and
no opinions." She set her bowl down. "But you would be gone."

"I would be eight."

"You would be gone," she said again, evenly, with no anger in it at
all, and he learned something about roads that
night that no one had taught him yet: that they cost the person who
stays more than the person who goes.

Then she reached behind the stove — which he had never once seen her
do in that particular way, with that particular slowness — and brought
out the tin that was not the flour tin and not the salt tin and had
never been opened in his hearing, and set it on the table between the
bowls without opening it.

"I have been saving for a road since before you were born," she said.
"I did not know whose."

"That isn't fair," he said. "You didn't know me."

"I knew somebody," she said. "Eat. Your father's at the Farfield job
till week's end — this road talk is between us."

He ate. The tin stayed shut on the table for the whole meal, and then
it went back behind the stove, and neither of them said anything more
about it — which in that house meant the matter was settled but not
finished.

---

The third month turned the corner the way cold weather does — all at
once, one morning, with the frost gone polite.

On the Sunday before the last week, Old Jack held court on the well
wall for anybody who hadn't heard the arrangement yet, which by then
was nobody, but Jack told it anyway, because telling was the office.

"Nuoding's a day and a half on foot if you're a boy and not a
cart," he said. "Two days if you stop at everything, which you will.
There's an inn at the crossroads that sells bone broth cheap to
children who carry their own bundles — and you'll carry your own,
both of you, because I'm an old man and not a mule."

"Two days?" said the blue-grass boy, who had appointed himself the
expedition's historian.

"Two days," Jack said. "And then the arch. And then somebody asks
your name, and you give it, and they write it down, and from that day
your name is in a book in a city that has never heard of this village
— and *that*, children, is how you leave Holy Spirit Village without
leaving it. The village goes in the book with you."

The boys were quiet at that, in the particular way of children who
have just heard something enormous dressed as logistics. Even Xiao San,
who had heard it all before at his own door, looked at the road south
of the well as if the road had changed.

Chen Xing did not say anything at all. He was busy.

The boy's own work grew. He had made a shape of his days by then: the
water, the wood, the errands his mother set and the ones she didn't
set and he did anyway, and after all of it the running — down the
lane, past the well, along the river road where the mud wanted your
shoes and gave them back twice as heavy. He ran until his lungs burned
and then a little past that, because something in him had learned, the way it learned the oak, that
if he ran past the burning in his lungs the breath came back even and
easy further on — and it kept happening without asking him.

The women at the well noticed. "He eats like he's feeding a forge,"
they said,
which was the closest the village came to saying what he was. They
laughed, and the remark went down the well with the rest of the talk.

On the last week, Jack came down the lane on his own business and
found the boy at the woodpile — not waiting to be seen, just working —
a boy with a woodpile in front of him.

Jack watched for a moment. "You know what a working student does," he
said.

"Works."

"Works," Jack agreed. "Lodging's free. Tuition's free. Food isn't —
food's worked for, in coin or in hands. Most children hear that and
think of the free parts first."

"I heard the food part first," the boy said, "because it's the part
that's true every day."

Jack rubbed his chin, the way he rubbed it when a story was starting
that he hadn't planned. "Hah," he said. "That."

He did not promise anything. He touched the brim of his hat with one
finger, which in Jack's language was a full ceremony, and went on down
the lane toward his own business, and the boy picked up the next piece
of wood and split it, and the split was clean.

That night the road did not come up at the supper table. Roads that
are coming do not need to. The tin sat behind the stove. The window
was open because the window was always open. Somewhere on the west
side a hammer stopped for the day, and the sound of stopping went out
over the roofs like a held breath, and the two boys — one in each
house, one going, one wanting — slept without dreaming of the road, because a road you know is
coming does not have to visit you in the night.

In the morning the woodpile was shorter and the lane was longer, and
the boy carried the first load down before the well had been opened
for the day, and nobody saw it except his mother, who had been awake
at the open window the whole time, and who — when the boy had gone
past with the wood up to his chin — said to the doorway, to nobody,
"Clean socks and no opinions. Hah. That family."

Then the bread came out, and the village woke, and the third month
went on counting itself down. Ten days short of the end of it,
give or take, Old Jack would put on his hat for his own business and
walk south. Nobody
in the village knew the date yet. The woodpile knew, in the way that
woodpiles know — by getting shorter.

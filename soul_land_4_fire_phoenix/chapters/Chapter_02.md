# Chapter 2: Warm Hands

Secrets did not vanish when doors closed.

They moved into schedules.

They hid inside transfer lists, housing applications, medical exemptions, nursery permits, and quiet conversations that stopped when the wrong person entered a room. The institute returned to its ordinary noise after the northern expedition, but ordinary noise had layers now. A corridor could sound normal and still make people walk softer. A meeting could be labeled environmental review and still leave the participants pale. A child could be given a birth certificate, a family name, a room with blue walls, and a future, while the question of where he had truly come from remained locked behind adults who had chosen love before ambition.

Yan Ke did not know the truth.

He knew enough to know there was one.

That was common in his work. Facility safety lived at the edge of other people's secrets. He saw which rooms drew more power than their listed function required. He saw which residential blocks received upgraded shields after an event no public report mentioned. He saw how often Lan Xiao came home late in the weeks after the expedition and how Nan Cheng carried herself like someone prepared to bite any hand that reached toward her child.

He did not ask.

He had his own child to carry home.

Yan Shuo'er slept badly during her first weeks of life, but not because she cried without end. She slept badly because she seemed to notice temperature changes before anyone else did. A vent turning on made her mouth twist. A blanket too heavy made her fists open and close in offended silence. If Lin Mei held her, she settled. If Yan Ke warmed his hands first and spoke before touching her, she settled faster.

"She is judging us," Yan Ke said one night, standing over the crib with the weary awe of a new father.

Lin Mei, seated beside the bed with her hair loose and her eyes half closed, gave him a look. "She is a baby."

"A severe baby."

"She has your eyebrows when she is annoyed."

"Then she is wise to be severe."

Yan Shuo'er made a small sound, not a cry yet, only a warning that the world was approaching unacceptable conditions.

Lin Mei reached down and brushed one finger along her cheek. Her skin was warm under her touch, but no warmer than it had been in the womb. That was what unsettled her most. Babies changed after birth. They entered colder air, louder rooms, stranger rhythms. They flailed at discomfort because discomfort was new.

Her daughter did flail sometimes.

Then, too often, she corrected.

A shoulder tucked. A breath caught and released. A cry rose, thinned, and became a stubborn little grunt instead. She did not become calm all at once. She learned the path toward calm faster than she expected a newborn body to learn anything.

The doctors said she was healthy.

Yan Ke repeated that sentence whenever he wanted to stop worrying.

Lin Mei did not worry less. She only worried more precisely.

Healthy, yes. Ordinary, no.

But ordinary was not required for love.

When Yan Shuo'er was old enough to wrap her whole hand around one of Lin Mei's fingers, Lin Mei began teaching her without calling it teaching. She would hold her when the small body ran too warm and breathe slowly until Yan Shuo'er's chest tried to match hers. She would hum old family songs about ember-birds that rode wind instead of fighting it. She would tap the tiny wrist, wait until her daughter stopped squirming, then smile.

"Fire first listens to breath," she told her, though she could not understand words. "If you remember nothing else, remember that."

Yan Ke overheard her from the doorway. "She cannot remember that."

"Her body can."

"That sounds like something your mother would say before making me carry training sandbags."

"My mother was correct about many things."

"Your mother said I had soft shoulders."

"You did."

Yan Ke looked wounded. Lin Mei pretended not to enjoy it.

In the cradle, Yan Shuo'er sneezed. A breath of warmth brushed the blanket. Not flame. Not power. Only warmth gathering too neatly for accident before it faded into the room.

Yan Ke stopped smiling.

Lin Mei placed her hand over the blanket and felt the heat disappear under her palm. "Do not make a face."

"What face?"

"The paperwork face."

"I have no paperwork face."

"You have three. This is the one for reports you do not want to file."

Yan Ke looked at his daughter, then at his wife. "Do we file anything?"

"What would we write? Our baby is warm?"

"Consistently warm."

"Fire-line family."

"Unusually consistent."

"Healthy."

That word settled between them, not as an answer, but as a choice.

Yan Ke exhaled. "Healthy."

They did not file anything.

The institute kept moving.

Sometimes people imagined institutions as grand stone things that changed only through public announcements. Yan Ke knew better. Institutions changed through maintenance requests. First came additional scans for the nursery floors. Then came updated family travel eligibility. Then came a quiet expansion of research housing on Heaven Luo Planet. Then came the message telling certain staff families to prepare for long-term relocation if they accepted the new post.

He read the notice three times before showing Lin Mei.

By then Yan Shuo'er could walk if she held furniture and fall if she forgot furniture existed.

She fell often.

She recovered from humiliation faster than injury. If a table betrayed her, she shouted at the table, slapped it with a warm palm, and tried again. If the floor betrayed her, she considered the floor with deep personal disappointment, then crawled away to find a more reasonable world.

Lin Mei watched her daughter haul herself upright beside the sofa while Yan Ke explained the relocation.

"Heaven Luo has better residential shields," Yan Ke said. "Better early education access. The institute wants continuity staff there. I am not high rank, but I am boringly reliable."

"You say that like boring is not one of your proudest virtues."

"In emergencies, boring people save dramatic people."

Yan Shuo'er let go of the sofa.

Both parents stopped breathing.

She took one step, then another. Her face became solemn with effort. A third step sent her tilting forward. Lin Mei started to move, but Yan Ke caught her wrist softly.

Yan Shuo'er's foot turned. Her arms flung wide. For a heartbeat she looked less like a baby losing balance and more like a small bird remembering that the air existed.

Then she sat down hard.

She blinked.

Her mouth opened.

No cry came.

Instead, she looked back at the sofa as if it had personally failed to applaud.

Yan Ke whispered, "That was new."

Lin Mei's fingers relaxed in his hold. "She corrected her weight."

"Babies do that."

"Badly."

"She also sat on her bottom."

"Also badly."

Yan Shuo'er slapped the floor once, decided the matter was settled, and reached for the sofa again.

Lin Mei looked at the relocation notice.

Better schools. Better shields. Better training rooms. More children from institute families. A wider world than corridors and cautious parents.

"We should go," she said.

Yan Ke nodded. "I think so too."

Yan Shuo'er pulled herself upright again, cheeks flushed with effort and pride.

"And we should buy softer floor mats," Yan Ke added.

"That is the first intelligent thing you have said today."

"I have been saving it."

The move to Heaven Luo did not feel like a grand migration to Yan Shuo'er.

It felt like boxes, strangers, new smells, and her father repeatedly counting bags while her mother refused to sit down until everyone else had already sat down. It felt like the hum of a transport ship deep in her bones, artificial gravity under small feet, window panels showing stars that were too far away for a child to understand. It felt like cold glass under her palms and Lin Mei's hand at her back, steadying her when the ship shivered during course correction.

Some children cried during the flight.

Yan Shuo'er did not cry at first. She watched light slide along the cabin wall with round amber-brown eyes, one hand pressed to the window, the other fisted in her mother's sleeve. Her hair had grown dark and soft, but under the cabin lights it showed a copper sheen whenever she turned her head.

Then the ship entered a patch of turbulence.

The cabin shook.

A baby wailed somewhere behind them. A luggage lock snapped shut. Yan Shuo'er's breath caught hard enough that Lin Mei felt it through her sleeve.

"Shuo," she said softly.

She looked up.

Her eyes were wide, not with panic alone, but with the offense of a child who had discovered the sky could be unreliable.

Lin Mei lowered her own breathing. "Listen first. Then answer."

She did not understand the words fully, but she knew the rhythm. In. Hold. Out. Warmth that had started to climb under her skin folded back toward her chest. Her little shoulders lowered. The ship shook again, and this time she flinched, but she did not cry.

Across the aisle, an older woman smiled. "What a brave little boy."

Yan Shuo heard praise and immediately straightened.

Yan Ke covered his mouth with one hand.

Lin Mei sighed. "Do not feed the fire."

"I said nothing."

"You laughed internally."

"That is not yet illegal."

On Heaven Luo Planet, the air tasted different.

Not to adults, perhaps. Adults named differences through climate reports, oxygen balance, city planning, and district quality. To Yan Shuo, the new world tasted like filtered wind, warm metal after sunlight, and rain held behind glass. The institute housing block rose in clean lines near the research district, its walls pale, its balconies shielded, its courtyards arranged so children could run without reaching the traffic levels beyond.

Yan Ke loved the safety layout immediately.

Lin Mei judged the training room fire suppression and found it acceptable after three complaints and one maintenance request.

Yan Shuo loved the courtyard.

It had soft paths, low climbing frames, a shallow light fountain that changed color when children ran around it, and benches where parents pretended to rest while watching everything. The first afternoon they let him down there, he ran until his cheeks flushed and his hair stuck to his forehead. He fell twice, shouted once, accepted water with the dignity of a victorious general, and discovered that other children existed in numbers too large to manage.

Most were louder than him.

This shocked him.

He adjusted quickly.

A little girl with braids took possession of the red climbing arch and declared it a spaceship. Two boys argued over whether the fountain was a sea or a reactor. A round-faced child tried to put a toy car into the fountain and was intercepted by three adults at once.

Yan Shuo stood in the middle of it all, bright-eyed, hands warm, trying to decide where loyalty was required.

That was when he saw the boy by the bench.

The boy had skin so fair the courtyard light seemed to like him better than everyone else. His eyes were large and clear, his cheeks soft, his expression gentle in a way that made even restless children lower their voices when they came near him. He held a small spirit-guidance car in both hands, watching the wheels turn but not pushing it along the ground.

A teacher from the early-child care group crouched beside him. "Xuanyu, do you want to play with them?"

The boy looked at the other children, then at the car.

"Car," he said, careful and soft.

"Yes, your car can play too."

He considered this, as though the car's consent mattered.

Yan Shuo stared.

Children stared for simple reasons. A bright toy. A strange voice. A face everyone else liked. Yan Shuo stared because something about the boy's stillness made the noisy courtyard feel uneven. Not dangerous. Heavy.

He took a step closer.

Warmth tightened under his ribs.

It was not like fear of the transport shaking. It was not like heat before crying. It was a quiet pressure, as if a great shadow passed high above him and his body noticed before his eyes could find it. His breath caught. His fingers curled.

The boy on the bench looked up.

For one moment, Yan Shuo wanted to step back.

He did not know why.

No thought explained it. No voice told him what to do. The small body that had learned warmth before words did what it had always done when pressure arrived. It held the edge. It found breath. It did not flare.

In.

Out.

The warmth in his chest steadied.

The boy blinked at him.

Yan Shuo marched forward with the serious courage of a child who had decided that if the world felt strange, it should be introduced properly.

"I am Yan Shuo," he announced.

The boy hugged the car closer, not frightened, only unsure.

The teacher smiled. "This is Lan Xuanyu. Can you say hello?"

Lan Xuanyu's lips moved once before sound came. "Hello."

His voice had a sweet roundness, younger than his face somehow, and Yan Shuo immediately decided that anyone who spoke like that should not be left alone with a car that was not moving.

"Cars go," Yan Shuo said.

Lan Xuanyu looked down at the car, then back up. "Go?"

Yan Shuo crouched, took his own little red shuttle from his pocket, and pushed it along the ground. It rolled too fast, hit his shoe, bounced sideways, and overturned.

Lan Xuanyu stared.

Yan Shuo stared too.

This was not the demonstration he had intended.

After a solemn pause, Lan Xuanyu laughed.

The sound was soft, delighted, and utterly unguarded. It struck the strange heaviness inside Yan Shuo like sunlight entering a room. His chest warmed again, but this time it was ordinary happiness rushing ahead of dignity.

"It crashed," Lan Xuanyu said.

Yan Shuo nodded gravely. "Bad pilot."

"You?"

"The car."

Lan Xuanyu thought about this, then nodded as if it made perfect sense.

They played on the ground beside the bench until the teacher stopped trying to guide them elsewhere. Yan Shuo's shuttle crashed often. Lan Xuanyu's car moved carefully, as though he worried it might get hurt. Yan Shuo built roads with his hands and declared some paths forbidden because the floor there was lava. Lan Xuanyu asked what lava was. Yan Shuo did not know exactly, but he knew it was hot and important, so he explained with confidence.

From across the courtyard, Lin Mei watched her child lean close to the beautiful quiet boy and felt something in her own martial soul stir.

Not alarm.

Attention.

Yan Shuo had stepped toward that child and then paused as if meeting wind. She had seen the hesitation. She had seen him breathe through it. Now he was laughing, whole and bright, his warmth normal again.

Yan Ke came to stand beside her with two cups of nutrient tea. "That is Lan Xiao's son."

Lin Mei accepted the cup. "You know them?"

"Work-near. Not friend-near. Lan Xiao is above my level, but everyone knows him. Hard not to."

"The boy is gentle."

"Very popular, from what I hear."

"Shuo walked to him like he was crossing a training line."

Yan Ke followed her gaze. The two boys were now arguing over whether a car could be brave. Lan Xuanyu seemed to believe it could. Yan Shuo seemed to believe bravery required crashing, which Lin Mei decided to correct before it became a family philosophy.

"Maybe he was shy," Yan Ke said, using the public pronoun because the courtyard was full of ears.

"Our Shuo?"

They both watched Yan Shuo stand, spread his arms, and declare himself a traffic barrier.

Yan Ke sipped his tea. "Perhaps not."

Lin Mei did not push the thought further. There was nothing to name. Children met other children. Some meetings carried small strangeness because children were small strange creatures. She would watch. That was all.

At the bench, Lan Xuanyu placed his car carefully in front of Yan Shuo's shuttle.

"Friend?" he asked.

Yan Shuo looked at him, surprised by how simple the word was and how serious it felt.

The pressure brushed him again, lighter this time, hidden beneath the boy's clear eyes and soft voice. His breath caught for half a beat. Warmth gathered around his chest, not burning, not fleeing.

He nodded hard enough that his hair fell into his eyes.

"Friend."

Lan Xuanyu smiled.

The courtyard seemed to accept it before the adults did.

After that, the days found patterns.

Yan Shuo and Lan Xuanyu met in the courtyard when family schedules allowed. Later they met in the child-care rooms, then the kindergarten attached near the institute district. Lan Xuanyu was loved almost immediately by teachers, children, and parents who tried not to stare too obviously at his face. He was obedient, sweet-tempered, and sometimes slow with words in a way that made adults soften around him.

Yan Shuo did not soften.

He translated.

If Lan Xuanyu took too long answering a question, Yan Shuo waited for him with the severe patience of someone guarding a gate. If another child tried to answer over him, Yan Shuo said, "He is thinking." If Lan Xuanyu gave away a toy because someone asked twice, Yan Shuo frowned and asked whether he had wanted to give it away. Sometimes Lan Xuanyu had. Sometimes he had not known refusal was allowed.

Yan Shuo began teaching him refusal with the seriousness Lin Mei taught breathing.

"Say no," Yan Shuo instructed one afternoon.

Lan Xuanyu held a blue block in both hands. "No."

"Louder."

"No."

"Mean it."

Lan Xuanyu looked down at the block, then up at the child waiting for it. His small face tightened with effort. "No. Mine now. Later you."

The other child accepted this because it sounded fair.

Yan Shuo beamed.

Lan Xuanyu smiled back, and the hidden heaviness in the air brushed Yan Shuo's skin like a large sleeping animal turning over. By then his body knew the shape of it. It still made him warmer. It still stole the start of a breath sometimes. But it no longer made him want to step back.

He did not know this was resistance.

He only knew Xuanyu was his friend and sometimes being near him felt like standing under a sky too big to shout at.

So he learned not to shout at it.

He breathed.

He stayed.

His body remembered staying.

The adults saw friendship.

They saw a bright fire-line boy and a gentle beautiful boy sitting together over toy cars, picture books, soft blocks, and snacks. They saw Yan Shuo become less reckless when Lan Xuanyu looked worried. They saw Lan Xuanyu speak a little more when Yan Shuo waited. They saw ordinary childhood give both boys something the institute could not design.

No one saw the deeper exchange.

No one saw how Yan Shuo's warmth tightened and steadied around pressure too old for him to name.

No one saw how repeated safety taught his forming martial-soul seed that this heavy presence was not an enemy, only a mountain beside the road.

No one saw how a boy who would one day awaken fire learned, before power, not to let a greater shadow decide his shape.

Years passed through small things.

Yan Shuo's legs grew steadier. Lan Xuanyu's words grew clearer, though a sweetness remained that made adults forgive too much. Yan Ke became comfortable in the Heaven Luo housing systems and complained only when regulations were written by people who had never tried to move actual families. Lin Mei found a fire-safe practice room and began teaching Yan Shuo child exercises that were not soul-power training, only posture, breath, and respect for heat.

"You do not own fire because you like it," she told him while he stood barefoot on the mat, cheeks puffed with concentration.

"I like it," Yan Shuo said.

"That is why I am warning you."

"Will I have fire?"

Lin Mei paused.

Every child asked eventually, when awakening became a word adults used around them. Some asked with fear. Some with greed. Yan Shuo asked as if fire were a neighbor he expected to visit soon.

"Maybe," she said. "Your mother's family carries fire. But the awakening decides what shape answers you."

"A bird?"

"Maybe."

"A big bird?"

"Maybe."

"A brave car?"

Lin Mei closed her eyes briefly. "No."

"Why?"

"Because your father would become unbearable."

Yan Shuo accepted this as a real law of the universe.

Later, when Lan Xuanyu came over to play, Yan Shuo repeated the lesson with improvements.

"Awakening tells you what is inside," he said.

Lan Xuanyu sat on the floor, lining cars in careful rows. "Inside where?"

Yan Shuo pressed a hand to his chest. "Here. Or hands. Or maybe everywhere."

"What do you want?"

"Fire bird," Yan Shuo said immediately.

Lan Xuanyu looked at his own hands. "I don't know."

Yan Shuo frowned. "You can want."

"Daddy says anything is okay."

"That is also wanting."

Lan Xuanyu considered this. "Then I want Daddy and Mommy happy."

Yan Shuo stared at him.

The answer was so Lan Xuanyu that it made him feel both warm and annoyed. "That is not a spirit."

"Oh."

"You can still want it."

"Then what do you want?"

Yan Shuo opened his mouth to repeat fire bird, but the words paused.

He looked at Lan Xuanyu, at the careful rows of cars, at the friend who gave things away too easily and smiled like sunlight was something he apologized for receiving.

"I want," Yan Shuo said slowly, "to stand with you when we know."

Lan Xuanyu did not fully understand.

Neither did Yan Shuo.

But Lan Xuanyu smiled anyway. "Together?"

"Together."

The word entered Yan Shuo's body like another kind of warmth.

Outside, evening gathered over Zi Luo City's lights. Vehicles moved through high lanes in orderly streams. Research towers glowed. Families returned home. Somewhere in the institute, sealed files continued sleeping behind authorizations Yan Shuo would not see. Somewhere far beyond the planet, histories older than either boy moved in directions no child could understand.

In a small room full of toy cars, two children made a promise with no ceremony at all.

It was not destiny yet.

It was only friendship.

For Yan Shuo's body, that was enough to learn from.

When the notice for the next awakening cycle finally reached the housing block, Yan Ke read it at the dinner table and went quiet.

Lin Mei set down her chopsticks.

Yan Shuo, who had been trying to balance a vegetable on the edge of his spoon, looked between them. "What?"

Yan Ke cleared his throat. "Your awakening registration is coming."

Yan Shuo went still.

Heat rose into his cheeks so quickly Lin Mei almost smiled.

"Fire bird?" he asked.

"Maybe," Lin Mei said.

Yan Ke folded the notice with unnecessary care. "Whatever answers, we meet it properly."

Yan Shuo nodded, solemn and shining.

Later that night, after he fell asleep, his hands remained open on the blanket as if waiting for wings.

In another apartment not far away, Lan Xiao stood beside his own son's bed with the same notice on his wrist display and a worry he could not share with neighbors.

The two fathers did not speak.

The two mothers did not compare fears.

The two boys slept under the same housing lights, close enough that one hidden pressure had already become part of the other's weather.

Morning would come.

The awakening rooms would open.

And the world would ask both children what shape their souls had kept inside.

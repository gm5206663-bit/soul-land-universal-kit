# Chapter 52 — Amiable Beasts

The strands went slack at the same moment, and Qian Lei came down out of the canopy with his knees taking the impact the way Lan had drilled into him, and he was angry about it before he had even registered why.

That was the trick of the thing. You did not feel the trade while it was working. You felt it the second it stopped.

The strand of golden-patterned Blue Silver Grass ran from Liu Feng's waist, through Qian's harness, back to Lan's hands at the rear of the line, three bodies on one wire, and every metre they gained had been bought out of the safety margin. Qian could see the arithmetic even sitting still. Two of them carried; one of them ran free to look around. Nobody had hands left.

"Safety level's down," he said.

"It is," Lan agreed, already unhooking Qian's line and re-tensioning it. "That's the price. Eyes up. You're the only one of us not spending anything, which means you're the only one of us who can see."

That was the part Qian had learned to respect and never liked. Lan did not carry him because Qian was tired. He carried him because a summoner thinking about his feet was a summoner not thinking about the forest, and in this round, thinking about the forest was worth more than a hundred metres of stride.

The coin was already in his hand. It usually was. It had the specific smoothness of something handled far more than combat required, and Qian had stopped noticing he held it some time in the second hour.

The sound arrived on his left, and it arrived *wrong*.

Not a roar. A texture. A long low impact with another impact answering it, over and over, with a rhythm that meant neither side was winning.

"Down," Qian said.

No shout in it. He was already behind a bush; Lan's grass went slack on both strands at once and Liu dropped out of a tree with the flat control of somebody who had decided to land before he had finished moving, and the three of them sat in leaf litter while the forest went on being a forest above them.

"Should I—" Qian started.

"No."

"My dragons are hundred-year earthen—"

"I know what they are." Lan's voice stayed level in the way that meant the argument was over before it had been offered. "That's why no. Listen to me, because I'd rather say it now than while something is coming at us. We do not have anything that kills a thousand-year beast in the time it takes to be counter-killed. Not you. Not Liu. Not me with grass."

The rumble came again, closer, and the ground under Qian's palm moved a little.

"Then we're dead," Qian said.

"We're *hidden*," Lan said. "Different thing. Liu. Look. Don't engage, don't test it, don't get ambitious."

Liu Feng was already up. "I don't get ambitious."

"You got ambitious in the second exercise."

"I get precise."

"That was *initiative*," Qian said, and Liu had the decency not to argue with the man being carried.

He went, and the grass on his waist paid for it, and Qian was left crouched in the dirt holding a coin with nothing to spend it on, which was arguably the single most uncomfortable position available to him.

Liu came back inside a minute, and he came back *wrong* — not fast, but with a stillness that Qian had only ever seen on him right after something had broken an assumption.

"Two soul beasts," Liu said. "Fighting. Not sparring, not a territory display — they are trying to end each other. Both around a thousand years. One's an ape, gold, and the fur does something with light. The other's a palm-type bear, and it is standing in one spot and daring the ape to come into it." He stopped. "Both are bleeding."

Qian felt the shape of it assemble itself in his chest before his brain caught up, because the round had trained that into all of them.

Two thousand-year beasts. Both wounded. Both, by any honest reading, at the end of what they had.

"The points on two thousand-year kills—" he began, very reasonably.

"Yes."

"Then what are we doing *sitting down*?"

"Being careful, and then going to look," Lan said. "Quiet. Same order. And put that away."

Qian put the coin away. He did it slowly, so that everyone present understood exactly what it had cost him.

---

The sound grew rather than arrived. Qian felt it in his teeth before he understood that what he was feeling was bodies hitting bodies.

They came up behind a trunk wide enough to hide three people with room to spare, and through the gaps, the clearing was a catastrophe.

The ape was two metres tall and every part of it was wrong for a bear's problem. Golden fur, and not decorative — light came through the canopy and the animal *handled* it, going bright at the edges, and when it jumped it left your eye behind and arrived. It used branches as an excuse to change direction in mid-air rather than as somewhere to land. Its arms came around like something dropped from a height onto a roof. Three strikes on the bear's ribs: two deflected, one absorbed by a body that did not appear to consider itself a target so much as a wall with opinions.

It could also throw the fur. Not as anything anyone would call an attack — a few strands came off the shoulders on a backhand swing, crossed the space, and one found the bear's open eye.

The bear was four metres and had clearly decided that moving was somebody else's work. Sepia coat, matted, scarred, and a V cut into the chest that held a slow turning light, unhurried, like something glimpsed through deep water. It did not chase. Every time the ape came inside reach, arms the width of Qian's torso came around and made the reaching expensive.

And it was not using what it had.

Liu named it, which meant it was real and not a hope.

"Gravity Control," Liu said, answering the thought before any of them had said it aloud. "Innate Soul Skill — a bounded field of weight. Against anything that charges at that bear, it is not a defence. It is a sentence."

Qian had read it once, the way you read the name of something you would rather not meet, and he had expected worse. The clearing had been written in that grammar for half an hour. The ape went in three times and came off the ground twice, and got up each time and went in again, which was not the behaviour of an animal being crushed — that was the behaviour of an animal being *herded*.

So: the field was real, and the bear was spending only what it needed to keep the ape off that ground.

Not one pound more.

"They're both ruined," Liu said, quietly, in the tone of somebody reading a report rather than describing a fight. On the ape's chest, three long lines ran down from the shoulder, laid on it by those arms, the gold fur clumped dark in the seams. The bear had one eye sealed with blood and gold hair stuck along its neck where the fur had found it. Neither animal was going to win anything today. They were both going to lose what they had left.

And Qian, who had been carrying the resentment about being the useless one, found the thing he was actually for.

"They're not biting," he said.

Both of them looked at him.

"Look at them. The ape could go for the throat — it's *right there*, the bear can't see that side. It's not going for it. It's hitting the ribs, the shoulders, the arms. And the bear isn't turning away from the eye, it's just not moving. Nobody in that clearing is trying to win quickly. They're trying not to lose."

"That's not how soul beasts fight," Liu said.

"No," Lan agreed. "It isn't."

Silence, except for the impact of it.

"Both species," Lan said, "are amiable. Neither one of them starts anything, and neither one of them keeps a grudge — that is the entire reason nobody bothers to hunt them, and it is the whole reason I have read about them instead of worrying about them." He was not looking at either of them. He was looking at the ground between them, and his hands had gone still in a way Qian had never seen before, which was the only thing in the whole morning that genuinely frightened him. "So one of two things is true. Either the guides are wrong—"

"They're not," Liu said.

"They're not," Lan agreed. "So the other one is true. Something out here is lying to them about what's worth fighting for."

---

Lan went wide, alone, unhurried, one hand keeping both strands loose so he could pull them either way, and he came around the flank at a distance that put the fight in the bear's attention rather than himself, because the bear was the one that could end him.

Ten metres out he changed angle. Then again. He was not looking at the animal; he had already got everything the animal was willing to tell him, and the animal had been telling him one thing for half an hour without pause: *I cannot leave.*

The third angle was the right one.

There was something between its legs.

Small. Almost comic in a clearing that size. A plant perhaps thirty centimetres from base to top, dark enough purple to read as a hole in the light, with a head that spread like a parasol and a stem that ran straight into soil. Beside it a large tree had been broken off — not cut, not decayed, *snapped*, at a height that said two things of considerable weight had used it as an argument.

The plant had been under the tree. The tree was gone. The plant was not.

Lan came back the way he had come, and did not speak for a while, which was the most frightened any of them ever heard him be.

"What?" Liu said.

"It's an herb."

"That's your news."

"It's an immortal herb," Lan said. "There's a category. I went through the documents on immortal herbs — all of them, because they were the only thing in that library nobody else wanted. From the head and the colour, a thousand-year maturity, and I'd rather be wrong about that than right. For a soul beast that isn't a supplement." He put the next part carefully, and Qian would remember the care later, the way you remember somebody choosing their words in front of you. "It is a thousand years. Consumed. Not trained toward. It's the one thing out here they cannot get any other way."

They looked at the clearing again with that in mind, and the clearing changed.

Qian heard himself say the word before he decided to, and it came out flat, like a diagnosis: "It's a stalemate."

"It's a stalemate," Lan said. "And it has been one since before we got here, and we walked into the middle of it thinking we were the hunters."

Neither of them could take it. That had been true the whole time and it had simply been too embarrassing to say out loud. The ape could probably kill the bear, eventually, at a cost — and the moment it picked the plant up it would have to *use* it, and using it meant being an ordinary frightened animal for as long as the dose took hold. The bear did the same arithmetic from the other side and came to the same place: it could hold that ground until it died. It could not leave that ground to eat.

"They're not fighting over a prize," Qian said, and his own voice sounded far away. "They're fighting to be the one that gets to be helpless."

"Yes."

"If we walk away, they die anyway."

"Yes."

"And if we walk in, we're the third thing in there, and both of them have been waiting for one."

Liu looked at him sideways. "You've been thinking like that all morning?"

"Someone has to," Qian said.

Nobody answered that, which was worse than being teased about it.

---

What they did instead was watch, because that was free, and the numbers did not stop arriving while they waited.

Two thousand-year kills would put Dorm333 somewhere it had never been on the board. Qian ran it twice and got the same answer both times and did not enjoy either one. And the shape of the whole round sat there in the middle of that clearing, exposed for once: the entire competition was teams walking toward the things they could not beat, and the ones who survived were the ones who had worked that out before the first swing.

Somewhere else in this forest, better than they were, Dorm336 was doing the same maths with a Rank39 in it. Qian did not say so out loud. He thought about the fact that Lan did not need to be told, and about how Lan kept looking at the space between two broken trunks, and he understood that their commanding teammate had just found something he could not price.

Neither animal moved.

"We should go," Lan said.

"We should *leave*," Qian said, "that's not the same thing and you know it."

"I know exactly what the difference is." Lan kept his voice down, unhurried, and it was the asking and not ordering in it that made Qian shut up — the fact that Lan Xuanyu, who had carried half of this round on his own arithmetic, was telling them plainly that he did not have the answer yet. "If we go in there, we take a thing off that ground, and then somebody has to decide what it's for. I'm not deciding that while two of us have nothing left to run on and I understand about a tenth of what's actually in that clearing."

"Then we come back," Liu said.

"We come back knowing more." Lan took them backwards, one pace at a time, keeping the grass slack until there was nothing for it to snap tight on. "We watch until the part I don't understand is small enough to survive being wrong about. That's the whole plan. Nobody's touching anything and nobody's promising anything, including me."

Behind them, unhurried, unhealed, two amiable beasts went on fighting a fight neither of them had started, over a plant neither of them could eat.

And the three of them crept back into the trees to think about it, which was, Lan was beginning to understand, the most dangerous thing in the forest a person could do while something they wanted was still in front of them.

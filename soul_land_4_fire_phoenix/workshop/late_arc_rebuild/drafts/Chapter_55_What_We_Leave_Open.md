# Chapter 55 — What We Leave Open

By midday, the Purple Zoysia had become a fourth presence in Dorm333’s formation.

It did not speak.

It did not move.

But every time the wind came from behind, Qian felt the forest notice it.

Lan kept the plant nested inside a loose cup of Blue Silver Grass at his side. The grass did not squeeze the roots. A crushed plant would be a broken condition before it became anything useful. Damp soil still clung beneath its dark-purple cap, and a faint bitter-green scent followed them through the trees no matter how carefully Lan folded leaves around it.

Liu stopped beside a split trunk.

His spear stayed low.

“Something has been pacing us.”

Qian looked into the undergrowth and saw nothing except ferns, old rainwater, and the long green shadows between roots.

“Human?”

“Too light.” Liu touched one print at the edge of a muddy patch. It had three narrow toes and a soft center that did not quite make a paw. “It circles when the wind shifts.”

Lan crouched without putting the Zoysia down.

“Looking for the plant.”

Qian’s hand went to the Coin of Summoning.

“Then it can keep looking somewhere else.”

A rustle answered from the brush ahead.

The creature that stepped out was not large. Its back barely reached Qian’s knee. Dark fur lay flat along its spine, and pale whiskers moved around a narrow snout that did not stop tasting the air. It had the hungry, deliberate stillness of an animal that had learned not to waste strength on things it could not eat.

Its eyes settled on Lan’s hand.

Then it lowered itself.

Liu shifted one foot forward.

“No,” Lan said.

Liu glanced at him.

“It is not attacking yet.”

“It will.”

“Maybe.” Lan’s gaze stayed on the creature. “But it came for a scent. We do not need to turn that into a kill because it is convenient.”

Qian had expected an order to open the Gate. He had expected Liu to make the next movement quick enough that he would only have to watch it happen.

The coin was warm against his palm.

He could try the other thing now. He could reach toward the animal with the thin, careful piece of intent he had learned to make. Not a command. Not even a sentence. A warning, perhaps. A picture of empty ground and no food.

The thought made his stomach tighten.

The Gold Silk Ape had answered because it had seen an opening it wanted. This creature had not asked him anything. It had only followed hunger.

“I could tell it we are not prey,” Qian said.

Lan’s eyes stayed on the narrow-snouted animal.

“Would it understand that?”

Qian looked at the creature’s low body, the way it watched the Zoysia rather than their faces.

“No.”

“Then do not make it carry a fear you cannot explain.”

The answer did not make the coin lighter. It made Qian lower it.

Instead, Lan held out one palm.

“Water.”

A small sphere rose above his fingers. He drew a little mud from the Zoysia’s root ball into it, not enough to expose the plant, only enough to cloud the clear water with the smell the creature had followed.

Liu understood first.

“The gully.”

A rain-cut channel ran downhill to their left. Its stones were slick, its current shallow but moving. Lan sent the sphere drifting over it. The moment it reached the water, he broke it.

Purple-stained earth spread across the surface and rolled downhill in a thin, wavering line.

The creature’s head snapped toward the gully.

For a moment, it looked back at Lan.

Lan did not move.

The animal chose the scent.

It crossed two paces toward the gully, then stopped and looked back.

Liu’s spear tip lifted a fraction.

The creature had not become stupid because a better smell appeared. It was measuring the distance between the water and the small, dark plant in Lan’s hand. Its body gathered itself, ready to test whether the three children would give ground before it committed.

Lan did not give it the opening.

Golden grass slipped low over the stones, not striking the creature, only brushing the dead leaves at its feet toward the gully. The motion was enough to make the animal turn its head again. The purple-stained water was already moving farther downhill.

Hunger chose for it a second time.

It slipped through the brush and vanished after the water.

Qian let out the breath he had been holding.

“That worked.”

“It bought a few minutes,” Liu said.

“That is what worked means in this forest,” Lan told him.

They crossed the stones before the purple-scented water could carry too far. Liu went first, finding each foothold with the spear shaft. Qian followed with the coin closed in his fist. Lan came last, holding the real plant above the splash line.

On the far bank, Qian looked back once.

The gully had swallowed the false trail.

“The ape will not smell that and think it is us?”

Lan’s expression changed only slightly.

“We do not know what it will smell when it wakes.”

The answer hurt because it did not try to repair itself.

Qian stared at the dark soil under the leaves around the Zoysia.

“We left it there.”

“We left it sleeping,” Lan said. “There is a difference.”

“Is there?”

Liu watched the far bank.

“If we had stayed until it woke, something else would have found all four of us. Or the plant. Or both.”

Qian did not argue.

Lan tightened the grass around the root ball just enough to keep the soil from slipping.

“We keep the terms we can keep,” he said. “We do not make up the rest because waiting feels kinder.”

Ahead, the forest sloped down into thicker shade.

They went that way.


The running reached Luo before the runner did.

He lifted one hand.

Song stopped with the shield half-raised. Yan folded the faint edge of his wings back beneath his shoulders and listened.

One set of feet.

Too fast for a careful route. Too uneven for someone who had chosen speed.

Then a second rhythm behind it.

Then a third.

Luo’s fingers touched the wet stone beside the dry watercourse.

“Three following,” he said. “One above the right bank. Two lower. The first one is hurt.”

Song looked at the narrow channel ahead. It ran beneath a tilted shelf of stone, then split around a thick curtain of roots. A person could pass through the left split if they turned sideways. A shield could not. The right side was wider but opened directly onto the higher bank.

“Exit?” he asked.

“Left split joins a lower wash after twenty steps,” Luo said. “The ground drops hard after that. One person can take it. A group cannot use it quickly.”

Yan looked at the root curtain.

“And us?”

“Main channel. If Song holds here. If no one breaks the shelf.”

The runner came through the ferns before anyone could say more.

She was moving on training rather than strength. Her uniform was torn at one shoulder. Mud streaked one side of her face, and a dark mark had formed beneath her lower lip where she had either fallen or been struck. She took three more steps into the channel before she saw them.

Her body jerked to a stop.

Yan knew the lift of that chin.

Even exhausted, even frightened, Ye Lingtong looked as though anger had arrived before she had.

“Yan Shuo?”

Her voice was rough.

For a heartbeat, recognition made Ye look younger than the forest had allowed anyone to be. Relief crossed her face—not because any classmate would have done, but because it was Yan. Then the relief tightened into anger at herself for showing it.

The private, stupid warmth of seeing him sat beneath the fear, worse than fear because it had no place in a channel with three pursuers. Yan was the bright, handsome Fire Phoenix boy she had spent years measuring herself against—quick-minded, disciplined, and now the class monitor everyone watched. He had seen her lose, seen her hurt, and never made either thing entertainment. The forest had chosen the one person before whom she least wanted to arrive bleeding and afraid.

“Move,” she said. “There are three.”

Behind her, something scraped across stone on the upper bank.

Yan did not ask whether the three had started it, whether Ye had been separated by accident, or whether the people behind her had a reason they would call clean. The answers would not change the channel before them.

Song set the shield at the mouth of the channel.

“Ye Lingtong,” he said. Recognition arrived a beat late through mud, blood, and the broken rhythm of her running.

“Same class,” Yan said.

Song’s eyes went once to Ye, then to the root split.

“We do not take on a fourth person we cannot get out.”

“We do not have to,” Yan said. “We give her the exit.”

Luo’s hand flattened against the stone.

“The left split holds one runner. The main channel holds us if Song keeps the mouth. That is all the ground offers.”

That was enough to decide.

A pale strike cut down from the right bank. Song caught it on the shield. White sparks scattered over wet stone. His shoulder drove backward half a step, then held.

Luo’s head turned toward the root curtain.

“Ye,” he said, not looking at her, “left split. Three steps on the darker stone. Then sideways through the roots. Do not touch the pale bark.”

Ye looked from him to the narrow opening.

“What about them?”

“They are not your problem for the next ten seconds,” Song said.

A shape moved above the shelf.

A second shape landed farther back without showing a face.

Yan stepped beside Song.

Red-gold warmth gathered low around his feet. He did not let it climb the walls. He did not let it open above the trees. The heat followed the wet channel’s edge and stopped in a shallow line before the root curtain, bright enough to make the path visible and narrow enough that it did not turn the watercourse into a beacon.

Ye stared at it.

“You can fly,” she said.

“So can mistakes,” Yan answered.

Her eyes flashed despite everything.

That was enough to tell him she was still herself.

Another pale strike came lower this time, aimed beneath Song’s shield rim. Luo caught the movement before it arrived.

“Down.”

Song dropped the shield edge. The strike hit stone and burst into harmless white fragments against the red-gold line.

The answer from above was not another strike.

A loose fist-sized stone came down from the shelf, then a second. Someone was testing the root curtain’s support, looking for a way to make the narrow exit close by itself.

Luo’s hand snapped toward the left wall.

“Song, one step in. Yan, keep heat off the roots.”

Song shifted before the third stone fell. The shield rose over the pale bark, taking the impact that would have struck the root curtain. The shelf groaned. A web of wet earth loosened above them.

Yan’s flame wanted to burn the falling leaves clear. Instead he lowered it until the heat only dried the slick stone under Song’s feet. The shield stopped sliding. The root curtain shivered but held.

The stones stopped.

Not because the people above had failed.

Because the easy way through had become expensive enough to question.

Yan felt the larger answer waiting behind his ribs.

It would have been easy to let it out. One hard flare would have filled the channel with heat. It might have sent the three figures away.

It might also have sent Ye into the wrong split, cracked the root shelf above Song, and told every listening thing in the forest exactly how much fire waited inside one boy’s hands.

He kept the warmth low.

“Go,” Luo said.

Ye moved.

Her first step landed on the dark stone. Her second almost did. Her injured leg buckled as the channel slanted beneath her.

Yan caught her forearm once.

Not to pull her toward them.

Only to turn her weight toward the roots Luo had named.

Ye tore free as soon as she had balance again.

“I had it.”

“You do now,” Yan said.

She disappeared sideways through the root curtain.

The three shapes at the upper bank did not rush the channel.

One came close enough that Song could hear breath behind the shield. Another moved along the right edge, testing whether the wider path would give it an angle past Yan’s low boundary. Luo shifted his feet toward the root curtain before the attempt fully formed.

“There is no fast line through,” he said.

The words were not a threat.

They were a fact.

Song’s forearm trembled once beneath the shield. He made it still.

The nearest figure stepped back.

For a few breaths, nobody moved.

Then the pressure changed. Stone clicked farther up the bank. A branch bent, released, and stopped making sound. The three presences did not vanish. They simply stopped offering the one easy pursuit they had brought into the channel.

Yan waited until Luo’s hand lifted from the ground.

“Not gone,” Luo said.

“No,” Song agreed.

“But not entering.”

Yan let the red-gold line fade before it could become more than it needed to be.

“Then we do not chase.”

Song pulled the shield free of the stone.

“We were not planning to.”

“I was making sure.”

“That was almost funny.”

Luo looked toward the roots.

“Ye will be at the lower fork,” he said.

Song looked once at the root curtain she had passed through.

“Then we hold the line long enough for her to stay ahead.”


The lower wash was narrower than it had looked from the channel.

Roots crossed it at shoulder height. A spill of pale stones led away beneath a canopy thick enough that the upper bank could no longer see directly into it. Ye stood at the fork with one hand against the wall, catching her breath without admitting she needed to.

When Song, Luo, and Yan emerged, she looked at the three of them as if she had not decided whether to be grateful or angry.

“You should not have done that,” she said.

Song leaned the shield against one knee.

“Probably.”

Ye’s gaze snapped toward him.

He shrugged once.

“You were about to get hit. We had an exit. Those are the facts I have.”

“You do not know who they are.”

“No,” Luo said. “Neither do you, apparently.”

Her mouth tightened.

Yan looked at the split routes. The left one climbed through ferns and then disappeared beneath low branches. The right followed the wash, slower and darker, with enough stone cover that a single runner could lose an angle if she did not panic.

“You were with Lu and Chang?” he asked.

Ye did not answer at first.

Then she looked away.

“I lost them.”

The words came out sharper than a plea.

“We were moving together. Then someone came from above and someone else cut the ground behind us. Lu told me to run because they were already on him.” Her hand closed around the torn edge of her sleeve. “I thought I could turn back after I lost them. I could not.”

Not killed.

Not safe.

Only lost.

Yan had known Ye long enough to recognize both parts she had omitted: she hated that running had been the thing she did correctly, and she hated still more that he had seen her do it.

He did not give either shame a name.

Luo pointed toward the right-hand wash.

“That line keeps cover longer. It also takes you farther from high ground. The left route is faster if no one is waiting above it.”

Ye followed his hand.

“You are telling me to leave?”

“We are telling you what the routes cost,” Song said. “The choice is yours.”

For a moment, the old Ye Lingtong would have heard that as an insult. Yan could see the answer rise in her face—the chin, the sharp breath, the need to prove that she did not require anyone’s map.

Then it changed.

She looked at the torn edge of her sleeve. At the mud on her knee. At the place where the pursuers had stopped being visible but had not stopped existing.

“The wash,” she said.

Luo nodded.

“Keep your weight close to the right wall. Do not step where the moss shines blue.”

Ye started down the route.

After three steps, she looked back at Yan.

“I did not ask you to save me.”

The words would have been easier if they were for a stranger. With Yan, they carried anger, gratitude, and the old private wish that he had seen her at her best instead of like this.

“I know.”

He did not turn the answer into a joke, a claim, or a debt.

That seemed to unsettle her more than an answer about debts would have.

She turned away before he could see what she did with it.

No one followed her.

No one asked her to promise anything.

The wash took her into shadow.


By the time the forest grew dark enough to make every root look like a waiting body, the creature from the gully had found the last of the muddy scent and stopped following Dorm333.

Liu knew because the silence behind them had changed.

“It is gone.”

Qian glanced at the grass-wrapped Zoysia.

“For now?”

“For now,” Liu said.

Lan did not correct either of them.

Qian remembered the first thing he had thought when he saw the Zoysia: five ranks. A number that did not belong to him, a shortcut that would not leave the simulation, something bright enough to make every other danger look smaller.

Now it was only a plant in wet soil.

Only a condition someone hungry had agreed to.

Only a weight that kept making strangers, animals, and even his own hand choose differently around it.

The plant still lay against Lan’s side, small enough to fit in two hands and heavy enough to change where three children could walk. Somewhere behind them, a wounded ape slept beneath roots with a skull-bone light buried at its brow. Somewhere else, an animal had followed the false trail down wet stone because hunger had made it believe in the easiest answer.

Lan looked ahead.

“The route stays open,” he said. “That does not mean it stays empty.”

Qian touched the coin at his palm.

“What does that mean for us?”

Liu answered before Lan could.

“It means we keep moving.”

On the lower wash, Yan listened until he could no longer hear Ye’s steps.

Song brought the shield back to his arm.

Luo took the darker route.

Behind them, the higher ground held its silence.

Ahead, the forest did not promise anything.

But it had stopped deciding every line for them.

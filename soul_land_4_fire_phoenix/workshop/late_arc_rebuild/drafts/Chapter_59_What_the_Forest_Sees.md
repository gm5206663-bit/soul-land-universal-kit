# Chapter 59 — What the Forest Sees

The older trees did not make the forest empty.

They only made its empty places easier to notice.

Qian saw one through a break in the roots ahead: a strip of pale stone where rain had washed the leaf mold away. It curved down between two fallen trunks and opened into a shallow basin of slate. Morning had reached that basin before it reached the ground under the trees. The stone held a weak, colorless shine.

Liu stopped at the edge of the roots.

“Too open,” he said.

Lan adjusted the grass cup against his chest. The Purple Zoysia sat inside it with the dry leaf litter still tucked around the root ball. The plant no longer dripped. That did not make it light.

The six-day term had not shortened because rain had made carrying it difficult.

“We need to cross it?” Qian asked.

Liu pointed with the butt of his spear. The dry cut behind them narrowed into packed earth and root walls. Ahead, the slate basin curved toward a seam in the hillside where trees grew farther apart.

“The cut ends there,” he said. “We can climb back into the wet ground, or take the stone.”

Qian looked at the stone again.

The rain had left it clean enough to show every dark mark across its surface: old cracks, damp seams, a scattering of loose shale. Nothing moved on it.

That was what bothered him.

He closed his eyes for one breath.

His Spirit Sea did not give him a map. It never had. It gave him pressure where there should have been none. A held breath behind the upper boulder. Weight on a root above the basin. The small, deliberate stillness of people who had stopped moving because they wanted someone else to cross first.

“There are people,” he said.

Liu did not ask how many.

Lan’s gaze traveled over the stone without hurrying.

“Where?”

“One near the upper boulder.” Qian pointed without lifting his arm fully. “Maybe another by that dead trunk. I cannot tell if there is a third. Someone is trying to be quiet.”

A voice answered from the boulder before either of the other boys could speak.

“Then stop pretending you do not see us.”

A child stepped out from behind the stone, hands open and empty at first glance. Mud had dried along the hem of the uniform. Another figure remained near the dead trunk, half-hidden by the pale bark.

Neither wore a familiar academy mark Qian could make out from this distance.

The first figure looked past Liu’s spear.

At the grass cup.

“What are you carrying?”

Lan did not pull the Zoysia farther under his arm. He did not display it either.

“Something we are responsible for,” he said.

The other figure came out from behind the trunk. His eyes went to the dark-purple cap through the loose grass, then quickly back to Lan’s face.

“Responsible,” he repeated. “That is a careful answer.”

“It is the answer you get.”

No one raised a weapon.

That made Qian’s palm grow damp around the Coin of Summoning.

The first stranger took one step down the slate. “We saw the overhang below. Fresh roots disturbed. Then we saw where your tracks left the water. We thought there might be a team here.”

“You found one,” Liu said.

The stranger’s attention moved to the spear, then to the narrow cut behind Dorm333.

“We are not looking for trouble.”

Qian had heard people say that before a difficult practice match. It usually meant they were trying to decide whether trouble would answer them.

“Then do not make it,” Lan said.

The second stranger’s gaze returned to the Zoysia.

“Is it a plant?”

Lan was quiet for a moment.

“It is not something we are trading.”

The first stranger’s mouth tightened.

“No one asked to trade.”

“You asked what it was.”

A dry leaf turned once across the slate between them. It made a tiny sound and stopped in a crack.

Liu shifted his feet without looking down. Qian knew what that meant now. Not attack. Ground.

The spear butt touched a root beside Qian’s shoe.

One tap.

Then another, farther left.

Qian followed the line with his eyes. A narrow cleft ran beneath the edge of the basin, hidden by a fallen slab and a spill of thin shale. It led toward the hillside seam Liu had marked. The route was too low to use quickly. It was also the only line that did not leave their backs open to the boulder.

The first stranger noticed Liu looking.

“You do not have to run,” she said.

“We are walking,” Liu answered.

She took another step down the stone.

The second stranger did too.

Qian’s sense tightened around the basin. The two in front were obvious now. The third pressure was not. It came and went behind the upper root wall, always just far enough that he could not tell whether it belonged to another person or to a wet branch settling in the wind.

He looked at the Coin.

One summon could fill the basin. A Ground Fire Dragon Lizard would make enough noise to turn every uncertain shape into an answer. It would also place a creature in open stone with human children on both sides of it.

It would make the plant look worth fighting over.

Qian kept the Coin closed.

“There is someone else,” he said quietly.

Lan heard him.

His face did not change, but Blue Silver Grass slid from the cuff of his sleeve and disappeared into the leaf litter near the hidden cleft.

“We do not know that,” Liu said, loud enough for the strangers to hear.

“We know enough to leave room,” Lan replied.

The second stranger laughed once, without humor.

“You have a lot of rules for three people carrying something through a forest.”

Lan looked at him.

“So do you. You just have not said yours yet.”

For the first time, the stranger’s attention moved away from the Zoysia completely.

The first one looked toward the root wall above the basin.

Qian saw it too late: not a third person, but a thin cord of vine looped around a low branch. Someone had tied it there before the rain and pulled it tight across the hidden cleft.

Not enough to hurt someone badly.

Enough to make the slate slide beneath a running foot.

Liu’s spear moved.

He did not thrust it at either child. The white shaft slid beneath the vine at the edge of the stone and lifted.

The line snapped free.

The branch above it sprang back. A fan of wet leaves and loose shale came down across the cleft with a rasping rush.

Qian flinched.

Lan’s grass caught the fallen slab before it could close the gap entirely. Blue-green strands tightened around a root on the far side, holding the slab a handspan above the ground. Not a bridge. A space.

“Now,” Liu said.

Lan moved first with the Zoysia held high and close. He did not jump. He lowered one foot through the opening, felt the rock take it, then passed under the trapped slab sideways so no leaf or stone brushed the root ball.

Qian followed.

The slate above him shivered against Lan’s grass. The open basin vanished behind the fallen stone, leaving only a gray strip of light at his back.

A hand caught the edge of the slab from the other side.

Not hard enough to pull it down.

Hard enough to test whether the boys would stop.

Liu was still on the basin side.

Qian’s breath caught.

Liu did not look toward the hand. He set the spear shaft into the narrow crack beside the slab, used the strength in his shoulders, and twisted.

The old root below the stone gave with a dry crack.

The slab settled—not onto anyone, but into the space between the two groups with a final scrape of shale.

The hand withdrew.

Liu came through the last opening as dust from the dry stone drifted around his knees.

For a moment, the three of them stood in the dim hillside cleft with their backs against cold rock.

On the other side of the slab, the first stranger said, “That was unnecessary.”

Lan looked at the thin line of light beneath the stone.

“So was the vine.”

No answer came at once.

Then the second stranger’s voice sounded farther away.

“We only wanted to know what you had.”

Liu rested the spear across his knees.

“Knowing is not free,” he said.

The silence after that did not sound like defeat.

It sounded like people deciding whether a narrow, broken opening was worth squeezing through after someone had shown them the cost.

Lan waited until the pressure at Qian’s senses had loosened. Then he adjusted the grass around the root ball. One dry leaf had shifted loose during the crossing. He tucked it back in with the same care he might have used on a scraped knee.

Qian watched him.

“They saw it,” he said.

“They saw enough,” Lan replied.

“Does that change anything?”

Lan looked down the cleft. It bent away from the slate basin and disappeared between two old roots where no one could see more than the next few paces.

“It changes what we know,” he said. “Not what we owe them.”

Liu took the lead.

Behind them, no one forced the fallen slab aside.


The ridge above the spillway did not remember the rain the same way the watercourse had.

Here, wind had taken most of it.

It moved through the upper leaves in long dry breaths, carrying the smell of stone heated briefly by a sun still hidden behind cloud. Song walked with the shield close to his body so it would not catch the low branches. Luo moved ahead where the ridge narrowed, stopping now and then to touch a scuffed patch of bark or watch the way loose needles collected along a root.

Yan kept his hands in his sleeves.

The warmth in them had faded hours ago. That was good. A red-gold line on wet stone could disappear into rain. A red-gold line on dry needles would be a message.

Luo stopped beside a broken stump.

“Three behind,” he said.

Song turned enough to look past Yan.

“Same people?”

“I do not know.”

Luo pointed down the ridge. The path there divided around a long seam in the rock. The left branch stayed under low branches but pinched until Song would have to turn the shield sideways. The right rose across an open shelf where wind had cleared every leaf away.

“Someone has taken the open side twice,” Luo said. “Then stopped before the shelf. The other prints keep to our line.”

Yan looked down at the disturbed needles.

“They are following?”

“They are measuring,” Luo said. “There is a difference until there is not.”

A voice came from behind the next trunk.

“We can hear you talking.”

Song sighed through his nose.

“That saves us some guessing.”

Three children came around the trunk in a loose line. They were close enough for Yan to see exhaustion in the set of their shoulders and the damp mud still dark on their boots. The one in front carried no weapon in hand. The boy at the rear did. The girl in the middle watched the shield first, then Yan’s sleeves.

“You are Yan Shuo,” she said.

Yan did not deny it.

“The fire from the ravine,” she continued. “And the red-gold mark near the wash. We saw it yesterday.”

Song’s grip settled more firmly on the shield handle.

Luo did not take his eyes off the ridge.

The girl glanced toward the split path.

“We have been trying to get out of the low ground since morning. You know the forest better than we do.”

“No,” Luo said.

The answer came so quickly that even Song looked at him.

Luo pointed to the left branch. “We know that route will pinch around the seam. We know the open shelf can be watched from the next rise. That is not the same as knowing what waits after either one.”

The boy at the rear shifted his weight.

“We are not asking you to promise anything. Just let us move with you.”

Song looked at the space between the trees. Three people behind his shield. Yan’s heat visible at the front. Luo walking ahead where he had to read every root before anyone else used it.

It was an easy picture to want.

Someone else made the way. Someone else took the first risk. You stayed close enough to use the answer after it had been paid for.

“And when the path stops being safe?” Song asked.

The front boy frowned.

“Then we help.”

“How?”

The question was not loud.

It held the ridge still anyway.

The boy looked toward Yan.

“He can fly if something goes wrong.”

Yan felt Song’s shield shift between them by half an inch.

Not protective of Yan.

Protective of the line they had made together.

Luo crouched near the rock seam. He brushed aside a layer of dry needles with two fingers. Beneath them, a crack crossed from the open shelf to the narrow branch. Rainwater had entered the crack during the night. Wind had dried only the top.

“The shelf is undercut,” he said. “If six people step onto it, it gives. If someone puts heat into that seam, it gives faster.”

The girl in the middle looked at Yan.

“You can keep it from giving.”

“For how long?” Yan asked.

She did not answer.

He could have.

A stronger flame through the wet fracture could fuse the outer stone for a few breaths. Wings could carry him over the open shelf to look for a better line. He could return with an answer too fast for the others to question.

But Song would still be on the ridge beneath a shield. Luo would still have to read ground already changed by heat. And the three strangers would still be standing behind them, waiting to discover whether Yan’s strength made them safer or merely made them willing to ask for more.

“We cannot lead you through a route we have not paid for ourselves,” Yan said.

The front boy’s expression hardened.

“You are already going that way.”

“No,” Luo said. “We are choosing one of two bad lines. That is different.”

The boy at the rear raised the weapon in his hand a fraction.

Song stepped forward.

The shield came up, not striking anyone, its broad face filling the narrow part of the ridge between the groups.

“No,” Song said.

One word.

Not an accusation.

Not a challenge.

A boundary.

The rear boy stopped.

For a breath, Yan thought the strangers might try to make the choice uglier for all of them.

Then Luo rose and pointed not at either branch, but at a thin crease between a boulder and the hillside. Ferns had grown over it, their fronds dry on top and dark with retained dampness beneath.

“There,” he said to Song. “The stone behind the ferns is solid. The gap takes the shield if you turn it flat. It opens below the seam. Not wide enough for six people to use without pulling the bank down.”

Song looked at the gap.

“Can we?”

“One at a time.”

Yan moved beside the boulder. A dead branch had wedged itself across the upper edge of the crease. It would scrape loose as Song passed beneath it.

“Which part holds?” he asked.

Luo pointed to a knot of pale wood near the trunk.

“Dry that. Not the rock.”

Yan placed two fingers against the knot.

The heat he used was small enough that the others could have missed it if they were not watching him. Dampness drew out of the wood. The knot tightened against the boulder instead of slipping free.

Song set the shield on its side and slid into the fern-covered gap first. His shoulders pressed stone. The rim of the shield caught once, then passed through when he shifted the weight exactly as Luo had told him.

“Clear,” he said from below.

Luo went next. He did not look back at the strangers as he lowered himself through the narrow opening.

Yan stayed at the top for one final breath.

The girl in the middle looked at the gap, then at the split ridge beyond it.

“We could follow,” she said.

“You could,” Yan replied.

The front boy’s eyes narrowed. “Are you telling us not to?”

“I am telling you it is not our route once we leave it.”

He did not say that they would be punished if they tried.

He did not say they were weak for waiting.

He did not make a promise about the ground beyond the ferns.

Song’s hand appeared from below the gap.

Yan took it only long enough to lower his weight past the stone. His wings did not open. The dried knot held. The branch stayed where it was until all three had passed beneath it.

Above, the strangers remained on the ridge.

No one attacked.

No one thanked them.

That was all right.

At the base of the hillside, Song pulled the shield back onto his arm. The fern cover closed behind them, leaving the ridge as a thin strip of pale sky through the roots.

“They will choose something,” he said.

“Yes,” Yan replied.

Luo listened to the wind on the other side of the seam.

“But not from inside our formation.”

They moved on before the strangers could decide that following was easier than making a road of their own.


The cleft beneath the slate basin ran farther than Qian expected.

By the time it opened, the air had changed from dry stone to leaf mold and old shade. Liu stepped out first beneath a tangle of roots where the ground sloped away from the basin. Lan followed with the Zoysia still held against him. Qian came last, listening for the scrape of stone behind them.

Nothing followed.

That did not make the strangers harmless.

It only meant the space between them belonged to both sides now.

Lan knelt beside a patch of packed earth and checked the grass cup. The root ball had held. No new water had found it. No loose shale had cut the leaves.

Qian watched his hands.

“They did not even know what it was,” he said.

“They knew it mattered,” Lan answered.

“That was enough.”

Lan looked up.

“Yes.”

Liu stood at the edge of the roots, spear low, not watching the plant and not watching the blocked cleft behind them. He was watching the forest beyond the slope, where the trees opened into a hundred places someone could decide to stand.

“We keep moving,” he said.

Lan rose.

The Zoysia disappeared beneath the loose grass and leaves again. Not hidden from the team that carried it. Not offered to every pair of eyes that might want to price it.


Below the ridge, Yan, Song, and Luo followed the solid stone until the fern line gave way to older forest.

The wind could no longer reach them cleanly there.

Song let out a breath he had been holding too long.

“They knew your name,” he said.

Yan looked at his sleeves.

“They knew a name.”

“They knew enough to ask you to make their route safe.”

Yan did not answer immediately.

Luo did.

“They will ask someone else if they find a different answer.”

Song gave him a sideways look.

“That is not comforting.”

“It is not meant to be.”

Yan almost smiled.

Then he looked through the trees at the direction the ridge had taken. Somewhere above, three other children would decide whether to cross open stone, turn back toward low ground, or make a route no one had given them.

He hoped they chose carefully.

He did not owe them a fire bright enough to choose for them.

The three continued beneath the leaves: Song with the shield close, Luo reading the quiet ground, Yan keeping the warmth in his hands where it could still belong to all of them.

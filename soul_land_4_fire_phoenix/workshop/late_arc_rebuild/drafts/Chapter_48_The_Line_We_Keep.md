# Chapter 48 — The Line We Keep

Yin Tianfan did not begin with the battle.

He began with the number still fading from the testing device.

Five hundred and five.

The light had already thinned from the display, but Lan Xuanyu could still feel the change inside his own mind. The room had edges it had not possessed before. The hum of the wall panels had a direction. Dust moved in the slant of afternoon light. Even Teacher Yin’s silence seemed to have weight.

Qian Lei and Liu Feng had been sent out with Ji Hongbin before the door shut.

For once, Qian had not tried to bargain his way back in.

Yin sat opposite Lan with both hands folded over his stomach. The usual amusement had not left his face completely. It had only gone somewhere deeper.

“Back then,” he said, “I was determined to enter Shrek’s inner court.”

Lan waited.

“I was in the Space Command System. I had good results. Better than good, if I am being honest. I learned quickly, saw angles other people missed, and liked winning through the place no one had guarded.” Yin’s mouth moved without becoming a smile. “Some teachers thought that was talent. Some thought it was a problem. Naturally, I listened to the ones who praised me.”

Lan could imagine that too easily.

A battlefield did not give a commander clean choices. Teacher Yin had taught him that from the first simulator lesson—usually by arranging a problem so unfair that Lan only discovered its shape after losing to it.

“I thought results answered every question,” Yin continued. “If an ambush worked, it worked. If a feint made an opponent panic, then panic was their weakness. Why should I apologize for seeing it first?”

His finger touched the table once.

“The final examination in my sixth semester was a fleet battle. Three students to a group. Each one commanded a fleet, and the three fleets together became a joint formation. I was the strongest commander in mine. My teammates were weaker.”

The word weaker did not sound cruel when Yin said it.

That was worse.

“They obeyed everything,” he said. “When a coalition of three other groups formed against us, they held the places I told them to hold. They acted as decoys. They took damage while I used harassment, false retreats, interference, provocation, and ambushes to tear the coalition apart.”

Lan’s hands settled flat on his knees.

“Were they willing?” he asked.

Yin looked at him.

“At the time? Yes. They trusted me. That was the problem.”

The sentence remained between them.

Outside, someone crossed the corridor too quickly, footsteps bright against the quiet. Then the sound disappeared around the turn.

Yin leaned back.

“The other groups finally asked to negotiate. They had more fleets than us at the beginning. They had lost too much by then. They wanted enough score left that they would not leave the exam humiliated. They offered cooperation against the remaining students.”

“And you agreed,” Lan said.

“I said I agreed.”

Yin’s eyes did not leave his.

“Negotiation changes a field. Formations loosen. Weapons cool. People begin looking for a shared route instead of an opening to kill through. I waited until they did that.”

Lan knew the answer before Yin gave it.

“Then I destroyed them.”

The office seemed smaller.

Yin did not dress the choice in clever language. That, more than anything, made Lan listen.

“My teammates were furious,” he said. “They asked why I had broken my word after we had already won. I told them war had no limit on deception. I told them strength was the only proof worth respecting.”

His fingers closed.

“They said things I did not want to hear. They said I had used them, made them bleed for my plan, then made the plan uglier because I wanted everyone beneath me.”

Lan felt a cold line move through him.

“And?”

“And I was eighteen and proud and very good at remaining calm toward enemies.” Yin’s voice had become quiet. “I was terrible at being questioned by people who knew me. So I turned my fleet on theirs.”

Lan did not speak.

“They had already lost ships while following my commands. They could not stop me. In a moment, the whole simulated battlefield went quiet.”

For a few breaths, neither of them moved.

Lan had commanded Qian and Liu before. He had told them when to run, where to stand, when to trust a summon whose teeth could have reached them first. He had always thought first about whether the plan could work.

He had not liked the next question.

What did the plan ask of the people carrying it?


Song Yichen disliked offices without somewhere useful to stand.

A training room had a wall. A field had an enemy. Even a medical bay had a bed with a purpose.

This room had a long table, three chairs, and a window facing the academy’s inner courtyard. Song had chosen the chair nearest the door. His taped shoulder pulled whenever he shifted, which gave him another reason not to sit too comfortably.

Luo Haoran sat beside him with his elbows off the table by visible effort. His fingers kept tapping against his own knee—once, twice, then still. Yan Shuo sat across from them, straight-backed and quiet, copper-red hair subdued under the classroom light.

Mu Zhongtian stood at the end of the table with a recorder open in front of him. Ji Hongbin leaned against the wall beside the window.

“No speeches,” Mu said before Song could prepare himself for one. “No ceremony. Your ranking is already recorded. We need the route while it is still clear in your heads.”

That helped.

Song nodded. “Then ask.”

Mu’s gaze moved to him first.

“When the ridge took the impact, why did your shield hold?”

Song looked at his wrapped shoulder.

“It did not hold the whole impact.”

Mu waited.

Song glanced once at Luo, then Yan.

“Luo called the angle. Yan’s pressure made the Dragon turn its flame-side away from the fissure before its body hit. I only had to anchor the part that would have folded the crack in on us.”

“Only,” Luo said softly.

Song gave him a look. “You know what I mean.”

“I do. Teacher Mu might not.”

Mu’s expression did not change, but he tapped a note into the recorder.

“Explain it, then.”

Luo’s fingers stopped tapping.

“The heat created false pressure lines,” he said. “The rock shook from the Dragons, but the fire made the vibrations arrive early in some places and late in others. If Song had braced where the first impact seemed to be coming from, the shield would have caught empty force and the actual strike would have hit the seam behind us.”

“And Yan?” Mu asked.

The room tightened by a small degree.

Yan’s public numbers had already become something people tried not to stare at. Rank39. Spirit Sea. Three purple rings. A Fire Phoenix that had made fire-dragon beasts hesitate.

It was easy to make a story that had one answer.

Song hated easy stories.

“Yan kept their flame from reaching us cleanly,” he said. “That was not the same as stopping their bodies.”

Yan’s eyes moved to him.

Song continued before anyone could make the sentence polite.

“If the report says his fire did everything, it is wrong. If it says Luo found every line alone, it is wrong. If it says I stood there and got hit, it is also wrong. We used one opening. That is what happened.”

Mu Zhongtian looked from one of them to the next.

Ji Hongbin spoke from the wall.

“Would you use the same route for points?”

Song frowned. “Against two Ground Fire Scarlet Dragons?”

“Against any situation where one person can make a larger answer by placing the other two in a smaller one.”

The phrasing landed hard enough that Song’s shoulder seemed to ache more.

Across the table, Yan did not look away.

Song had known Yan long enough to recognize the stillness. It was not anger. Anger was easier. This was Yan hearing the real question before anyone gave it a gentler name.

Luo answered first.

“Not without shorter calls,” he said.

Mu looked at him.

“In the fissure, I had too much to read,” Luo continued. “Heat, pressure, falling stone, the Dragon’s movement, the line Song could still hold. I kept using full sentences because I wanted all of you to understand the reason. By the time I finished one, the next line was already changing.”

Song said, “You kept us alive.”

“I also nearly gave Yan a direction after it had stopped being safe.”

Yan’s hand rested on the table. It did not move.

Luo met his eyes.

“If I say hold, you hold. If I say cut, you stop feeding the seam. If I say out, no one argues about points until we are out.”

Yan’s expression softened by almost nothing.

“Agreed,” he said.

Song turned toward him. “That is not the whole answer.”

“No.” Yan’s voice stayed level. “It is not.”

For a moment, the room waited with Song.

Yan looked down at the recorder, then at the line of sunlight caught on the table’s edge.

“If I have an answer that makes the field easier for me and harder for both of you,” he said, “then I say what it costs before I use it. You can refuse the position. You can call me back.”

Song’s first response rose fast.

“You are not asking permission to fight.”

“No.”

“Then do not make it sound like that.”

Yan’s eyes returned to his.

“I am not.”

The answer was quiet.

It did not bend.

“I am saying no score is worth treating either of you as a thing I can spend because I am stronger. We survived the qualifier as three people. I will not learn the wrong lesson from it.”

Luo looked toward the window.

Outside, a group of younger students crossed the courtyard in a noisy uneven line. None of them knew what the three people in this room had been offered, refused, or nearly buried beneath.

Song let out a breath through his nose.

“That is better,” he said.

Mu Zhongtian’s face had gone unexpectedly tired.

“Good,” he said. “Keep the rule. Do not turn it into a speech you remember only when a teacher is present.”

Ji pushed away from the wall.

“The next assessment begins in three days. You will recover, train, and learn whether those short calls still work when you are exhausted. Do not expect the next round to preserve the shape of your last one. You have a result. Now make yourselves capable of surviving the attention it brings.”

Song stood carefully.

“Yes, Teacher.”

Luo rose beside him. “Hold. Cut. Out.”

Yan repeated the three words once, not as a vow for the room but as if testing their weight.

“Hold. Cut. Out.”


Yin Tianfan had been expelled.

The words did not arrive with anger. They arrived with the tiredness of someone who had carried them long enough that they had worn smooth at the edges.

“The exam ended,” Yin told Lan. “I ranked first. No one disputed it. I had destroyed the coalition, then my own group. My results were perfect.”

Lan heard the wrongness before Yin named it.

“Shrek removed me anyway.”

Yin gave a short laugh that held no amusement.

“I was furious. It was a simulation. No real soldiers had died. The coalition had surrounded us first. I thought every argument was hypocrisy from people who could not command as well as I could.”

“What did your teacher say?” Lan asked.

“That Shrek could accept ugly tactics against enemies. Ambushes. Lies. Retreats that were not retreats. War did not become cleaner because a commander wanted to feel noble.” Yin’s gaze settled on the window behind Lan. “But a commander who destroyed allies because they objected to him was not fit to command anyone. Today it was a simulated fleet. Tomorrow it would be people who could not be rebuilt after the screen went dark.”

Lan lowered his eyes.

The difference was not whether a commander used a teammate’s strength.

A team existed because people offered strength to one another.

The difference was whether the commander remembered the offering could be refused.

“I did not accept that at first,” Yin said. “I lost Shrek. I lost the chance at its inner court. The academy made sure no advanced Space Command faculty would take me. For two years, I lived inside the anger of it.”

His hand tightened once around the other.

“Eventually I found my old teammates. I apologized. One forgave me. One did not. Both were right. Then I joined the military as an ordinary soldier. No certificate. No famous teacher’s recommendation. It took twenty years to climb through the ranks, become a warship officer, command a small fleet, and receive the rank of major general.”

“And then?”

“And then the old record caught me again.”

The words were simple.

Yin’s smile was not.

“People called me the Elusive Fox. They meant it as praise. I had ability. I had my own way of fighting. But the road ended where it ended. So I came here.”

Lan thought of the man who had fired on his own student during a simulator lesson and called it awareness training. He thought of every ugly angle Yin had taught him to see.

Then he thought of the space between using a trick and becoming the trick.

“Teacher,” Lan said, “do you still think Shrek was wrong?”

Yin considered that instead of answering quickly.

“Sometimes,” he said at last. “Less than I used to. If I had continued the way I was, perhaps I would have turned into someone who could win every battle and leave nothing worth protecting behind him.”

He leaned forward.

“That is why I teach you what I know. A principle without ability can watch people die and call itself clean. Ability without principle becomes the disaster it claims it can prevent. Remember both.”

“I will,” Lan said.

“Good.”

The heaviness in Yin’s face shifted just enough for Lan to become suspicious.

His teacher’s eyes brightened.

“You have real talent for command, Xuanyu. A fleet commander decides whether countless people return home. Space Command is not a lesser road. It is a road wide enough to hold a world. When you enter Shrek, will you choose it?”

Lan almost answered.

The stars rose in his mind first: maps, routes, ships, signals, a thousand decisions made before a danger became visible to anyone else.

Then another image came after it.

A body too weak to survive the enemy reaching it.

Teacher Ji’s unyielding demands. Teacher Nana’s warnings. The gold and silver currents inside him, neither of which could be trusted to stay gentle merely because he wanted them to.

The door opened hard.

“Silly boy,” Ji Hongbin said. “This is a routine.”

Yin’s expression broke into offended dignity.

“Old Ji. I have been speaking from the heart.”

“Your heart has hooks.”

Ji walked in with his arms folded. He had clearly returned from somewhere else in the academy; dust marked one cuff, and his gaze was sharper than it had been when he had led Qian and Liu away.

“You tell him your wound, make him feel the shape of your regret, then put Space Command at the end while he is most likely to say yes. Where is the fair competition?”

“Everything I said was true.”

“Truth can still be arranged.”

Lan looked between them.

Teacher Yin’s story had been true.

Its lesson had been true.

The question at the end had also been placed with care.

That did not make the lesson useless.

It made the teacher speaking it unmistakably Yin Tianfan.

Ji turned to Lan.

“Your world is not only the stars. Individual cultivation, mecha, battle armor—those matters too. A commander who cannot survive the enemy reaching him does not command for long.”

Yin snorted. “And a Soul Master who can only win one fistfight at a time cannot save a fleet.”

“No one asked him to decide today.”

“I asked him to consider it.”

“You asked him to agree.”

Lan stood before their argument could become another lesson made of collateral damage.

“Teachers,” he said, “I understand why both of you care. But I do not have the right to choose Shrek’s direction before I have earned the right to enter it. Let me reach that point first.”

For a heartbeat, the two older men were silent.

Then Ji nodded.

“That is sensible.”

Yin pointed once, sternly enough to be theatrical.

“Think about fleets while you are being sensible.”

“Think about living through them,” Ji said.

The office door opened again before Yin could answer.

Mu Zhongtian stood there, and this time his expression held no interest in their argument.

“Lan Xuanyu,” he said, “your teammates are waiting in Soul World. It is time to draw your prize.”


Qian Lei had apparently recovered from training the moment the word prize reached him.

He was standing beside Liu Feng outside the simulation room with both hands clasped in front of himself, the picture of a student attempting dignity around a dangerous opportunity.

“Sister Ling will be there?” he asked Mu Zhongtian.

“She will,” Mu said.

Qian’s face did something complicated.

Liu Feng noticed. “You look frightened.”

“I look respectful.”

“You look like someone who expects a trap.”

“Respect and caution are closely related.”

Lan arrived beside them, and Qian’s attention jumped at once.

“Captain. I have trained. I have suffered. I am ready to receive the universe’s apology.”

“You have not been wronged by the universe,” Liu said.

“That is exactly the sort of statement made by people the universe has not tested properly.”

Lan’s mouth curved despite himself.

Then the three entered the pods.

Soul World opened in pink light.

Ling Yiyi waited in the training ground beyond it, hands folded and smile gentle enough to make Qian stop two steps farther away than he normally would have.

“Sister Ling,” he said carefully.

“Qian Lei.” Her smile deepened. “Why do you look as if I am about to take something from you?”

“I would never accuse Shrek Academy of such a thing.”

Liu murmured, “He would. He is choosing survival.”

Ling’s eyes moved to Liu, then back to Qian.

“Congratulations to all three of you,” she said. “You took first place in the Heaven Luo qualifier. Your consolidated score is also first across the Federation at this stage. Heaven Luo Planet has gained considerable honor through you.”

Mu Zhongtian inhaled behind them.

Even with the ranking already known, those words landed differently when they came from Shrek.

Lan bowed. “Thank you, Sister Ling.”

Then he lifted his face, open and hopeful.

“Since we are first across the Federation, is there an additional reward? Perhaps an extra Lucky Wheel draw?”

Qian’s eyes immediately brightened.

Liu Feng closed his eyes for one brief second.

Ling Yiyi looked at Lan as though reassessing an opponent.

“No.”

Lan blinked. “But drawing because we placed first is already different treatment from teams that did not. If results can create different rewards, then Federation first—”

“Then you may decline your existing draw,” Ling said pleasantly. “Would that be fairer?”

Lan shook his head at once.

“That would not be fair to us. Other first-place teams would still draw.”

Qian let out a soft sound of admiration.

Liu said, “Do not encourage him.”

Ling Yiyi’s smile turned dangerous at the edges.

“One draw each,” she said. “No more.”

“Understood,” Lan said with immediate sincerity.

“Of course you do.”

She pressed one hand into the air.

Pink light unfolded before them, first a narrow seam, then a doorway with no wall around it. Something bright and moving waited beyond, too quick to identify.

Qian swallowed.

“Captain,” he whispered, “if luck has a formation, put me somewhere important.”

“The center of discipline,” Liu said.

“A brutal formation.”

Lan looked at both of them.

Yin’s voice still remained somewhere behind his thoughts.

Do not forget the people carrying the plan.

He held out his hand.

“Together,” he said.

The three stepped into the pink light.

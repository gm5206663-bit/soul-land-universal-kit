# Chapter 58 — What the Morning Takes

Morning did not arrive as light.

It arrived as water finding the lowest place in the hollow behind the uprooted tree.

Lan woke when a cold thread reached the heel of his hand.

For a moment he did not move. Rain still tapped above them, softer now, falling from leaves instead of sky. Liu sat at the mouth of the hollow with the spear across his knees. Qian slept against the inner roots, one hand closed around the Coin of Summoning as if he had been afraid to let it go even in dreams.

The Purple Zoysia rested between Lan and the stone.

Its cap remained dark purple. Its leaves had not torn. But the soil around its roots had changed.

Water had found the grass cup during the night.

Lan touched the edge of the root ball. Mud came away on his fingertips, thinner than it had been when they left the first shelf. A pale root hair lay exposed against the grass.

He drew the cup closer.

Liu looked back.

“Problem?”

“Not yet.” Lan kept his voice low. “It needs to stop sitting in water.”

Qian opened his eyes.

“The rain stopped.”

“The sky stopped,” Liu said. “The ground is still thinking about it.”

He was right. Water ran under the roots around them, quiet but steady. The hollow had sheltered them from the drops. It had not sheltered the plant from everything the drops became.

Lan loosened the Blue Silver Grass around the root ball just enough to let trapped water drain through the lower strands. He did not shake the plant. He did not pull the mud apart to make the problem look smaller.

The Zoysia had been rooted when they found it.

It had been pulled free under terms they were still carrying.

That did not mean it could be carried carelessly because the condition had become inconvenient.

“We need drier ground,” he said.

Qian pushed himself upright.

“Then we go up?”

Liu shook his head before Lan answered.

“Up is where the ape went.”

Qian looked toward the rain-black branches above the hollow. There was no gold between them. No unsteady brow-light. The ape had left nothing behind except a few bent twigs too high to reach and a direction that belonged to it.

“We do not know that,” Qian said.

“We know enough.” Liu’s hand rested on the spear shaft. “It chose high ground. That does not make it a pathfinder.”

Qian’s mouth tightened.

Lan understood the feeling beneath it. The ape had chosen to cross a rotten cedar with them. It had watched them through the night. The absence felt like a question that should have an answer if Qian looked hard enough.

But an answer taken from a trail was still a kind of demand.

“We find our own ground,” Lan said.

He lifted the plant again. Water dripped once from the grass cup and darkened the root below it.

Liu crawled out of the hollow first.

The world beyond it had become gray.

Clouds still pressed against the canopy, but dawn had thinned the dark enough to show what the rain had changed. The lower slope was no longer a slope. It was a broad slick of soil broken by exposed roots and shallow rivulets. The old seam they had climbed during the night had washed half-closed behind them.

Liu studied the ground for a long time.

Then he pointed downhill, not toward the watercourse, but across it.

“Gravel rise.”

Qian followed the line of his spear. A low mound stood beyond the runoff, barely higher than the surrounding mud. It was ugly ground. A storm-fallen tree had stripped the upper soil away, leaving stones, broken roots, and a wedge of packed earth beneath an angled trunk.

“It is open,” Qian said.

“Only from here,” Liu said. “The lower side drops away. The rain did the rest for us.”

Lan looked at the Zoysia.

The gravel would drain. The angled trunk would keep the first hard sun from striking the plant too directly once the clouds broke. They could change the grass cup, wrap the root ball in drier earth, and keep moving before the damp became rot.

It was not shelter.

It was workable.

A sound came from below.

All three of them went still.

Not a shout. A boot slipping in mud, caught before it became a fall.

Liu lowered himself behind the uprooted roots and looked through the gap beneath them. Lan stayed where he was, one arm around the Zoysia. Qian did not touch the coin.

A shape moved through the lower trees.

It was too far to see a uniform clearly. Rainwater ran off a shoulder. The person moved with one hand pressed against a trunk, then paused beneath the overhang Dorm333 had abandoned in the night.

The hollow had not been empty long enough to forget them.

Qian’s breath caught.

“They found where we were.”

“They found dry ground,” Lan said.

The figure stayed beneath the stone only a moment. Another shadow passed farther down the slope. Neither came toward the uprooted tree. Neither looked uphill.

Liu waited until both sounds had dissolved into the runoff.

“Now,” he said.

The crossing was only twenty steps.

It would have been easy on a dry day.

Liu went first anyway, spear held across his body rather than forward. He found each stone beneath the mud with the butt of the shaft. Twice he changed direction without explaining why. Lan trusted the change. The water around his boots carried small leaves downhill in a fast brown line.

When Liu reached the middle, he turned.

“Lan. Give me the grass around your wrist. Not the plant.”

Lan sent a thin strand of Blue Silver Grass across the runoff. Liu caught it and looped it around an exposed root on the far side. It was not a bridge. It was a promise that if the ground slid, Lan would have something other than wet air in his hand.

Lan stepped into the water.

Cold struck through his shoe. The mud moved under him at once, trying to take his heel farther down than it should go. He leaned toward the grass line, held the Zoysia high with his free arm, and waited for the earth to stop being a decision.

Liu’s spear butt drove into the stone ahead of him.

“Left,” Liu said.

Lan took the left stone.

The next step held.

He crossed the rest without looking at the people below.

Qian came last.

The water had risen around the stones while Lan crossed. He could feel it pulling at his ankles. The coin in his fist was warm from his palm, and for one bad second he thought about opening the Gate. Something large and strong could cross this in a breath. Something large and strong could carry all three of them out of the mud.

Then he imagined it arriving in the open, loud and confused, while the two figures below lifted their heads.

He kept the coin shut.

Liu did not praise him for it.

He only held the spear steady until Qian reached the gravel rise.

Lan knelt beneath the angled trunk. The ground there was dry under the top layer of stones. He did not set the Zoysia into the earth. The plant was still in their keeping; the six-day term had not changed because rain made carrying harder.

Instead, he opened the grass cup and let the wet outer mud fall away in small pieces. Liu used the spear tip to scrape a shallow bed in the packed soil. Qian gathered dry leaf litter from under the trunk, checking each handful for insects before he gave it to Lan.

Together they rebuilt the root ball.

Drier earth beneath.

The original soil at the center.

Leaves around the outside to take the remaining dampness.

Blue Silver Grass loose enough to breathe, tight enough that the first run would not spill it again.

It took only a few minutes.

It felt longer because nothing fought them while they did it.

That was the point.

When Lan lifted the plant, its leaves no longer dragged with water.

Qian looked toward the high branches one time.

No gold appeared.

“The ape could have found this ground,” he said.

“Maybe,” Lan replied.

“But it did not owe us that.”

Liu tested the gravel under his boot.

“And we did not need it to.”

The three moved on before the lower shelter became a story someone could follow uphill.


Luo waited until the first gray light reached the lower edge of the boulder pocket.

It did not make the place brighter.

It made the rain lines visible.

Water fell from the upper stone in thin silver threads. One of them ran straight across the entrance. Another carried a faint wash of grit down from the slope above. Luo watched the grit gather at the same place three times before he spoke.

“They are using the hollow below as a stop.”

Song woke without lifting his head.

“The shape?”

“Or more than one. I cannot tell.”

Yan looked past Luo toward the watercourse. The first hollow had disappeared behind roots and rain, but the route leading to it had not. It was still the easiest dry line off the lower wash. Anyone who wanted to use the storm instead of fighting it would choose it.

Exactly as Dorm336 had.

Song reached for the shield.

“Can we wait them out?”

Luo shook his head.

“Not here. This pocket has one entrance and the slope above it carries sound.”

A loose pebble skipped down the outer stone. It tapped once, then twice, then stopped against a root.

No voice followed.

Yan did not need one.

“We leave before they decide the water is lying,” he said.

The route beyond the boulders was worse than it had looked in the night. A rain-carved spillway cut across the hillside, too wide to jump and too shallow to call a stream. Water rushed through it over flat black stones. On the far side, a shelf of roots rose toward the higher forest.

The shelf had one weakness.

A dead cedar lay across the spillway, wedged beneath a stone tooth. Its bark had peeled away in strips. The far end hung over the water with nothing beneath it except white churn and the roots it might reach if it fell.

Song looked at it.

“That is getting familiar.”

“It is not the same,” Luo said.

“That is almost reassuring.”

Luo crouched at the edge of the spillway. He did not touch the water. He watched the foam turn around the first stone, then the second. When he pointed, it was not at the cedar’s middle.

“Start low. The left side has a dry ridge under the bark. At the far end, there is a root under the water. Step over it, not on it.”

Song eyed the shield.

“It will snag.”

“It will,” Luo agreed.

Yan studied the stone tooth holding the near end of the trunk. A small crack ran beneath it. The storm had washed mud into the crack and made the whole support slick.

“I can dry that,” he said.

Song looked at the rain around them.

“Quietly?”

“Yes.”

“And if it moves?”

“Then you hold the trunk while Luo crosses.”

Song gave him a level look.

“That was not an answer.”

Yan almost smiled.

“No. It was the part I could promise.”

Song nodded once.

That was enough.

He set the shield beneath the cedar’s near end. The rim fit against the stone tooth only after he angled it hard enough that the shield edge bit into the mud. His shoulders tightened under the weight.

Luo crossed first.

He stayed low, fingers spread across the wet wood. The spillway threw water against the trunk in irregular pulses. Each one made the cedar quiver. Luo stopped whenever it did, waited for the rhythm to settle, then moved again.

At the far end, he disappeared beneath the root shelf and raised one hand.

“Now.”

Yan placed two fingers against the crack.

Heat entered the stone slowly.

Not enough to make the rain steam. Not enough to send red-gold light through the trees. The mud darkened, then held instead of sliding. He felt the work in his wrist, a close, controlled ache.

Song shifted his shield a fraction.

The cedar settled into the brace.

Yan crossed.

The bark gave under his first foot. His wings opened just enough to catch the fall before it became weight on the trunk. The movement was small. A feathered pressure against wet air. Nothing that rose above the boulders.

He found the dry ridge Luo had named.

One step.

Then the next.

Behind him, a shape moved at the mouth of the boulder pocket.

Song saw it too.

He did not look away from the shield.

“Go,” he said.

Yan reached the far root and turned back.

Song’s knee was already deep in the mud beside the spillway. The shield held the cedar in place. The shape above the pocket did not descend. It did not need to. Waiting was its own kind of pressure.

Yan could have made the trunk bright.

He could have burned the route clean, turned the water to a wall of steam, made the figure choose distance.

It would also show exactly where Song stood.

“Luo,” Yan said.

Luo had one hand under the far root. His eyes remained on the current.

“Two breaths after the next surge,” he said.

The water struck the cedar.

Once.

Again.

Song pulled the shield free.

The trunk dipped.

Yan caught the near edge with a thin pull of his wings, not lifting it, only taking enough of its fall that Song’s first step found the dry ridge instead of the water. Song crossed low and fast, dragging the shield with both hands.

The third surge hit as he reached the far side.

The cedar dropped into the spillway behind them.

Water swallowed the sound.

The shape at the boulder pocket did not follow across.

Maybe it could not.

Maybe it did not want to.

Maybe it was not one of the people who had stood above the first hollow at all.

Luo did not give any of those maybes a name.

“Up,” he said.

The root shelf rose into denser forest. Song took the lead this time, shield held close so it would not catch wet branches. Yan came behind him. Luo moved last, pausing twice to brush away marks where the mud had accepted a boot too clearly.

They did not erase the route.

They only refused to write it for someone else.

At the top of the shelf, the trees thinned around a crooked stone outcrop. A cavity beneath it faced away from the spillway and away from the watercourse. It was too narrow to call comfortable. It was wide enough for three children to sit back-to-back with the shield between them and listen to the forest from a direction no easy path favored.

Song lowered the shield.

“That one has two exits,” he said.

Luo checked the dark on either side of the outcrop.

“Two slow exits.”

“Better than one fast mistake.”

Yan looked down through the dripping roots. The watercourse was gone from sight. So was the boulder pocket. So was the line where Ye had disappeared hours before.

He did not try to find it.

Morning light finally reached the upper leaves.

It did not make the forest safe.

It only showed which ground had been too easy to keep.


The gravel rise did not stay private once the sky began to pale.

Liu saw it in the lower trees first.

One of the two shapes that had used the stone overhang had come out from beneath it. The figure did not look toward Dorm333’s rise. It stood with its head tilted, listening to the runoff and to whatever answer the other person gave from farther down the slope.

The distance between them was enough.

It would not remain enough if daylight made the wrong branch shine.

Liu touched the lowest root beneath the angled trunk.

“There is a dry cut behind this rise,” he said. “It joins the old root line and turns away from the water.”

Qian looked at the thin space between two stone shelves. It was not much of a route. The Zoysia would have to be held close. Liu’s spear would have to go first. If the two people below came uphill, there would be no room to turn around quickly.

“We just finished making this ground work,” he said.

Lan looked at the plant in his arms.

“We made it work long enough to carry it safely,” he answered. “That is not the same as being allowed to stay.”

Qian wanted to say that the people below had not seen them. That waiting was not the same as hiding. That there were always more roots and more rain and more reasons to keep moving until movement became the only thing they knew how to do.

Then he saw the dark-purple cap through the loose grass.

The Zoysia could not choose which shelter to leave. The ape had chosen its own branch. The unknown people below could choose their own overhang. The plant only had the hands carrying it.

“Fine,” Qian said. “The dry cut.”

Liu slipped through first. The gap was narrow enough that he had to angle the spear behind him. Lan followed with one shoulder against the stone, holding the grass cup high so no root brushed the wet wall. Qian came last and used the small stones Liu had disturbed as a guide instead of looking back at the shapes below.

The cut led them into a line of older trees where the rain had never reached the ground directly. By the time the lower slope was only a sound behind them, the gravel rise had disappeared too.

No one had claimed it.

That was better.


At the stone outcrop, Song did not sit immediately.

The cavity had two slow exits, as Luo said. But daylight made slow exits visible in ways darkness did not. Rain still fell from the leaves, and every drop that struck the shield made a small bright sound.

Song turned the shield over in his hands.

“We keep leaving places because someone else might use them,” he said.

“Someone else does use them,” Luo replied. “That is the problem.”

Yan watched the dark gap below the spillway. The cedar was gone beneath the water now. Its absence had made one route impossible. It had not made the people on the other side disappear.

“What if they do not follow?” Song asked.

“Then we have spent a morning moving carefully,” Luo said. “Nothing worse happens.”

Song looked at Yan.

“And if they do?”

Yan thought of the boulder pocket, of the shape that had waited there without showing a face, and of the easy answer a bright line of fire would have offered.

“Then we are not standing where they expect us to be,” he said.

Luo nodded toward the fern-covered side of the outcrop.

“Water runs behind that ridge. It is not a path yet.”

Song lifted the shield.

“Then we make it one before it becomes somebody else’s.”

They did not rush the ridge. Song took the weight where the wet stones leaned together. Luo tested the fern-hidden ground before either boy stepped on it. Yan kept his fire inside his hands and shoulders, ready for balance, not spectacle.

When the three of them crossed behind the outcrop, the cavity remained empty.

It did not point toward the watercourse.

It did not point toward Ye.

It became only another place rain had touched.


By the time the clouds began to lift, Dorm333 had left the hollow without the ape.

Dorm336 had left the watercourse without knowing who had used it after them.

The Purple Zoysia remained held, dry enough to carry and still not theirs to spend.

The rain had taken the night’s cleanest shelters.

It had not taken the choices that made the next ground possible.

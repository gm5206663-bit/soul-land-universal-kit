# Chapter 60 — What We Will Not Spend

The ground beneath the older roots gave way slowly.

Not as a fall.

As a choice.

Liu stopped where the hillside thinned into pale dirt and stone. Ahead, the trees had been cut back by wind or hands; Qian could not tell which at first. The trunks on the right carried small fresh nicks, each one no wider than a fingernail. They followed a broad shelf across the slope.

The shelf looked dry.

It also looked quick.

Lan shifted the grass cup higher against his chest. The Purple Zoysia rested beneath the leaves and loose woven blades, its root ball held close enough that Qian could see the careful way Lan’s elbow did not press it.

Liu crouched at the first nicked trunk.

“Not wind,” he said.

He touched the mark with one finger, then rubbed the dust from it between thumb and forefinger.

“Fresh.”

Qian looked down the shelf. It curved around a stand of thin trees and disappeared behind a rise where sunlight lay hard against the ground. Beyond the rise, the forest fell away toward lower green. It was the first easy-looking route they had seen since leaving the slate basin.

“That is what they want people to take?” he asked.

Liu did not answer. He moved two paces farther out, keeping his weight close to the hillside. The butt of his spear touched the pale earth once.

The sound came back wrong.

Not stone.

Not packed ground.

A soft, empty knock.

Lan’s gaze settled on the shelf.

“What is under it?”

“Old water,” Liu said. “Or nothing.”

He pointed past the curve. Between two low bushes, the pale dirt darkened in a long thin line. It was easy to mistake for a shadow. Once Qian saw it, he could not stop seeing how the shelf sloped toward it.

A rain cut had hollowed the ground beneath the dry surface. The upper layer remained in place only because roots still held it together. A running foot, or several feet following one another, would find out what the rain had taken.

“We can go around?” Qian asked.

“Not on this side.”

Liu angled the spear toward the left. Below their feet, a line of thick roots ran down the hill into shadow. The roots were older than the dirt around them. They crossed a narrow underbank where water had carved the slope away, then disappeared behind a leaning cedar.

“That way is slower,” Liu said. “And the root nearest the bottom is rotten.”

Lan looked from the hard sun on the shelf to the roots below.

“Can it hold us?”

“It can hold one at a time if we make it.”

Qian closed his eyes.

The forest did not become clear when he did that. It became more crowded.

A bird shifted in the cedar above them. Water moved somewhere below the roots. Farther right, beyond the nicked trunks, came the soft irregular pressure of people trying not to make sound. Two, he thought. Maybe three. They were not close enough for him to catch words. They were close enough that the stillness did not belong to the hill anymore.

“They are following the cuts,” he said.

Lan did not ask who.

“Behind the rise?”

“Near it.” Qian opened his eyes. “They do not know we are here yet. But they are looking at the same ground.”

Liu’s face stayed turned toward the pale shelf.

“If we put tracks on this side,” he said, “they will see them before they see us.”

Qian understood.

Three clear prints out onto the dry-looking ground. A bent grass blade from Lan’s cup caught on a nicked trunk. One turn of Liu’s spear butt in the dust.

Anyone who had seen the purple cap through grass would know what those signs meant.

They would believe Dorm333 had taken the shelf.

By the time they learned otherwise, the roots below would have carried Lan, Qian, and Liu beyond the rise.

It was a good way to make distance.

It was also a way to make someone hurry after a thing they thought they could take.

Qian’s fingers found the edge of the Coin inside his sleeve.

Not to open it.

Only because his hand needed somewhere to go.

“We could make it look real,” he said.

The words sat between them.

Liu did not tell him he was wrong.

Lan looked down at the grass cup. One narrow blade from its outer cover had loosened in the climb. It lay against his wrist, green-brown and ordinary. It did not look like a promise. It did not look like six days of waiting, or a wounded creature that might never return, or the fact that strangers had begun looking at the team as though it carried an answer.

But it was part of the thing they carried.

“No,” Lan said.

Qian lifted his eyes.

Lan’s voice was quiet enough that the people beyond the rise could not have heard it.

“We do not make our condition into someone else’s fall.”

Liu nodded once.

“The roots, then.”

The decision did not make the lower route less difficult.

It only made it theirs.

Liu planted the spear beside the first root and tested the earth with the heel of his hand. A skin of dirt crumbled away under his fingers. Beneath it, the root was wet, but alive.

“This one takes Lan’s weight,” he said. “Qian, you stay above until he reaches the cedar. Watch the shelf. Do not make anything loud.”

Qian nodded.

Lan crouched before the descent. Blue Silver Grass slipped from his sleeve, not spreading across the hill, not reaching for the people beyond the rise. It found the living roots and wound around them in narrow lines. The strands tightened where the wood bent away from the earth, giving the root a little more hold.

“That will not save the rotten one,” Liu said.

“It only has to save the living ones,” Lan answered.

Liu lowered himself first.

He did not use the spear as a weapon. He set its shaft across two roots and pressed down until it stopped sliding. Then he shifted his feet along the hillside, testing the lower line with every movement. Dirt fell from under his heel and vanished into the underbank.

“Here,” he said.

Lan moved.

The grass cup made him awkward. He could not turn his shoulders freely or catch himself with both hands. He kept the Zoysia high beneath his chin, one arm around the root ball, the other braced against the tree.

Qian watched every place where Lan’s shoe met wood.

At the shelf above, a twig broke.

The sound was small.

It made his Spirit Sea tighten anyway.

Two shapes had reached the rise. They had not seen Dorm333. Qian could feel their pause, the attention pulling along the line of nicks toward the open dirt.

One of them stepped onto the shelf.

Qian’s breath held.

The ground did not break. Not yet.

The figure stopped after two steps.

A voice came through the thin trees, too muffled for Qian to understand. Another answered. The first did not run.

It turned back.

Qian let the Coin go.

Below him, Lan had reached the cedar. Liu waited under the root line with the spear wedged across the gap.

“Qian,” Liu said.

The rotten root lay between them.

It was broad enough for a foot and soft enough that Qian could see gray fibers where the bark had split. Beyond it was the cedar trunk, then the lower ground. The spear formed a second narrow line across the worst of the gap, but only if he kept his weight low.

Qian started down.

Halfway across, the root gave a slow wet groan.

He froze.

“Not forward,” Liu said. “To the trunk. Turn your heel.”

The instruction was simple. Doing it without sending the root down with him was not.

Qian breathed through his nose. He turned his heel against the living root at his left, shifted one hand from the slick bark to the spear shaft, and moved the rest of his weight toward the cedar exactly as Liu had shown him.

The rotten root tore loose behind his ankle.

The spear bowed.

Blue Silver Grass caught the living root above Qian’s shoulder. Lan’s face went pale with the effort, but the root held long enough for Qian to land beside Liu in the lower shadow.

The broken root dropped into the underbank with a long rush of dirt.

No one was hurt.

Above them, the pale shelf answered with silence.

Liu pulled the spear free and looked up at the gap they had crossed.

“No one else uses that now,” he said.

Lan did not sound relieved.

“No,” he said. “No one does.”

Qian looked toward the rise through the roots. The two distant pressures had moved away from the edge. He could no longer tell whether they had chosen another path or simply stopped because the ground had told them to.

That was not something Dorm333 could decide.

Lan tucked the loosened grass blade back into the cup and rose carefully.

The descent had cost them time. The sun had moved farther across the upper shelf. The easier line still lay above them, bright and unused.

Liu pointed down the shadowed bank.

“We keep the hard ground,” he said.

They did.


By the time the sun found the open side of the ridge, it had dried the outer needles but not the soil beneath them.

Luo noticed that first.

He stopped beside a red-barked tree whose roots had been exposed by the rain. At its base, a narrow gully cut diagonally through the slope. From where Song and Yan stood, the gully looked like an easy descent: dry leaves scattered on top, a clean line between two ridges, enough room to move quickly without turning sideways.

Luo crouched and brushed the leaves aside.

The dirt under them was dark.

“Do not step there,” he said.

Song looked past him.

“It looks better than the ridge.”

“That is why someone will.”

Luo pointed to the red bark. Thick resin had gathered in old scars along the trunk. A few dry flakes had fallen into the gully. The whole channel smelled sharp in the warming air.

“The water went under the leaves,” Luo said. “The bank has opened below. If it slides, it does not stop until the bottom.”

Yan followed the gully with his eyes. It vanished behind a stand of dead brush, then bent toward the lower forest. From above, the curve seemed to point exactly where the three of them might want anyone looking for Fire Phoenix to believe they had gone.

A little heat on the resin would make a bright, thin smoke.

Not a wildfire.

Not even a flame large enough to cross the gully.

Just a red-gold answer visible through the branches. Anyone who had watched his fire before would read it. Anyone who wanted him to make a road might follow it.

They would descend into the channel because the smoke told them someone capable had chosen it.

Behind the next ridge, voices moved through the trees.

Not close.

Close enough.

Song heard them too. His hand closed around the shield handle.

“They are coming from the high side,” he said.

Luo listened for another moment.

“Three,” he said. “Maybe more behind them. They are not on our track yet.”

Yan looked at the resin again.

One controlled flare would cost almost nothing. The warmth already lived near the surface of his hands. He could light the scar, turn the next set of eyes downhill, and give Song and Luo a clean minute to take the ridge.

It would be efficient.

It would also make the dangerous ground look chosen.

Song watched him looking.

“We are not making the wrong way bright,” he said.

Luo rose slowly.

“If they enter that gully at speed, the first person pulls the soil loose for everyone behind. The bend hides the break until it is too late to turn.”

The words did not accuse Yan of wanting that.

They did not need to.

Yan had left a small warmth turn once before because it had belonged to the team’s own line. Rain had blurred the ground then. They had not known who, if anyone, would read it. Now people knew his name. They had already asked his fire to tell them where to place their feet.

A trace was no longer only a trace if someone chose to make it an instruction.

“No,” Yan said.

He drew his hands farther into his sleeves.

Song looked toward the ridge behind them.

“Then what do we have?”

Luo had already moved to the other side of the red-barked tree. There, a low seam ran between the trunk and a wall of gray stone. Rainwater had carried mud into it, making the narrow climb look smooth and useless.

“Up,” he said. “Not far. Then behind the stone.”

Song stared at the seam.

“That is not a path.”

“It is not a fast one.”

Luo pressed two fingers into the mud and pulled them back. Beneath the wet brown surface, a flat ledge ran diagonally upward. There were three handholds, no more, each hidden by moss.

“The trunk takes your shield if you turn it flat,” he said to Song. “Yan goes second. I go last. Do not pull on the upper moss. It is holding mud, not stone.”

Song set the shield against the trunk and tested the seam with one boot. The mud slid half an inch under his sole.

“Needs drying,” he said.

Yan stepped beside him.

Luo pointed to a strip no wider than two fingers along the inner ledge.

“Only there. If you heat the rest, the mud loosens.”

Yan touched the stone with the side of his hand.

The fire he used did not leap. It did not colour the trees. A narrow warmth ran through the wet line beneath his palm. Water drew out in a faint thread of steam, then stopped. The edge of stone emerged rough and pale under the mud.

“Now,” Luo said.

Song turned the shield sideways and wedged its lower rim into the first handhold. He climbed with one shoulder against the trunk, the shield taking enough of his weight that the mud did not shear away beneath him.

At the second ledge, the shield caught.

Song did not force it.

“Too wide,” he said.

“Lower your left elbow,” Luo replied. “The stone angles out under it.”

Song changed the angle by a breath. The shield slid past the stone. He reached the upper lip and held the strap tight around his wrist.

“Set,” he said.

Yan went next.

The gully waited just below him, dry leaves unmoving over wet, hollow dirt. Beyond it, the nearby voices had grown clearer. One child called something through the trees.

Yan could not make out the words.

He did not need to.

He kept his gaze on Song’s shield and the pale strip of stone Luo had uncovered.

The ledge was small. His fingers scraped moss. The warmth in the rock had already faded, but it had left enough roughness beneath his soles. Song leaned down from above, not reaching to pull him, only holding the shield steady so Yan could use the edge that had been made for all three.

Yan climbed.

A clump of mud broke loose behind his heel and fell into the gully without sound.

Luo came last. He erased nothing from the lower seam. He did not smooth the moss back into place or hide the stone they had used. The route was too narrow to become a promise for anyone else, and changing it would only make the slope less honest.

At the top, he pressed his palm to the ridge until the three of them could move behind the gray wall of stone.

Below, the voices stopped near the red-barked tree.

Song held still.

No red-gold smoke rose from the resin.

No fire pointed down into the gully.

After a moment, one of the voices moved away from the drop. Another followed. The forest took their sound and scattered it among the trees.

Luo let out a breath.

“They will choose their own ground,” he said.

Song looked at the sun beyond the ridge. It had climbed high enough to make the long route ahead obvious.

“We lost the quick way.”

“Yes,” Yan said.

The answer did not need defending.

They had lost the quick way when they decided it could not belong to them alone.

Song picked up the shield. Luo took the front again, reading the shadow line beneath the stone. Yan kept his hands closed around the small remaining warmth and followed where all three could go.


Under the cedar, the air stayed cool long after the slope above had turned white with sun.

Liu led through the lower roots without trying to make the ground easier than it was. The underbank narrowed twice. At the second narrowing, Lan had to turn sideways so the grass cup did not scrape the stone. Qian went ahead, feeling the dirt before each step with the edge of his shoe.

The path did not reward them for choosing it.

It only continued.

When the roots finally opened onto a shallow fold of dark earth, Liu stopped and listened uphill.

Nothing came down the broken line behind them.

Qian listened too. The human pressure had thinned into ordinary forest sounds: birds, leaves, the quiet settling of dirt where the rotten root had fallen.

“They did not follow,” he said.

Lan looked at the Zoysia.

“We do not know that.”

Qian nodded. He did know the difference now.

Not seeing someone was not the same as sending them away.

Liu glanced back only once.

“The shelf was not ours to spend,” he said.

Lan’s fingers rested around the grass cup.

“No,” he replied. “Neither were they.”

Then the three of them moved beneath the cedar shade, slower than they had planned, carrying the condition without turning it into an answer for anyone else.

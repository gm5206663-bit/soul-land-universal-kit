# Chapter 51 — The Cost of Quiet

Qian Lei looked at the Gate of Summoning as if it had just agreed with Liu Feng.

That was offensive enough on its own.

Lan had explained the road in a few calm sentences: stronger Soul Power, stronger Spiritual Power, stronger Soul Rings. The Gate would not become reliable because Qian wished harder. It would answer more cleanly when the person opening it had enough strength to hold the answer.

Simple.

Unfairly simple.

“I have a plan,” Qian said.

Liu kept watch from the nearest root. “That sentence usually requires a warning.”

“Within one year, I reach Spiritual Power five hundred and Soul Power Rank30.”

For a moment, Lan said nothing.

Then he nodded.

“That is a clear goal.”

Qian’s chest rose a little.

It fell again almost at once.

“Spiritual Power is the problem.”

The joke had left his voice. He turned the Coin of Summoning over once between his fingers, not activating it.

“Meditation is slow. Summoning helps, but it helps because it takes something out of me. When I overdraw Soul Power, I am tired. When I overdraw Spiritual Power…” He stopped, looking down at the dirt caught in the lines of his palm. “My head feels as if someone has split it and forgotten to put it back together. My hands become useless. My legs do not feel attached properly. I cannot make the Gate answer, and if I force it, I do not know what will answer instead.”

Liu’s spear tip lowered slightly.

Qian continued before anyone could make the confession easier for him.

“A summon is not a pet just because I called it. If I am weak enough, something on the other side might decide I am the easier meal.”

The forest held still around them.

Its silence was not kind. It simply did not care which children understood their limits before those limits mattered.

Liu sat back on his heels.

“Then do not force it alone,” he said. “But if you always stop before it hurts, you will always have the same limit.”

Qian looked at him.

“That is easy for you to say. Your spear does not open a door that might eat you.”

“No.” Liu’s answer was immediate. “It opens a road that gets me hit first. We all have something we avoid because it costs too much.”

His right hand flexed beside the White Dragon Spear.

The loose sleeve hid the faint silver under his skin. It did not hide the memory of what the Soul Bone had demanded when it fused. Liu had gained strength from it. He had also learned that strength did not arrive as a favor.

“If pain is the price,” Liu said, “then the only question is whether it buys something.”

Qian’s mouth opened for an argument.

Nothing came out.

Lan watched him for a moment before speaking.

“If we only stayed together in the Elite Junior Class,” he said, “your present strength would be enough for many battles. Liu and I could cover the places where the Gate leaves you weak. But Shrek is not asking whether we can stay in the same formation forever.”

Qian’s fingers closed around the coin.

Lan’s voice stayed soft.

“Someday you will have to stand firmly even when the people you trust are farther away than one command. I think you will need to be harder on yourself.”

Qian stared at him with a hurt expression that became suspicious.

“And you are saying this as someone who enjoys being hard on himself?”

Lan’s smile turned bitter.

“I do not have that choice. Teacher Ji and Teacher Yin made it for me a long time ago.”

Liu gave a short breath through his nose.

Qian brightened despite himself.

“True. There are nights you return to the dorm looking as if Teacher Ji used you for spear practice and Teacher Yin used the pieces to test a new warship.”

“Once,” Liu said, “you fell asleep on the floor before reaching your bed.”

Qian pointed at Lan. “I had to check whether a captain’s meal allowance could be inherited.”

“You took my bun,” Lan said.

“I safeguarded it from waste.”

Even Liu smiled.

The laughter did not solve the Gate.

It did make the next breath possible.

Lan rose first.

“Continue carefully,” he said. “No heroic overdraft in a forest we do not understand.”

Qian stood with a dramatic sigh.

“Measured suffering. A terrible compromise.”


By the time the rain began, Yan had stopped thinking of the roots above them as a ceiling.

They had become a weight.

The low canopy slowly thinned as Dorm336 followed the warmer current out of the basin. For a while, the route was only wet soil, lichen-dark trunks, and Luo’s occasional hand against a root or stone. Song took the front where the trail narrowed, shield angled low enough to push hanging vines aside without announcing every movement with metal.

Yan stayed behind them.

Not because he needed protection.

Because the space behind the formation was the one place a flare could most easily become everyone else’s problem.

The first drops struck leaves above them with a soft, separate rhythm.

Then the forest accepted the rain all at once.

Water slid down bark. Moss darkened. The thin mineral scent beneath the basin’s vapor disappeared under wet earth and green rot.

Luo stopped at the lip of a ravine.

Below, brown water moved between exposed roots. It was not deep enough to become a river in an ordinary place, but the rain had gathered every slope above it into one impatient current. The opposite bank was only thirty or forty paces away. A set of great roots crossed most of the gap, braided together where two old trees had grown toward each other before one of them fell.

Above the ravine, a higher route ran along the trunk of the fallen tree.

It was narrow.

It was open to the gray sky.

Yan looked at it once and understood the attraction. A short flight. A controlled burn through the snagged branches. He could be on the other side before Song had finished measuring the first root below.

Luo crouched near the water instead.

“The current changed in the last few minutes,” he said. “It is rising.”

Song studied the braided roots.

“Can they take us?”

“Not all at once.” Luo pointed with two fingers. “That one is hollow. The center holds. The far side has a grip point if it does not shift.”

Then his hand moved toward the upper bank.

A vine there had been cut cleanly, not broken by rain. Near it, the mud had been pressed flat and then half-washed smooth.

“People,” Luo said.

Song did not turn his head.

“How long?”

“Not long enough to call it old. Not recent enough to call it a warning.”

Yan looked up through the rain. Nothing moved on the high bank. The forest had too many places to hide three children and too many places to hide a soul beast that would enjoy finding them first.

The round’s rules came back without anyone repeating them.

Separate score.

Final kill.

Half a team’s work for killing a team member.

A clean cut vine could mean another group had passed through.

It could mean they had taken the higher route.

It could mean nothing useful at all.

Yan pointed toward the fallen trunk.

“I can clear that line.”

Song followed the gesture. “You can.”

“I can cross before the water rises farther.”

“And then?”

Yan looked at him.

Song rested one hand on the rim of his shield.

“That route has no exit for me with this. Luo can move quickly until he cannot. You can fly. The rest of us become something you come back for after you have solved the problem.”

The words were not angry.

That made them harder to reject.

Yan let the possibility of the high route go.

It would have been fast.

It would not have been theirs.

“Lower crossing,” he said.

Luo nodded once.

“Song first. I call the roots. Yan stays at the rear and watches the upper bank. Do not put heat into the water unless I tell you where.”

Song gave him a look.

“You have become very comfortable giving orders.”

“You asked for an exit before pressure commits.”

Song’s mouth moved.

“That is annoyingly correct.”

He stepped onto the first root.

The braided surface bowed under the shield’s weight but did not break. Rain slid over it in silver lines. Luo moved a short distance ahead, light on his feet, never committing his full weight until he had tested the next place with heel, palm, and the brief vibration that traveled through his body when he listened to a surface.

“Center,” Luo called.

Song shifted.

“Left hand. Not the pale bark.”

Song’s shield edge caught a protruding root. He used it as a brace and moved again.

Yan stayed on the near bank until Song had crossed the first third. A thin line of red-gold heat warmed the split in the root behind Song’s heel—not enough to flare, only enough to dry the water that had gathered there and keep the fibers from sliding apart under the next step.

The rain hissed where it touched the heat.

Steam rose in a thread and vanished under the canopy.

“Too much?” Yan asked.

“No,” Luo said. “Again at the dark seam. Then stop.”

Yan did.

For several breaths, the crossing became a rhythm.

Song held.

Luo read.

Yan cut only where the call made a place for it.

Then the ravine changed its mind.

A surge struck from upstream, carrying leaves, broken bark, and the heavy force of water that had found a steeper route somewhere above. The center root lurched sideways.

Song’s shield slammed down. Stoneplate Tortoise pressure thickened around him as he drove the shield rim into the braid and took the first wrenching pull through both arms.

Luo was already on the far half of the crossing.

“Right wall!” he shouted. “There is a hollow seam. Open it now or the root goes!”

Yan saw it.

A narrow channel, choked with old resin and stone, ran through the right wall of the ravine. If the surge entered it, the water would divide. If it did not, it would tear the crossing sideways with Song still on it.

A full flare would have shattered too much.

It would have turned rain into a white wall and put red-gold light above the trees.

Song’s boots slid a finger-width.

The shield groaned.

Yan lifted one wing.

“Hold,” he said.

Song did not look back.

“I am trying.”

Yan’s fire struck the resin seam in a single compressed pulse.

Red-gold light filled the ravine.

The resin burst. Water tore through the opened channel with a sound like something huge taking one breath. Steam exploded upward, dense and white, carrying a red heart through the rain before the wind could pull it apart.

The main current lost just enough force.

Song drove forward.

The root stopped sliding.

Luo dropped flat on the far side and caught the higher braid with both hands, calling, “Now. Three steps. Center. Jump!”

Song moved on the word.

Yan crossed behind him, boots striking the roots only where Luo had named them. The last support gave way as Yan reached the far bank. It twisted into the ravine, tore free, and vanished beneath brown water.

For a moment, all three stood in the rain without speaking.

Song lowered his shield slowly.

His arms shook once before he made them still.

Luo looked upstream through the thinning steam.

“The high line is gone to us,” he said.

The route might still exist for someone who could climb, fly, or gamble with a falling tree in hard rain. For Song and Luo, it had ceased to be an answer.

Yan looked at the place where the red-gold steam had risen.

Even now, a faint warmth clung to the ravine wall.

“Visible,” he said.

“Yes,” Luo answered.

Song rolled one shoulder, then the other.

“Necessary,” he said.

Yan met his eyes.

Song did not make the word gentle.

Neither did he take it back.


Dorm333’s first day became longer after that.

The Gate opened. A summoned creature pushed through a thicket. Liu made space when it did not. Lan watched what answered them and changed direction by inches, then by a streambed, then by the way the trees began to stand farther apart.

They met soul beasts, but none that made Lan stop and reconsider the whole forest.

A low, broad animal broke from a patch of ferns and charged Qian’s knees. Liu turned it aside with the shaft of his spear before it could become a fight worth naming. Later, a cluster of sharp-clawed things dropped from a branch line and fled after golden grass tightened around their escape. A hundred-year presence tested their formation from deeper brush, then withdrew when Qian’s Gate opened and the first uncertain growl came through.

They took what could not be avoided.

They did not make every movement into a hunt.

Qian’s complaints became less frequent as the day wore on.

That worried Lan more than the noise had.

When Qian finally stopped beneath a tree and said, “I am fine,” the words had none of their usual performance.

Liu checked the color of his face and said, “Sit.”

“I was going to.”

“You were going to fall politely.”

“I have manners.”

Lan let Qian sit and took the outer watch while Liu studied the ground ahead. The forest was changing slowly enough that certainty would have been foolish, but the signs had weight. The weaker beasts were no longer as common. The spaces between their calls were farther apart. Each new direction asked more from the three of them than the last.

By the time the light began to turn green-gray, Lan judged they had covered close to twenty kilometers since the morning.

It was a meaningful distance.

It was not a result.

The horde found them before they could decide whether to keep moving in the dark.

Liu felt it first through the vibration in the soil.

His head came up.

Lan heard the answer a breath later: too many feet; bodies pushing through brush in one continuous mass; the blunt crack of smaller trees giving way.

Qian rose too fast from meditation.

“What is that?”

“Not ours,” Liu said.

Lan did not wait for a better description.

“Up.”

Golden grass shot toward the nearest branches. Liu moved first, spear finding holds where there should have been none. Qian followed with less elegance and more urgency. Lan came last, pulling the grass free before the first dark bodies reached the base of the tree.

They pressed close to wet bark.

Below, the horde passed.

It was not one species. Horns, scaled backs, blunt skulls, and smaller shapes flowed through the undergrowth in a frightened, hungry-looking movement that did not care what it crushed. One beast stopped directly beneath Qian’s foot.

Qian shut his eyes.

The creature sniffed at the trunk.

Liu’s hand tightened around the spear.

Lan held still enough that the bark bit into his palm.

Then something farther along the forest roared.

The horde changed direction as one body and went after it.

The ground kept trembling long after the last shape had vanished.

No one spoke until the silence returned in pieces.

Qian opened one eye.

“I believe,” he whispered, “that we have made a wise tactical decision not to become part of that.”

Liu climbed down first when Lan nodded.

“It was not a decision. It was the only option.”

“Then I wisely approved it.”

They found a place between old roots where Qian could meditate while the other two took turns resting and keeping watch. Soul World asked a great deal from their minds, but the nutrient fluid in the pods kept their real bodies from hunger and thirst. That did not make a wet forest night comfortable. It only removed one way to fail.

No other team crossed their path.

The absence was not safety.

It was scale.

Somewhere in a forest large enough to swallow their voices, other groups could be gathering points, making alliances they could not see, or deciding that strangers with scores were easier prey than soul beasts.

Lan took the second watch.

Qian’s breathing was thin but steady. Liu slept with one hand near the spear. Rainwater ticked from leaves above them.

Lan listened to the dark until it became morning.


Song found a hollow under a leaning stone shelf before the rain became cold enough to make rest impossible.

It was not dry. It was merely less wet than the rest of the forest.

For the moment, that counted.

Luo checked the slope twice before he allowed them to sit. Song placed the shield where it could catch falling stone or a direct approach. Yan kept his fire unlit.

The decision made the darkness feel larger.

Song leaned his head back against the rock.

“Next time,” he said, “we take the route that does not include water trying to take my arms off.”

Luo rubbed rain from his eyes.

“Next time, tell the forest before it rains.”

“That was not a denial.”

Yan looked out at the ravine. The broken crossing was somewhere behind the curtain of water. The steam from his pulse had long since disappeared, but he could still picture it lifting past the trees.

A signal.

A mistake, if it had been made for speed.

A necessity, because Song had been slipping and the water would have taken the formation apart.

He did not regret it.

That did not mean he liked it.

“We rest in turns,” he said.

Song closed his eyes without actually sleeping.

“Command noted.”

Luo’s head tilted toward the higher bank.

“I heard stone move after the steam rose.”

Song opened one eye.

“Beast?”

“I do not know.”

“Team?”

“I do not know.”

Yan’s gaze stayed on the rain.

They had no score worth stealing yet. That did not make the rule harmless. A team did not need to know a number before deciding a visible Fire Phoenix might be worth following.

“Then we do not decide for it,” he said. “We decide our next ground.”

No one argued.

The night passed in short pieces.

At first light, the rain had weakened to a dripping breath through the leaves.

Luo was already crouched at the edge of their shelter.

Song rose, shield in hand.

“What?”

Luo did not answer at once.

He pointed toward a patch of mud where the water had begun to drain away.

Their own tracks came from the ravine: Song’s heavier, shield-braced marks; Yan’s lighter steps behind; Luo’s careful prints that sometimes nearly disappeared. Above them, another trail cut across the slope.

The rain had softened it.

It had not erased it.

Three sets of footprints had come down from the upper bank after the crossing.

They reached the edge of the ravine.

Then turned toward Dorm336’s route.

Song’s expression settled into something hard.

“Following us?”

“Maybe,” Luo said. “It could be the same direction. It could be people who saw the steam and wanted to know what made it.”

Yan looked at the faint red stain still caught in one split of bark near the crossing.

The fire had saved the team.

It had also made the team legible.

“That is enough,” Song said.

Yan nodded.

“Enough to change how we move.”


Morning mist lay low among Dorm333’s trees.

Qian woke with more color in his face and immediately reached for the Coin of Summoning.

Lan put out a hand.

“Wait.”

Qian froze.

“We are not using it?”

“It is not that.” Lan looked into the calm forest. “Yesterday was too quiet.”

Qian blinked at him.

“My perception is sharp. I did not sense danger.”

“Not danger,” Liu said. “Unease.”

Qian’s expression said those were close enough to be unhelpful distinctions.

Liu went on.

“We need more than survival. We moved carefully all day. If every other team took a greater risk, our score may be too low. Only ten teams pass. We cannot spend seven days protecting a position that does not qualify.”

Lan looked at him.

That was the thought he had not been able to make comfortable.

“Yes,” he said. “That is it.”

Qian pointed at Liu with slow accusation.

“When did you become the person who understands what Captain is thinking?”

Liu pointed at his own temple.

“I reflected.”

“That is terrifying.”

“Foolish people need luck,” Liu said. “You have plenty.”

“I have enough to avoid listening to you.”

Lan let the argument live for one breath.

Then he stood.

“Today we move faster. Liu opens the path. Qian, keep the Gate ready, but do not spend it without a reason. We take risks after we see them, not before.”

Qian nodded, more serious now.

Liu lowered the White Dragon Spear.

Lan let golden-patterned Blue Silver Grass coil at his fingers.

“This test is watching more than strength,” he said. “Courage. Care. Strength. Adaptability. Luck. We need all of them.”

They entered the mist at a quicker pace.

On the far side of a different stretch of forest, Song lifted his shield.

Luo looked once more at the tracks behind them.

Yan did not open his wings.

Dorm336 moved deeper anyway.

# Chapter 54 — What a Favor Costs

The Gold Silk Ape did not lead them like a friend.

It led them as if the forest had failed to understand an argument it had been having long before three human children arrived.

It moved ahead in short bursts, never far enough that Qian lost the shape of its gold through the leaves, never close enough that Lan’s hand could have reached it without being bitten. The claw marks on its shoulder had been washed clean. They had not stopped being wounds. Every so often its injured arm folded nearer to its body, and every time it did, Qian felt the remembered pressure of that first intent contact tighten behind his eyes.

Not words.

Not trust.

A sharp direction. A hurt place. Hunger pressing behind both.

Liu watched the branches above them instead of the ape.

“It is taking us somewhere.”

Qian rubbed the coin at his palm.

“It is very good at making that sound like bad news.”

Lan did not look away from the animal.

“It may be.”

“That is comforting.”

“It was not meant to be.”

The Purple Zoysia rested inside the fold of Lan’s grass-woven pouch, its dark cap hidden beneath damp leaves and careful hands. Qian could feel the ape notice it every time the wind shifted the scent between them. It did not turn back. It only moved faster.

That bothered him more than a snarl would have.

A snarl had one meaning.

This had a reason.

The forest changed around them by degrees. The ground rose. The trunks grew closer together, their roots folding into one another above a shallow drop where rainwater had carved a dark, dry channel. The ape stopped at the lip of it and went still.

Qian stopped too.

The Gold Silk Ape’s head turned toward him.

The contact came before he chose it.

Three sets of gold eyes in the upper branches.

A flash of spotted fur.

The feel of small hands that had once clung to a larger arm, then an absence too sudden to fit inside a thought.

Qian’s breath caught.

The ape’s anger struck after the image. Not clean anger. Old anger, carried long enough that it had worn a route through everything else.

He lowered the coin.

“There are three,” he said quietly. “It knows them.”

Liu’s grip moved on the White Dragon Spear.

Lan crouched at the edge of the channel. He did not ask Qian to force more from the ape. The animal had already given them more than it owed.

A leaf shifted above.

Then another.

Three lean shapes appeared between the branches across the channel. Gold-eyed Leopards. They were smaller than the Gold Silk Ape, but the way they held the wood made the size feel irrelevant. Their paws gripped bark without sound. Their eyes were gold only when the thin light found them, and each gaze rested on the ape with the cold patience of something that knew it had numbers.

Qian’s shoulders tightened.

“They are waiting for it to jump.”

Lan watched the middle leopard blink once.

“Gold-eyed Stare,” he said. “Do not meet the eyes if they turn toward you. It can lock a body for a breath.”

Qian glanced at him.

“You know that from looking at them?”

“From how the ape refuses to look back.”

The Gold Silk Ape gave a low, ragged call.

The leopards answered with silence.

Liu shifted one foot behind a root.

“We leave?”

The ape struck its fist against the tree beside it. Bark jumped loose.

Qian felt the demand behind the movement before Lan spoke.

“It wants us to help.”

“Help it kill them,” Liu said.

“Yes.”

The answer sat between them with no easy place to put it.

Lan’s gaze stayed on the leopards.

“We help it live through the first exchange,” he said at last. “After that, it chooses what it can still pay for.”

Qian frowned.

“That is not the same thing.”

“No.” Lan’s voice was quiet. “It is the part we can promise.”

For a moment, the ape only stared at him.

Then it climbed.

It did not move toward the leopards in a straight line. It crossed behind a broad trunk, rose through a ladder of roots, and showed them its wounded side as if it had forgotten how much that cost. One leopard dropped lower. A second flowed along a branch above it. The third stayed where it was, watching.

“Qian,” Lan said. “Gate on the ground. Not in the trees.”

Qian’s fingers closed around the coin.

“Short answer?”

“Short answer.”

The Coin of Summoning spun free.

His yellow rings rose under wet leaves, darker than they had once been but still far from the color he wanted. The Gate opened at the channel floor with a sound like air being pulled through a narrow place.

A Ground Fire Dragon Lizard forced its heavy head through first.

Red scales caught the dim light. Smoke slipped from its nostrils as it found its footing among the roots. It was too large for the trees and knew it immediately. Its tail struck a root. Its body turned toward Qian’s pointing hand.

“Stay low,” Qian muttered. “Please understand low.”

The lizard did not understand language.

It understood the three predators above it.

A leopard moved.

Its eyes flashed.

The Gold Silk Ape was already in the air.

For one impossible beat, its body stopped answering it.

Qian saw it happen because the ape’s anger went abruptly blank. Its arms opened. Its feet lost the branch. All three leopards came at once: one from the front, two from either side, claws already reaching for the place where gold fur thinned over its throat.

Lan’s grass snapped upward.

A golden vine caught around the ape’s ankle and pulled down and back.

It was not enough to save it by force. It changed the fall by the width of a hand.

That was enough.

The first leopard’s claws closed on bark. The second struck the empty space where the ape’s neck had been. The third twisted aside before it hit its own packmate, but its landing branch cracked under all that shifting weight.

The Ground Fire Dragon Lizard roared from below.

A ball of fire crossed the channel and burst against the branch in a hard, red flare. Not broad enough to burn the canopy. Not gentle enough to be ignored.

The three leopards scattered.

The ape landed on the far wall, one hand in mud, one knee bent too sharply. For half a breath it looked at the golden vine around its ankle.

Then it looked at Lan.

The vine loosened.

The ape sprang again.

This time it was not bait.

Gold hair rose along its back in a shivering sheet. Qian had seen that hair cut through air before. He had not seen the ape spend so much of it at once.

“Down,” Lan said.

Qian did not ask why. He dropped behind the lizard’s shoulder as the first storm of fine gold needles tore through the branches above them.

Leaves burst apart. One leopard made a sound that did not last long enough to become a roar. Another hit the channel floor hard, rolled, and came up with one foreleg bleeding dark against its spotted fur.

It saw Qian.

The lizard was too slow to turn.

Liu was not.

The White Dragon Spear cut across the space between one breath and the next. It struck the leopard’s shoulder and redirected the leap rather than trying to stop all of its weight. The animal crashed against a root beside Qian, claws scoring wet wood close enough that splinters caught in his sleeve.

Qian stumbled backward.

Liu was already between him and the next line of teeth.

“Gate,” Liu said.

“I am doing the Gate.”

The Ground Fire Dragon Lizard lowered its head and charged. The wounded leopard tried to turn away. A burst of flame struck the earth in front of it, forcing it toward the far wall instead.

The Gold Silk Ape arrived there first.

It caught the leopard at the shoulder. Both bodies hit the mud. The ape’s injured arm shook once as it fought for position, then its good hand found the animal’s neck. Qian looked away before the motion finished.

The channel held only the smell of wet earth, smoke, and torn leaves.

It did not feel cleaner.

Above them, a second pair of gold eyes opened.

Lan’s grass tightened around Qian’s waist and pulled him behind a root just before the stare found the place where he had been standing. Cold pressure washed across the bark. The root whitened along one narrow line.

Qian’s teeth clicked together.

“That was close.”

“It was aimed for close,” Lan said.

The last two leopards had separated. One remained in the branches, moving carefully now that the lizard’s fire had made every lower route dangerous. The other had dropped to the ground beyond the channel, where it circled away from the ape and watched Lan instead.

It had learned.

So had Lan.

He drew silver-patterned Blue Silver Grass through the mud, not toward the leopard’s legs but across the open strip between it and the lizard. Thin ice awls pushed up from the ground at uneven angles. They were not a wall. They were a question with only bad answers.

The leopard chose the branches.

The ape chose the same tree.

Their fight moved too quickly for Qian to follow cleanly. Gold flashed. Spotted fur twisted. A claw caught the ape’s side and opened a new line through the cleaned wound. The ape did not retreat. It drove the leopard into the trunk hard enough to shake every leaf above it.

The third leopard came down from the other side.

Liu saw it first.

“Spear line,” he said.

Lan’s golden grass struck the trunk beneath the leopard’s paws. The bark split. Its landing changed. Liu’s spear met it low, not with the strange force that had ended the bear, but with all the timing and distance he had. The shaft caught under its chest and threw its balance sideways.

The Ground Fire Dragon Lizard’s head slammed into it a heartbeat later.

The lizard’s summon-light had already begun to thin. It held long enough to pin the leopard against the roots with its weight and one smoking foreclaw.

Then it faded back through the Gate.

The leopard was still moving when the light left.

The Gold Silk Ape dropped from the tree.

It did not look at Lan, Qian, or Liu before it crossed the channel. It put itself between them and the last living enemy. Its hair was sparse now. Its breath came in hard, ugly pulls. But when the leopard lunged free of the roots, the ape met it with both arms.

The struggle ended beneath the branches where no one could see all of it.

When the forest finally went quiet, the ape remained crouched beside the three bodies.

No one approached at once.

The ape’s shoulders rose and fell.

Qian could feel the grief coming off it in short, scorching pieces. Not satisfaction. Not even relief. The thing it had wanted was over, and whatever had been missing before the fight was still missing after it.

Lan lowered the pouch containing the Purple Zoysia deeper beneath his grass.

He did not mistake the silence for permission.


The break in the canopy looked small from below.

From the lower ridge, it was a pale scar between two walls of wet green. The sun had moved behind cloud, turning that slice of sky the color of old metal. A loose slope of gray stone climbed toward it, steep enough that one bad step would send a person sliding into the thorn-choked wash at its base.

Luo studied the slope for a long time.

“The top opens into a shallow shelf,” he said. “After that, the ridge bends away. There is a dry watercourse below it. If we reach that, the high ground cannot keep the same angle.”

Song shifted his shield higher on his arm.

“And what is watching the shelf?”

“I cannot tell from here.”

Yan looked at the pale opening.

The instinct to rise moved under his shoulder blades before he made the choice consciously. Wings would give him the answer in seconds. Wings could also make him the only answer, which was how mistakes began.

“I look,” he said. “No fire. I return on your call.”

Song’s eyes went to the slope, then to Yan.

“Not on my call.”

Yan waited.

“On the call we decide now. If you lose the line or we lose sight of you, you come down. No chase because you see something interesting.”

Luo’s mouth moved at one corner.

“That was almost responsible.”

Song gave him a flat look.

“I have been injured carrying responsibility all morning.”

Yan felt the answer he could have made—quick, easy, dismissive—rise and pass without taking shape.

“What is the call?” he asked.

Luo looked up at the break in the leaves.

“One wingbeat down. Two back. Three means we leave whether you like what you saw or not.”

Yan nodded.

Song planted the shield at the foot of the slope, its face angled toward the opening above. It was not a fortress. It was a line that meant Yan had somewhere to return to and Song had somewhere to hold if stone, beast, or stranger answered the movement.

Luo stepped three paces to the right, where the fallen slope narrowed against a black seam in the rock.

“Use the air above that seam,” he said. “The left side is hollow.”

Yan let his wings out only far enough to lift.

Red-gold did not blaze through the forest. The color stayed banked close to the feathers, heat moving inward instead of outward. One beat carried him above the lower brush. A second lifted him through the wet break in the canopy.

For an instant, the forest opened.

The ridge ahead fell away into a winding gray channel choked with stones and low ferns. Beyond it, the terrain folded down again beneath an unbroken roof of leaves. No figure stood on the shelf. No pale strike waited at its edge. Farther back, rain-dark branches moved under wind and nothing more certain.

But the channel existed.

It was a route that did not ask Song to climb an open trunk or Luo to guess which shadow held a person.

Yan turned.

A stone came loose under Song’s boot.

The sound was small.

Luo heard the answer beneath it before Yan did.

“Back,” he called.

The slope slid.

Song drove the shield down. Loose shale poured around its rim in a gray rush. Yan dropped toward the black seam, wings folding before he landed. The stone under his boots gave way at once.

He caught the edge of the shield with one hand.

Song caught his wrist with the other.

For one breath, the whole slope tried to take all of them.

“Right seam,” Luo said, already flat against the rock. “Heat there. Only there.”

Yan saw the narrow line Luo meant. Water had worked into it for years, opening a crack beneath the sliding shale. If it broke wider, the shelf above would follow.

He pressed two fingers to the stone.

A thread of red-gold heat entered the seam.

Not a blast.

Not enough to turn the forest bright.

Just enough to dry the wet fracture and bind the smallest moving edges before the weight above could pull them free.

Steam hissed against Yan’s knuckles.

Song held.

Luo counted under his breath.

The shale slowed.

Then stopped.

Yan climbed the shield’s edge with Song’s grip still locked around his wrist. When he reached stable ground, Song did not let go immediately.

“You saw the route?” Song asked.

“Yes.”

“Worth that?”

Yan looked at the sealed line in the rock, at Luo’s mud-streaked hand still against the slope, and at the channel lying beyond the shelf.

“Yes,” he said. “Because we all know it now.”

Luo rose first.

“The shelf is still bad,” he said. “The channel is better. One at a time. Song anchors. Yan keeps heat off the loose face unless I name a seam.”

No one called it a victory.

They had found one way forward and spent effort proving it could hold all three of them. The high ridge behind them remained hidden by rain and leaves. It might be empty. It might not.

The absence of an answer was not an answer.

But the dry watercourse was theirs to choose.


By the time Lan crossed the channel, the Gold Silk Ape had stopped moving among the bodies.

It sat with its back against a root, one long arm hanging over its knee. Gold fur lay scattered through the mud where it had spent too much of itself. Its chest rose and fell with visible effort.

Qian kept a respectful distance.

“Do I ask if it is all right?”

Liu looked at the torn ground.

“No.”

“Fair.”

The ape reached toward one of the leopards. Its fingers worked carefully through blood-dark fur and broken leaves. When it pulled back, something rested in its palm.

It was small enough to fit there.

A little skull, bright gold beneath the mud. Where empty sockets should have been, two thin golden lenses caught the light.

Qian stared.

“That cannot be normal.”

Lan’s expression changed in the way it did when a piece of the forest stopped being only a problem and became a decision.

“A skull Soul Bone,” he said.

Liu’s hand closed around the spear.

“For us?”

The ape looked from the bone to Lan.

Qian felt no clear offer through the coin. Only uncertainty. The animal had found something strange inside an enemy it hated. It knew the humans had helped it live. It did not know what such a thing meant to them.

Lan did not reach for it.

“This is a simulation,” he said. “We cannot take it outside.”

Qian blinked.

“Then it is still valuable here.”

“To it,” Lan said.

He looked at the ape’s thinning fur, its wounded shoulder, the way its body had begun to sag now that the fight no longer held it upright.

“Tell it that it may be able to fuse it.”

Qian looked at him.

“And the Zoysia?”

Lan’s grass shifted around the hidden purple plant.

“The agreement is still the agreement.”

For the first time since the leopards fell, the ape’s eyes fixed on the pouch.

Hunger passed through the contact so sharply that Qian almost flinched.

He sent the terms back as plainly as he could.

The plant stays held.

Six days remains six days.

The bone is different. It came from this fight. It is yours to choose.

The ape’s fingers tightened around the small skull.

For a long time, it did not move.

Then it touched the bone to its own brow.

Gold light spread outward in a thin, uncertain halo.

Qian took one step back.

The ape’s knees folded. Lan’s grass caught no more than the fall, lowering it against the roots without binding it. The bone seemed to sink into the fur at its forehead. The gold around it faded, returned, and faded again.

“It is sleeping,” Qian whispered.

“Fusing,” Lan said. “Or trying to.”

Liu looked through the trees in the direction they had come.

“We cannot stand here for six days.”

“No,” Lan said.

The answer came too quickly to be cruel.

They made a small shelter with fallen branches and broad leaves, not a cage and not a claim. Liu placed the branches where rain would break around them without closing the path out. Lan cleaned the newest blood from the ape’s side with a water sphere and let the rest of the forest remain the forest. Qian sat beneath the nearest root with the coin resting against his palm, listening for the shallow, uneven rhythm of another living mind without trying to enter it.

Night collected around them.

They kept watch in turns.

No new enemy came.

No promise arrived either.

At first gray light, the ape had not moved beyond the small rise and fall of its chest. A faint gold gleam remained beneath the fur at its brow. The Purple Zoysia still rested in Lan’s keeping.

Qian looked toward the sleeping animal.

“What do we do?”

Lan watched the path ahead, then the way back, then the heavy quiet beneath the roots.

“We do not make its sleep a chain,” he said. “And we do not spend the herb to buy an answer it cannot give yet.”

Liu nodded once.

“So we move.”

“We move carefully.” Lan looked at Qian. “Leave the terms where it can find them when it wakes. Nothing more.”

Qian did not pretend he could make the message beautiful.

He sent only what was true.

Alive.

The plant remains.

The forest has not stopped.

When the ape answered, it was no clearer than the first time: warmth around a dark purple shape, pain beneath gold light, and an instinctive refusal to die beneath roots while the world went on without it.

It was enough to know it had heard.

Lan rose.

Liu took the lead again.

Qian put the coin away last.

On the lower ridge, Yan folded his wings as Song brought the shield up from the stone. Luo stepped into the dry watercourse first, testing the ground that no one else had been able to see from below.

Neither team had come away with a clean answer from the ground it had chosen.

One had left a wounded ape sleeping beneath roots.

The other had left an unanswered height behind them.

Both kept moving.

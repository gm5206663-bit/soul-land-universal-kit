# Chapter 60 — The Price of Keeping

On the fifth evening of the six-day term, the Gold Silk Ape came back for the Purple Zoysia.

It did not arrive through Qian Lei’s Coin.

It came down a rain-dark trunk in plain sight, one hand after the other, moving too slowly for an animal that had once crossed a clearing like a flash of gold. The small skull-bone light at its brow flickered beneath matted fur. Every third step seemed to surprise its own body.

Lan Xuanyu saw it first.

“Stay where you are.”

Liu Feng had already turned, spear low. Qian’s hand closed around the Coin but did not open it.

The ape landed on a root shelf twenty paces away.

Its eyes went to the pouch at Lan’s waist.

The Purple Zoysia lay inside, wrapped in damp leaves and the last of the soil Liu had lifted with it. The plant had survived five days of motion, rain, and careful hands. Its bitter-green smell had become familiar enough that Qian hated noticing it.

The ape took one step forward.

Lan did not reach for his grass.

The animal’s injured shoulder had scabbed over badly. The newer wound from the leopards remained open beneath clotted fur. The flicker at its brow came again. This time the ape’s left hand clenched against empty air as though it had forgotten where the root shelf ended.

Qian watched its feet.

“It is unstable.”

“I can see that,” Liu said.

The ape heard him. Its lips pulled back from its teeth.

Lan raised one hand, palm open.

“The term is not over.”

The words did not need to be understood exactly. The distance, the pouch, and the way Lan stayed between them were clear enough.

The ape’s gaze never left the Purple Zoysia.

Then a cord snapped tight above them.

Lan looked up.

Three barbed lines crossed the windfall clearing where they had stopped to rest. One ran from a high branch to the root shelf behind the ape. Another lay half-hidden in wet leaves near Liu’s feet. The third had been looped around the trunk at Lan’s back, close enough that the first pull tore the pouch from his waist.

The Purple Zoysia flew into the open.

Qian moved.

So did the ape.

A hooked weight struck the root shelf between them. The barbed cord jerked sideways, dragging the plant toward the middle of the clearing.

A voice came from behind the fallen trees.

“Leave it.”

No name. No academy badge in sight. Just a direction delivered by someone who had waited long enough to know exactly what mattered.

Liu’s spear snapped toward the cord around his feet. The shaft knocked the barbs aside before they could close. Lan’s grass shot after the pouch, golden strands catching its corner a handspan before it struck the mud.

The ape landed where the plant had been.

Its skull-bone light flared.

For one breath, every root in the clearing seemed to lean away from it.

“Three people,” Liu said. “High left. Low right. One behind the trunk.”

He did not know that by magic. A boot had shifted on wet bark above them. A cord had tightened in the brush. A shallow breath had caught behind the trunk as the first trap worked.

Lan pulled the pouch toward himself.

The line above him tightened again.

A second hook caught the grass instead of the pouch and yanked hard. Lan’s footing went out from under him. He hit one knee in mud, the Zoysia swinging between the trap’s pull and his own grip.

The ape lunged.

Qian opened the Gate.

The Ground Fire Dragon Lizard forced its red-scaled body into the clearing with a roar, too large for the crowded roots and angry about it at once. Qian pointed between the ape and Lan.

“Hold that ground. Low.”

The lizard lowered its head and planted itself sideways. It did not attack the ape. It did not need to. Its body made a wall of scale and heat where there had been open mud.

The ape struck it anyway.

Gold fur burst from its back. One fist hit the lizard’s shoulder. The summon slid half a body-length through the mud but did not turn.

The skull-bone light at the ape’s brow went wild.

A pressure wave tore through the clearing.

Qian did not feel thoughts. He felt air strike his face and saw the animal’s body seize around the force it had released. The ape’s arm locked. Its knees bent too far. It had not chosen the effect cleanly enough to use it twice.

“Now,” Lan said.

Liu ran.

He did not go for the ape. He did not go for the unseen people either. The cord holding Lan’s grass passed around the low trunk at the right edge of the clearing. Liu drove the White Dragon Spear under it, twisted, and used the entire shaft as a lever.

The cord snapped.

The pouch fell.

Lan caught it against his chest.

A pale force struck the mud where Liu had been standing.

Liu pivoted out of it, but the next line had already come. Barbs wrapped around the spear shaft near his hands and pulled him toward the root shelf.

The people in the brush had adapted. The plant was no longer loose. So they took the one person who could cut the lines closest to it.

Liu did not call for Silver Edge.

He planted his rear foot, turned the spear instead of fighting the pull directly, and let the barbed line drag the shaft free of his palm.

For a moment, the spear belonged to the trap.

Then Liu stepped inside the line.

His elbow drove into the cord where it crossed the root. The barbs tore his sleeve and bit shallowly into his forearm. He caught the cord with his left hand, pulled it down just far enough to make the hidden anchor show, and kicked the stake loose.

The line went slack.

His spear dropped into the mud.

One of the unseen contestants broke cover at last, not to fight Liu, but to reach the freed pouch from the opposite side.

The ape saw the movement.

It did not look at Lan for permission. It did not wait for Qian to touch the Coin.

It launched past the Dragon Lizard in a ragged blur.

The contestant tried to turn. The ape’s good hand caught the person’s shoulder and drove them into the wet ground. A second hunter’s cord snapped around the ape’s waist.

The Gold Silk Ape screamed.

Its skull-bone light flared again.

This time the light did not push outward. It folded back into the ape’s own body. Gold flashed under its fur in thin, broken veins. The animal went rigid over the fallen contestant, one arm raised and trembling.

Lan saw what could happen next before it did.

Not because he could read the ape.

Because it had stopped moving and the other hunter had drawn the cord tight around its ribs.

“Liu,” he said.

Liu had recovered his spear. Blood darkened his sleeve. He looked from the ape to the human pinned beneath it.

There was a clean strike available.

A spear through the ape’s side would end the immediate danger. It might also earn a score. It would turn every word spoken over the Purple Zoysia into something none of them could take back.

Liu drove the spear point into the root beside the cord instead.

The shaft bent. The metal edge cut through wet wood and trapped line together.

The cord broke.

The ape recoiled from the sound as if it had been struck.

The contestant beneath it rolled away, coughing, and disappeared behind the fallen trunk. No one pursued.

The third hunter had moved closer while they watched the ape.

A hand caught the pouch at Lan’s chest.

Lan’s grass tightened around the Zoysia before the fingers could close. The attacker pulled. Lan pulled back. For an instant, the small plant hung between two sets of hands, its root ball shedding dark wet soil into the mud.

The attacker’s grip shifted toward the stalk.

Lan let go of the pouch.

The sudden release carried the other person backward one step.

Golden grass snapped around the pouch instead of the stalk and dragged it low, skimming it under the Ground Fire Dragon Lizard’s planted body. The lizard stamped once, hard enough to throw mud across the attacker’s legs.

The summon-light around it thinned to almost nothing.

Qian felt the Gate pull at the back of his skull.

“Hold,” he said, though he did not know whether the lizard could hear anything but its own fading body.

It held for two more breaths.

Then it vanished.

The pouch landed in front of Qian.

He dropped to both knees, gathered the Purple Zoysia with both hands, and pressed the loosened soil back around its roots. His fingers shook so badly that the first clump fell apart.

A shadow crossed him.

The ape stood between Qian and the clearing.

It was swaying. The skull light at its brow had gone dim, but it had not gone out.

Across the roots, the three hunters had regrouped behind cover. They had seen the plant. They had seen the ape. They had seen enough of the team’s answers to make a second attempt different from the first.

Lan rose slowly, mud on one knee, grass spread low around Qian and the plant.

Liu retrieved his spear. Blood ran along his forearm and dropped into the leaves.

No one gave a speech.

The people beyond the windfall measured the ape’s shaking body, the distance to the Zoysia, and the remaining daylight.

Then one cord slipped free of a branch.

A second followed.

They withdrew through the wet trees without taking the obvious line back to the clearing.

They had failed to take the plant.

They had succeeded in making its existence public.

Qian stayed on the ground until the tremor in his hands slowed.

Lan crouched beside him.

“Roots?”

“Still together.” Qian looked at the clotted soil. “Mostly.”

“Good.”

The ape turned toward the pouch.

Liu lifted the spear.

Lan did not.

The Gold Silk Ape took one unsteady step forward. It stopped at the edge of the grass line. Its eyes fixed on the Purple Zoysia. Hunger made the moment simple. The tremor in its injured arm made it complicated again.

Qian could have raised the Coin.

He did not.

He had already spent what he could ask from it today.

Lan set the pouch on the root between them, still inside the circle of his grass. Not an offering. Not a taunt. A visible truth.

The ape looked from the plant to the three boys.

Then it looked toward the place where the hunters had gone.

The condition had made the plant a promise. The fight had made it a target. The skull-bone fusion had made the ape’s own body unreliable. None of that gave Lan a way to make the animal stay, and none of it gave the animal a clean way to take what it wanted.

The ape’s fingers opened and closed once.

It turned away.

The movement was not a retreat ordered by anyone else. It was not gratitude. It was not forgiveness.

It climbed the fallen trunk, hesitated when the skull light flickered, then forced itself onward into the darkening forest.

Qian watched until gold fur disappeared between the trees.

“Should we follow?” he asked.

Lan looked at the open route behind the ape, then at the Zoysia in Qian’s hands.

“No.”

The answer hurt because it was the only one that kept the agreement from becoming a leash.

Liu wrapped the torn sleeve tighter around his forearm.

“The term is still not over.”

Lan nodded.

“And now other people know what we carry.”

Qian pressed the root soil gently back into place.

For five days, the Purple Zoysia had been a promise they could keep by careful movement.

After the windfall, it was a promise that could cost them a field.

They rose before dark finished closing around the clearing.

No one took the route the hunters had used.

No one followed the ape.

They carried the plant forward anyway.

# Chapter 52 — What the Ground Keeps

The Purple Zoysia did not look important enough to make two wounded soul beasts refuse to leave it.

It stood under the Palm Tree Bear’s foreleg in a clump of torn earth: one dark stalk, a round purple cap, a little mud caught along the edge of its leaves. A careless step would have flattened it.

Nothing in the clearing was careless.

The Gold Silk Ape sprang from a broken branch and came down with both fists aimed at the bear’s blind side. Gold fur rose around it in a bright, hard sheet. The Palm Tree Bear shifted one massive shoulder, took the first impact, and raised its paw.

The air thickened.

The ape’s next landing drove it deeper into the wet soil than it should have. Its knees bent. Its wounded shoulder dipped. The bear’s paw passed through the place where its head had been a moment earlier.

Qian Lei watched from behind a fallen trunk and tightened his fingers around the Coin of Summoning.

“It is using gravity again.”

Lan Xuanyu did not answer at once.

The bear’s V-shaped chest mark gave a slow, uneven turn. Its good eye followed the ape but never lost the ground beneath its own feet. Every time the Gold Silk Ape tried to draw it away from the plant, the larger beast yielded only far enough to strike.

“It is protecting the Zoysia,” Liu Feng said.

“Or using it to keep the ape close,” Lan said.

Qian looked at him.

“Those are different?”

“They change what it does when we enter.”

The Gold Silk Ape landed in a crouch. Three claw wounds ran from its shoulder across its chest. Its breaths were fast. It had speed, anger, and almost no space to use either one. The bear had less speed, but its weight made the whole clearing belong to it.

Qian’s first thought was points.

The second was the plant.

The third, arriving later and therefore feeling more honest, was that the ape was going to be crushed if it tried that same angle again.

“If we help it,” he said, “it might take the herb and leave.”

“It might,” Lan said.

“Then why help?”

Liu did not look away from the clearing.

“Because the bear is stronger.”

“That is not enough.”

“No,” Lan said quietly. “It is not.”

The ape feinted right. The bear’s gravity field pulsed. The animal’s injured front leg buckled as it changed direction. It caught itself with one long arm and snarled low enough that Qian felt the sound in his ribs.

Lan’s gaze narrowed.

“Because if we want the Zoysia, we cannot take it from under both of them without deciding what we are willing to do to either one.”

Qian’s hand went still.

A clean answer would have been easier. Fight the bear. Take the plant. Count the score. Let the injured ape either survive or not.

A clean answer would also have been a lie about what he had just watched.

“The simulation does not make them unreal inside it,” Lan said. “Their pain matters while we are causing it. That does not mean we cannot fight. It means we know what the fight costs before we call it necessary.”

Liu’s jaw tightened.

“The bear will not let us take the herb. The ape will not let us leave with it.”

“Correct.”

“So what is the plan?”

Lan looked at Qian.

Not at the Coin.

At him.

“You contact the ape.”

Qian’s throat went dry.

“It is not one of mine.”

“I know.”

“It is hurt, angry, and has better reasons to hate humans than to listen to one.”

“I know.”

“The last beast I tried to reach through the Gate nearly bit me.”

Liu glanced at him.

“That means it understood something.”

“Thank you, Liu. That is very comforting.”

Lan crouched beside the trunk so that the three of them were level.

“Do not give it words. Do not ask it to trust us. Give it one useful thing: the bear’s next pressure point and an angle it can choose if it wants.”

Qian stared at the clearing.

The ape did not know him. It did not need a human’s plan. Even if the Coin could carry a shape of intent across the distance, the animal might take the thought as a threat or a trick.

“And if it comes for us?”

“Liu makes the line. I pull you clear. We leave.”

Qian saw the part Lan did not say.

The bear could catch them before they turned.

He saw it because Lan did not pretend otherwise.

“I keep the replica ready,” Qian said.

“Only if we need a road more than surprise.”

“Fine.”

Qian closed his eyes.

The Coin’s square opening settled behind his own sight. He did not force a command into it. A command would pretend a relationship that did not exist.

Instead he reached for the clearing’s simplest rhythm.

The bear’s chest mark turning.

The heavy pause before the ground thickened.

The narrow strip behind its left shoulder where the animal could not turn quickly without shifting off the Zoysia.

He sent that shape outward.

The answer came back like a blow.

Pain. Hunger. The raw bright fury of a creature that had bled for too long while something larger refused to leave the thing it needed.

Qian nearly broke the contact.

Under the rage was the rhythm he had sent.

The ape had felt it.

Its gold eyes snapped toward the fallen trunk.

Lan rose into view.

He did not step toward the plant. He released one low note through the clearing, enough for his bloodline pressure to brush the air without becoming a threat. The Palm Tree Bear’s chest mark dimmed for a fraction. The Gold Silk Ape froze.

Then the ape looked back at the bear.

Qian held the angle open.

The Gold Silk Ape leaped.

Gold hair burst from its body. The bear raised one paw. Pale gravity pressure poured across the space between them.

The ape changed direction in midair.

Too slowly.

Lan’s golden-patterned Blue Silver Grass slipped low through the brush and brushed the ape’s rear leg.

Not a tether.

A shift.

The ape’s body moved less than the width of a hand. The gravity field passed beside it instead of through it.

The Gold Silk Ape landed on the bear’s shoulder and drove both fists down.

The Palm Tree Bear staggered backward.

The clearing shook. Mud struck the Zoysia’s leaves.

“Liu,” Lan said.

Liu was already moving.

He crossed the opening in a white line, spear held behind him to keep its shaft out of the bear’s reach. He did not attack. He crouched once beside the crushed roots and lifted the Purple Zoysia with the soil still holding its base.

The Gold Silk Ape saw him.

Its head snapped around.

Qian felt its fury change direction so sharply that his teeth hurt.

Lan’s grass tightened around the ape’s leg. The bright support it had lent left all at once. The creature’s limbs became heavy with its own wounds.

Liu was back at the tree line before the ape could cross the clearing.

The Palm Tree Bear came up from the mud.

Not quickly.

Not finished.

“Back,” Lan said.

They withdrew into the trees with the plant between them.

Qian’s contact snapped shut. His knees weakened. Liu caught his elbow without taking his gaze from the clearing.

The Gold Silk Ape dragged itself upright.

The Palm Tree Bear found its feet.

Neither beast looked at the other now.

Both looked at the Purple Zoysia in Lan’s hands.

Qian swallowed.

“This did not make anything better.”

“It made the next thing visible,” Lan said.

The bear roared.

It charged the ape, not the boys.

The ape tried to move. Its wounded leg answered late.

Qian reached for the Coin again before anyone told him to.

He showed the ape one clear image: the herb, the bear, the human hands holding the herb away from both of them.

Not an offer.

Not yet.

A reason to survive the next few breaths.

The ape’s eyes flicked to them.

Then it threw itself aside as the bear’s paw came down.

Lan’s grass caught the bear’s rear leg at the instant its weight shifted. The animal’s charge twisted. Its paw tore a furrow through mud instead of crushing the ape.

The ape climbed the bear’s shoulder in a blur of gold and pain.

Its arms locked around the bear’s neck.

The huge body bucked once.

Twice.

Then fell.

The Gold Silk Ape remained on top of it, swaying.

The bear still moved beneath it.

Lan looked at Liu.

Liu’s expression did not change, but his right hand tightened around the White Dragon Spear.

“Do you have the line?” Lan asked.

“I have one.”

“That is all we need.”

Silver crescent marks woke beneath Liu’s skin. He did not make the skill larger than it had to be. The pale arc traveled along the spear and crossed the clearing once.

The Palm Tree Bear went still.

Warmth moved through the three boys: the assessment’s recognition, real enough to record a score and empty enough not to make the clearing clean.

Liu lowered the spear.

“I did not like that,” he said.

“You do not have to like it,” Lan answered. “You do have to know why you did it.”

The Gold Silk Ape bared its teeth.

It had lost the only other creature between it and the herb.

It had not forgotten that humans held it.

Qian raised the Coin slightly.

“I am not going to call it a friend.”

“Good.”

Lan set the Purple Zoysia on the ground without releasing the soil around its roots. Golden-patterned Blue Silver Grass spread through the wet leaves. Silver strands followed, laying a clear boundary between the boys and the ape.

Qian sent the only honest terms he could.

Six days.

The herb afterward.

The ape could travel with them where it judged the forest survivable. It could leave if a danger came that neither side could face. No command. No cage.

The answer was not agreement at first.

It was hunger so strong Qian had to brace himself against it.

Then came injury. Wariness. A deep recoil from the bright bloodline pressure Lan had used twice already.

The ape did not trust them.

It did not need to.

It came forward one step and stopped outside the reach of Lan’s grass.

Qian sent the terms again, smaller this time. Choice, not demand.

The ape touched one vine with the back of its scarred hand.

Then it nodded once.

Qian’s breath left him in a rush.

“It will go with us,” he said. “But if something is too strong, it leaves.”

“That is reasonable,” Lan said.

The ape’s eyes stayed on the herb.

Lan lifted a small water sphere and let it drift toward the claw marks on its shoulder. The animal recoiled. The water touched blood and grit, washed both gently away, and drew back.

Its breathing eased by a fraction.

Not trust.

A first return.

Lan took up the Purple Zoysia again.

“Six days,” Qian repeated.

The ape dipped its head.

The clearing smelled of torn roots and blood. A dead bear lay in the mud. The ape was wounded, hungry, and free to leave whenever fear outweighed hunger. Three boys had a plant valuable enough to turn their decision into a promise.

Nothing about it was safe.

That was why the promise had to be real.

# Chapter 55 — What We Leave Open

Luo Haoran heard the runner before he saw her.

He lifted one hand.

Song Yichen stopped with his shield half-raised. Yan Shuo folded the faint outline of his wings beneath his shoulders and listened.

One set of feet.

Too fast for someone choosing a route. Too uneven for someone who expected to reach the end of it.

Then a second rhythm behind it.

Then a third.

Luo touched wet stone beside the dry watercourse.

“Three following,” he said. “One on the right bank. Two lower. The first runner is hurt.”

Song looked toward the channel ahead. It ran under a tilted stone shelf, then split around a curtain of roots. The left split was narrow enough to require a sideways turn. The right side was wider, but it opened directly toward the higher bank.

“Exit?” he asked.

“Left split reaches a lower wash in twenty steps,” Luo said. “One person can take it quickly. A group cannot.”

Yan looked at the root curtain.

“And us?”

“Main channel. If Song holds here. If no one brings the shelf down.”

The runner broke through the ferns.

Her uniform was torn at one shoulder. Mud streaked one side of her face. A dark mark had formed beneath her lower lip. She took three steps into the channel before she saw them.

Her body stopped.

Yan recognized the lifted chin even before the name reached him.

“Yan Shuo?”

For the length of a breath, relief crossed Ye Lingtong’s face.

She erased it so quickly that someone who did not know her might have missed it. Yan did not. The old private warmth she had never given a name to was inconvenient enough on ordinary academy days. Here, bleeding and out of breath in a channel full of bad angles, it was only one more thing she refused to let rule her.

“Move,” she said. “There are three.”

Something scraped across stone on the upper bank.

Yan did not ask whether the people behind her had started the conflict, whether Lu Qianxun and Chang Jianyi were safe, or what would happen if those answers were ugly. The answers would not change the channel before them.

Song set the shield at its mouth.

“Ye Lingtong,” he said, recognition arriving a beat after Yan’s. “Same class.”

“Same route for ten seconds,” Yan said. “After that, she chooses.”

Song’s eyes went from Ye to the root split.

“We do not take on a fourth person we cannot get out.”

“We do not have to,” Yan said. “We give her the exit.”

Luo’s palm flattened against the stone.

“The left split holds one runner. The main channel holds us if Song holds the mouth. That is what the ground offers.”

Ye looked toward the narrow opening.

A pale strike cut down from the right bank.

Song caught it on the shield. White sparks jumped across wet stone. His shoulder drove backward half a step, then held.

Luo turned toward the roots.

“Three steps on the dark stone,” he told Ye without looking at her. “Then sideways. Do not touch the pale bark.”

“What about you?”

“You are not our fourth formation member,” Song said. “You are the person with the route that fits.”

A second pale line came lower, aimed beneath the shield rim.

“Down,” Luo said.

Song dropped the edge. The strike hit stone, scattered into white fragments, and died against the channel floor.

Yan stepped beside him.

Red-gold warmth gathered at his feet. He did not let it rise to the roots or flare above the shelf. The heat followed the wet edge of the channel and stopped well short of the narrow exit, enough to mark where someone would have to cross if they entered.

Ye saw it.

“You can fly,” she said.

“So can mistakes,” Yan answered.

Her mouth tightened, almost against her will.

A loose stone dropped from the shelf. Then another.

The people above were not trying to force Song’s shield directly. They were testing the roots above Ye’s escape, looking for the point where one hard impact would turn the split into a closed wall.

Luo’s hand snapped toward the left side.

“Song, one step in. Yan, heat off the roots.”

Song shifted before the next stone fell. His shield rose over the pale bark. The shelf groaned. Wet earth loosened above them.

Yan lowered the heat further, using it only to dry the slick stone beneath Song’s feet. The shield stopped sliding. The root curtain shook but held.

The easy pursuit had become expensive.

That did not mean it had failed.

A third strike came from the upper bank, not at Song but at the dark stone Luo had named for Ye. The fragment hit and skidded toward her foot.

Ye moved before Yan could.

She kicked off the right wall, let the stone take the place where her heel had been, and caught the curtain with both hands. Her injured leg buckled on the landing.

Yan caught her forearm once.

Not to pull her back toward them.

Only to turn her weight toward the root line before it slid out from beneath her.

She tore free the instant she was balanced.

“I had it.”

“You do now,” Yan said.

The answer was not praise. It was not a claim. That was why it stayed with her for an unwelcome second.

She disappeared sideways through the roots.

The three figures above did not rush the main channel.

One came close enough that Song could hear breath on the other side of his shield. Another moved along the wider path, testing whether the bank gave an angle past Yan’s low boundary. Luo shifted his feet toward the root curtain before the move fully formed.

“There is no fast line through,” he said.

Not a threat.

A fact.

Song’s forearm trembled beneath the shield. He made it still.

The nearest figure stepped back.

For several breaths no one moved.

Then the pressure changed. Stone clicked farther up the bank. A branch bent, released, and stopped making sound. The three presences did not vanish. They simply stopped offering the direct pursuit they had brought into the channel.

“They are re-routing,” Luo said.

“Not gone,” Song added.

“No,” Yan said. “But no longer in front of us.”

The red-gold line faded before it could become a beacon.

“Then we do not chase,” Song said.

“I was not planning to.”

“That was almost funny.”

Luo pointed toward the roots.

“Lower wash. Keep the shield close. They now know how we stand when we have stone.”

Song took the shield free of the ground. A fresh crack ran near its lower rim, too thin to break it and too real to ignore.

“We know something too,” he said.

“That they can throw rocks?” Yan asked.

“That they prefer the ground to decide for them.”

The three moved through the root split.


The lower wash narrowed under a thick canopy. Ye waited at the fork with one hand against the wall, catching her breath without admitting she needed to.

When Song, Luo, and Yan emerged, she looked from one to the next as if gratitude had arrived in the same place as anger and neither could leave first.

“You should not have done that,” she said.

“Probably,” Song answered.

Her gaze snapped toward him.

He shrugged once.

“You were about to get hit. We had an exit. Those are the facts I have.”

“You do not know who they are.”

“No,” Luo said. “Neither do you, apparently.”

Ye’s mouth tightened.

“I was with Lu and Chang.”

Yan waited.

“We found a route along the high ground. Someone was already using it. The first strike cut the ground behind us. Lu wanted to hold the open line. Chang could not move fast through the roots.”

She looked at her torn sleeve.

“I took the lower line because it was the one route that did not put all three of us under the same attack.”

“You did not get lost,” Yan said.

“No.” The word came sharp. “I chose a split. Then they followed me instead of the others.”

The truth did not make her safer. It did make the running hers.

“Why keep moving alone?” Song asked.

“Because if I turned back, I led them to Lu and Chang. Because if I stopped, I made their job easier. Because I am not a message someone else gets to pass along while I wait.”

The words were hard. They were also tired.

Luo pointed down the right-hand wash. It turned under stone cover, darker and slower than the climbing path to the left.

“The wash keeps cover longer. The left route is faster if nobody has taken the height again.”

Ye followed his hand.

“You are telling me to leave.”

“We are telling you what the routes cost,” Song said. “You choose.”

The old answer showed first: lifted chin, sharp breath, the impulse to take the fastest path because accepting the slower one might look like accepting help.

Then Ye studied the torn edge of her sleeve, the mud on her knee, the places the unseen pursuers might have already measured.

“The wash,” she said.

Luo nodded.

“Keep your weight against the right wall. Do not step where the moss shines blue.”

Ye started down the route.

After three steps, she looked back at Yan.

“I did not ask you to save me.”

“I know.”

He did not make the answer a joke, a debt, or a reason for her to stay.

Her eyes held his for an instant. Then she turned away before the private feeling on her face could become someone else’s burden.

No one followed her.

No one asked for a promise.

The wash took her into shadow.

Song watched until her footsteps disappeared.

“Now what?”

Luo touched the new crack in the shield’s lower rim.

“We do not give them that channel twice.”

Yan looked toward the route they had abandoned, then down the darker wash beyond Ye.

The encounter had not added a teammate. It had not settled a feud. It had not made the people above them harmless.

It had cost them a line of ground and shown strangers how their formation breathed.

“Then we build the next answer before they make us use it,” he said.

They turned away from the channel.

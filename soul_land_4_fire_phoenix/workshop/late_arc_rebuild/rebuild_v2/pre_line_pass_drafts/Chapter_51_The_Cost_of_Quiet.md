# Chapter 51 — The Cost of Quiet

By the end of the first day, Qian Lei hated the Gate of Summoning.

He hated the way it emptied him after it closed. He hated the soft, heavy weakness that replaced the clarity in his head. He hated that every good summon made him want to call another one, even when the part of him that understood consequences had begun to shake.

The Ground Fire Dragon Lizard had opened their first path. The next summons had been smaller: a broad-backed creature that shoved through vines, then a skittish horned beast that fled before it found anything useful.

None of them had lasted long.

Now Qian sat beneath a tree whose roots rose high enough to give them three walls. Liu watched the nearest path with the White Dragon Spear across his knees. Lan stood behind them, looking through the leaves at the forest deepening toward night.

“We went almost twenty kilometers,” Liu said.

“We did not find a single thing worth twenty kilometers,” Qian answered.

“You found several things that wanted to bite you.”

“That is not the same as progress.”

Lan looked down at him.

“It might be.”

Qian groaned.

“That is a terrible captain answer.”

“It is an honest one. We know more now. The outer beasts are weaker. The forest becomes denser and quieter as we move inward. The teams that decide danger means stop will not get far enough to matter.”

Qian pulled his knees closer.

“My summon does not agree with the part about mattering.”

Liu’s gaze shifted from the path to him.

“Then make it stronger.”

Qian looked up.

“That is your whole answer?”

“You want a stronger Gate. You want shorter recovery. You want a summon that lasts when it matters. Your Spiritual Power is part of all of it.”

“It hurts when I push too far.”

“It hurts when you train your spear arm too far,” Liu said. “It hurts when Lan comes back from Teacher Ji’s drills looking like he has been dragged behind a transport. Pain does not make a method correct. It also does not make effort optional.”

Qian’s fingers found the Coin in his sleeve.

“Easy for you. You do not feel your whole head become useless.”

“No,” Liu said. “I feel other things become useless.”

The bluntness made Qian pause.

Liu flexed his right hand once. The silver under his skin did not show, but the movement was careful.

“I received a weapon yesterday. That does not mean I throw it every time I am afraid. I have to learn what it does before it makes a choice for me.”

Lan crouched beside them.

“Qian, no one is asking you to destroy yourself for a faster summon.”

“That sounds like a trap before the next sentence.”

“But if you want to stand in Shrek on your own feet, you cannot keep treating the weakness as a reason never to approach your limit.”

Qian looked away.

The forest had grown quiet enough that he could hear a beetle moving inside a rotting branch.

“Five hundred Spiritual Power,” he said.

Lan waited.

“Rank thirty too. One year.”

Liu nodded.

“That is a goal. Not a complaint.”

Qian pointed at him.

“You are unbearable after getting a Soul Bone.”

“I was unbearable before.”

Lan’s mouth curved.

Then all three of them heard the rustle.

Not close.

Not one body.

The sound moved through the low growth in a broad, uneven front.

Liu was standing before Qian finished rising.

“Up.”

Lan caught Qian’s arm. They climbed the nearest tree without argument. Qian’s weakness made the first branch feel farther away than it had any right to be. Liu waited beneath him until his foot found bark, then followed last.

The horde passed below.

Small horned bodies moved between the roots in dozens. Their hooves struck leaves in a fast, nervous rhythm. Something larger followed behind them, unseen, driving the smaller beasts onward.

Qian held his breath.

A branch bent near Lan’s shoulder.

The larger thing passed beneath them in a rush of dark fur and heavy limbs. It did not look up. It only wanted the horde moving ahead of it.

After it was gone, the silence came back wrong.

Qian released the breath he had been holding.

“That was close.”

“Not close enough to count as a fight,” Liu said.

“Thank you. I feel better.”

Lan’s gaze stayed on the trail the horde had made.

“We move at first light.”

“Carefully,” Qian said.

Lan did not answer at once.

“Faster,” he said.

Qian turned toward him.

Lan looked into the black forest beyond the branches.

“If all we do is avoid danger, then other teams take the deeper ground while we congratulate ourselves for surviving the edge. Tomorrow, we take risks we can price.”

The answer frightened Qian.

It also made him feel less tired.


Dorm336 did not sleep beneath the stone lip.

The steam column from the basin had drifted north before fading, but the forest did not forget direction as quickly as people wanted it to.

Song sat with his shield against a low boulder. The metal still bore a dark wet smear where the basin creature had struck it. Luo had washed mud from his palms in a trickle of cold water. Yan stood a few paces away, listening to the trees.

“No fire,” Song said.

Yan did not turn.

“I know.”

“That was not an accusation.”

“It sounded like one.”

Luo looked up from his hands.

“Both of you are tired.”

Song’s mouth flattened.

“That is an observation.”

“It is also why someone will make a bad call if we keep pretending the basin did not change anything.”

The forest below the lip had been quiet for an hour. Quiet was not proof.

Yan looked toward the north, where the steam had gone.

“It changed something.”

Song waited.

“It told anyone close enough that a heat-user crossed there. Maybe it told them more.”

“Then we move,” Song said.

A thin sound cut through the dark.

Not a branch.

A cord.

It snapped around Song’s shield rim and pulled hard.

Song rose on instinct. The shield took the force. A black line vanished into the trees to his left, taut enough to bite into metal.

“Down,” Luo said.

Song dropped the shield edge.

A second cord passed over it, aimed where Yan’s throat had been.

Yan folded sideways. The line struck the boulder and wrapped around it with a dry hiss.

Luo was already moving toward the water trickle.

“Two,” he said. “One left of the cedar. One lower.”

A voice came from the dark.

“Fire Phoenix.”

It was young. It tried to sound older.

Song tightened his grip.

“Wrong route,” he called back.

“You made a route.”

The first cord jerked again. Song let it pull the shield half a step toward the cedar.

Yan watched the line angle through the dark.

“They want the shield turned.”

“So?” Song asked.

“So the second one can take your open side.”

A pale shape moved at the lower edge of the boulder.

Song swung the shield down. The shape withdrew before the rim reached it, leaving a wet scrape across the stone.

No ring colors. No academy mark. No name.

But not nothing.

They knew the basin had announced a heat-user. They had followed the most obvious route and chosen the teammate who could make the formation turn.

Luo pressed one hand to the ground.

“The boulder narrows behind us. The trickle cuts under it. One person can pass. Shield cannot.”

Song looked at the narrow black gap.

“Then we do not pass.”

“They are waiting for the front,” Luo said. “The gap is not for all of us.”

Yan’s eyes moved from the cords to the wet stone around the boulder.

He understood.

“No flare,” Song said.

“I know.”

“Say the cost.”

Yan looked at him.

The words from the office returned in the worst possible way: say what it costs before you use it.

“Heat the cord anchors,” Yan said. “Not enough to burn the forest. Enough to make them let go. It will light this side of the ridge for a moment.”

“And then?”

“Then they know the basin was us.”

“They already think it was us.”

“They will know.”

Luo’s fingers traced the cold trickle toward the gap.

“One at a time. Yan goes last. Song gives me the shield only after the cords slacken. If they rush, I cannot call a clean path.”

The black line tightened again.

Song felt the pull in his shoulder.

“Do it,” he said.

Yan placed one hand against the wet stone.

The heat did not rise in a wave.

It traveled low.

A red line moved through the puddles toward the cords. Steam lifted in narrow hissing threads. For one breath, the forest beyond the boulder glowed from below.

Someone swore in the dark.

The left cord went slack.

Song tore the second free before it could tighten again.

“Luo.”

Luo slipped into the gap beneath the boulder, shoulders turned sideways. Song crouched behind him and held the shield over the opening. A pale strike clipped the shield’s upper edge and sent a shock through his arm.

Yan did not answer it with more fire.

He stepped into the open long enough that the red line on the wet ground drew every eye toward him.

The two figures moved.

That was what Luo needed.

“Now,” he said from inside the gap.

Song shoved the shield into the narrow opening. Luo caught it from the other side. Song followed, scraping his wrapped shoulder against stone. Yan came last, folding his wings tight enough that the rock tore the edge of one feathered silhouette from his back before he was through.

The boulder blocked the next strike.

For a few seconds, no one spoke.

On the far side, the watercourse fell away under ferns. They could move downhill, but the red steam behind them would keep hanging in Song’s memory.

Yan looked once toward the blocked gap.

“They saw enough.”

Song adjusted the shield on his arm. His shoulder burned.

“Then next time they will bring a better answer.”

Luo nodded.

“And next time, we do not let them choose the ground first.”

They left the boulder behind.

The people who had followed the steam did not follow through the gap.

That was not a victory.

It was information both sides had paid for.

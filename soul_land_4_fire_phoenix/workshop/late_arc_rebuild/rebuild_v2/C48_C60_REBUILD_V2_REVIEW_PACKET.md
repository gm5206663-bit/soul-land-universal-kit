# Chapter 48 — The Line We Keep

The number had vanished from the testing screen.

Lan Xuanyu could still see it.

Five hundred and five.

The room had not grown brighter after the result. It had grown sharper. The faint whir inside the wall panel separated from the hum of the lights. Dust crossed the stripe of afternoon sun in small, individual turns. Even the silence across the desk had an edge.

Yin Tianfan watched him notice it.

“Spirit Sea,” he said.

Lan did not answer immediately. He was afraid that if he did, the word would become too large for the room.

Yin leaned back in his chair. His hands rested over his stomach, familiar and loose. Only his eyes were not amused.

“Now that you can see a little farther,” he said, “you need to learn what not to mistake for an answer.”

Lan’s fingers settled on his knees.

Outside the office, Ji Hongbin had taken Qian Lei and Liu Feng away. Qian had tried one hopeful question about the Lucky Wheel before Ji told him to use his breath for training instead. The door had closed on Qian’s complaint.

Yin waited until the corridor was quiet.

“When I was at Shrek,” he said, “I thought I understood command.”

Lan looked up.

“I was good at it. Better than good. I could find the weak place in a formation before most people admitted one existed. I knew when an enemy wanted to be chased. I knew when fear made someone look at the wrong screen.”

His thumb touched the table once.

“People called that talent. Naturally, I believed them.”

Lan had heard Teacher Yin boast before. This did not sound like boasting.

“My final examination was a fleet exercise. Three students. Three fleets. We were supposed to function as one formation.”

“You were in command?”

“I behaved as though I was.”

The answer landed harder than yes.

Yin looked toward the window behind Lan. The academy courtyard beyond it was crowded with younger students changing classes. Their voices reached the office as a thin, harmless noise.

“A coalition formed against us. Three other groups. We were outnumbered before the first signal sounded.”

“And you won?”

“I made my two teammates hold the places I named. I told them when to draw fire. When to retreat. When to let a hostile fleet believe it had found a weakness.”

Lan could picture it too clearly: three formations spread across black space, a screen full of numbers, someone waiting for an order that would decide whether a ship lived long enough to receive the next one.

“Were they willing?” he asked.

Yin’s eyes returned to him.

“They trusted me.”

That was not the same answer.

“The coalition lost ships. More than they expected. Eventually they asked to negotiate. They offered to cooperate against the remaining students.”

Lan knew what came next before Yin spoke.

“You agreed.”

“I said I agreed.”

Yin’s mouth barely moved.

“People loosen a formation when they believe a fight has changed shape. They stop looking through every viewport for the next killing line. I waited for that.”

The office felt smaller.

“Then I destroyed them.”

Lan did not look away.

“They had begun as enemies,” Yin continued. “There was nothing strange about defeating them. But my own teammates were angry. They had taken losses because they trusted my plan. They asked why I had broken my word after we had already won the field.”

“And what did you say?”

“That war did not reward people for being kind.”

Yin’s hand closed.

“I said a commander should use every opening he could see.”

Lan waited.

“At eighteen, I was very calm when an enemy challenged me. I was not calm when the people beside me did.”

The courtyard voices blurred into the distance.

“I turned my fleet on theirs,” Yin said. “They were already damaged. They could not stop me.”

For a few breaths, Lan could hear nothing but the wall panel’s steady hum.

“They were your allies.”

“They had been.”

Yin did not soften the words.


Song Yichen chose the chair nearest the door.

The choice irritated him immediately. It was a chair. It did not need choosing. But the room had three chairs, one long table, a recorder in front of Mu Zhongtian, and no wall worth putting at his back.

His shoulder tugged beneath its wrap when he settled.

Luo Haoran sat beside him, elbows carefully off the table. His fingers tapped against his knee once, twice, then stopped when he caught Song looking.

Yan Shuo sat across from them. In a field, Yan’s presence seemed to make space bend around him: copper-red hair, straight posture, heat held just under the surface. In the office he looked younger. Not weaker. Just no longer able to let the forest speak first.

Mu Zhongtian opened the recorder.

“No speeches,” he said. “I need the route while it is still honest.”

Song nodded. That helped.

Mu’s gaze went to him.

“When the ridge failed, why did your shield hold?”

Song glanced at his shoulder.

“It did not hold all of it.”

Mu waited.

“Luo called the line. Yan made the Dragon turn its fire-side away from the crack. I held the part that would have folded on us.”

“Only held it,” Luo murmured.

Song gave him a look.

“You know what I mean.”

“I do. Teacher Mu needs the whole answer.”

Luo’s fingers stopped moving.

“The heat changed the rock before the body hit,” he said. “The fire made one seam answer early and another late. If Song had braced where the first vibration came from, he would have taken empty force. The real break would have opened behind him.”

Mu noted it down.

“And Yan?”

For an instant, all three of them watched Yan.

Rank thirty-nine. Spirit Sea. Three purple rings. Fire Phoenix.

Song had heard adults say the words in lower voices ever since the result. Not admiration exactly. Calculation.

“If the record says Yan did everything,” Song said, “it is wrong.”

Yan’s eyes shifted toward him.

Song kept going.

“His fire made the opening. It did not stop the Dragon’s body. Luo found what would break. I took the force that remained. We used one opening together.”

Mu’s expression did not change. But he set the recorder down.

“Would you use the same route for points?”

Song frowned.

“Against another Dragon?”

“Against any problem where one of you can make the answer larger by making the other two smaller.”

That was a different question.

Luo answered before Song could.

“Not without shorter calls.”

Mu looked at him.

“In the fissure, I had too much to read. Heat, impact, falling stone, the Dragon’s turn, the line Song could hold. I kept explaining every reason. By the time I finished a sentence, the next line had changed.”

“You kept us alive,” Song said.

“I also almost called Yan into a place that had stopped being safe.”

Yan rested one hand on the table.

Luo met his eyes.

“If I say hold, you hold. If I say cut, you stop feeding the seam. If I say out, no one asks about points until we are out.”

Song felt the words settle where the last few days of noise had been.

Yan nodded once.

“Hold. Cut. Out.”

“That is not the whole answer,” Song said.

Yan’s gaze did not leave him.

“No.”

The quiet lengthened.

“If I have an answer that makes the field easier for me and worse for both of you,” Yan said, “I say what it costs before I use it. You can tell me to pull back.”

Song’s first reply came too fast.

“You do not need permission to fight.”

“I know.”

“Then do not make it sound like you do.”

Yan’s mouth tightened, not in anger.

“I am saying you are not pieces I spend because I am stronger.”

Luo looked toward the window.

Song’s shoulder hurt. The pain felt cleaner than the thought underneath it.

“We survived as three,” Yan continued. “I will not learn the wrong lesson from that.”

Mu picked up the recorder again.

“Good,” he said. “Keep the rule when no teacher is listening.”

Ji Hongbin pushed away from the wall.

“The next assessment starts in three days. Recover. Train. Learn whether those calls mean anything when you are tired and no one is impressed by your last score.”

Song stood.

“Teacher.”

Luo rose beside him.

“Hold,” he said.

Song answered, “Cut.”

Yan looked from one to the other.

“Out.”


Yin Tianfan was still watching Lan.

“The exam ended,” he said. “I ranked first.”

Lan said nothing.

“Shrek expelled me.”

The word did not arrive with anger. It arrived as something carried so long its edges had worn smooth.

“I thought the academy was wrong. It was a simulation. No one had died. I had defeated stronger numbers with better tactics.”

“What did your teacher say?” Lan asked.

“That deception against enemies was part of war. An ambush. A false retreat. A lie that saved the people behind you. None of that made a commander unfit.”

Yin’s gaze lowered to his folded hands.

“But a commander who destroyed allies because they questioned him was not a commander. He was a danger with a fleet.”

Lan felt the sentence settle beside everything Yin had taught him in the simulator.

A trick could save someone.

A trick could also become the only thing a person knew how to do.

“I did not accept it for a long time,” Yin said. “I lost Shrek. I lost the faculty I wanted. Later I found the teammates I had wronged. I apologized. After that, I joined the military as an ordinary soldier. Twenty years later I commanded a small fleet and held the rank of major general.”

“And then your record stopped you.”

Yin laughed once.

“You listen too well when the story is embarrassing.”

“It matters.”

“It did.” Yin’s amusement faded. “It still does. They called me the Elusive Fox. I had ability. I had a way of fighting. But I had taught everyone exactly what I was willing to do to win.”

Lan looked at the dust crossing the sunlit floor.

“Do you still think Shrek was wrong?”

Yin took his time.

“Some days. Fewer than before. If I had kept going as I was, perhaps I would have won every battle and left nothing worth protecting behind me.”

He leaned forward.

“Ability without a line becomes the disaster it claims to prevent. A line without ability can watch people die and call itself clean. Remember both.”

“I will.”

“Good.”

The heaviness moved out of Yin’s face with suspicious speed.

“You have a real talent for command, Xuanyu. When you reach Shrek, the Space Command System—”

The door opened.

“Silly boy,” Ji Hongbin said. “This is a routine.”

Yin sat up straighter.

“Old Ji. I was speaking from the heart.”

“Your heart has hooks.”

Ji came inside with his arms folded. Dust marked one cuff. His eyes moved from Yin to Lan.

“He tells you about his wound. He lets you feel the shape of his regret. Then he puts a faculty application at the end while you are most likely to say yes.”

“Everything I said was true.”

“Truth can still be arranged.”

Lan looked between them.

Yin’s story was true.

Its warning was true.

The question at the end had been placed carefully too.

That did not make the warning useless. It made Teacher Yin unmistakably himself.

Ji turned to Lan.

“Individual cultivation matters. Mecha and battle armor matter. A commander who cannot survive the danger reaching him commands no one for long.”

“And a Soul Master who wins one fistfight at a time cannot save a fleet,” Yin said.

“No one asked him to decide today.”

“I asked him to consider it.”

Lan rose before their argument found a new target.

“I understand why both of you care,” he said. “But I have not earned the right to choose Shrek’s direction yet. Let me earn the right to enter first.”

Ji nodded.

“That is sensible.”

Yin pointed at him with theatrical severity.

“Think about fleets while being sensible.”

“Think about living through them,” Ji answered.

The door opened again.

Mu Zhongtian stood in it. He looked past their argument.

“Lan Xuanyu,” he said. “Your teammates are waiting in Soul World. It is time to draw your prize.”


Qian Lei had apparently recovered from training the instant the word *prize* reached him.

He stood beside Liu Feng outside the simulation room, hands clasped in front of himself with a seriousness that fooled nobody.

“Sister Ling will be there?” he asked Mu.

“She will.”

Qian’s face did something guarded and hopeful at once.

Liu noticed.

“You look frightened.”

“I look respectful.”

“You look like someone expecting a trap.”

“Respect and caution are closely related.”

Lan arrived beside them. Qian’s attention snapped to him.

“Captain. I trained. I suffered. I am prepared to receive the universe’s apology.”

“You have not been wronged by the universe,” Liu said.

“That is exactly what someone untested by it would say.”

Lan smiled despite himself.

Soul World opened in pink light.

Ling Yiyi waited beyond the transition, hands folded, smile gentle enough to make Qian stop two steps farther away than usual.

“Congratulations,” she told them. “First place on Heaven Luo. First in the Federation at this stage.”

Mu drew in a breath behind them.

The ranking was already known. It sounded different from Shrek.

Lan bowed.

“Thank you, Sister Ling.”

Then he looked up, expression open.

“Does Federation first earn an extra Lucky Wheel draw?”

Qian’s eyes lit at once.

Liu closed his eyes.

Ling looked at Lan for a moment.

“No.”

“If first place earns a draw, then—”

“Then you may decline the draw you have,” Ling said pleasantly. “Would that be fairer?”

Lan shook his head immediately.

“That would not be fair to us.”

“One draw each,” Ling said. “No more.”

“Understood.”

“Of course you do.”

Pink light opened in front of them: a seam, then a doorway without walls. Something quick and colored turned beyond it.

Qian swallowed.

“If luck has a formation, put me somewhere important.”

“The center of discipline,” Liu said.

“A brutal formation.”

Lan looked at both of them.

Yin’s voice remained somewhere behind his thoughts.

Remember the people carrying the plan.

He held out his hand.

“Together,” he said.

The three stepped into the light.

# Chapter 49 — What They Carry

The floor beneath Lan Xuanyu’s shoes did not look like a floor.

It turned too quickly for that.

Bands of color raced beneath the three of them, bright enough to hurt if he stared directly. Pink light enclosed the enormous wheel without forming a wall. There was nowhere to lean, nowhere to mark, nowhere for one person to stand where another already stood.

Ling Yiyi’s voice came from everywhere at once.

“Walk once around. Choose a place. When you stop, your reward will appear. You may not move after that.”

Qian Lei looked down at the colors rushing beneath his feet.

“What are the colors?”

“If I knew that,” Ling said, “it would not be called a Lucky Wheel.”

“That seems deliberately unhelpful.”

“It is deliberately lucky.”

Lan put a hand out before Qian could answer.

“Keep walking.”

Liu Feng had already understood. He moved on Lan’s left, light on his feet despite the unease in his shoulders. Qian stayed between them, trying not to look as though the spinning ground wanted to throw him away.

Lan released a thread of Spiritual Power.

The world did not slow.

It divided.

One band beneath the color carried a clean, fresh feeling that made his thoughts lift. Another pressed against his awareness with something sharp and animal. A third felt blank, as though it swallowed the attempt to understand it.

None of the sensations stayed where it began.

The wheel turned. The possible answers turned with it.

“There,” Lan said. “Qian.”

Qian stopped so suddenly that his hands came up for balance.

Purple light rose around his feet.

For a beat, nothing happened.

Then the pleasant voice spoke again.

“Increase of five hundred years to all current Soul Rings.”

Qian stared down.

His rings appeared one after the other, yellow and yellow again. Their color did not change. Their depth did. The soft gold darkened until both rings looked as though they had been held longer in a furnace.

Qian blinked.

“That is it?”

Liu stared at him.

“You received five hundred years on both rings.”

“I know what she said. I am checking whether it is polite to be happy before something bites me.”

Nothing bit him.

Qian’s grin arrived slowly, then all at once.

“Captain.” He turned to Lan. “My Gate is going to listen to me now.”

“Your Gate listened before.”

“It listened with concerns.”

Ling made a sound that might have been a laugh.

Lan watched Qian’s rings fade. The fresh aura had passed beneath him several paces before he stopped. No pattern held long enough to use. Luck was not a formation after all.

“Liu,” Lan said.

Liu walked three more steps and stopped.

The wheel accelerated.

Lan felt a cold, ominous pressure leap across the shifting bands and settle under Liu’s feet.

Silver light climbed Liu’s legs.

“A Spirit-Pagoda-made two-thousand-year right-arm Soul Bone,” Ling announced. “One supplementary Soul Skill.”

For once, Qian had no joke ready.

A small object appeared in Liu’s open hands. It was pale silver, shaped like a shortened human arm bone, its surface too smooth to look natural. Even at a distance, Lan could feel a faint sharpness against his skin.

Liu did not close his fingers around it.

“Me?” he said.

“No,” Qian said solemnly. “The wheel is testing whether you can carry it with sufficient gratitude.”

Liu’s expression did not change.

“It can test you next.”

“I have already been improved by destiny. Do not be jealous.”

Lan almost smiled.

Then Ling’s voice said, “Lan Xuanyu.”

Lan took one more step.

He stopped over the blank band.

The floor seemed to fall away beneath him. Something distant moved through his awareness, not hostile or kind, only deep. A moment later, a card formed in the air before him.

“Access to the Elementary Spirit Ascension Platform once.”

The three of them returned to the pink training ground in a blink.

Qian looked between the bone and Lan’s card.

“So,” he said, “I become more reliable, Liu becomes dangerous, and Lan gets a place none of us understand.”

“It is a Spirit Pagoda training space,” Mu Zhongtian said from behind them. He had followed them through the portal with Yin and Ji. His eyes rested longest on the silver bone. “An opportunity there can increase ring or bone years through combat. It is expensive. It is limited. And it will not help Lan if he enters it immediately before another qualifier.”

Lan turned the card over in his fingers.

“Then I wait.”

“Correct.”

Qian opened his mouth.

Yin pointed at him.

“No. The answer is not that you all enter it tonight.”

“I did not say that.”

“You made the shape of the thought.”

Liu was still looking at the bone.

Ji Hongbin held out a hand.

“Let me see.”

Liu placed it there.

The letters in the accompanying light were simple enough to understand once Ji read them aloud: **Silver Moon Wolf Right Arm Bone. Silver Edge. A crescent of condensed force through the arm or an external weapon.**

The room went quiet.

Liu’s White Dragon Spear lay across his back. Lan could see the thought travel from the bone to the spear and back again.

A new weapon did not have to be large to change every distance around it.

Yin looked at the three of them.

“The reward belongs to the team. We decide who can use it most safely and effectively.”

Qian’s face tightened before he could stop it.

Liu saw.

“Me,” Liu said.

Yin’s brows rose.

“You are certain?”

“If it suits someone else better, they take it.”

“You won it,” Qian said.

“We won the qualifier.” Liu’s eyes stayed on the bone. “Without Lan, I do not get near this wheel. Without you, we do not get through half the forest. If it gives one of us a better chance to keep the others standing, that is where it goes.”

Lan remembered Yin’s office.

A plan could use a person.

It could also remember that the person had chosen to be part of it.

Ji turned the bone once in his hand. Then he gave it back to Liu.

“It is made for your arm and your spear. You will fuse it. We will make sure you survive the lesson.”


The first lesson was pain.

Liu sat on the floor of Yin’s office with his right sleeve pushed above the elbow. The bone rested in his palm. Lan stood close enough to help. Qian stood farther back, which was a brave choice for someone who had admitted twice that he did not enjoy watching other people hurt.

“Focus,” Ji said. “Soul Power into the bone. Follow its pull. Do not fight the sensation without thinking.”

“That is not the same as not fighting it,” Liu said.

“No.”

Liu drew a breath and released the White Dragon Spear.

Silver light answered.

The bone dissolved into it.

Liu’s hand closed hard enough that his nails cut into his palm. His arm swelled under the skin as silver lines raced from wrist to shoulder. The first sound he made was small. The second he swallowed.

Qian’s hands curled at his sides.

“Is it supposed to—”

“Yes,” Ji said.

The third pulse bent Liu forward.

Lan moved before he had decided to. Golden-patterned Blue Silver Grass wound around Liu’s arm, not tight enough to restrain it, only close enough for the warmth of Lan’s bloodline to meet the silver force directly.

Pain struck up Lan’s own arm at once.

Not the same pain. An echo of it.

He held on.

Liu’s spear gave a clear, sharp sound. The silver current around his arm hesitated. Its wild shine dimmed enough that he could breathe again.

“Do not let it take the whole arm,” Lan said.

Liu’s face had gone white.

“It is trying.”

“Then make it yours.”

Liu looked at him once.

He did not say thank you. There was no room in him for it.

He closed his fingers around the handle of the White Dragon Spear.

The silver lines slowed.

Minutes passed without anyone pretending they were short. Sweat darkened Liu’s hair. The spear’s white light curled around his right arm in thinner and thinner threads until the swelling began to ease.

At last, the silver settled into crescent markings beneath his skin.

Liu let out a breath that trembled on the way out.

Ji crouched beside him.

“Soul Power?”

Liu focused. His eyes widened.

“Almost twenty-nine.”

“Good. You will not use the skill because it is there. You will learn what it costs before you let it decide anything for you.”

Liu nodded.

Qian came closer at last.

“That looked terrible.”

“It was.”

“Then I am glad it was you.”

Liu’s mouth moved at one corner.

“Honest.”

“Always.”


No pink light waited for Dorm336.

By the end of the second preparation day, Song had stopped expecting one.

The three of them stood in an unused practice yard behind the dormitory. Evening had made the flagstones cool. Yan’s heat lay in a narrow strip across the ground, not fire, not even a visible glow until the air above it bent.

Song placed the edge of his shield over the strip.

The metal warmed. His forearm warmed with it.

“Too wide,” he said.

Yan lowered the heat.

Luo watched the thin distortion climb the stones.

“Now it disappears before it reaches the wall,” he said. “That means no one on the far side reads where it began.”

Song shifted the shield.

“And if I have to move?”

“Then the line moves with you,” Yan said.

“That is how someone notices it.”

Yan did not answer quickly.

In the field, every useful answer seemed to have a cost. The trouble began when one person decided everyone else could afford it.

Luo walked to the practice yard’s far edge. A row of old posts stood there, half buried in grass. He pointed to the third.

“If the heat reaches that post, it is visible from the dormitory path.”

Song looked at Yan.

“What do we do if a fast exit needs more than the line?”

Yan’s wings stirred once beneath his shoulders.

“We say it.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the first answer.” Yan faced him. “You call hold if you can make ground. Luo calls cut if the heat is making the route worse. I call out if the only way through makes both of you targets.”

Luo’s gaze stayed on the posts.

“And if all three calls disagree?”

For the first time, Yan smiled without ease.

“Then we do not pretend the answer is clean.”

Song held his shield over the faint warmth until the metal cooled.

It was a hard limit rather than a solution. At least all three of them could see it.

The next morning, the academy led them toward the simulation rooms.

Six Heaven Luo teams waited in the corridor. Lu Qianxun stood with Ye Lingtong and Chang Jianyi behind Dorm333, all three trying not to look as though the previous round had rearranged the room around Lan.

Ji Hongbin faced them.

“Your first result does not carry you through the next one,” he said. “Listen to the rules. Then make your own decisions.”

The pods opened.

Liu flexed his right hand once. The silver beneath his skin did not show.

Qian touched the Coin at his sleeve, then looked at Lan.

“Do I look more reliable?”

“No,” Lan said.

Qian sighed.

“You are all cruel to growing talent.”

They entered the pods.

# Chapter 50 — Two Maps of the Forest

The rule voice carried no age, only indifference to whether anyone understood it in time.

“Teams will enter separately. Each team’s points are its own. The final blow on a soul beast grants the corresponding score. Eliminating another participant grants half of that participant’s accumulated score. The remaining half stays with the eliminated participant’s team. The assessment lasts seven days.”

The light above the simulation field brightened.

“Go deeper. Survive. Choose.”

Lan Xuanyu listened without moving.

Around him, five other Heaven Luo teams shifted their feet. The assessment rules gave them one comfort and took it away in the same breath: they would not begin by fighting one another, because they would not begin together.

Lu Qianxun stood behind Lan with Ye Lingtong and Chang Jianyi. He met Lan’s eyes once and inclined his head.

If they met later, that gesture might matter.

If they did not, it was only a gesture.

Ji Hongbin stepped in front of the six teams.

“Cooperation is possible if you earn it,” he said. “No one is ordered to surrender their score or their judgment. Lan Xuanyu may take overall command if the situation allows it. Until then, every team survives on its own ability.”

His gaze stopped briefly on Dorm336.

Yan Shuo’s face did not change. Song Yichen’s hand rested against the rim of his shield. Luo Haoran had already begun watching the floor, as though a pattern might be hidden in the light beneath their shoes.

Ji looked back at everyone.

“Do not confuse the last round’s result with safety.”

The countdown began.

Lan found Qian’s sleeve. Liu found the other side of the formation without being told.

Three.

Two.

One.

The world turned white.


Lan landed in a crouch.

Forest closed around him before his eyes had finished adjusting. Old trunks climbed out of the ground in every direction. Vines crossed between them at shoulder height. The canopy was so dense that daylight arrived green and broken.

Qian breathed out beside him.

“Please say this is the easy part.”

Liu had already released his White Dragon Spear.

“It is not.”

Lan pulled them behind the nearest tree. Qian went in the middle automatically. Liu held the outer angle. The formation was small enough that it did not need a speech.

“First, we learn where we are,” Lan said.

“No map?” Qian asked.

“No map. No score display. No promise that the nearest danger is the one we can see.”

Liu glanced up into the canopy.

“Scout radius?”

“Three hundred meters. Do not chase anything. If you hear a fight, return before you decide what it means.”

Liu nodded once and vanished through the roots.

Qian looked at the Coin in his palm.

“Summon?”

“Not until we know whether something is already watching us.”

Qian’s rings rose when he focused. Both were yellow. Both were darker than they had been yesterday, their color carrying the uncomfortable reminder that a reward did not make the forest kinder.

Lan released a few silver-patterned strands into the undergrowth. Ice awls formed low around the tree, not as a wall, only enough to punish anything that rushed them straight.

His new Spirit Sea spread farther than it had before, offering a scattered map of damp roots, small lives under bark, and wind moving over leaves.

Nothing had found them yet.

Liu returned quickly.

“Nothing large close. The ground rises north. There is a broken vine line east. I found tracks but not fresh enough to tell team or beast.”

Lan considered the light. The forest felt old rather than empty.

“The rules said go deeper,” he said. “The stronger beasts will tell us whether we are moving correctly. We do not rush the first opening we see.”

Qian grinned faintly.

“Which means we rush the second?”

“It means we make the first opening.”

He nodded toward the Coin.

Qian understood.

The Gate of Summoning opened with a circular pull in the air. Smoke rolled across the roots. A heavy red head pushed through.

The Ground Fire Dragon Lizard hit the forest floor with both forefeet and shook itself hard enough to send wet leaves flying.

Qian pointed toward the north.

“Path. Low path. Do not decide you are a tree.”

The lizard did not understand the words. It understood the gesture and the thicket ahead. It pushed forward, scales brushing vines aside. When a thick plant whip lifted toward its face, the lizard opened its mouth and sent a fireball through the leaves.

It showed its wounded side as it crossed a ladder of roots, making itself look slower than it was. One leopard dropped lower. A second flowed along the branch above it. The third remained still, gold eyes fixed on the ape’s next movement.

“Qian,” Lan said. “Gate on the ground. Short.”

Qian held the Coin out.

His rings rose, yellow darkened by the Lucky Wheel’s gift but still yellow. The Gate opened at the channel floor with a pull of air. The Ground Fire Dragon Lizard forced its heavy head through, red scales scraping root and stone as it found its footing.

“Stay low,” Qian murmured. “Do not become a tree.”

It answered the scent of three predators above it by lifting its heavy head.

A leopard moved.

Its eyes flashed.

The Gold-eyed Stare caught the ape midair. Its limbs opened. Its feet lost the branch. The other two leopards came from either side, claws already aimed for its throat.

Lan’s golden grass snapped upward.

It caught the ape’s ankle and shifted the fall backward by the smallest possible measure.

That was enough.

The first leopard landed on bark. The second crossed empty air. The third twisted away from its own packmate, but the branch cracked under all the altered weight.

The Ground Fire Dragon Lizard roared.

A fireball crossed the channel and burst against the branch in a short red flare. It did not set the canopy alight. It did make every leopard move.

The ape hit the far bank, rolled, and came up on one knee.

For a moment it looked at the grass around its ankle.

Then it looked at Lan.

Lan released the vine.

The ape leaped again.

Gold hair shivered from its back in a hard bright sheet.

“Down,” Lan said.

Qian dropped behind the lizard’s shoulder as fine golden strands shredded leaves overhead. One leopard cried out. Another fell through branches and struck the channel floor hard, its foreleg bent wrong beneath it.

It saw Qian.

The lizard was too slow to turn.

Liu moved first.

The White Dragon Spear crossed the space in one clean line. He struck the leopard’s shoulder and redirected it into the root beside Qian. Claws scored wet wood close enough to send splinters across Qian’s sleeve.

“Gate,” Liu said.

“I am doing it.”

The Ground Fire Dragon Lizard lowered its head and charged. A burst of flame hit the mud ahead of the wounded leopard, forcing it away from the tree line instead of toward it.

The Gold Silk Ape arrived at the far wall first.

It caught the leopard at the shoulder. Both bodies hit mud. The ape’s injured arm shook once. Its good hand found the animal’s throat.

Qian looked away before the movement ended.

A cold line passed through the roots near his cheek.

Lan’s grass tightened around his waist and pulled him behind the trunk. The Gold-eyed Stare struck the bark where he had been standing. Frost-white pressure crawled along one shallow groove in the wood.

“That was aimed for close,” Lan said.

The remaining leopards had separated.

One held to the branches, careful now that fire had made the lower routes dangerous. The other circled on the far side of the channel, watching Lan instead of the ape.

It had learned.

Lan sent silver-patterned Blue Silver Grass across the open strip between it and the lizard. Uneven ice awls rose from the mud, not a wall, only ground that would punish a straight leap.

The leopard chose the trees.

The ape chose the same trunk.

Their fight became glimpses: gold, spotted fur, bark struck hard enough to shake wet leaves loose. A claw reopened the ape’s shoulder. The ape drove the leopard into the trunk and held it there with both arms.

The third leopard came down from the opposite side.

“Spear line,” Liu said.

Lan’s grass struck the trunk beneath its paws. Its landing changed by half a step. Liu met it low with the spear, throwing its balance sideways.

The Ground Fire Dragon Lizard’s head slammed into it a heartbeat later.

The summon-light around the lizard had thinned almost to nothing. It held long enough to pin the leopard against the roots with one smoking foreclaw.

Then the Gate took it back.

The leopard was still moving.

The Gold Silk Ape crossed the channel before anyone else could.

It put itself between the boys and the last enemy. Its hair was sparse. Its breath had become ragged. But when the leopard lunged free of the roots, the ape met it with both arms.

The struggle ended under the branches where none of them could see all of it.

When the forest went quiet, the ape crouched beside three still bodies.

Qian kept the Coin lowered. Grief showed in the way the ape’s shoulders rose and fell, the way one long hand passed once over the smaller gold-furred shape on the far bank before returning to its own wound.

The favor had done what Lan promised.

It had kept the ape alive through the first exchange.

It had not made the cost small.

The ape drew something from the fur near one leopard’s head.

A small skull rested in its palm, bright gold beneath mud. Thin golden lenses filled the places where eyes should have been.

Lan’s expression changed.

“A skull Soul Bone.”

Liu’s grip tightened around the spear.

“For us?”

The ape looked from the bone to Lan.

The ape held the skull bone toward Lan. The question required no Coin.

Lan left his hands at his sides.

“This is Soul World,” he said. “We cannot take it outside.”

“It is still valuable here,” Qian said.

“To it,” Lan answered.

He looked at the ape’s thinning fur, its reopened shoulder, the tremor running through its legs now that battle no longer held it upright.

“Let it choose.”

The Gold Silk Ape touched the small skull to its brow.

Gold light spread in a thin, uncertain halo.

Its knees folded.

Lan’s grass caught its fall and lowered the ape beneath the roots without binding it. The bone seemed to sink into the fur at its forehead. The light flickered, faded, returned.

“Is it sleeping?” Qian asked.

“Fusing,” Lan said. “Or trying to.”

The ape had asked for help and paid with what remained of its strength. Now it held an unknown piece of the forest inside its head, too weak to move and too proud to let them turn that weakness into ownership.

Liu looked down the path they had taken.

“We cannot wait here for the rest of the agreement.”

“No,” Lan said.

They built a shelter with an open path. Liu placed fallen branches so rain would break around the roots without blocking the animal’s way out. Lan cleaned the fresh blood from its shoulder with a water sphere and drew his grass back afterward. Qian left a single deliberate message at the edge of the Coin’s reach.

Alive.

The plant remains.

The forest has not stopped.

The answer arrived as warmth around a dark-purple shape, pain beneath the unsteady gold at its brow, and an instinctive refusal to die under roots while the world went on without it.

That was all.

At first gray light, the Gold Silk Ape had not moved beyond the shallow rise and fall of its chest. The Purple Zoysia remained in Lan’s keeping.

“Leave its way open,” Lan said.

Liu nodded.

“And carry the term.”

Qian put the Coin away last.

They left the roots open behind them.

# Chapter 55 — What We Leave Open

Luo Haoran heard the runner before he saw her.

He lifted one hand.

Song Yichen stopped with his shield half-raised. Yan Shuo folded the faint outline of his wings beneath his shoulders and listened.

One set of feet.

Too fast for someone choosing a route. Too uneven for someone who expected to reach the end of it.

Then a second rhythm behind it.

Then a third.

Luo touched wet stone beside the dry watercourse.

“Three following,” he said. “One on the right bank. Two lower. The first runner is hurt.”

Song looked toward the channel ahead. It ran under a tilted stone shelf, then split around a curtain of roots. The left split was narrow enough to require a sideways turn. The right side was wider, but it opened directly toward the higher bank.

“Exit?” he asked.

“Left split reaches a lower wash in twenty steps,” Luo said. “One person can take it quickly. A group cannot.”

Yan looked at the root curtain.

“And us?”

“Main channel. If Song holds here. If no one brings the shelf down.”

The runner broke through the ferns.

Her uniform was torn at one shoulder. Mud streaked one side of her face. A dark mark had formed beneath her lower lip. She took three steps into the channel before she saw them.

Her body stopped.

Yan recognized the lifted chin even before the name reached him.

“Yan Shuo?”

Relief crossed Ye Lingtong’s face.

She buried it as if it were another wound. Yan, of all people. The old private warmth had no usefulness in a channel full of bad angles.

“Move,” she said. “There are three.”

Something scraped across stone on the upper bank.

Yan left questions of blame, Lu, and Chang for later. The channel had already asked enough.

Song set the shield at its mouth.

“Ye Lingtong,” he said, recognition arriving a beat after Yan’s. “Same class.”

“Same route for ten seconds,” Yan said. “After that, she chooses.”

Song’s eyes went from Ye to the root split.

“We cannot carry a fourth person through that split.”

“We open it for her,” Yan said.

Luo’s palm flattened against the stone.

“The left split holds one runner. The main channel holds us if Song holds the mouth. That is what the ground offers.”

Ye looked toward the narrow opening.

A pale strike cut down from the right bank.

Song caught it on the shield. White sparks jumped across wet stone. His shoulder drove backward half a step, then held.

Luo turned toward the roots.

“Three steps on the dark stone,” he told Ye without looking at her. “Then sideways. Do not touch the pale bark.”

“What about you?”

“You are not our fourth formation member,” Song said. “You are the person with the route that fits.”

A second pale line came lower, aimed beneath the shield rim.

“Down,” Luo said.

Song dropped the edge. The strike hit stone, scattered into white fragments, and died against the channel floor.

Yan stepped beside him.

Yan banked red-gold warmth along the wet edge of the channel, below the roots and shelf. The line marked exactly where an intruder would have to cross.

Ye saw it.

“You can fly,” she said.

“So can mistakes,” Yan answered.

Her mouth tightened, almost against her will.

A loose stone dropped from the shelf. Then another.

The people above were not trying to force Song’s shield directly. They were testing the roots above Ye’s escape, looking for the point where one hard impact would turn the split into a closed wall.

Luo’s hand snapped toward the left side.

“Song, one step in. Yan, heat off the roots.”

Song shifted before the next stone fell. His shield rose over the pale bark. The shelf groaned. Wet earth loosened above them.

Yan lowered the heat further, using it only to dry the slick stone beneath Song’s feet. The shield stopped sliding. The root curtain shook but held.

They had bought Ye a breath, nothing more.

A third strike came from the upper bank, not at Song but at the dark stone Luo had named for Ye. The fragment hit and skidded toward her foot.

Ye moved before Yan could.

She kicked off the right wall, let the stone take the place where her heel had been, and caught the curtain with both hands. Her injured leg buckled on the landing.

Yan caught her forearm and redirected her weight toward the root line before it slid out from beneath her.

She tore free the instant she was balanced.

“I had it.”

“You do now,” Yan said.

She carried the bluntness with her for one unwelcome second.

She disappeared sideways through the roots.

The three figures above did not rush the main channel.

One came close enough that Song could hear breath on the other side of his shield. Another moved along the wider path, testing whether the bank gave an angle past Yan’s low boundary. Luo shifted his feet toward the root curtain before the move fully formed.

“There is no fast line through,” he said, stating the fact rather than offering a threat.

Song’s forearm trembled beneath the shield. He made it still.

The nearest figure stepped back.

For several breaths no one moved.

Then the pressure changed. Stone clicked farther up the bank. A branch bent, released, and stopped making sound. The three presences did not vanish. They simply stopped offering the direct pursuit they had brought into the channel.

“They are re-routing,” Luo said.

“Not gone,” Song added.

“No,” Yan said. “But no longer in front of us.”

The red-gold line faded before it could become a beacon.

“Then we do not chase,” Song said.

“I was not planning to.”

“That was almost funny.”

Luo pointed toward the roots.

“Lower wash. Keep the shield close. They now know how we stand when we have stone.”

Song took the shield free of the ground. A fresh crack ran near its lower rim, too thin to break it and too real to ignore.

“We know something too,” he said.

“That they can throw rocks?” Yan asked.

“That they prefer the ground to decide for them.”

The three moved through the root split.


The lower wash narrowed under a thick canopy. Ye waited at the fork with one hand against the wall, catching her breath without admitting she needed to.

When Song, Luo, and Yan emerged, she looked from one to the next as if gratitude had arrived in the same place as anger and neither could leave first.

“You should not have done that,” she said.

“Probably,” Song answered.

Her gaze snapped toward him.

He shrugged once.

“You were about to get hit. We had an exit. Those are the facts I have.”

“You do not know who they are.”

“No,” Luo said. “Neither do you, apparently.”

Ye’s mouth tightened.

“I was with Lu and Chang.”

Yan waited.

“We found a route along the high ground. Someone was already using it. The first strike cut the ground behind us. Lu wanted to hold the open line. Chang could not move fast through the roots.”

She looked at her torn sleeve.

“I took the lower line because it was the one route that did not put all three of us under the same attack.”

“You did not get lost,” Yan said.

“No.” The word came sharp. “I chose a split. Then they followed me instead of the others.”

The truth did not make her safer. It did make the running hers.

“Why keep moving alone?” Song asked.

“Because if I turned back, I led them to Lu and Chang. Because if I stopped, I made their job easier. Because I am not a message someone else gets to pass along while I wait.”

The words were hard. They were also tired.

Luo pointed down the right-hand wash. It turned under stone cover, darker and slower than the climbing path to the left.

“The wash keeps cover longer. The left route is faster if nobody has taken the height again.”

Ye followed his hand.

“You are telling me to leave.”

“We are telling you what the routes cost,” Song said. “You choose.”

The old answer showed first: lifted chin, sharp breath, the impulse to take the fastest path because accepting the slower one might look like accepting help.

Then Ye studied the torn edge of her sleeve, the mud on her knee, the places the unseen pursuers might have already measured.

“The wash,” she said.

Luo nodded.

“Keep your weight against the right wall. Do not step where the moss shines blue.”

Ye started down the route.

After three steps, she looked back at Yan.

“I did not ask you to save me.”

“I know.”

He did not make the answer a joke, a debt, or a reason for her to stay.

Her eyes held his for an instant. Then she turned away before the private feeling on her face could become someone else’s burden.

Ye left with neither escort nor bargain. The wash took her into shadow.

Song watched until her footsteps disappeared.

“Now what?”

Luo touched the new crack in the shield’s lower rim.

“We do not give them that channel twice.”

Yan looked toward the route they had abandoned, then down the darker wash beyond Ye.

The encounter left Ye on an independent route, a crack in Song’s shield, and three observers who had learned how their formation breathed.

“Then we build the next answer before they make us use it,” he said.

They turned away from the channel.

# Chapter 60 — The Price of Keeping

On the fifth evening of the six-day term, the Gold Silk Ape came back for the Purple Zoysia.

It came down a rain-dark trunk in plain sight, one hand after the other, moving too slowly for an animal that had once crossed a clearing like a flash of gold. The small skull-bone light at its brow flickered beneath matted fur. Every third step seemed to surprise its own body.

Lan Xuanyu saw it first.

“Stay where you are.”

Liu Feng had already turned, spear low. Qian’s hand closed around the Coin but did not open it.

The ape landed on a root shelf twenty paces away.

Its eyes went to the pouch at Lan’s waist.

The Purple Zoysia lay inside, wrapped in damp leaves and the last of the soil Liu had lifted with it. The plant had survived five days of motion, rain, and careful hands. Its bitter-green smell had become familiar enough that Qian hated noticing it.

The ape took one step forward.

Lan kept one hand open at his side.

The animal’s injured shoulder had scabbed over badly. The newer wound from the leopards remained open beneath clotted fur. The flicker at its brow came again. This time the ape’s left hand clenched against empty air as though it had forgotten where the root shelf ended.

Qian watched its feet.

“It is unstable.”

“I can see that,” Liu said.

The ape heard him. Its lips pulled back from its teeth.

Lan raised one hand, palm open.

“The term is not over.”

The distance, the pouch, and Lan’s place between them carried the meaning.

The ape’s gaze never left the Purple Zoysia.

Then a cord snapped tight above them.

Lan looked up.

Three barbed lines crossed the windfall clearing where they had stopped to rest. One ran from a high branch to the root shelf behind the ape. Another lay half-hidden in wet leaves near Liu’s feet. The third had been looped around the trunk at Lan’s back, close enough that the first pull tore the pouch from his waist.

The Purple Zoysia flew into the open.

Qian moved.

So did the ape.

A hooked weight struck the root shelf between them. The barbed cord jerked sideways, dragging the plant toward the middle of the clearing.

A voice came from behind the fallen trees.

“Leave it.”

No name. No academy badge in sight. Just a direction delivered by someone who had waited long enough to know exactly what mattered.

Liu’s spear snapped toward the cord around his feet. The shaft knocked the barbs aside before they could close. Lan’s grass shot after the pouch, golden strands catching its corner a handspan before it struck the mud.

The ape landed where the plant had been.

Its skull-bone light flared.

For one breath, every root in the clearing seemed to lean away from it.

“Three people,” Liu said. “High left. Low right. One behind the trunk.”

A boot shifted on wet bark above them. A cord tightened in the brush. A shallow breath caught behind the trunk as the first trap worked.

Lan pulled the pouch toward himself.

The line above him tightened again.

A second hook caught the grass instead of the pouch and yanked hard. Lan’s footing went out from under him. He hit one knee in mud, the Zoysia swinging between the trap’s pull and his own grip.

The ape lunged.

Qian opened the Gate.

The Ground Fire Dragon Lizard forced its red-scaled body into the clearing with a roar, too large for the crowded roots and angry about it at once. Qian pointed between the ape and Lan.

“Hold that ground. Low.”

The lizard lowered its head and planted itself sideways, making a wall of scale and heat where open mud had been.

The ape struck it anyway.

Gold fur burst from its back. One fist hit the lizard’s shoulder. The summon slid half a body-length through the mud but did not turn.

The skull-bone light at the ape’s brow went wild.

A pressure wave tore through the clearing.

Air struck Qian’s face as the animal’s body seized around the force it had released. The ape’s arm locked. Its knees bent too far. The unstable effect would not answer twice.

“Now,” Lan said.

Liu ran.

Liu drove for the cord holding Lan’s grass, where it passed around the low trunk at the right edge of the clearing. He slid the White Dragon Spear beneath it, twisted, and used the entire shaft as a lever.

The cord snapped.

The pouch fell.

Lan caught it against his chest.

A pale force struck the mud where Liu had been standing.

Liu pivoted out of it, but the next line had already come. Barbs wrapped around the spear shaft near his hands and pulled him toward the root shelf.

The people in the brush had adapted. The plant was no longer loose. So they took the one person who could cut the lines closest to it.

Liu planted his rear foot, turned the spear instead of fighting the pull directly, and let the barbed line drag the shaft free of his palm.

For a moment, the spear belonged to the trap.

Then Liu stepped inside the line.

His elbow drove into the cord where it crossed the root. The barbs tore his sleeve and bit shallowly into his forearm. He caught the cord with his left hand, pulled it down just far enough to make the hidden anchor show, and kicked the stake loose.

The line went slack.

His spear dropped into the mud.

One of the unseen contestants broke cover at last, not to fight Liu, but to reach the freed pouch from the opposite side.

The ape saw the movement.

It launched past the Dragon Lizard in a ragged blur.

The contestant tried to turn. The ape’s good hand caught the person’s shoulder and drove them into the wet ground. A second hunter’s cord snapped around the ape’s waist.

The Gold Silk Ape screamed.

Its skull-bone light flared again.

This time the light folded back into the ape’s own body. Gold flashed under its fur in thin, broken veins. The animal went rigid over the fallen contestant, one arm raised and trembling.

Lan saw the danger in its halted body and the cord drawn tight around its ribs.

“Liu,” he said.

Liu had recovered his spear. Blood darkened his sleeve. He looked from the ape to the human pinned beneath it.

There was a clean strike available.

A spear through the ape’s side would end the immediate danger. It might also earn a score. It would turn every word spoken over the Purple Zoysia into something none of them could take back.

Liu drove the spear point into the root beside the cord instead.

The shaft bent. The metal edge cut through wet wood and trapped line together.

The cord broke.

The ape recoiled from the sound as if it had been struck.

The contestant beneath it rolled away, coughing, and disappeared behind the fallen trunk.

The third hunter had moved closer while they watched the ape.

A hand caught the pouch at Lan’s chest.

Lan’s grass tightened around the Zoysia before the fingers could close. The attacker pulled. Lan pulled back. For an instant, the small plant hung between two sets of hands, its root ball shedding dark wet soil into the mud.

The attacker’s grip shifted toward the stalk.

Lan let go of the pouch.

The sudden release carried the other person backward one step.

Golden grass snapped around the pouch instead of the stalk and dragged it low, skimming it under the Ground Fire Dragon Lizard’s planted body. The lizard stamped once, hard enough to throw mud across the attacker’s legs.

The summon-light around it thinned to almost nothing.

Qian felt the Gate pull at the back of his skull.

“Hold,” he said, though he did not know whether the lizard could hear anything but its own fading body.

It held for two more breaths.

Then it vanished.

The pouch landed in front of Qian.

He dropped to both knees, gathered the Purple Zoysia with both hands, and pressed the loosened soil back around its roots. His fingers shook so badly that the first clump fell apart.

A shadow crossed him.

The ape stood between Qian and the clearing.

It was swaying. The skull light at its brow had gone dim, but it had not gone out.

Across the roots, the three hunters had regrouped behind cover. They had seen the plant. They had seen the ape. They had seen enough of the team’s answers to make a second attempt different from the first.

Lan rose slowly, mud on one knee, grass spread low around Qian and the plant.

Liu retrieved his spear. Blood ran along his forearm and dropped into the leaves.

Beyond the windfall, the people measured the ape’s shaking body, the distance to the Zoysia, and the remaining daylight.

Then one cord slipped free of a branch.

A second followed.

They withdrew through the wet trees without taking the obvious line back to the clearing.

They had failed to take the plant.

They had succeeded in making its existence public.

Qian stayed on the ground until the tremor in his hands slowed.

Lan crouched beside him.

“Roots?”

“Still together.” Qian looked at the clotted soil. “Mostly.”

“Good.”

The ape turned toward the pouch.

Liu lifted the spear.

Lan lowered his hand.

The Gold Silk Ape took one unsteady step forward. It stopped at the edge of the grass line. Its eyes fixed on the Purple Zoysia. Hunger made the moment simple. The tremor in its injured arm made it complicated again.

Qian left the Coin at his side. He had already spent what he could ask from it today.

Lan set the pouch on the root between them, still inside the circle of his grass. The visible plant was truth enough.

The ape looked from the plant to the three boys.

Then it looked toward the place where the hunters had gone.

The condition had made the plant a promise. The fight had made it a target. The skull-bone fusion had made the ape’s own body unreliable. None of that gave Lan a way to make the animal stay, and none of it gave the animal a clean way to take what it wanted.

The ape’s fingers opened and closed once.

It turned away.

No one had ordered the movement, and it carried neither gratitude nor forgiveness.

It climbed the fallen trunk, hesitated when the skull light flickered, then forced itself onward into the darkening forest.

Qian watched until gold fur disappeared between the trees.

“Should we follow?” he asked.

Lan looked at the open route behind the ape, then at the Zoysia in Qian’s hands.

“No.”

The answer hurt; anything else would have turned the agreement into a leash.

Liu wrapped the torn sleeve tighter around his forearm.

“The term is still not over.”

Lan nodded.

“And now other people know what we carry.”

Qian pressed the root soil gently back into place.

For five days, the Purple Zoysia had been a promise they could keep by careful movement.

After the windfall, it was a promise that could cost them a field.

They rose before dark finished closing around the clearing and carried the plant forward by a third route.

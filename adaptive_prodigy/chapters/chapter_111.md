## Part 1: The Metal That Lost

Lin Hao carried the plates to the foundry the next morning, strapped flat against his pack in the cord he had tied in the valley, and laid the best three on He Qiao's bench like a man laying down cards he meant to keep.

The one-armed smith had been feeding a fire. He set his tongs down. He did not touch the plates at first; he walked around them the way he walked around a piece he was deciding whether to buy, his single eye moving over the way the dark-gold lines ran through the old-iron shelves.

"Where," he said, "did a working student with a day-old token get Thousand-Plate armour-hide."

"Off a Thousand-Plate," Lin Hao said. "Cull ticket. The one that came back empty twice. It came back empty because the other two teams were hitting the face of the plates. You don't hit a roof on the shingles. You find where the shingles have to move. It had a seam behind the jaw. I used it."

He Qiao's eye came up off the metal and onto Lin Hao's face, and stayed there a long moment. "You killed it through the join."

"It killed a team through not having one." He sat down on the bench across from him. "I'm not here to sell it. The bounty's paid. I'm here because this is the best metal I've ever held in my life and I don't know enough to waste it on something wrong. The plates are ten-thousand-year hide, dense as anything, and there's an energy in them — feel it, right along the grain — that doesn't belong to ordinary iron. I want to know what it wants to become."

That got the old smith's hands moving. He turned the largest plate over, ran his thumb along the lapping edge where Lin Hao had levered it whole from the carcass along the natural seam, and his eyebrows, which had been at their normal resting height of *unimpressed*, climbed.

"You took it apart along the joins," he said. "You didn't cut a single plate. You unshingled a dead beast like a roof at end of lease." He looked up again. "Most fifth-ranks cut. You read. That's not the fifth-rank trick, boy. That's the trick that keeps going when the rank stops mattering."

"The metal," Lin Hao said again, because He Qiao was the kind of man who would happily talk craft for an hour and not answer the question once.

"The metal," He Qiao agreed, and finally took up a scriber to tap the plate. The note that came back was deep and slow and did not fade. "You're right about the energy. That's not iron-energy, that's the beast's own ten-thousand-year accumulation settled into the plates it grew. Armour-hide like this has one use on this campus, and it's the use the whole outer court is bleeding points to chase. It's foundation stock for battle armour." He set the scriber down. "For one-word work it's beyond overkill — wasteful, even; one-word suits take thousand-refined ordinary metal and don't need a material this alive. This is two-word grade. Maybe better. The energy in it wants to be *spirited*, not just refined. It wants a sixth-rank hand." He paused. "It wants a hand I don't have."

Lin Hao felt the shape of that sentence settle over him. "You're sixth rank."

"I'm sixth rank and one arm and forty years past the age I was going to be seventh." He Qiao said it without self-pity, the way he said everything — flat, a fact from the bench. "There's exactly one hand in this association that this metal is ready for, and it belongs to a man who hasn't been down to the outer foundry in three years because nobody down here has been worth his walk. But that's not your problem this morning." He slid the plates back. "Keep them. Don't sell a single one to the exchange — they'll underpay you like you're a first-year with a lucky rock. Store them dry. When you've got a suit of your own worth hanging them on, or when you've found the hand that can spirit them, you'll thank me. Metal that good doesn't spoil."

Lin Hao wrapped the plates back in their cord. He filed the sentence about *the one hand* away in the margin without comment, the way he filed everything that named a door he had not yet been shown.

The foundry door banged open at that exact moment with the force of a small weather system, and Tang Wulin came through it at speed, trailing the smell of coke and an expression Lin Hao knew well — the look of a boy who has been carried somewhere enormous and is still not sure whether he was kidnapped or promoted.

## Part 2: The Badge

Wulin saw him and changed course the way water changes course toward the lowest point in the floor.

"You're alive," Lin Hao said.

"I'm a member of the Blacksmiths' Association," Wulin said, and held up his chest as evidence. Pinned to it was a badge so crudely hammered it looked like a child's idea of a hammer, black and lumpen and unmistakable. "He put it on me. The red-haired man. He came through the dining hall and picked me up and carried me to a workshop and made me forge two pieces of meteoric iron and then did nine soul rings at me and told me to be his disciple."

Lin Hao set the wrapped plates down very carefully, because this was more information than the bench usually got in a year. "Nine."

"Four purple, four black, and a red one." Wulin held up fingers and then gave up on fingers. "He was no weaker than Elder Cai. He's the president of this entire association, Lin Hao. His name is Feng Wuyu and he is a Saint Blacksmith and he said he would burn to ashes anyone who touched a hair on my head if I became his disciple." He sat down hard on the bench beside the plates. "I told him I already have teachers. Mu Chen, and Mang Tian. I told him if I betrayed them I could betray him too. He didn't like it but he couldn't argue it, so I said I'd ask Mu Chen if Feng could *also* guide me, and I agreed to join the association so he'd let me leave, and he pinned this on me and shouted that I wasn't allowed to join any other association and that if anyone tried to lure me I should use his name." He stopped for breath. "He's completely mad. I forged two pieces of meteoric iron to spirit-grade thousand-refining. Forty-nine strikes of the Disorder Splitting Wind. My hammers are spirit-refined now and they drink my blood essence and the metal told me what it was feeling. I can see why you like doing this. I'm also very tired and I may have made an enemy of a Title Douluo by accident."

"You didn't make an enemy." Lin Hao was reading the badge, then the man, then the badge again. "You did exactly the right thing and you did it on instinct, which is the part that's going to matter. Listen to me, because this is the one thing I know better than anyone in this city." He turned on the bench to face him fully. "Never give up the teachers who made you for the one who wants you. A man who asks you to betray your first master to become his second is telling you exactly what he'll be to you the day someone more interesting comes along. You held the line. That's worth more than the nine rings. But — and don't miss this — you also didn't say no to him. You left the door open. That was right too. He's a Saint Blacksmith, Wulin. You don't turn your back on a hand like that. You make it so he and Mu Chen are both on your side and neither one of them can call it betrayal. Ask Mu Chen exactly what you promised. Let him decide. Then walk through every door both of them open."

Wulin absorbed this. "You've been in this association a day and you already know how its president operates."

"I've been in the association a day and I registered through the foundry master out there, who has six rings' worth of reasons to think carefully about the man above him." Lin Hao nodded at He Qiao's back. "But I'll tell you what I didn't know until ten minutes ago." He tapped the crude black badge. "I walked in here yesterday as a fifth-rank nobody and got my token from a one-armed sixth-rank who watched two heats and sent me on my way. You got carried through the front gate by the president himself. We're in the same association, captain. We've been smithing the same metal on opposite sides of a courtyard wall. The fourth floor, free tables, our own metal. That's not a coincidence, that's a roof, and we're both under it now."

The magnitude of it seemed to land on Wulin a beat late. "We can work the same workshop."

"We can work the same workshop."

For a long moment neither of them said anything, and in the silence you could hear the whole foundry — the bellows, the ring of hammers on a dozen tables, the coke shifting in the fires — and under all of it the thing that the two of them had been doing separately in Eastsea, in competition and in secret and at opposite ends of a town, now given a single room to happen in.

## Part 3: The Two Freaks

Gu Yue found them there an hour later, or rather she found Wulin first and Lin Hao second, which was the order she did most things.

She said nothing at all about the fact that Lin Hao had been gone into the wild for a day and a night and had come back carrying enough strange metal to outfit a workshop. She looked at him for one beat longer than she looked at Wulin — a look that went over the dried streambed-mud he hadn't fully scrubbed off his sleeve, the fresh-healed nick at his jaw, the way he sat loose and straight and *rested* in a way he had not sat in a year, the particular quality of a man who had finally done the thing he'd been starving for — and she filed all of it the way she filed everything, into the drawer she never opened in front of anyone.

"You," she said to Wulin, "have a badge."

"He has a president," Xie Xie said, crowding in behind Xiaoyan. "Wulin has his own personal Title Douluo. I heard it from three people on the way over. The red-haired one carried him off like a sack of rice and now he's the man's apprentice."

"Not apprentice," Wulin said. "It's complicated."

"It's not complicated," said Xiaoyan, who had been doing arithmetic on her fingers. "Both of you are fifth-rank blacksmiths and both of you are in the association that makes battle armour, and the association's president personally recruited the captain, and Lin Hao walked in and registered himself off the street. Do you know what the rest of the outer court calls the two of you?" She didn't wait. "The two freaks. Someone already worked out that the working-student block has the only under-fourteen fifth-rank smiths most of them will ever meet, and there are *two* of them in the same dorm. People are going to want metal from you. Lots of people. Battle armour runs on thousand-refined stock and the entire outer court is trying to make battle armour."

Lin Hao exchanged a look with Wulin. They had, both of them, already done this arithmetic on separate benches.

"Then we price it like craftsmen and not like children," Lin Hao said. "Every suit of armour in this place needs thousand-refined metal that the makers and designers can't produce themselves. Shen Yi said it in the first lecture — commission the smiths, befriend the good ones. We are the good ones. We don't undersell. We don't refuse our own. And we don't work so hard at other people's armour that we forget our own."

"Your own." Gu Yue's voice was very level. She had not sat down. "You mean you've already started thinking about a suit."

He thought about the plates under the cord, the ten-thousand-year hide full of an energy that wanted a seventh-rank hand. He thought about Shen Yi's sixty percent. He thought about his own body, the thing that learned the shape of whatever it touched and became it, and he said the thing he had said at the fountain and meant more now, after a beast had spent an afternoon trying to kill him with armour he could now read like his own hand.

"I've never worried about the compatibility. The metal and I have always come to an understanding." He said it as craft, and Gu Yue heard craft, and Wulin heard craft, and that was the whole truth the room was cleared for. "What I worried about was having a reason and a roof and the metal. I've got the roof as of this week. I've got better metal than I deserve as of yesterday. And I've got the same reason everyone in this building has. So yes. I've started thinking about a suit. The same way everyone here has."

Gu Yue looked at him for that held beat. Then she nodded, once, and said nothing else, and turned to examine Wulin's badge with the particular thoroughness she gave to things she was not going to comment on.

## Part 4: The Hand That Walks

He Qiao found him again at the end of the afternoon, when the others had gone and Lin Hao was wrapping the plates to store them dry against the workshop wall.

"Your captain," the old smith said, leaning in the doorway with his one arm folded. "The one Feng Wuyu carried off. He's good?"

"He spirit-refined two meteoric irons in an afternoon with spirit-refined hammers." Lin Hao tied the cord. "Forty-nine of the Disorder Splitting strikes. I've never seen it. I've only ever read about the number."

He Qiao let out a sound that was nearly a whistle. "Then the president's found his boy, and good for him; that old man has been trying to adopt every smith under twenty on the continent for a decade." He watched Lin Hao's hands work the cord. "Which leaves you."

"Leaves me?"

"The president doesn't walk down to the outer foundry. Hasn't in three years. But he comes up on to this floor, now and then, when the word gets interesting." He Qiao's single eye was steady on him. "Metal doesn't lie to a Saint Blacksmith, boy. You walked in here off a cull ticket carrying ten-thousand-year armour-hide that you unshingled by reading the joins, and you forged two demo heats for me like it was nothing. The foundry talks. By the end of the week that talk goes up through the floor. So I'll give you the same advice you gave your captain and you can do with it what you like." He pushed off the doorframe. "When a man like Feng Wuyu comes looking — and he will, because that metal in your pack is the kind of story that walks itself up four flights of stairs — you remember two things. Don't turn your back on a hand that strong. And don't you *dare* betray whoever first taught you to read metal the way you read it. The Saint won't respect the first. He'll think twice about the second, same as he did with your captain."

Lin Hao thought of Mang Tian, and Mu Chen, and Tie Jin in the scrap pavilion who had sent the wire that brought him here. "I won't."

"Good." He Qiao turned to go, then over his shoulder: "Store the plates on the high rack. Dry. And boy—" He stopped. "Whatever you're holding back in those heats of yours — and I've been doing this forty years, I can taste it when a man's forging at walking pace — when you finally hit something in this building that makes you stop holding it back, I want to be within earshot. That's all. Just earshot."

He went out. Lin Hao stood a moment in the foundry's dying heat with the cord in his hands and the plates stacked dry on the high rack, and thought about doors.

The association had a president who was nine rings of fire and a hammer that could make metal into soul. He had a brother under the same roof now instead of across a town. He had foundation metal that wanted a hand above his rank. And he had an old one-armed smith who had watched two easy heats and somehow, clean through the craft of it, tasted that the easy heats were not the real ones.

*He's right,* Lin Hao thought, and slung his pack. *I'm forging at walking pace. The whole city's getting walking pace. The only thing I've hit that made me move at a real one was an armoured pig two hours west with two teams' worth of graves under its belt.*

He looked up, toward the ceiling of the foundry and the three floors above it where the president's tables were, somewhere in the direction of the fourth floor.

Then he went down the stairs, and out into the Shrek evening, already deciding that the next cull ticket he pulled would not be a ten-thousand-year. He had worked out the margin on that one in an afternoon. It was time to find out what the rung above it cost.

## Part 1: The Stands

The Class Promotion Tournament for the lower grades was, in the ordinary run of years, the Academy's quietest event — a formality conducted on the main platform while the upper grades studied and the kitchen staff took a long lunch.

This year, the kitchen staff did not get a long lunch.

The stands around the main platform filled an hour before the first bell — first-graders, then second-graders who had heard, then third-graders who had heard *of* the hearing, and finally a scattering of Intermediate students who had no business there and attended anyway with the grave faces of people guarding a secret. The rumor had done what rumors do: it had grown legs, then a personality, and by tournament morning the story of the worst class had acquired a shape that could be told in any corridor in three sentences.

*They beat class one in joint training. Four to one. The transfer girl does six elements. And the one with the hawk-eyes — the Listener, the Association's Friday boy — he called a rank-fifteen sword to the worst class in the whole grade, in front of everybody, and meant it.*

None of this was precisely true. All of it was true enough.

Class five arrived as a class — all twenty-two of them, walking together, which was itself a statement, because classes did not walk together. The combat team walked at the front because the class had simply, over nine weeks of nightly disassembly, decided without vote that this was the geometry. Wulin, who had been punched-out-of-window famous since the first week and had never once enjoyed any of it, kept his eyes on the platform. Xie Xie walked with the particular bounce of a boy whose entire tournament preparation had consisted of losing to better opponents on purpose. Gu Yue walked her few paces apart, and the crowd's eyes followed her the way water follows a tilt, and found nothing to hold onto. And Lin Hao walked on the outside edge, where the standing-room crowd was thickest, because he had learned something in the last eleven days that had changed the texture of every morning:

The hawk was quiet.

Not the fence-quiet, not the weather-quiet. Since the night of the question — the night the hawk had looked *at him* for the first time in seven hundred and sixty years, and asked in pressure what it had never needed to ask — it had gone into a stillness Lin Hao had no file for. It was present. It fed. It sat its far water in his spiritual sea like a held note. But the leading — the half-breath-ahead marking that had been his since the Spirit Pagoda, the hand on his shoulder in the dark — the leading had gone soft. Waiting. As if the question, whatever it was, had to be answered before the hawk would speak again.

He had not answered. He did not know how. He was, for the first time since he was six years old, flying on his own reading — and he had discovered, with the specific humility of a boy who has been given more than he understood, that his own reading was *good.* Not the hawk's. His. Ten years of watching the hawk watch had taught the student; the student could, it turned out, see.

*All right,* he thought at the stillness in him, as the class took its place. *If this is the test — I'll take the match on my own eyes.*

The stillness did not answer. It was, he was beginning to understand, extremely good at waiting.

## Part 2

The storm-light in his hair caught the yard light when he turned his head, and the class-four boy opposite him had spent the first four seconds of the match looking at it instead of at the sword, which was the last mistake he made that morning.
: The First Match

Director Long Hengxu took the tournament platform at the first bell with the expression of a man presiding over paperwork.

"The Class Promotion Tournament of the first grade begins today," he announced. "Matches proceed by class standing. Class re-organization will proceed at the tournament's end; until then, every class keeps its number and its pride." A pause. "The first match: first grade's class five — against first grade's class four. Teacher Wu Zhangkong. Have you confirmed the number of participants from your class?"

Wu Zhangkong stood at the platform's edge with his hands behind his back. "Four."

A ripple went through the stands. At the class four bench, their teacher Kong Hanwen — a broad man with a voice built for objection — objected.

"Four? My class confirms three. The format is matched numbers, Director — if class five fields four, class four is entitled to—"

"Class four confirmed three," Long Hengxu said, consulting his slate with the patience of bedrock. "The confirmation was filed yesterday. The entitlement, Teacher Kong, was to file a fourth." He looked up. "Did class four find one?"

Kong Hanwen's mouth opened. Kong Hanwen's mouth closed. Class four had eleven students of whom three were worth platform time, and everyone in the first grade knew it the way they knew weather. "…The confirmation stands," he said, and added, to Wu Zhangkong's profile, with the air of a man landing a blow, "Full of confidence, I see. We shall see what happens on the stage, then."

Wu Zhangkong's lips moved slightly. Wulin, standing nearest, caught perhaps half of it — something about confidence, and something else that sounded, improbably, like an apology forwarded through three years of paperwork. Then Long Hengxu was raising his hand, and the platform's soul-guidance ward was humming up around the ring like a kettle coming to boil, and there was no more time for anything but the match.

Class four took the platform first, and the stands' noise thinned into the particular quiet of an audience doing arithmetic.

Jia Long led — class four's strongest, an Assault-type with a bear-class martial soul and the build to match it, already half-expanded under its amplification, his yellow eyes carrying the flat confidence of a boy who had never been punched by anyone who meant it. Behind him and to the left, Lin Zunyuan, slim, watchful, a long staff held like a promise — Support-type, the class's amplifier, the piece that made Jia Long more than Jia Long. And at the rear edge, Mo Si — short sword, Agility-type, rank thirteen, quick hands and quicker eyes, positioned the way quick things position themselves: to be elsewhere the moment anyone decided where he was.

It was a good arrangement. It was, in fact, the arrangement class four had drilled for a month: the bear in front, the staff behind, the knife free.

Class five took the platform. And did nothing.

That was the first thing the stands noticed, though it took most of them until afterward to understand they had noticed it: the four of them walked to their positions without releasing a single soul ring — no flash of white or yellow, no martial souls singing out for the audience — and stood, and waited, the way four people wait at a door they have already opened in their heads.

"Begin," said Long Hengxu.

Lin Hao spoke once. Not loudly. The four of them had spent nine weeks agreeing in advance on what each of them was for, and the agreement had simplified speech down to its studs.

"Bear's a wall," he said. "The staff makes the wall taller — behind him, left. Xie Xie, the staff. Wulin, the wall. The knife's mine."

It was, afterward, the only thing any of the four said in the entire match, and every one of them had already moved while the last word was still in his mouth.

Lin Hao took the knife.

Mo Si was class four's free piece — short sword, Agility-type, rank thirteen, quick hands and quicker eyes, positioned to be elsewhere the moment anyone decided where he was. He launched himself at Lin Hao with the committed speed of a month of purpose, the short sword already tracing the line toward Lin Hao's ribs—

and Lin Hao met him. Not with evasion. With the Stormbringer, out of the air and into the knife's path, the flat of the blade meeting the short sword's spine and *turning* it — not blocking, redirecting, the way a smith turns a bar that's come at him off-angle. Mo Si's own speed carried him past, and Lin Hao did not watch him go. **Wind-Step** — one step, no wind-up, and he had crossed the platform to where Mo Si would be, not where Mo Si was, the hawk-eyes reading the recovery before the boy's foot had found the floor.

Mo Si came back around — fast, faster than a rank thirteen had any business being, the short sword flicking in three lines at once, a triangle of steel meant to force a choice—

and Lin Hao chose all three. The Stormbringer was in each line a half-breath before the knife arrived, not because he was faster but because he had read the triangle before Mo Si committed to it. The third meeting rang, and Mo Si's short sword went skidding out of his hand across the platform, and Lin Hao's free hand came up — not a fist, not a grab — and the wind around it *compressed,* folding into a ribbon of moving pressure along his forearm: the Gale Talon, laid not at range but along his own skin, a rending edge of air that stopped a finger's width from Mo Si's throat.

Mo Si froze. The Gale Talon's edge hummed, close enough to lift the fine hairs on his neck, close enough that the stands — which had learned to count — saw the air *move* around the fourth boy's hand and forgot to.

"Yield," Lin Hao said. Walking volume. The same volume as the tactical call. Mo Si, who had a wind-blade at his throat and a sword somewhere behind him, yielded.

At the center of the platform, the match was already resolving. Xie Xie — freed of the knife, which had been Lin Hao's from the first word — had gone around the bear's flank to the staff behind it, and the Light Dragon Dagger's flat had tapped Lin Zunyuan's forehead with a small wooden note, and Long Hengxu's voice had already rung *out* over the staff-boy's stupefied face. And Jia Long, seeing his amplifier down and his knife yielded, had committed everything to the one threat left — the forge-boy walking at him — and roared the bear-soul's amplification up and swung—

—and the ground under Jia Long went wrong.

Nothing dramatic. No flare, no sound. A thumb of air, no more — Lin Hao's, laid across the bear-boy's planted heel at the exact half-breath his weight committed forward, the smallest piece of the Gale Talon, the sort of nothing that happens to anyone, anywhere, once or twice a life. Jia Long's foot slipped. His catching hand, sweeping out to close on the fist coming at his chest, arrived a hand's width off its line—

Wulin's fist arrived on its line.

The stands heard it from the back rows. Jia Long — amplified, braced, four kilograms of bear-soul growth on his frame — flew back seven or eight meters and sat down hard against the ward, and a single blue vine crept out of the impact's edge like a question, looked around, and vanished, because there was no longer anyone to ask.

Mo Si looked at the wind-blade still humming at his throat. He looked at the bear sitting against the ward. He looked at the girl in the middle of the platform who had not moved, whose hand had risen perhaps a finger's width at the match's start and settled, unused, back to her side — a cup, kept full, never poured.

He let his hands go slack. It was, everyone agreed later, the wisest decision of his career.

Ten seconds. The ward hummed down. And only then — because the record required it, and Long Hengxu's slate was waiting — did class five release their martial souls.

The platform lit.

*Yellow— no. White.* The stands corrected themselves in a ripple: the golden-threaded grass at Wulin's hand threw a ring white as first snow — the gold was in the *grass,* not the ring; a ten-year soul, the cheapest thing in the plaza, and the boy holding it had just put a bear through a wall with it. Yellow at Xie Xie's feet — one ring, a hundred years old, what a noble family's money could buy: the dagger-boy's chin said everything his mouth was too smart to, which was that all that money had still only ever bought him *one.* Yellow, still and unbothered, under Gu Yue. And at the fourth boy on the outside edge, where the wind was doing the small unnecessary thing it always did around him — *two.* Two yellow rings, stacked like a promise and its proof, under a sword that had been called to the worst class in the grade in front of the whole plaza and had never once looked like it regretted it.

On the class four bench, Kong Hanwen stood very still, doing the arithmetic his face refused to keep. Ten seconds. Four rings. Three of his students were picking themselves up and one of them was, he noticed, *laughing* — the released, incredulous laugh of a boy who had just discovered the exact size of the world.

"Class five wins," Long Hengxu announced, in the voice he kept for weather reports. Then he consulted his slate, and performed, aloud, the piece of arithmetic half the stands had not yet understood to be necessary — because the Class Promotion Tournament promotes as it goes, and always had. "As of this moment, you are first grade's class four." A pause, bedrock-solid. "Tomorrow: class three." And in the stands, the gospel adjusted mid-sentence: *oh — no. They were class four now.* Nobody, it should be said, had the slightest intention of calling them that.

## Part 3: The Naming

The stands emptied slowly, the way stands do when the conversation is better than the event. By dinner the first grade had done what the first grade always did with a thing it could not stop saying: it had started trying to name it.

*Trio* came first, and stuck fastest, because names travel at the speed of the obvious — Wulin's fist, Xie Xie's flash, Gu Yue's six impossible elements: the forge, the light, and the flood, three shapes that made a sentence. It was a good name. It was even, in its way, the canon shape of things — the three who had ended the match in ten seconds had ended it in a grammar all their own.

The naming ran into trouble, as naming does, at the edges.

"There's *four* of them," a second-grader pointed out at the dining hall's long table, with the aggrieved precision of a boy who liked lists.

"Well, what do you call the fourth?" someone said. "Wulin's the fist, Xie Xie's the flash, the transfer girl's the flood. The fourth one didn't even throw a punch."

"He didn't have to," said a girl from class five's own twenty-two, with the flat finality of a person repeating gospel. "He took the knife-boy. Sword met the knife three times and the knife went *skidding* — and then there was wind at the boy's throat and he yielded. My cousin's in the class. She says nobody even saw the wind come. She says the four of them train like — like one animal with four legs. She says the fourth one's the head."

"The *head?*"

"You heard me."

The name did not stick — heads are invisible, and the first grade wanted shapes — but something else did, born at the third-window table when Xie Xie, refueled and therefore legislative, slammed his glass down mid-argument:

"Fine! FINE. It's the Trio — and the weather!" He swept an arm at Lin Hao, who was eating his fourth bun with the peace of a boy who had been having this exact argument since Tuesday. "You three are the storm! He's the *barometer!* Nobody sees him coming because there's nothing to see — the knife-boy yielded to *wind* and nobody saw the wind — the weather just *happens* and he was standing there already, he was standing there *yesterday*—"

"I was at the forge yesterday," Lin Hao said, around the bun.

"PROVING MY POINT."

The table went down. Even Yun Xiao's ledger-page face cracked, and Zhou Zhangxi laughed so hard the benches moved, and at the next table a boy who had been calling it the Trio all afternoon tried *the Trio and the Weather* on for size and found, to everyone's surprise including his own, that it fit.

Two tables away, Mu Xi ate alone, as she ate always, with the Association's monthly ledger sheet folded beside her tray — the one with the new line on it, *eleven purifications* — and she watched the third-window table the way she watched strike counts: carefully, and without arriving anywhere. The boy with the two rings was laughing with his back to her. She had beaten him at nothing, lost to him at nothing, and spoken to him never, and the ledger of it took up, she had noticed with displeasure, a persistent corner of her mind.

*Tomorrow is class three,* she thought, folding the sheet, and left the rest of the thought unsaid even to herself, which was how she said everything.

And the word that reached class five's table last, carried by a fourth-grader with the importance of a herald, was the one that mattered:

"Class three's got a two-ring," he announced. "A proper Soul Grandmaster — two rings, a hundred years apiece, and class three's been drilling him as their whole plan." He leaned in, enjoying himself. "Your weather-boy's got two rings too, sure — but everyone knows his came off that strange hawk of his. Class three's is bone and iron, and everyone's saying tomorrow's the real first match."

And then, because heralds cannot leave the best-shaped part of a story untold, he added what the whole grade already knew — that above class three, above class two, the ladder ended where ladders end: at class one, where the first grade's strongest trio sat like a held answer. **Wang Jinxi** and his Bone Dragon King — two yellow rings on a dark-attribute dragon soul that drank the light around it. **Zhang Yangzi** and his Shadow Phantasm Eagle. **Wei Xiaofeng.** Three two-ring Soul Grandmasters, drilled since enrollment for the day the grade's arithmetic would be tested against theirs.

"That's the top, though," the herald finished, fairly. "Tomorrow's class three."

Xie Xie's glass went down. Wulin's bun paused. Gu Yue's chopsticks did not change rhythm at all.

Lin Hao finished chewing, and swallowed, and felt — under the table, under the bun, under the whole loud ordinary evening — the stillness in him turn its head, just slightly, toward tomorrow.

"Yeah," he said. "Sounds like the real one."

## Part 4: What Waiting Sounds Like

The roof, after. The harbor wind. The city doing its nightly accounting of lights.

Wulin was below, doing what he did after victories — writing the match down in the forge-temperature hand: *Jia Long, 4 meters plus 3, the vine came out late, ask why. Xie Xie, the not-attacking — 9 weeks to learn it. Gu Yue, the finger that didn't pour.* He wrote the way he forged, and Lin Hao had stopped reading over his shoulder weeks ago, not from politeness, but because the pages had started, without either of them deciding it, to read like the two halves of one ledger.

Lin Hao sat on the tiles and did inventory instead, because inventory was his forge.

The match, laid out honest: ten seconds, one sentence, no strikes. The slip of air under the heel — his, and only barely; a thumb of wind, the smallest thing the hawk had ever taught him, used at the exact half-breath because *he* had seen the weight commit. Not the hawk's half-breath. His. The call — *the staff makes the wall taller* — his eyes, his grammar, his four legs walking. The rings on display: two yellow, Federation-logged, crowd-counted, no longer a secret and never once a boast.

And underneath it all, the stillness.

He had fought a tournament match on his own reading, and the reading had held, and somewhere in the holding he had understood what the question — whatever it was — had actually been asking. Not *can you see.* The hawk had known he could see; the hawk had been reading for him since the Pagoda. The question underneath the question, the one carried in seven hundred and sixty years of pressure, was older and simpler and far more frightening:

*Can you see — without me?*

The wind came in off the harbor with its long argument. Below, the city counted its lights. In room eleven a pen scratched its ledger, and in the girls' dormitory a lamp burned steady as a filed report, and in the spiritual sea of a ten-year-old on a roof, something vast and patient and increasingly *someone* sat its far water and waited for an answer it was in no hurry to receive.

Lin Hao lay back on the tiles and gave it the only answer a boy can give a thing like that — not a word. Not yet. A direction.

*Tomorrow,* he thought. *There's a real two-ring tomorrow, and I'll fight him on my own eyes, and you can watch me do it.* He closed his own eyes, and smiled a little at the absurdity of offering a hawk a showing. *That's the best I've got. Watch.*

The stillness, on its far water, did not answer.

But when sleep came — late, the way it came after hard days — it came with the particular quality of a room where someone has, very quietly, pulled up a chair.

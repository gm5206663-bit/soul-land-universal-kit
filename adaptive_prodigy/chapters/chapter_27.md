## Part 1: The Room After

They put the seven of them in a room with chairs and water and a man in white who looked at each of them in turn and decided things.

The man looked longest at Wang Jinxi, and second-longest at Lin Hao, and Lin Hao noticed both.

"You bit your tongue," the man said.

"I bit the inside of my cheek."

"You have a laceration on the right forearm consistent with a crystallisation contact." The man turned the arm over with the efficient disinterest of a person who had turned over a great many arms. "How many contacts."

"One."

"And you know what one means."

"One means I've got five left in that spot and six anywhere else," Lin Hao said. "It's a count, not a wound. I said so at the time, in the forest, out loud, to three people who were too busy to appreciate it."

The man wrote something. "The mark will fade. In the platform it isn't real, and your body knows that, and it will take the hint in a week or so. If it hasn't faded in ten days, come back." He capped the pen. "You were in for ninety-four minutes."

"I know."

"The elementary session is forty."

"I know."

The man looked at him for a moment with an expression that had nothing to do with medicine, and then he stood up, and said, "You're all fine," to the room, and left, and the room let out a breath it had been holding since the forest.

## Part 2

The second-year who ran the equipment shed had stopped asking him for his name, which he had taken for familiarity until he noticed that the man still could not look at him directly, the way people could not look directly at the storm-gray lock at his temple and pretended afterwards that they had been looking at the wall.
: The Debrief

Long Hengxu did most of it, because Long Hengxu did most of everything, and he did it in the order he had written down.

Zhang Yangzi: four minutes eleven seconds, the flock, the button. "You will go back in," Long Hengxu said, "and you will not attack the bird." Zhang Yangzi's face did the thing it did when he was pleased and trying not to look pleased, which was to go slightly redder. "An hour," he said. "I want an hour."

Xie Xie: the Scarlet Demon Trees, the Longtail Mouse, the Horned Dragon. "I killed a hundred-year soul beast," Xie Xie said, "and then I ran from the next one, and I want it noted that I ran *well.*" Long Hengxu noted it.

Wang Jinxi: emergency extraction, ten minutes of treatment, willpower described by a man in white as very strong. Wang Jinxi said nothing at all, and his hand, which was resting on the arm of the chair, did not stop moving for the whole of the debrief, and nobody mentioned it.

Gu Yue: the Crystal Bear, the eleven teleports, the flight. Long Hengxu looked at his notes, and then at her, and said, "You judged a thousand-year soul beast in under a second and chose correctly. That is rarer than anything else that happened today." And Gu Yue said, "I ran away," in a voice designed to end the subject, and Long Hengxu, who had dealt with a great many students, let it end.

Tang Wulin: the Horned Dragon, the corpse, the Crystal Bear, the neck. Long Hengxu put the pen down for this one. "You lifted a Horned Dragon," he said. "I have been the director of this academy for nineteen years and I have never seen a student lift a Horned Dragon." And Wulin, who took compliments the way he took everything, slightly sideways and without knowing where to put them, said, "It wasn't very heavy," and Xie Xie made a sound like a kettle, and the room broke, and it was the first time any of them had laughed since the forest.

And then Long Hengxu came to the last line on his paper, and the room went quiet, because everybody had been waiting for the last line.

"Lin Hao," he said. "Ninety-four minutes."

"Yes."

"You were assigned the elementary tier. You fought a pack, a Horned Dragon and a thousand-year Crystal Bear. You took a crystallisation contact and did not press your button. You stayed in for fifty-four minutes past the session length, and you were not retrieved because Teacher Wu told us not to retrieve you." He looked up from the paper. "Do you understand why that is a problem?"

"Because I'm ten."

"Because you're *measurable,*" Long Hengxu said. "A ten-year-old who clears the elementary platform in one session is a curiosity. A ten-year-old who clears it in one session and stays in for another hour and comes out with a count on his arm and a plan in his head is a *file.* The Spirit Pagoda keeps files. There is a man in this building who has one of yours in his pocket, and he did not put it in the ledger where it belongs, and I have not decided yet whether that is irregular or whether it is the first sensible thing anyone has done about you." He capped the pen. "Go home. All of you. Eat something. Nobody meditates tonight."

## Part 3: What the Man in the Doorway Was Waiting For

Wu Zhangkong was in the corridor.

Not walking. Not waiting in the way people wait, which is standing with an errand in their face. He was standing with his arms folded and his back against the wall and the particular stillness of a man who had arrived before everyone else and intended to leave after.

"Lin Hao," he said.

"Yes."

"Walk with me."

They walked. Wu Zhangkong did not say anything for the length of the corridor, and then the length of the courtyard, and then the length of the path that went down toward the groundskeeper's shed, and Lin Hao — who had spent four months learning which of this man's silences were empty — walked beside him and said nothing either, because this one was full.

At the shed Wu Zhangkong stopped, and drew the Skyfrost Sword.

Lin Hao had seen it four times. Four times it had been in a hand and moving, and both times that mattered it had ended a fight in two strokes. He had never once seen it drawn slowly, and he understood, watching it come out of the scabbard an inch at a time, that he was being shown the weapon rather than the work.

"What do you see," Wu Zhangkong said.

"A sword."

"Look again."

Lin Hao looked again. He looked the way he looked at steel, because it was the only way he had, and what he saw was: a blade about a metre long, straight, single-edged for the last third, with a spine thick enough to take a hammer and an edge ground to a geometry so fine that the light did not so much reflect off it as *decide* about it. And under that, because he could not help it, the grain — the folded weather inside the bar, ten thousand layers of a smith's decisions, and the decisions were *conservative.* Whoever had made this sword had not tried anything. They had done the correct thing, over and over, for a very long time.

"Somebody made that over years," Lin Hao said. "And they never once showed off."

Wu Zhangkong put it away.

"That," he said, "is the most accurate thing anyone has ever said about my sword, and I have owned it for thirty-one years." He sat down on the shed step, which was not a thing he did, and Lin Hao sat down beside him, which was not a thing he had been invited to do. "I have been your teacher for four months. In four months I have taught you a stance, a stroke, and one sentence about reading. Do you know why it's been so little?"

"Because you're mean."

"Because I have been *finding out.*" Wu Zhangkong looked at the shed roof. "A teacher who teaches too early teaches his own sword. Every master does it, I did it, my master did it to me — you meet a child with a gift and you cannot help it, you pour yourself in, and thirty years later the child is a very good copy of you and neither of you can work out where the road went wrong." He turned his head. "So I taught you almost nothing. I hit you instead, and I watched what you did with the hitting, and I waited to see whether you would start making things I had not given you."

"And?"

"You made three strokes against a wolf pack that I did not give you, that you did not have previously, and that you could not have made from anything I taught you. You made them in the moment, under pressure, against an opponent that was reading you." Wu Zhangkong said it flatly, the way he said everything, and it landed anyway. "That was ninety-four minutes ago and I have not stopped thinking about it since."

## Part 4: The Part He Could Not Teach

"Here is what a swordsman is," Wu Zhangkong said. "Not what a good fighter is. Not what a strong one is. Those are different trades and the world confuses them constantly.

"A swordsman is a person whose sword can say something. Not *do* something — do is easy, I can teach a dog to do. Say. A sword that speaks has a meaning behind the stroke, and the meaning is the swordsman's own, and it is the reason the stroke works on things it should not work on." He picked up a splinter of wood from the ground and turned it in his fingers. "There are five rooms. Foundation is the body. Technique is the vocabulary. Threshold is when the sword stops being a tool — you passed that in the gauntlet, and I marked it, and I said nothing, because saying it would have made it yours instead of true. The fourth room is Sword Intent, and you were standing in the doorway of it today with two swords and a bleeding mouth, and you did not know it."

"I was counting."

"You were *meaning* something," Wu Zhangkong said. "You left a sword in a wound and walked away from it. Do you understand what that is? That is not a technique. No form I have ever seen contains it. You spent a weapon — a weapon, the most expensive thing you own — as a *sentence,* because the sentence was worth more than the sword. That is the fourth room. That is Sword Intent, and you walked into it backwards while doing arithmetic."

Lin Hao was quiet for a moment.

"And the fifth room."

"The fifth room is Sword Innate." Wu Zhangkong put the splinter down. "In the fifth room there is no sword. I don't mean you don't hold one. I mean the sword stops being the reason. You could take a stick, a chopstick, a finger, and it would still be *sword,* because the sword is not the object anymore, it's the — " he stopped, and Lin Hao watched him stop, watched the man who had six rings and two ten-thousand-year souls and a sword that thirty-one years of correctness had made go looking for a word and not finding one. "I can't tell you," Wu Zhangkong said. "That's the whole of it. I can't tell you, because I'm not there."

"You're not."

"I have been in the fourth room for twenty-six years." He said it without any self-pity at all, which made it worse. "I am a very good swordsman. I have beaten people I should not have beaten. And I have never once been able to put the sword down, and I have thought about it every day for twenty-six years, and I have concluded that the reason is that my sword is *mine* — it is the Skyfrost Sword, it came with a name and a school and four hundred years of other people's decisions in it, and I have spent my whole life being extremely good at somebody else's sentence." He looked at Lin Hao. "You don't have that problem. You never had a school. You have a hammer, a hawk, a bad stance I fixed, and four months of me being careful. Whatever your sword is going to say, it isn't going to be a quotation."

"So what do I do."

"That's the part I can't teach," Wu Zhangkong said, "so I'm going to give it to you as homework instead, which is the only thing a teacher can do with the part he can't teach." He stood up, and brushed the shed step off behind him out of long habit, and looked down at the boy on it.

"Make a style."

"...What?"

"A style. Yours. Named, and built, and written down, with forms in it that I have not seen and that you cannot do yet. Everybody's sword comes out of somewhere, and yours came out of a smithy, so it had better look like one." He started up the path. "I want the name by the end of the month. I want the first form by the end of the term. And I want you to understand that I am not being encouraging, I am being *selfish,* because I am fifty-four years old and I have been in the fourth room for twenty-six of them, and I would like to see the fifth before I die, and you are the only chance of it I am going to get."

"That's the most honest thing you've ever said to me."

"Don't get used to it," Wu Zhangkong said, and went up the path, and did not look back, and Lin Hao sat on the shed step for a long time afterwards with his arms aching and his cheek stinging and a thumbprint of cold crystal on his forearm, thinking about rooms.

## Part 5: Reading the Grain

He started that night, on the roof, with a stick.

Not the Stormbringer. That was the first decision and it took him an hour, and the reason was Wu Zhangkong's, though it took him the hour to understand it: if he built the style with the Stormbringer he would build it out of the Stormbringer, and the Stormbringer was a martial soul, and a martial soul came with a shape already in it, and he would end up exactly where Wu Zhangkong had ended up, which was extremely good at somebody else's sentence.

So: a stick. Roof tile warm under him, harbour wind doing its long argument, and a stick he had picked up off the training ground that was slightly too light and slightly too long and entirely ordinary.

The name arrived before the forms did, which surprised him, because he had assumed names came last.

**Reading the Grain.**

Not a school. Not a lineage. A *method* — and the method was one sentence, and the sentence was his father's, and his father had never once known he was teaching swordsmanship: **you do not cut metal where you want it to open, you cut it where it is already willing.**

He said it out loud to the harbour, and heard how it sounded, and thought: *that's either the truest thing I've ever said or I'm ten years old on a roof with a stick, and it's probably both.*

The first form took the rest of the night.

He built it out of the ledger, because the ledger was the only thing he had that was entirely his — seventy-one lines, and then eighty-three, each one a fight, each one an answer, and the answers had shapes. He laid the shapes out on the roof tile in his head the way he would lay out a bar, and he looked for the place they were all *willing,* and he found it, and it was very simple, and it was so simple that he laughed, and Wulin called up from the bunk below to ask whether he was all right, and he said yes.

The first form was this: **you do not strike first.**

Not a defence. Not a wait. The form *began* with the opponent's commitment, because a commitment was the only place in a fight where anything was already willing, and every technique he had ever made that had worked had worked because he had arrived at a place somebody else had already agreed to be. The Grain Cut. The Question. The Answering Stroke. The Unwritten Stroke. Four forms, four fights, and all four of them had begun with somebody else.

So the first form of Reading the Grain was a form you could not do alone. You needed a person. You needed their decision. And the strike itself was the smallest part of it — the last three centimetres of a thing that had started a full second earlier, on the other side of the roof, in somebody else's mind.

He named it **Open Hand**, because his father opened the bar before he ever struck it, and because it looked, from the outside, exactly like nothing at all: a boy on a roof with a stick, not moving, waiting for a partner who was not there.

At four in the morning he got up and did it against the harbour wind, and the wind did not commit, and the form did not work, and he stood there with the stick in his hand and understood the second thing about his own style, which was going to take him years.

*It only works on something that decides.*

Which meant it would work on every person he ever fought.

And it would never, ever work on a wolf.

"Right," Lin Hao said, to the dark water, and he was grinning, and he did not stop grinning for the rest of the night. "So that's what I'm for, is it. Not the beasts. The *people.*"

The hawk, eight hundred and seventy-four years of condensed wind riding quietly inside him, did not comment. But it turned its head, and Lin Hao felt it turn, and for the first time in four years he had the distinct and entirely unsupported impression that it was pleased.

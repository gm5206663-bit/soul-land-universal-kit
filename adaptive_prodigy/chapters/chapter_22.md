## Part 1: What Not Holding Back Sounds Like

The first thing Lin Hao learned about Wu Zhangkong not holding back was that it had a sound.

Holding back had a sound too, and Lin Hao had known it for five months without knowing he knew it — the particular whistle of a white wax pointing stick moving at the speed a man uses when he intends to teach rather than to hurt. It was quick. It was precise. It was, he understood now in retrospect, *merciful,* and he had never once mistaken it for slow, but he had also never understood that there was another gear, because there is no way to hear the difference between a man's second speed and a man's first until the man shifts.

The shift happened on a Tuesday, in the fourth minute of the evening session, and it sounded like a sail catching.

The stick arrived at Lin Hao's left ribs. He had read it — he had read it completely, the angle, the intent, the half-beat where Wu Zhangkong's shoulder committed — and he had moved, and the move was correct, and the stick was there anyway, because the stick had been moving faster than the read.

Lin Hao went down. Not far. He went down onto one knee in the training-ground dirt with his left arm locked against his side and his breath somewhere above him, and the six of them stopped, because in five months Wu Zhangkong had hit Lin Hao four times, and the four times had looked, from the outside, like accidents.

This did not look like an accident.

"Again," Wu Zhangkong said.

"You broke my rib," Lin Hao said, from the ground, in the voice of a boy conducting an inventory.

"I bruised it. Get up."

"I'm conducting an inventory. There's a process." But he was up, because the process was, in fact, already finished, and because the part of him that conducted inventories had been quietly handed over to the part of him that wanted the next one. He rolled the shoulder. He put his feet in the smith's stance that Wu Zhangkong had called wrong in front of the whole class, and then — because he had spent the eleven days since thinking about nothing else — he moved his back foot four centimetres and turned his hip eleven degrees, and the stance stopped being a smith's stance and became, for the first time, a swordsman's.

Wu Zhangkong looked at his feet for a moment.

"Better," he said, which was the second kind thing he had said to Lin Hao all week, and which came, as always, without any of the dressing. Then the stick came again.

## Part 2

"You know they talk about you," Xie Xie said, somewhere in the middle of the second hour, lying on his back with his arms behind his head. "Not the fighting. The *face.* There are three girls in class two who have a *system* for walking past the yard. A system, Lin Hao. With a schedule."

"That's ridiculous."

"It's extremely well organised and I have watched it happen four times."
: Six

He was hit six times that evening.

This is the number that mattered, and the six of them counted it out loud, because the six of them had spent five months believing that four was a ceiling — Xie Xie kept the count on his fingers, and by the fourth hit had stopped teasing, and by the sixth had gone quiet in a way that Lin Hao found considerably more alarming than the hits.

"Six," Xie Xie said, afterwards, sitting on the ground with his arms around his knees. "In one *evening.*"

"Yes."

"In five months he hit you *four times.*"

"Yes."

"And you're *happy?*" Xie Xie looked at him with genuine distress. "Hao. Look at me. He hit you six times and your face is doing the thing."

"My face is doing the thing because I learned six things," Lin Hao said. He was lying on his back in the dirt, which was the position he did his best thinking in, and he held up his own hand and counted on it, because he was his father's son and he did not trust memory for numbers. "One. He commits with the shoulder but he *aims* with the wrist, so if I read the shoulder I'm reading a half-beat old. Two. The stick is slower than the arm on the way out and faster on the way back, which means the second hit of any pair is the one to dodge, and I've been dodging the first. Three. He breathes in before a real one. Not a big breath. A small one. I've been hearing it for months and filing it under *weather.*" He put a finger down. "Four. He does not like my left side because my left side is honest, and he's been going there all month hoping I'd fix it, and I didn't fix it, I *rotated* it, and that's the difference between a stance and a stance. Five—"

"You're doing this out loud."

"I'm *filing,* Xie Xie, there's a difference, and you're interrupting. Five. When he wants to teach me something he hits me somewhere it won't matter, and when he wants to find something out he hits me somewhere it will. Six were somewhere it will. Which means he isn't teaching me tonight." Lin Hao sat up. "He's *measuring* me."

The training ground had gone quiet, and Lin Hao became aware — the way he became aware of most things, slightly after the fact — that all six of them were looking at him, and that Wang Jinxi was looking at him the hardest, and that Wu Zhangkong had stopped at the edge of the ground on his way out and had not, in fact, left.

"What did you get?" Wu Zhangkong said.

Lin Hao looked at him. "That I can read you at about forty percent."

The six made various noises.

"Forty," Wu Zhangkong repeated.

"I get the shape of it. I don't get the timing. You're six rings and two of them are ten thousand years old and I'm ten, so forty percent is not a complaint, it's a *measurement,* and I want it said that I'm not complaining." Lin Hao got to his feet, and winced, and did not let the wince become anything. "How much were you holding back before tonight?"

"All of it," Wu Zhangkong said, and left, and the seven of them stood in the dirt and understood that they had just been told, in the flat voice, that the man who had been hitting them for five months had never once hit them.

## Part 3: The Answering Stroke

On the fifth day, Wu Zhangkong brought a second stick.

Not for hitting. He brought it because he had decided to teach, and because Wu Zhangkong taught the way he did everything else, which was by arriving with the equipment already in his hand and not explaining it until you had failed without it.

"You fight with a sword," he said.

"Yes."

"You fight with a sword the way a man who has only ever met swords fights with a sword. Which is well. It is not the same thing." He put the second stick into his left hand and held the pointing stick in his right, and stood there with his arms at his sides, and Lin Hao understood that he was being shown a posture rather than a technique, and that the posture was the lesson. "There are five things a person can be doing with a blade. Holding it. Aiming it. Moving it. Committing it. And knowing which of those they are doing. You are very good at the first four. You are the best at the first four that I have ever seen in a child, and I want to be precise about that, because I want you to hear the second half."

"The second half."

"The fifth one is not a skill. It is a *decision,* and it has to be made before the other four, or the other four are just noise." He raised the second stick an inch. "Tonight: the Answering Stroke. You will not learn it tonight. You will learn what it costs."

And then he attacked, and the lesson turned out to be that the Answering Stroke — the counter-form, the stroke that arrives at the place your opponent's own commitment has already promised — is not a movement you make with your arm. It is a movement you make with your *patience.* You have to be willing to be almost hit. You have to stand inside the arriving blade for the whole of its travel and let it come, because the answer only exists at the end of the commitment, and a boy who flinches a half-beat early gets the shape of the answer without the substance of it.

Lin Hao was hit eleven times learning it.

He was hit eleven times because eleven times he moved early, and Wu Zhangkong said nothing at all about it, only hit him again, and on the twelfth exchange Lin Hao stood still inside a stick that was coming at his collarbone and felt — for a duration of time so short that he could not afterwards say whether it had been real — the entire architecture of the attack. Not the angle. The *promise.* The place Wu Zhangkong had already agreed to arrive at.

And the Stormbringer went there before the stick did, and rested, and Wu Zhangkong stopped.

The training ground had gone quiet again, and the six of them were pretending very hard not to be watching, and Wu Zhangkong looked at the sword resting a hand's width from his own wrist and said, "What did that feel like."

"Like you'd already told me," Lin Hao said. "You told me where you were going. Not on purpose. You just — you can't commit to a strike without saying where it's going, and I've been hearing it for months and I thought it was a *sound,* and it isn't a sound, it's a decision, and the decision happens before the movement, and if I wait for the decision instead of the movement I don't have to be fast at all."

Wu Zhangkong was quiet for a long moment.

"That is the Answering Stroke," he said. "You have the shape of it. You will have the substance of it in a year, and you will have it in a year rather than ten because you are the first student I have had who heard the decision before he was shown it." He put the second stick down. "Your homework is not to practise it. Your homework is to notice that everything I have taught you so far has been *reading,* and to ask yourself why a swordsman's whole curriculum is reading." He turned to go, and stopped. "And Lin Hao."

"Sir."

"The stance you fixed. Keep it. I'm not going to correct your feet again, and if they go wrong I'm going to let them go wrong until you lose a fight to them, because a thing a teacher fixes belongs to the teacher." He left, and Xie Xie said, from the ground, "That's the meanest thing I've ever heard anybody say as a compliment," and Lin Hao stood in the middle of the training ground with his sword still in his hand and his feet four centimetres from where they had been for a year, and felt — very distinctly, the way you feel a joint come into line — something in him *set.*

Rank thirty came in the fourth week, and it did not feel like the others. The others had arrived — a settling, a joint coming into line. This one arrived and then *stopped,* the way a lift stops at the top floor, and Lin Hao sat up in the dark at four in the morning with the specific and entirely new sensation of having run out of staircase.

Twenty-one to thirty was the second-ring band. He was at the top of it. And the thing above him was not a rank he could earn, because the RANK RULE did not care how hard he had been hit: you cannot pass a bottleneck without a ring, and a third ring needed a soul that had crossed a thousand years, and the hawk was at seven hundred and seventy and had never once been in a hurry about anything.

Eleven hits, a stance, and one stroke had taken him the length of the band. There was nothing left to be corrected into.

"So that's the shape of it," he said aloud, to a dormitory, because thinking out loud was how he finished an argument. "I can be taught. I can be fought. I can be *wrong* at somebody until I'm right. But I can't be hurried, and neither can the bird."

The hawk, eight hundred and forty-five years of condensed wind riding quietly inside him, did not comment. It never did. But Lin Hao had spent four years learning which silences were empty, and this one was not empty — it was the silence of something that had its own schedule, and had known all along that the boy would arrive at the top of the stairs and have to sit down.

## Part 4: Seventy-Eight

The ledger grew by seven lines in three weeks.

Six of them were Wu Zhangkong, and they were the strangest six lines in it, because every other line in the ledger was a line about a *person* — Mo Si's knife, Qin Lang's feed-line, the bear's honest momentum — and these six were lines about a *speed.* He had never had to learn a speed before. Everyone else in his life had moved at a speed he could arrive at, and Wu Zhangkong moved at a speed he could only *predict,* and prediction was a different skill, and it turned out that he had one, and that it was good, and that it had been sitting there unused the way a tool sits unused in a drawer until a job arrives that needs it.

The seventh line was Xie Xie.

Not a new answer — he had eleven of those already, and Xie Xie had not invented a twelfth in three weeks, because Xie Xie was, for all his speed, a boy with a fixed vocabulary of violence. The seventh line was different. It was the line where Lin Hao finally wrote down *why* he and Xie Xie had never once, in months of mornings, actually hurt each other badly — and the answer, when he wrote it out, was so obvious that he sat on the bunk above Zhou Zhangxi's snoring for a full minute feeling like an idiot.

They were moving at the same tempo. Not the same speed — Xie Xie was faster, and had always been faster, and would go to his grave being faster. The same *tempo.* They arrived at decisions in the same beat, which meant that every time Xie Xie committed, Lin Hao was already committing to the answer, and every time Lin Hao committed, Xie Xie was already somewhere else, and the two of them had been doing this so long that it looked like skill and was actually *rhythm,* and rhythm was the thing you could not fake and could not teach and could not buy.

He wrote it down. And then, because he was Lin Hao and because a thing written down was a thing that could be used, he thought about what rhythm was *for.*

Wu Zhangkong had said, in the mecha lecture, that two soul masters could fuse — could speak with one voice — if their souls were compatible enough. Lin Hao had been ten years old for four months and had heard that sentence the way everyone heard it, as a fact about the world, like weather. Now he lay in the dark above Zhou Zhangxi and turned it over, and looked at the seventh line, and understood that he was holding a fact about the world in one hand and a fact about himself in the other, and that the two facts had the same shape.

He did not say anything about it. Not to Xie Xie, not to Wulin, not to anyone, and this was unusual for him, because Lin Hao said everything — Lin Hao's entire method of being alive was to say the thing, loudly, first, before the thing could say him. But this one he kept, and he kept it for a reason he could not have articulated, which was that saying it out loud would make it a *plan,* and it wasn't a plan. It was a door. And doors were not plans. You did not schedule a door. You arrived at it, one day, when you were heavy enough to open it, and until then you left it alone and did your work.

Eleven days later, Wu Zhangkong paired him with Xie Xie for the evening session, and did not say why, and Lin Hao — who had spent eleven days not saying anything — fought his oldest friend for twenty-two minutes across a training ground that had gone completely silent, and understood that the man with the smoky green eyes had arrived at the same door from the other side, and had looked at it, and had decided, without saying so, to leave it exactly where Lin Hao had left it.

Neither of them mentioned it. That was the whole of the conversation, and it was the most respectful thing anyone had ever done for him.

## Part 5: one hundred and eighty-four

The growth was not a surprise, and it was not gradual, and it was not the sort of thing that happened to people who sat in meditation and breathed.

It happened on the ninth day, at four in the morning, in the dark, and it woke him up.

He had taken the stick on the left ribs nine times in three weeks and the bruise had gone through its colours, and his soul power had been working on the problem the way his soul power worked on everything — quietly, in the background, the way the smith's fire works on a bar you've already put down. What had changed on the ninth day was that the *reading* caught up with the *body.* For eight days he had been able to see Wu Zhangkong's wrist and unable to move in time. On the ninth day he moved in time. Not all the way. Not reliably. But the gap closed, and something in him — the thing that had been fed a hundred-year soul at eight and a half, the thing that had eaten nine years of pressure and asked for more — took the closing of that gap and *converted* it.

one hundred and eighty-four became one hundred and eighty-four overnight.

Six points. From one closed gap. He lay in the dark and did the arithmetic, because he was his father's son, and the arithmetic said that the gap he had closed against Wu Zhangkong was worth more than the entire tournament had been, and the reason was not that Wu Zhangkong was stronger than a tournament. The reason was that Wu Zhangkong was *harder,* and hardness was the fuel, and he had spent three weeks being fed the hardest thing in Eastsea City and had not noticed he was eating.

"Hao," Wulin said, from the bunk below, in the voice of a boy who had not been asleep either. "Your soul power is making a noise."

"My soul power doesn't make a noise."

"It's making a noise. It's doing the thing it does when you've been sitting in the sun." A pause. "Did you grow again?"

"Eight."

"In your *sleep?*"

"In my sleep."

Wulin was quiet for a moment. "That's not fair."

"It's not," Lin Hao agreed cheerfully, and rolled over, and went back to sleep, because the arithmetic was finished and the numbers were put away and there was training in four hours and a man with a stick who had, apparently, never once hit him.


Rank thirty arrived in the fourth week, and the moment it did, everything stopped. Not the training. The *rank.* Twenty-one to thirty was the second-ring band and he was standing at the top of it, and the wall in front of him was not a wall he could climb — it was a wall that needed a third ring, and a third ring needed a hawk that had crossed a thousand years, and the hawk was at seven hundred and seventy and had never once, in four years, been in a hurry.

"You're at the top," Wulin said that night, in the tone of a boy who had been keeping count for four months and had not said so.

"I'm at the top of the stairs," Lin Hao agreed. "There's a door at the top. The door is locked and the key is a bird."

"That's the weirdest thing you've ever said."

"It's the truest thing I've ever said. Everything from here on costs something I can't forge and can't fight for. I have to *wait* for it, Wulin, and I have never once been good at that, and I've got a feeling I'm about to find out what I'm made of.

## Part 1: What Shrek Did

They registered. Xie Xie did the talking, because Xie Xie always did the talking, and because the man behind the desk had looked at Lin Hao's name for four seconds longer than a name takes, and Xie Xie had stepped half a pace to the left without being asked, which was the most useful thing anybody did all morning.

That was the part nobody had expected, because Shrek Academy was not a thing that entered public tournaments, and Lin Hao had watched them stand at the back of the stands for four days and assumed the standing was the whole of it.

"We'll just join another academy temporarily before the match," Ye Xinglan said. She said it the way you say a thing you have said many times. "No one will say anything if we do that."

"You can do that."

"People do it all the time."

"Shrek does it."

"Shrek does it," Ye Xinglan agreed, "and nobody says anything." She looked at him sideways. "I'm telling you rather than letting you work it out, because you work things out too fast and I wanted to be the one who said it first, for once."

Lin Hao looked at her.

"That's the first thing anybody's said to me in this city I didn't already know."

"I know," Ye Xinglan said. "That's why I said it."

## Part 2: What The Girl Was

Her name was Mu Xi, and Lin Hao had not met her, and she had been in the smithy hall when he hit the coppertite eighty-one times, and she had not said anything.

He heard about her from Xu Lizhi, because Xu Lizhi appeared to know everybody and had no sense of the things one did not say.

"She's a fourth rank," Xu Lizhi said, eating dried fish. "She's fourteen. In the blacksmithing world she's—" he made a gesture with the fish, "—they call her a proud girl sent from the heavens. Her father's the president of a branch. She was the youngest fourth rank in forty years until about eighteen days ago."

"Until when."

"Until you." Xu Lizhi said it without any particular feeling. "You did it at ten. She did it at fourteen. And she was in the hall, and she watched you do it in eighty-one strikes, and she didn't say anything, and I've learned that not saying anything is how you know."

---

## Part 3: What She Said

She came to the smithy on the sixth day. Lin Hao was at a forge, because he had been given a forge, and because he had decided a forge was the only place in Skysea City where he knew what he was doing.

She stood in the doorway about eleven seconds.

"You're the one," she said.

"Yes."

"Eighty-one strikes."

"Yes."

"I've been a fourth rank four months," Mu Xi said. "I was the youngest in forty years. I was fourteen, and my father's the president of a branch, and I've been called a proud girl sent from the heavens since I was eleven, and I worked for it. I want you to know I worked for it. That's the part I need you to hear."

"I hear it," Lin Hao said.

"You don't."

"I do."

"You did it in eighty-one strikes at ten years old," Mu Xi said, "and an old man on a stage called you terrifying, and you've been a fourth rank eighteen days, and I've been one four months, and I want you to tell me what the difference is, because I worked four months and you worked eighteen days and I can't work out what you have that I don't, and I've been standing in this doorway eleven seconds deciding whether to ask."

Lin Hao set the hammer down.

"I don't know," he said.

"You don't know."

"No."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the true one," Lin Hao said. "I've said *I don't know* about myself four times in three weeks and two other people have said it about me, and I've stopped pretending I have an account of it, because I don't. I hit a bar of coppertite eighty-one times and I didn't ask it, I told it, and I'd never done that before, and I can't tell you why it worked. I'd be lying if I said I could."

Mu Xi stood in the doorway.

"That's worse," she said.

"Yes."

"If you'd said you were better than me I could've been angry. If you'd said you'd worked harder I could've worked harder. You've said you don't know, and there's nothing I can do with that."

"No," Lin Hao agreed. "There isn't."

---

## Part 4: What He Offered

He thought about it about nine seconds, which was a long time for him, and then said the thing he had not planned.

"I'll help you," he said.

Mu Xi looked at him.

"What."

"The competition's got three days left. You're a fourth rank and I'm a fourth rank, and I did it in eighty-one strikes and you did it in however many, and the difference isn't that I'm better — it's that I stopped asking and started telling. I can show you what that felt like. It might not transfer. But you've been standing in a doorway eleven seconds, and I'd rather say this than wait for you to ask."

Mu Xi was quiet a while.

"You're offering to help your rival."

"Yes."

"Why."

"Because you're the first person who's resented me for the right reason," Lin Hao said. "Everybody else has been pleasant. Xu Lizhi is pleasant about a storage ring. Ye Xinglan told me something I didn't know because she wanted to be the one who said it first. You're the only person who stood in a doorway eleven seconds deciding whether to ask me something, and you asked, and I don't know, and I'd rather show you than have you not know."

Mu Xi looked at him a long time.

"That's the most annoying thing anybody's ever said to me."

"I know."

"I'm going to take you up on it."

"Good."

"And I'm still going to beat you."

"I'd like that," Lin Hao said. "Gu Yue has beaten me five times and I haven't worked out how both things are true. I'd like a second instance, because one might be a mistake."

---

## Part 5: What Wulin Said

He found Wulin on the seawall, which was where Wulin went when he was working something out, and Wulin was eating dried fish, which was what Xu Lizhi had given him, and which Wulin had accepted with the gravity of a person receiving a thing.

"You've been in a smithy two days," Wulin said.

"Yes."

"With the girl."

"Yes."

"Gu Yue's been watching the matches without me three days and doesn't like it, and Xu Xiaoyan won in the daytime and nobody said anything about it, and you've been in a smithy with a girl who resents you." Wulin ate the dried fish. "I want somebody to notice I've been on a seawall two days with dried fish."

"I notice," Lin Hao said.

"That's the sixth time you've said that."

"I know."

"Say it to somebody else."

"No," Lin Hao said. "I've decided noticing the things nobody says is the whole thing, and you're the one nobody says them to, and you've been on a seawall two days, and I'm not going to stop."

Wulin was quiet a moment.

"That's the nicest thing anybody's said to me all week," he said. "I'm saying it out loud rather than keeping it, because you taught me that saying a thing is the same act as thinking it, and I didn't know I was learning it."

Lin Hao sat down on the seawall.

"I didn't teach you anything."

"You said it four times in front of me," Wulin said, "and the fourth time I said it back, and I noticed I was doing it."

Lin Hao looked at him, and understood that this was the thing he had not known he was doing, and that Wulin had noticed it before he had, and that this was the fourth time in three weeks somebody else had told him something about himself he had not worked out.

"I'm glad you're here," he said.

"I know."

"That's worth saying."

"I noticed," Wulin said. "That's the seventh time."

## Part 6: What Lin Hao Wrote

That night, in the room that smelled of the sea, he took the book down and wrote the number first, because the number never asked anything of him.

*Two hundred and forty-nine.* It had been two hundred and eighty-six. Seven points, somewhere between standing in a smithy and telling a girl he didn't know that he didn't know. He could not say which had done it. The not-being-able-to-say had become a habit, and he had started to find the habit a little frightening, and had not decided what to do about that.

*One thousand three hundred and seventy-four.*

*Mu Xi resented me for the right reason. Everybody else has been pleasant, and pleasant is easy, and I've discovered I've been the only person in a room for four years, and that being pleasant to isn't the same as being seen by, and that the person who resented me for the right reason is the first person who's seen me at all.*

*I told her I don't know. That's the fourth time I've said it about myself and the third time somebody else has said it about me, and I've stopped pretending I have an account of it.*

*She said that's worse — that if I'd said I was better she could've been angry, and if I'd said I'd worked harder she could've worked harder, and that I don't know is nothing she can do anything with.*

*That's the truest thing anybody's said to me about myself, and it came from a girl who resents me. The truest things have all come from people who resent me or who've said I don't know. None of them have come from people who are pleasant.*

And then the line at the bottom, which he wrote last.

*I offered to help her. I don't know why. I've noticed I don't know why I do most of the things that matter, and the not-knowing is the thing I'd have got wrong if I'd thought about it.*

*She's going to beat me. I'd like that. Gu Yue has beaten me five times and I haven't worked out how both things are true, and I'd like a second instance.*

He closed the book.

The lock at his temple lay gold across his cheekbone, and the marks on his forearms were raised and had been raised for thirty-two days, and the hawk turned over in him — *one thousand three hundred and seventy-four* — and did not pull toward anything.

Lin Hao lay on his back in a city that smelled of salt and hot metal, and thought about a girl in a doorway who had resented him for the right reason, and about the fact that he had offered to help her and did not know why, and about the fact that the not-knowing-why had become the most common thing about him.

He slept.

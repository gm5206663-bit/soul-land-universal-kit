## Part 1: Two Wings, One Sky

The golden light was a young man with wings.

Lin Hao reached the street in time to see them open — pure white, unfolding from the golden-haired boy's back the way a door opens onto a room full of noon, and with them a holy aura that climbed until the orange street-lamps seemed ashamed of themselves. Three soul rings rose beneath him.

All three were purple.

"Three thousand-year rings," Xie Xie breathed, and for once there was no joke in him. "On a second-year. He's — that's the Angel Clan blood, that's not a thing you *train* to."

The red-haired girl turned around in the light. Lin Hao had time to notice that she did not look frightened; she looked tired, the way a person looks tired when a small dog has decided to be a wolf at her for the fourth time that week. Then she spread her arms, and the darkness answered her — it swirled and twisted and went solid into a pair of wings the exact opposite of his, jet-black, and a dark aura spread out against the gold like ink poured into a lit pool.

Two rings rose under her. Both purple.

"White wings are the Holy Angel," Wulin said slowly. "It's a legend. And black wings are… the other one. The Fallen Angel."

"It seems I was right." The golden-haired boy — Yue Zhengyu, he would name himself in a moment — smiled and flapped, golden light radiating. "How interesting. So very interesting. Two rings versus three. You don't stand a chance. Surrender and let me take you to be judged."

The girl pursed her lips. Her eyes flashed; darkness folded around her.

"Trying to run?" he snorted, and his first ring blazed, holy radiance surging out to dispel the dark; his second lit and he drove the light before him and flew at her on a flap of white wings. "I told you. You have two and I have three. Stop resisting."

The sneer on the girl's face was the last thing Lin Hao saw before she did the one thing nobody expected.

She furled her wings. She withdrew her martial soul entirely. The dark aura vanished, the rings sank, and she stood in the street in her apron with her hands at her sides, and she said, clearly, into the face of three thousand years of holy light:

"This is Shrek, you dumbass."

A wave of force split the clouds over the street.

It did not come from either of them. It came down from above — sharp, clean, indifferent as a god closing a book — and it obliterated the light and the dark both in the same instant and pressed the golden-haired boy out of the air and onto his knees in the road. A tall man stood in the sky, his figure indistinct, and his voice came down over the whole street like a bell over a courtyard.

"Who dares break the laws of Shrek City?"

Yue Zhengyu found his tongue first. "Honorable enforcer. I am Yue Zhengyu of the Angel Clan, second grade, class one. I discovered a Fallen Angel on campus — and everyone knows Fallen Angels become evil soul masters. I acted to capture her, to take her before my clan for judgement."

The girl did not argue her bloodline. She reached into her apron and took out a card, and held it up.

"Respected enforcer. I am a working student of this academy. Shrek has already verified my identity and determined that I am not an evil soul master."

Lin Hao felt the little group around him go very still.

*A working student.* Wulin's thought was so loud it might as well have been spoken. Xie Xie made a small, strangled sound. The girl with the black wings and two thousand-year rings — who had faced three on even ground and then simply *stopped*, because she had known the sky would answer — was a working student. Like them. She did not give her name. Lin Hao noted that too: she gave the enforcer her card and no name, the way a person does when a name is a thing she is careful where she leaves it.

An attractive force drew the card up to the enforcer. He looked at it, and turned to the boy on his knees. "Her identity as a working student is confirmed. It is impossible for her to be an evil soul master. I will not punish you — you believed you were apprehending an evil soul master. But the next time, you will be punished two-fold. Now break it up."

The card drifted back down. The enforcer was gone in a blur, and the street was just a street again, orange-lit and full of people who were very suddenly, very intently, looking at their own feet.

The girl put her card away. She walked three steps toward the kneeling boy. She punched a fist out toward him, stuck her thumb up, and then, slowly, turned the thumb to point at the ground.

"You—!" His eyes went red.

She snorted. She turned and walked into the dark between two lamps, and was gone the way water goes into a drain — not running, just *no longer there*, and Lin Hao, who tracked entrances and exits the way other people tracked weather, could not afterward have said exactly which alley she took.

Yue Zhengyu made to chase. His butler's hand closed on his arm.

"You must not offend the dignity of Shrek, young master." The older man's voice was low and steady. "Since the academy itself has confirmed her identity, there is nothing to be gained by going further."

"So they're saying a Fallen Angel is *not* an evil soul master." The boy's voice had gone ugly. "It seems Shrek is—"

"Young master." A ripple of power rode over the words, flattening them before they could finish. "Watch what you say."

Yue Zhengyu shook him off and stormed away into the gold-stained night.

No one said anything for a while. The five of them stood in the street with the rest of the crowd, and Lin Hao watched the crowd as much as he watched the empty sky, because the crowd was the more informative thing. The students of Shrek had watched a second-year Angel Clan prodigy press three purple rings on a waitress, and they had watched the sky knock him down, and not one of them looked *surprised*. They looked the way people look at weather they have seen before.

"Now this," Xiaoyan said softly, "is Shrek City."

## Part 2: The Same Gate

Back in the shabby dormitory, not one of them could sleep, which was the opposite of what a fight in the street should do to a body.

Xie Xie whispered it to Wulin, but he whispered it in the room where all of them could hear, because there was no point whispering anything in a room divided by one hung cloth. "Captain. Don't you think there's something strange about working students? In the dining hall everyone got respectful — even a little *afraid* — the second you said it. The examiner who beat me into the ground during the trials. And that red-haired girl tonight, with the black wings and two purple rings. Both of them are working students. Maybe we should look into this."

"We're new here," Wulin said. He was lying on his back, staring at the plank ceiling. "Our first job is to learn how this place breathes. We're still small in it. Don't go looking for trouble and don't go looking for secrets — not until we've put down roots. Put the curiosity on the shelf. We can open it later."

"That's," said Xie Xie glumly, "the second time someone sensible has told me to be quiet tonight."

Gu Yue rose without a word and hung the cloth that split the room, the way she did everything — by means that left the curtain rod untroubled by her ever having stood on a stool — and the four of them (five, counting the margin) settled to meditate.

Lin Hao did not sleep for a different reason. He had an appointment at dawn.

The practice yards behind the outer-court classrooms were empty at that hour, the flagstones still dark with the night's dew, when he walked in and found Ye Xinglan already there. She had not come in uniform. She wore a plain grey training tunic and had the Stargod Sword out of its sheath and resting across her shoulders, and she was doing slow forms in the grey light, one after another, the way a person does who has done the same ten forms every dawn since she was old enough to hold a blade.

"You came," she said. She did not open her eyes.

"I said I would." He drew the practice sword he had borrowed from the yard rack — plain iron, no name, the kind of blade the academy left out for anyone. "The sword you used in thirty-one seconds was yellow along the edge. I'd like to see what four months put on it."

"Then don't blink."

She opened her eyes and came at him.

It was not the spar from the exhibition. In Skysea she had been two yellow rings and a prodigy's confidence, fast and proud and readable, and he had beaten her footwork by deciding where she was going to be. This morning she was three rings and a thousand-year spirit and, more than that, she was a girl who had spent every dawn since then inside the one state he knew better than anyone alive — the man-and-sword-as-one, the place where the blade is not held so much as *agreed with*. Her first cut did not come from her arm. It came from the whole of her, the way a stroke of weather comes from the whole sky, and starlight ran along the iron edge of the practice blade she had drawn to match him.

He met it in the margin.

He did not use Wind-Step. He did not light a ring. He met her with the plain borrowed iron and the plain, enormous truth of a body that had been built on an anvil and fed a ten-thousand-year dragon, and he answered her the way he answered everything worth answering — exactly enough, and not a breath more. The first exchange rang three times across the empty yard and left her pushed back a single step.

The single step was the point. She felt it, the way a swordsman always feels it: he had stopped her cut without trying to, the way a wall stops a thrown cup, and there had been no technique in it she could name.

"You're not even lit," she said. Her eyes were very bright.

"It's dawn," he said. "I didn't come here to show you rings. I came to see your sword."

She came again. He let her push him, for the most part — across the yard, around the dew-dark stones, through the forms she knew and two she invented on the spot, the starlight on her blade thickening as her third ring answered her need. She was good. She was, ring for ring, better than anyone he had ever sparred who was not trying to kill him; her sword-consciousness was real and deep and hard-won, and there were moments where the Stargod Sword and the girl holding it genuinely ceased to be two things. He answered those moments with the borrowed iron and let her feel, through the steel, that there was a rung above the rung she stood on — not a wall, not a ceiling, a *rung*, one more step on the same ladder she was already climbing.

At the end she drove a two-handed cut down at him with the third ring blazing, starlight pouring like a fall of sparks, and he turned it aside with the flat of his blade and put the point of his practice iron — gently, precisely, and without hurry — in the hollow of her throat.

The yard was quiet. The dew dripped off a stone.

"You could have done that," she said, breathing hard, "on the first cut."

"I could have done it before you drew." He lowered the blade. "Where would the fun in that have been."

She stared at him. Then she laughed — a short, startled sound, the laugh of a girl who decided things and had just, for the first time in a year, had a decision taken away from her without losing. "You held back the entire time."

"I held *forward*," Lin Hao said. "There's a difference. Back would have been ignoring you. Forward is standing on the next rung and letting you feel it under your own feet when you catch up." He set the borrowed iron back in the rack. "Your sword's at the gate. You know that. You've known for a while. You don't need me to tell you where the rung is — you need another ten thousand cuts, and you already know that too."

She was quiet for a long moment, sheathing the Stargod Sword with slow care. "The thing in the drink shop," she said at last. "When my blade went quiet. That wasn't you being strong."

"No."

"That was you being *further*. On the same ladder." She slung the sheath across her back, and looked at him with the directness that was the whole of her, the thing he had liked from thirty-one seconds onward. "All right. Then I'm not behind a stranger. I'm behind a man who's standing on the rung I'm climbing to. I can work with that." She turned to go, then over her shoulder: "I'm going to take that gate, Lin Hao. And when I do, I want you in the yard the next dawn."

"You can have the yard," he said. "Bring me the sword that takes it."

She was gone into the morning, and he stood a moment in the empty practice yard with the grey light coming up over the stones, and thought that of all the things Shrek had handed him in his first day, a swordswoman who heard a wall as a rung was near the top of the list.

Then the bell rang for the morning, and he went to find the others, because the working students had jobs to collect.

## Part 3: The Name at the Counter

Wulin and Xiaoyan were up already when he got back, sitting cross-legged facing each other by the thin light, cultivating their Purple Demon Eyes in the old paired rhythm. They washed. They faced the first problem of the day, which was breakfast — Wulin still had black steamed buns, which he hoarded like a dragon — and then they went, as working students did at the start of every day, to the administration building to be told what they would earn.

The administrator looked up from his papers. "You four — five — continue cleaning Spirit Ice Plaza today. You did well yesterday. Keep it up."

"Teacher." Wulin leaned in with the particular face he wore when money was involved. "Besides cleaning the plaza — can we take other jobs?"

"You want more?" The man stared. It was normal for working students to struggle under the work; it was not normal to ask for extra. Then he shrugged and handed over a soul screen. "Fine. Look through the list and choose."

They were crowding the screen over Wulin's shoulder when the door opened and someone came in.

"Teacher. I've finished my job."

The administrator's whole manner changed. "The design plans are done? That's fast. Yuanen — your efficiency keeps improving." He took the student's card and added contribution points to it. "Go on, take another."

Lin Hao did not need Wulin's elbow in his ribs. He had already turned his head.

The boy — the young man, it was hard to tell — was utterly unremarkable. Ordinary face, ordinary build, ordinary clothes, the kind of person you looked at and immediately looked away from because there was nothing for the eye to catch hold of. He moved to the counter, scrolled the job list, chose something, and turned to leave. On the way out he gave Wulin's group exactly one nod — small, level, the recognition of one working student for a knot of others — and went out into the morning in silence.

*Yuanen.*

Lin Hao watched the door close, and behind his eyes a line of small, unremarkable facts slid into the same slot. The administrator's pleasure — *your efficiency keeps improving*. A finished set of design plans before most students were awake, which meant a second profession at a level the academy paid well for. The walk, which did not waste motion. The single nod, given only to working students. And the bearing of the person underneath the ordinariness, which was not ordinary at all to the part of him that had spent his whole life reading the weight a body carried: there was a great deal of power in that plain frame, folded away the way the waitress had folded her wings.

The waitress. Red hair. Black wings. Two purple rings. A working-student card. A name she had not given. A way of leaving that he could not track.

*Yuanen.*

He said nothing. Not here. Not in front of the counter, and not even to Wulin yet; a name noticed in the margin was a tool, and a tool shown before its use was a tool spent. But he filed it the way he filed everything that mattered — in the book no one read — and he put one quiet line under it. The examiner who had walked Xie Xie into the ground. The waitress who had faced an Angel Clan prodigy on even ground and then let the sky do her fighting for her. The unassuming student at the counter who finished design plans before dawn. The working students were not poor children the academy tolerated. They were a *selection*.

He was beginning to understand what was being selected.

Wulin, at the screen, had found the metalwork. "I'll take this one. First-grade thousand-refining."

The administrator stared again. "Are you certain? Points are deducted for failure, and you're expelled if you can't cover your tuition — all of you working students owe three hundred contribution points a month. Three hundred, in addition to feeding yourselves."

"Certain." Wulin tapped the screen. He did not explain that a first-grade thousand-refining job paid one thousand points, or that he had crossed into the fifth rank and his success rate on first-grade work was near total. Lin Hao watched him make the choice and thought it through himself: the math of Shrek. Outer court, over a thousand students, every one of them driving toward battle armor rather than mecha — and thousand-refined metal was the bedrock of every one-word suit. The academy did not lack for smithing work; the academy *was* the demand. The points were a leash, yes; he had called them that the first day. But a leash pulled both ways. A leash meant the thing on the end was worth holding.

Outside, Xie Xie slid up to Wulin. "So that guy's name is Yuanen."

"Still carrying the grudge?" Wulin said. "You can't beat him right now. And I think we misunderstood the age thing — second-year students here aren't a year older than us. They're about three."

"We don't know that for certain," Xie Xie retorted, without much heat. "The academy takes anyone fifteen and under. We got in at thirteen. Maybe he got in younger."

"You can buy contribution points off me with money," Wulin offered, patting his shoulder.

"Boss, you're not that much of a money-grubber—"

"I really am. Self-reliance is a Tang Sect tradition. If you want points, build your second profession — and mark me, those points buy more than food and tuition before we're done."

Lin Hao walked a half-step behind them, the morning sun on his face, and said none of what he knew. He had his own smith's summons in his bag, the Shrek Blacksmiths' Association door that would open on his own rank, and he had a name now — *Yuanen* — and he had a guild that was not a guild but a selection, and all three of those things would keep.

## Part 4: The Door

The opening ceremony was held in Spirit Ice Plaza, washed clean by their own labor, and it was there that Lin Hao finally felt the size of the place.

Six grades. Each a single class of roughly a hundred students, give or take a dozen. A little over seven hundred in the outer court altogether — and the eye ran over them and kept running, because the oldest students in the plaza were not boys. They were men and women into their thirties, standing in their grade's block with the solemnity of people who had chosen a mountain over a life, extraordinary cultivation coiled in them like springs, and they were still outer court, still ungraduated, still earning their place year on year. Students graduated a mundane academy in their early twenties. Here were people who had laid their twenties down on the same flagstones the five of them stood on.

"If Wu Zhangkong were put into an outer grade," Xiaoyan murmured, doing the same arithmetic, "he'd only be about fifth grade."

"That," said Wulin, with feeling, "is the most frightening sentence anyone has said this week."

Then the teachers rose into the air over the plaza, and Lin Hao's attention found the figure at the center of them, and he felt a particular stillness settle over him — the stillness of a man who has just watched a card he had been keeping turn over in someone else's hand.

Elder Cai.

She stood at the head of the faculty in her plain dark robes, indifferent as frost on iron, and the administrator's introductions gave her title the way one gives a fact too large to need emphasis: **Cai Yue'er, headmaster of the Shrek outer court.**

*Of course she is.* Lin Hao thought it with something close to admiration. The woman who had handed him the ordinary transcript on purpose. The woman who had kept Gu Yue's disciple door open instead of slamming it. The woman who had said *points aren't coins, blacksmith* and *I keep one book, you keep one, keep yours honest.* She was not an examiner they had survived. She was the jurisdiction. The battle-armor-before-twenty bar she had set them had not been an elder's idle demand; it had been the head of the outer court telling them the price of her own house.

Her speech was short. Shrek did not force cultivation. Education was not compulsory. There was a minor examination every year, and a major one every three; failing the minor twice meant expulsion, and so every grade shrank year on year — that was the seven hundred, that was why some of them were thirty-five and still not graduated. The sixth graders who had not met the requirements should be prepared to lay down everything before they turned thirty-five. The new students might relax if they liked — and then they might not be here in three years. The faculty would teach; the facilities stood open; everything cost contribution points. They would learn the importance of contribution points soon enough.

"That's it?" Xie Xie whispered. "That's the whole speech?"

"She said everything a headmaster needs to," Lin Hao said quietly, "and charged us nothing for it. I'd be careful of that, if I were you. Free words from her are the most expensive thing in this plaza."

The ceremony ended. The teachers in the air split off toward their grades.

Wulin's face lit when he saw two of the descending figures — Wu Zhangkong, and Shen Yi, coming straight toward the new-student block. Then the light on his face stiffened, and Lin Hao saw why. There was a third figure with them, dark-robed and frost-composed, and her lips were curved into the smallest, most satisfied smile Lin Hao had ever seen on a human being who was not currently eating.

Elder Cai landed in front of the new class.

"I," she said, "will be personally managing this year's new students." She did not raise her voice; the whole block heard her anyway. "Beside me are my assistant teachers — Teacher Shen Yi, and Teacher Wu Zhangkong." She let a beat pass. "Now. To the classroom."

Lin Hao felt the group around him fold. Wulin's eyes went wide. Xie Xie's jaw hung. Xiaoyan made a small sound like a kitten that has just noticed the lid going on. Gu Yue's lips thinned to a line — the closest thing to a flinch she ever allowed herself, the woman whose disciple-door Cai had held open, the student who had said no and who was now, it appeared, going to be taught by the door-holder every single day.

He almost smiled. *She never lost a night's sleep over the no. She didn't need to. She owns the room we'll be sitting in.*

The lecture theatre was on the first floor and built for three hundred, which made the hundred-odd first-years look scattered and small. Shen Yi took the roll and called the seating in her clear, implacable voice, front and center, no more than five meters from the lectern:

"Tang Wulin — row one, seat one." Center. Wulin scrambled in.

"Gu Yue — row one, seat two." His right.

"Xie Xie — row one, seat three." His left.

"Xu Xiaoyan — row one, seat four." Beside Gu Yue.

Lin Hao waited for the dismissal of the working students to the back of the house — the speech had already reorganized them once, in the plaza, drifting the five of them toward the rear on the strength of their status. He had already assumed the margin. That was where he sat.

Shen Yi turned a page. Her eyes found him with no particular expression at all, and he understood, the way one understands weather before the words arrive, that the list was not done.

"Lin Hao — row one, seat five."

A beat. The pause was so small that only he would ever have caught it; it was the beat in which Elder Cai, behind the lectern, did not move and did not need to, and Shen Yi read the next name off a sheet that had clearly been settled before any of them had entered the room.

"Beside Xu Xiaoyan. Up front."

He rose and walked the length of the theatre under the eyes of a hundred strangers and sat down in seat five — the fifth of the five working students placed dead-center in the first row, in arm's reach of the lectern, where not a word or a note or a breath could possibly be missed. It was not punishment. Wulin got the same seat. It was something else, and he knew exactly what it was, because she had told him in the registration office in a line he had been turning over ever since:

*A marked boy is a watched boy.*

She had chosen to keep the book she could not yet classify not on the shelf, not in the restricted cabinet, but open on the desk directly under her hand — where she could read it whenever she liked, and where the reading itself might, in time, write the page she was waiting for.

"One hundred and one students in this year's class," Shen Yi reported. "One hundred and one counted. Everyone is present."

Elder Cai regarded the lecture theatre with her frost-on-iron gaze, and let it settle — for one half-second longer than anywhere else — across the first row.

"You heard the ceremony speech, so I won't repeat it. Here at Shrek, your teachers will show you the door. It is up to you to step through it." She folded her hands. "That is enough for today. The rest is yours."

Lin Hao sat in seat five, five meters from the woman who owned the room, and thought about doors. About the door Gu Yue had walked through first into the golden light. The door the enforcer had answered. The rung Ye Xinglan called a gate. And the door a headmaster had just shown all five of them — the first of the working-student selection — by seating them so close to her that she could watch them decide whether to step.

He opened his notebook to a fresh page.

At the very top, he wrote one line, in the careful hand he used for the book nobody else read.

*The teacher shows the door. The question is which side of it she's standing on.*

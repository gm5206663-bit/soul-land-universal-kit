## Part 1: The Ticket

The job board in the suppression hall was three walls of parchment, colour-coded by what the guild thought would come back to collect the money. White and yellow tickets you could read and yawn through. Purple ones made working students count their rings. The single black-rimmed ticket pinned crooked in the far corner made the clerk look up twice when Lin Hao's hand reached for it.

"Ten-thousand class, ridge-armoured boar," the clerk said, reading it off like he was contractually obliged to warn every taker once. "Iron-Tusk. It ranged up out of the lower country after the month's storms and it's been working the ore trail. Three supply trains, two haulers killed. It's wounded — a hunter got a spear into its off-shoulder two weeks back — and a wounded ten-thousand beast is meaner than a healthy one. Suppression, full salvage rights to the taker, eleven hundred points. Standard black class." He did not say the part they always left unspoken, but his eyes said it: this was rated for a team. Three four-ring masters, or one older, calmer one. Not a first-year with a school tag on his bag.

Lin Hao read the ticket properly, the way he read a piece of metal before he bought it — the grain of it, the places it was likely to fail. Three trains raided on the same stretch meant a territory, a den, an animal that had learned the road and liked it. Wounded off-shoulder meant it would favour the left and turn right last. Ridge-armoured boar meant a back plated in horn that read like poorly-quenched steel — hard on the surface, soft at the seam, and the seam ran where the shoulder met the spine. Eleven hundred points and the salvage of a ten-thousand-year hide and a pair of tusks that would buy a great deal of workshop time.

He had spent weeks on easy jobs, the safe tickets, the half-meals. He was a working student with a workshop to rent and a brother to feed and a standard he was tired of walking around. He took the ticket down.

He knew what the black rim on the parchment meant. White tickets were nuisance beasts for the working students who needed a few points before meals; yellow were honest work; purple made you send word to your teacher. A black-rimmed ticket was the guild admitting, in ink, that the thing out there would kill a careless four-ring master, and pricing the job accordingly. The eleven hundred points on the bottom line was more than a first-year earned in two months of hall-scutwork, and the salvage of a ten-thousand-year hide would cover a season of workshop rent by itself. It was not a number a boy turned down if he was tired of the small jobs.

He thought it through the way he thought through a commission with a hard deadline. He had four rings. One of them was black, which changed the arithmetic quietly — his fourth soul skill was a band sharper than any ordinary four-ring master carried. He had two souls in him, a body that knits overnight and did not feel the cold, and a sword teacher who had spent years telling him the margin lived in the preparation, not the moment. What he did not have was a team, and what the ticket wanted was three men. The gap between "three men" and "one careful man" was a gap you could close with the one thing a working smith understood better than most of the academy: setting up before the metal ever went in the fire. A trap of ground and timing cost no soul power at all.

"Alone?" the clerk said.

"I work faster alone," Lin Hao said. It was not bravado; it was the smith's habit, the man who measures a job and knows the size of his own hands. "Write it. If I don't come back, the points don't cost you anything."

The clerk wrote it. Outside, the autumn air off the ranges was thin and clean and full of the smell of cold iron, which out in that country was not a smell at all but a direction.

## Part 2: Reading the Ground

He did not go looking for the boar the first day. That was the difference between a four-ring master who walked into a black-class ticket and a fool. He walked the ore trail.

Three days of reading sign. Where the haulers had been pulled off the road, the ground told him the beast had come from uphill and gone back uphill the same way — it had a den on high ground, not down by the water as a healthy boar would choose. The wound had pulled it up into the cold. He found the wallow where it lathered the shoulder in mud against the pain; he read the depth of the prints and timed them against the moon, the way he'd been reading hammer-strikes against metal since he was small — rhythm, weight, the tell of an off-balance beat. The left tusk cut deeper into the earth than the right. It carried the good shoulder into the turn last, slow and heavy, like a door whose hinge had gone.

On the third morning he climbed above the wallow and found the ridge itself: a long spill of broken quarry stone where the mountain had dropped its iron-rich rock a hundred years ago and left a natural rampart, loose underfoot, sloping to a dead end against a cliff. A stretch of bad footing between two walls of scree. He stood in it for a long while and listened to the stone shift under his boots, and he did something no one who only understood soul power would think to do. He built a trap out of the country itself.

He set loose scree to slide on a line he marked with his eyes. He wedged two of the largest blocks so they would come when he needed them. He picked the exact patch where the bad footing met the cliff base — a narrow lane where a big animal, charging on a lame right side, could not turn and could not climb and could only pile up against the stone. He worked at it for a day with no soul power at all, just hands and a borrowed pry-bar and the kind of patience a smith uses to set a forge before the iron ever goes in. He thought about the animal, too, the way he thought about any customer's metal — what it wanted, what it feared, what it would do when the heat came. A ridge-boar was not clever the way a pack-beast was clever; it could not be out-thought, only out-arranged. It would run the trail it knew, which was the uphill line. It trusted the plated ridge over its back and charged threats head-on, because for ten thousand years charging had worked. The lame right side was the one crack in a design that otherwise had none. None of this was cleverness on his part. It was just reading: the same skill he used on a bad ingot, finding the fault line before he put the first strike on it.

He slept the third night in a crook of the cliff above the trap and woke before the cold left the air, and he checked every wedge and every sliding stone twice, the way a smith checks his quench water before committing the blade. The whole job, he already understood, would live or die in these three quiet days. The chase itself would only be the forging — the metal already shaped by the forge.

Then, at dusk, he went down to the wallow and he made enough noise to be the easiest thing on the mountain.

## Part 3: The Charge

It came like a bad omen, the ground starting to move before the animal was visible — three tons of ridge-armoured fury driving uphill out of the treeline, the good left tusk leading, the plated ridge across its back the colour of old foundry scrap. Ten thousand years of a life spent charging things made the ground afraid of it. A four-ring master who met that head-on with soul power and courage lost; the math had no good answer, which was why the ticket was rated for a team.

Lin Hao did not meet it head-on.

He ran. Up the quarry ramp, light-footed on the scree he had spent the day learning, the boar's momentum carrying it up the funnel behind him far faster than it could turn — and at the mouth of the lane he did the one thing the ridge hide could not answer. He didn't cut the armour. He set his footing and brought the Frost Abyss Sword, not with power but with the whole of Sword Consciousness, the man-and-sword-as-one certainty that puts an edge exactly where the seam already wants to part, and he drove it into the ridge seam where the shoulder met the spine on the beast's slow right side — the bad side, the late-turn side — one clean, deep, perfectly-placed stroke into the quenching fault in the horn. He held it there a heartbeat, rode the throw, and then he was gone, off the line, while the animal screamed and the wound that had already been there ripped wide along the weakness he'd opened.

Then the mountain did the work for which he had paid it a day. The wedged blocks came down behind the beast as it floundered on the lane of loose stone, and the scree slid, and three tons of ridge-armoured ten-thousand-year boar piled itself up against the cliff base in a heap of bad footing and its own fury, hamstrung by terrain, the plated shoulder jammed, the great head turned wrong.

## Part 4: The Edge of the Thing

It did not die easy, and he would have been ashamed of the story if it had. A ten-thousand beast trapped is still a ten-thousand beast, and it got a foreleg free and the good tusk came across in a blow that would have folded a lesser man's ribs through his back. He took it on the Frost Abyss domain a hand's-breadth thick — not the domain at war, not the full unhidden thing, just the cold folded small around him like a shield-brace — and the tusk slid on the ice and took him across the forearm instead of the chest, and even so it threw him into the scree hard enough that he saw the dark at the edge of the afternoon.

He was on one knee in the loose stone with the boar wrenching itself free and his breath gone and his sword hand numb, and it was at that exact edge — hurt, half-spent, a tusk coming up for the second blow that he did not have a clean answer for — that he felt it. Not reached it. Felt the distant wall of it.

The way a boy swimming far out in a dark lake for the first time puts his foot down expecting bottom and there is no bottom, and for one cold breath he understands the true size of the water. There was a place further on in his own sword, a frequency under the steel he had never touched, a depth his man-and-sword-as-one awareness only floated on the surface of — Sword Soul, the place the old legends pointed at, where the whole inner sea rang in the blade. It was there. It was impossibly far. He could no more cross to it in that moment than the swimmer could walk on the floor of the lake. He only understood, in one clear cold breath, how much deeper the water went than the shore he stood on.

Then the breath ended, because the tusk was still coming, and he was a rank-45 four-ring master with a wounded animal and a trapped lane and a day's good preparation, and that was plenty — he did not need to be anything more than what he was.

## Part 5: The Kill and the Walk Back

He used what he had, not what he didn't. He read the boar's next charge the way he read the metal — where it would come apart — and he did not meet it with power. He stepped into the one opening the jammed shoulder made, the late right side, and he did the small, unglamorous, smith's work of cutting: the deep seam-stroke driven twice more into the fault he had already opened, one to widen it, one to find the thing underneath, with Gale Talon speed and Still Water steadiness and the domain's cold to make the beast's great head come slow. The third stroke found the spine through the ruined seam, and the biggest thing on that mountain sagged into its own trap and stopped.

He stood there a long while after, breathing, forearm laid open to the bone where the tusk had crossed it, already closing because that was the jiao in him, the deep water knitting the wound together whether he asked it to or not. He did not whoop. He did not feel like a legend. He felt like a boy who had correctly measured a job and then been very lucky and very good, in about that order, and who had learned one thing worth more than the eleven hundred points: the size of the water. Sword Soul was real. He had felt the wall of it. And it was a thousand dawns away, which was exactly where a thirteen-year-old's sword realm ought to be.

He took the ridge plates first, because they were the prize, and he worked the joints the way Mu Chen had taught him back in Eastsea, with respect for a material that a beast had grown over ten thousand years and that some smith would pay to lay hands on. The horn-plate across the back read like poorly quenched steel even off the beast — too hard on the surface, soft and fibrous at the seam — and he noted the fault to himself as he cut, the smith's eye never quite turning off; it was the same weak line he had driven the sword into, and seeing it laid open confirmed the kill the way a clean fracture confirms a good strike. The two great tusks he took whole. The core, small and cold and dark in the chest cavity, he wrapped against his own heartbeat. The rest — too heavy for one boy's back even if the back was his — he marked with the guild's token for the haulers to bring down, because taking more than he could carry was how the careful men ended up dead on the trail beside their own salvage. He walked down the long ore trail in the dark with his arm still closing and the points not yet paid and the trap he had built sitting empty up on the ridge.

No one at Shrek saw him go and no one saw him come back, which was as it should be; it was a contracted job on contracted ground, not a thing that rang any note across the academy. In the suppression hall the clerk looked at the tusks, looked at the blood soaked into Lin Hao's sleeve that no longer had a wound under it, looked back at the tusks, and stamped the ticket complete without saying a word about teams.

Eleven hundred points, plus the salvage. He went and fed the five of them properly for the first time in a month, and he did not tell anyone how close the second tusk had come. That night in the workshop he sat with the Stormbringer across his knees and did not reach for the deep water he had felt once; he only polished the steel and practised the first Still Water stroke, slow, fifty times, because the thing he had learned out on the ridge was not a gate he could open. It was the distance to the gate. He was on the right shore, and he was exactly as far from the far one as he should be.

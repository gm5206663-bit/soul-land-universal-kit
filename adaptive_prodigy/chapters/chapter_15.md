## Part 1: Four Photographs

Zhou Zhangxi arrived at room eleven the way weather arrives — all at once, and without knocking.

"It's not good. Xie Xie, Wulin, hurry up and come see. A group of people are at the academy gates — hundreds of them — and they're holding *pictures.*" He was bent double, hands on knees, the roster-keeper's usually careful face gone round with running. "Four pictures. One of each of you. They're calling for the academy to hand you over, and I'm afraid the teachers will come out soon, and — and one of them is *enormous.*"

Xie Xie was off his bed before the sentence finished, fury already assembled: "If it's those guys from yesterday, we'll just beat them up again. Bullying people into paying protection fees, yet those thieves dare come to the academy and cry *thief—*"

"Hold on." It was Wulin who caught him — not by argument, by the arm, the way you catch a kettle. And it was Wulin's voice, slow and heavy, that put the room's temperature back where it belonged. "Don't be so impulsive. This shouldn't be that simple. Don't forget where we are — this is Eastsea Academy, a Soul Master's academy. If they dare come *here,* knowing that, it means they're confident in their backing. We're still far too weak for whatever that backing is." A beat. "We should go find Teacher Wu first."

"I stand with Wulin," Gu Yue said, appearing in the doorway with her jacket half on and her expression entirely assembled.

And Lin Hao was already in his shoes, because while the argument had been happening his mind had been doing what it now did fastest — running the forge arithmetic of yesterday. A brother with a broken arm. A brother with a *position,* because men like Guang Long did not own lanes for fun; lanes were small change, and small change flowed *up.* "He'll be there in person," Lin Hao said. "A man who sends underlings doesn't commission four portraits. Go. All of us."

Wu Zhangkong's schedule was strict — after breakfast he meditated, and finding him was not difficult. The door opened on a teacher in a traditional white chang pao, ice-cold, one eyebrow already assembled for trouble.

"What cultivation questions do you have?"

"Teacher Wu, we've caused some trouble…" And Wulin, a little nervous and entirely courageous, retold the whole of yesterday — the lane, the jars, the neck that couldn't be cut, the gold.

Well. Most of the gold. The scales left out; the file kept. Three of them had agreed on the walk over. The fourth had agreed before they'd asked.

Wu Zhangkong listened with an indifferent expression that had gone, by degrees, very still. "It seems you guys actually have some ability," he said at last, coldly — which, class five was learning, was his register for *pride.* And then the white chang pao was moving past them down the hall, delicate and light, rustling in a wind the corridor did not have, blue hair drifting up from the shoulders, and the four students of the worst class in the grade followed their teacher toward the gate the way iron filings follow a magnet: without any sense of having decided to.

## Part 2

The marks on his forearms had stopped fading. That was new — they used to be gone by morning, and now they simply stayed, faint as a healed scratch, the shape a feather makes when it is pressed flat. He had stopped rolling his sleeves down in front of people, because there was no longer anything to hide by evening.
: The Gate

Outside the great gates of Eastsea Academy stood an impenetrable crowd of at least two hundred fierce and burly people holding all sorts of weapons — and that was the part Lin Hao's eyes went to first, past the size of it, past the noise, the way his father's eyes went to the grain before the shape of a bar.

*Soul devices.* Federation-prohibited ones, on a dozen hips and shoulders; standard-issue ones on the rest. He had seen the make-work in the Association's repair yard — trigger groups, charge cells, the cheap stamped housings of things designed to let men who would never hold a ring hold something like one. The age of soul technology had shortened the old distance between soul masters and commoners, and two hundred men with devices were the arithmetic of it, standing in a lane-mob's shape but ordered like a parade. Not a gang. A *unit.*

Above them rode a banner: **MURDER MUST BE PAID WITH LIFE! DEBTS MUST BE PAID! HAND OVER THE CULPRIT!** And in front, on a stretcher, swaddled in gauze like a jar in straw, lay Guang Long. And above the banner, held up for the academy to see, four photographs — a forge-boy, a dagger-boy, a girl with an unbothered face, and a fourth boy with a sword and a look the photographer had caught mid-turn, hawk-eyed, which was the only reason the picture frightened anyone.

At the front of it all stood the enormous one. Two meters at least, shaved head with small sideburns, bare arms corded like ship's cable — Guang Long's face rebuilt bigger and meaner, an upgraded edition of a bad book. Lin Hao did not need the hawk to read the pressure coming off the man; he could have read it in his *boots,* the way the stones lay quieter around those feet. Beside him, Xie Xie's knuckles had gone white on his dagger-hilt, and Wulin had stopped in the middle of a step — the stillness Lin Hao knew: the forge, hearing a hammer it did not recognize.

Long Hengxu was already there with a dozen teachers, and his voice went out over the gates like a dropped portcullis. "What kind of people are you, to actually dare to cause trouble in front of the academy?"

"My name is Guang Biao. The one on the stretcher is my younger brother." The voice was not loud; it did not need to be — it simply *arrived,* like thunder that had skipped the sky. "I have not come as the mecha brigade's captain, but merely as an elder brother with a seriously injured younger brother. Bring out the culprits who caused this."

And Lin Hao watched his director's face change, and filed the words that caused it: *mecha brigade.* Captain. Master regiment rank — one grade under the city's chief executive and answerable to nothing in this lane. Five hundred federation soul mecha garrisoned in Eastsea City, and every burly man behind that shaved head was a Mecha Master with combat time on his record. This was not a protection fee come back for a rematch. This was a *city* at the gate of a school.

"Chief Guang Biao," Long Hengxu said, and the harmoniousness of his tone was its own kind of steel, "there must be some misunderstanding. There are four *children* in your photographs—"

"Children? My brother had been beaten so badly." The flat of a hand gestured at the stretcher, and two hundred men shifted weight on command, and the sound of it was the sound of a wall leaning. "I have given Eastsea City enough face by waiting here. If you do not hand those people over in twenty minutes, we shall search for them ourselves."

"Captain Guang Biao, you shouldn't go overboard! This is an academy — a place for cultivating students. Which of your lot *hadn't* graduated from an academy? I will not hand over the people you seek. If you have the ability, then activate your mecha and step over my dead body. Otherwise, don't think of taking one step into Eastsea Academy."

And the students of the academy — the students who grumbled at Long Hengxu's assemblies and his curfews and his weather-report voice — felt their blood boil, and stepped up behind him. Seniors first. Then everyone. And Lin Hao found himself walking forward too, with Xie Xie at his shoulder and Wulin's heat at his back and Gu Yue's silence at his flank, four small bodies adding themselves to the academy's line the way four rivets add themselves to a hull — not because it changes the arithmetic, but because a hull is *made* of rivets.

Guang Biao took one step forward.

The ground *roared.* The whole gate of Eastsea Academy trembled like a struck gong, and the front line of students swayed and went down, and the tremor walked up through Lin Hao's boots and into his hollow bird-bones and the hawk inside him flared—

—and held. Because the man was a Soul Emperor. Six rings. The kind of thing a rank-24 boy measured the way you measure the sea: not in units. *This one isn't ours,* Lin Hao decided, in the calm under the fear, the way you decide a bar is not for today's forge. *This one's the teacher's. Ours is the gate.* He said it aloud, walking volume, without turning his head: "Whatever happens next — nobody past us. Juniors behind. Now." And class five — twenty-two students who had been given a season of reasons to trust the fourth boy's reading — *moved,* and the juniors went behind, and the line held.

"I only believe in strength," Guang Biao was saying, over the ringing stones. "Where is your sense of righteousness, sheltering the culprits who wounded my brother? Since my fist is bigger, I shall go and find those I seek. I'll see who has the ability to stop m—"

"I dislike talking about reasoning the most," said an ice-cold voice from inside the academy. "Talking with our fists is much better."

## Part 3: White Robes and Blue Sword

The spectators felt a blur, and then a person was standing in front of Eastsea Academy.

White chang pao. Coral-blue hair falling down his back. Inky-green eyes that caught the morning and did not warm it. The temperature at the gate dropped several degrees the way a room drops when a window opens onto winter, and Guang Biao — Guang Biao, whose momentum had been a tornado a sentence ago — felt that momentum break apart against something the way a wave breaks against a glacier.

"Teacher Wu. It's Teacher Wu…" The cheer started in class five's corner of the crowd and could not, afterward, be attributed to class five alone.

"Who are you?" Guang Biao's eyes narrowed, rebuilding.

Wu Zhangkong mildly said: "Those four students you are seeking are my students. If you can defeat me, you may proceed. Otherwise, get lost."

He said it the way other men said the time. And Lin Hao — watching from the line he was holding, the Stormbringer very quiet in its sheath, his whole self tuned the way it only tuned for metal and for this — understood, hearing it, that he was not listening to a threat. He was listening to a *statement.* A true sword speaks first, Teacher Wu had told him, nine weeks and a lifetime ago. This was what that meant when the sword had finished growing: the sentence *is* the strike. The rest is paperwork.

"Ha ha ha! Good — I haven't met someone who dared speak to me this way before!" Guang Biao's laugh was a furnace with the door kicked open. Yellow light poured off him — and the rings came: **yellow, yellow, purple, purple, purple, purple.** Six. A Soul Emperor's six. His body expanded a half meter, metal-gray sheen sheeting from forehead to heel, and three spirit souls rose over him — two yellow lizards, identical, and a purple python vast as a moored boat. A thousand-year soul. The crowd of students made the sound of a crowd understanding it was watching history it would get wrong in the retelling.

His momentum formed into a tornado and roared across the gate at the white robes, and everyone at Eastsea Academy felt winter descend—

—and a pale blue flash answered it, and the tornado's fury halved, and then was simply not.

Wu Zhangkong stood in the same spot he had been standing, white robes afloat, right hand raised, and in the hand was a long sword the color of a frozen lake at depth. The tip like an autumn spring; the light along it flowing like a waterfall. White clothes, blue sword, suspended in the air like an immortal — and Lin Hao's breath stopped for the *sword* before it stopped for the man, because the hawk-eyes saw what the crowd could not: the blade was not reflecting the light. The blade was *deciding* it.

Rings of light rose from beneath the hilt — small rings that wandered along the sword's body rather than standing under a man's feet, tool-soul rings — and their colors went through the gate like a sentence through a room. Yellow. Yellow. Purple. Purple.

**Black. Black.**

The air stilled. The sky itself seemed to frost at the edge of the horizon. Ten-thousand-year soul rings — *two* of them — riding a blade held by a man in his twenties, and Guang Biao's tornado-momentum turned to stone, and every soul master in the crowd forgot, together, how to make sound.

Then Wu Zhangkong lowered his head and looked at his own sword, and the last cold thing about him softened. He looked at the Skyfrost Sword the way Lin Hao — Lin Hao, who had watched his father talk to hot metal his whole childhood — recognized with a start like a hand on the heart: the way a smith looks at the first blade he ever folded. *It had been ages since he'd used it,* that look said. *Old friend.*

"Hou!" Guang Biao bellowed — cornered now, two hundred subordinates deep, unable to be seen backing down — and charged, armored body bursting with metallic light, the mountain-fist swinging up at the white robes with everything a six-ring emperor owned.

"Teacher, be careful!" Wulin's shout tore out of the line.

Wu Zhangkong did not move. He rotated his right hand — all six rings vanished from the blade at once — and gently raised the tip, and met the mountain head-on.

*Ding. Ding. Ding. Ding. Ding. Ding. Ding.*

Seven brittle notes. At the first, the tyrant-dragon charge stopped, the way a wave stops at a seawall it has misunderstood. Notes two through seven were scales — Guang Biao's armored scales — flying off his fist, his forearm, his shoulder, one after another, bright as fish in light. Seven notes, seven steps backward, and the onlookers understood, dazed, that they had witnessed one sword stroke wearing seven disguises. And under the ice-qi that had entered him like needles along the veins, Guang Biao found every spark of soul power in his body committed to defense, and his soul skills would not answer, and his martial soul — the Armored Dragon that had carried him from nothing to a city's fear — had gone *quiet,* the way animals go quiet when something older walks into the clearing.

Wu Zhangkong's deep green eyes had turned purple. He thrust.

It was the simplest movement possible. A beginner's thrust. His eyes never left his own blade, from start to finish, and the sword arrived at Guang Biao's chest and the emperor's blood began to freeze — and the purple python, faithful where its master's courage was not, reared and whipped its thousand-year tail at the swordsman's flank to save him—

—and Wu Zhangkong did not dodge. Did not look. An azure light soared along him, and the tail met the light and was *cleanly cut,* and the spirit soul's scream went through the gate like a wire through wax, and its luster dimmed, and the Skyfrost Sword was already past it—

—stopped one inch from Guang Biao's nose.

The cold radiating off that inch was a net of swords, a death-aura laid over the emperor's whole body like a shawl. His palms, raised in the last reflex of a man who had clawed up from nothing, were pierced and frozen the pale blue of the blade.

"Your—" Guang Biao's voice quavered, and it was not the cold doing it. "Your eyes. You're from *that place…*"

"I was expelled from that place," Wu Zhangkong said, indifferently. "The reason was because my temper wasn't good. As such — don't let me see you again."

The blue light vanished. The white robes turned. And the whole area stayed in a silence so complete it was as if everyone's voices had frozen along with the emperor's hands.

## Part 4: What the Gate Remembered

The roar came all at once, the way thaw comes — the entire academy, intermediate and advanced, teachers and gatekeepers, screaming one name that had started as a joke in the girls' rows two years ago and was now the only word the morning had: *Prince Charming! Prince Charming!* And class five stood in the middle of it with twenty-two flushed faces and twenty-two chests improbably high, as though the six rings had been theirs by adoption — which, in the arithmetic of children, they now were.

Up on the teachers' wall, two sisters watched, and neither was cheering.

Ye Yingrong's beautiful eyes were glazed. "Sister," she murmured, "I didn't like his icy looks initially. Although he is handsome, he's like ice. Yet it seems I've fallen for him. He's handsome enough to forget those flaws."

Beside her, the woman who had waited two years for this morning said nothing for a long moment. Ye Yingluo's eyes followed the white robes across the yard with an expression her students would never have believed belonged to her — hunger, and arithmetic, and a wound that had not closed and had just been reopened wider. "Six rings. He really has six rings — two of them *black.* I thought I had a chance at pursuing him. I see now I will never even have my revenge." Her voice dropped to something flat and settled. "From what he showed just now, he could duel and beat a seven-ring Soul Sage. There's no doubt — he is from *that place.*"

"Sister, aren't you too vindictive?"

"No one has ever rejected me. He was the first." Ye Yingluo's beautiful eyes burned with a stubbornness two years old and freshly fed. "He shall pay the price for that. And his *students* are the road to it." She turned from the yard, already composing the match in her head — the dragon, the eagle, the fusion, the grudge — and the class-one teacher's smile, had anyone been watching it, would have kept a sensible person up at night. "Let the tournament finish. I'll take my price on the platform, where he has to watch."

And here is the thing she did not know, and could not have known, and which would have changed her whole plan if she had known it: **she was counting five students, and the class had six.**

In a class without him, her road runs through Xie Xie's speed and Wulin's hands and Gu Yue's weather, and all three of those are things a woman of her rank can predict and price and beat. **She had priced the class.** She had done it that morning, on the wall, while her sister was falling in love, and the arithmetic had come out comfortable.

What she had not priced was a boy who reads a sword the way other people read a face — because that is not a thing you can price until you have seen it, and the only people in the yard who had seen it were the five students who had stopped being surprised by it, and Wu Zhangkong, who had stopped being surprised by it two years before he met any of them.

**She would find out at the tournament, in front of four hundred people, and the finding-out would cost her more than the rejection had.** That is the butterfly, and nobody in the yard — least of all her — could see it from where they were standing.

Across the yard, on the stones where his tornado had died, Guang Biao stood frozen for over ten minutes, while the chill walked slowly out of his body and his pulse remembered its job. When his hands finally unclenched, blood dripped from the frozen piercings in both palms.

*That guy is terrifying. He is definitely no nobody.*

"Captain — what about us?" one of his lieutenants muttered, hand on a soul device. "Shall we activate the mecha?"

Guang Biao's backhand sent the man staggering. "Bullshit! If you want to die, don't drag me along. Wiping away today's matter is already difficult — activate the mecha at a *school,* and do you think any of us survive the Administrative Department? Go. Go back." And he stood a moment longer over his brother's stretcher, and something in the set of his shoulders — for anyone who could read that language, which included one boy by a gate who read metal and men the same way — was not rage. It was guilt. A poor family, two gifted sons, money for one. A brother pushed past his window and into the lanes, and an elder's purse behind every jar he broke. *That was why the lanes had a Guang Long.* That was why the gate had a Guang Biao.

Lin Hao filed that too. The shelf did not complain.

A wave of ice-cold soul power swept the four of them off the yard and set them, upright and unruffled, at their own dormitory door.

"Return and continue training," said Wu Zhangkong, icy as usual, already walking away.

"Teacher — aren't you going to blame us?" Wulin called after the white robes, because it had been their fight that morning started.

Wu Zhangkong gave him a glance over one shoulder. "Did all of you do anything wrong? Why would I blame you?" And then, flatly: "You are not allowed to leave the Academy at night anymore. Rest days included." The white robes went on. And then, without turning — the voice dropping one degree, aimed now not at a class but at a single student who had spent the whole fight watching a blade instead of a battle, and who had been *seen* doing it: "Lin Hao. You watched the sword, not the fight."

"Yes, teacher."

"Then tell me what you saw."

Lin Hao considered the nine weeks of homework in his arms — the servant's sword, the crutch of the eyes, the sentence his blade was too polite to finish. "The second strike was decided before the first one landed," he said. "The seven notes weren't seven strikes. They were one strike, and six of them were the *door.* And you never looked at him once. You looked at your sword — because your sword already knew where he was." He paused, and added, because the smith in him could not leave the truest part unsaid: "You didn't defeat him, teacher. You *answered* him. He just wasn't the question your sword was asking."

The white robes stopped. For one held moment, the back of the Icily Arrogant Prince Charming stood very still in the corridor.

"Then stop making your sword wait," Wu Zhangkong said, and was gone.

In room eleven, Xie Xie gripped Wulin's arm and would not, for several minutes, be embarrassed about the trembling. "I'm not cold — I'm *excited.* Did you feel it? The moment Teacher Wu drew his sword, it was as though the surroundings changed — the world froze with its appearance! That's an expert. A *true* expert. It would be amazing if I could be that powerful one day."

"Then we should quickly get back to meditating," Wulin said, slapping the hand away. "You've been sitting at rank twenty for a year — don't you know how to work hard?"

"Weren't *you* excited?"

"Yes. What good does it do me? Teacher Wu trained slowly up to where he is. We can only train hard as well."

In room twelve, Lin Hao lay on his narrow bed with his arms full of a sword that had not been drawn all morning and had never, he understood now, mattered more. Because he had seen it — the finished shape of the thing he was becoming. Not the rings. Not the ten thousand years. The *attention:* a man who had condensed his whole spiritual sea into one edge and then trusted it absolutely, the way the sky trusts its own weather. Wu Zhangkong's sword did not read and wait. Wu Zhangkong's sword did not even read. It *spoke,* and the world arranged itself around the sentence — and somewhere between the seventh note and the frozen nose, Lin Hao had understood what his own homework actually was.

Not to make the Stormbringer faster. Not to make it sharper.

To make it *certain.*

On the far water, the hawk watched the last of the frost leave the air above the yard, and turned once on its perch — not toward the harbor tonight, but toward the dormitory wing where the white robes had gone, and then, slowly, toward the boy. As if to say: *that* is a bird worth flying after. As if to say something else, which it did not, because the question between them was still the only language they shared, and it was, tonight, very nearly a conversation.

Tomorrow was class one. At the top of the ladder, a dragon waited, and an eagle, and a fusion that had made a sound the reference books had never described — and behind them all, in a teacher's cool office at the front of the building, a woman with a two-year wound was sharpening something that had nothing to do with swords.

The worst class in the grade slept like soldiers. Two of them, in the dark, smiled.


Rank twenty-six came in the week after the lane, and it came the way everything came to him now — bought. He had been hit by a rank twenty-seven with two lizard souls and had lived, and his soul had filed the whole of it, and the filing cost a rank to store.

The hawk, eight hundred years old and heavier in him than it had been before the lane, did not comment. It never did.

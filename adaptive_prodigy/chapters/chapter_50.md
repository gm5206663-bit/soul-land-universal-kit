## Part 1: What Wu Zhangkong Did Not Warn Him About

The needles came out of the sky without a sound.

That was the part Lin Hao's ledger had no entry for. He had twelve platform runs and a hundred and one lines and he had learned to hear a thing coming — the crack of a branch, the displacement of air, the particular silence a large animal makes when it decides. He had a category for everything that had ever tried to kill him.

Ivy-green needles, falling silently, in a curtain, from nothing.

No category.

Wu Zhangkong's hand closed on his shoulder and the world went **sideways.**

Not fast. That was the frightening part — it was not fast, it was *elsewhere*, and Lin Hao's feet were doing something he had not asked them to do, and there were **lights** — several, twinkling, in a line behind them and then in front of them and then in a place that was not either — and the needles came down into the ground where he had been standing and the earth went **rotten black** in a circle, and the plants around it made a sound.

Lin Hao heard the plants make a sound.

He would think about that for a long time.

They landed on a branch. Wu Zhangkong put him down and did not let go of his shoulder, which Lin Hao understood was not a kindness and was a **hold.**

"What was that," Lin Hao said. His voice was steady. He was surprised by that.

"That was you nearly dying."

"No — the *moving.*"

Wu Zhangkong looked at him.

"**Ghost Shadow Perplexing Track,**" he said. "It is a Tang Sect technique."

---

## Part 2: What It Meant

Lin Hao stood on a branch in a forest that did not exist with his hand on a sword and his heart doing something it had not done since the grave, and he understood that he had been given a thing.

Not taught. **Given.**

"You joined four days ago," Wu Zhangkong said. He said it in the flat voice, which was the only voice he had for the things that mattered. "You have not been given anything. You have been told what battle armor is and taken to an auction and walked into a forest."

"You just used it on me."

"I used it **near** you. There is a difference, and in about four months you will understand how large." Wu Zhangkong's hand was still on his shoulder. "But you are in the Tang Sect, and you are in a place where that is the only way to move that does not get you killed, and I am not going to watch you die of good manners."

Lin Hao looked down at the rotten black circle where he had been standing.

"How long has the Tang Sect had it."

"Eight thousand years."

"And you've just given it to a boy who's been in the organisation for four days and who nobody can categorise."

Wu Zhangkong was quiet for a moment.

"Yes," he said. "And I did it anyway."

## Part 3: What Lin Hao Did That Wu Zhangkong Did Not Expect

They went on.

Wu Zhangkong's stated purpose was arithmetic: *bring the spirit soul up to two thousand years, just under the current limit, because we should not waste the trip.* Lin Hao understood the arithmetic and did the arithmetic and understood that the hawk was at one thousand two hundred and eighty-eight and that two thousand was a long way, and that the man beside him intended to walk him there.

And then the forest went **afraid.**

Lin Hao had felt the hawk go still. He had felt it pull. He had not, in twelve platform runs, felt a *forest* do anything at all, and he understood immediately that this was not the hawk.

It was the trees.

"Wu Zhangkong," he said.

"I feel it."

"Something's frightened them."

"Everything is frightened of something."

"No." Lin Hao said it carefully, because he was working it out, and because he had learned that the flat version got further. "Not frightened *of* something. Frightened. The way a room is frightened. There's a direction to it."

Wu Zhangkong looked at him.

And Lin Hao understood — because he had spent four days learning to read this man, and four months before that failing to — that Wu Zhangkong was deciding whether to trust a ten-year-old's instinct in a forest that did not exist, and that the deciding was costing him something, and that he had already decided at the grave and was only now catching up with himself.

"Point," Wu Zhangkong said.

---

## Part 4: What Was There

Lin Hao did not meld his consciousness into anything.

He wants it noted. Wulin could do that — Wulin had a plant for a soul and could ask a field a question and get an answer, and Lin Hao had watched him do it and been furiously jealous for about eleven seconds. Lin Hao had a hawk and a sword and a blacksmith's eye, and what he did was **read.**

He read the way the undergrowth leaned.

Not the wind. There was no wind. The undergrowth leaned the way grass leans away from a door that has just opened, and it leaned **northeast**, and the leaning had a *depth* to it, and Lin Hao had spent four years reading the grain of metal and understood grain the way other people understood weather.

"Northeast," he said. "About forty metres. And it's big."

Wu Zhangkong cut.

The **Skyfrost Slash** went through the black fog and left a line of frost-covered plants behind it, and the fog came apart, and the howl came, and the thing that came out of it was not in any record Lin Hao had ever read.

**Two metres tall. Black. A head of blazing gold hair.** A shimmering **purple** aura around it, and bright **topaz** eyes, and on its face — and this was the part Lin Hao would write down, and would write down accurately, because he had learned that accuracy was the only thing he had —

**incredulity.**

It looked at them the way a person looks at something that should not be in its house.

"That's a ten-thousand-year soul beast," Wu Zhangkong said. He said it the way he said everything. "I don't recognise it either."

---

## Part 5: What He Was Given

Lin Hao did not fight it.

He wants that noted too, because he had spent six days believing that he was the largest thing in a room, and he had stood on a branch and looked at a two-metre black ape with gold hair and understood, in about one second, that he was not the largest thing in a forest and had never been the largest thing in anything except a classroom.

Wu Zhangkong fought it.

And Lin Hao watched, because that was what there was to do, and because he was a blacksmith and a blacksmith watches how a thing is made — and what he watched was a man who had **compressed his soul power so thoroughly that there was no fluctuation to sense**, which was a thing Wu Zhangkong had explained in a corridor four months ago as theory and was now doing forty metres away to something that had been alive for ten thousand years.

The Skyfrost went **sapphire.**

Lin Hao had seen it burst a one-metre fireball. That had been a demonstration. This was not a demonstration.

It lasted eleven seconds.

And when it was over, the ape was somewhere it was not going to be a problem, and the forest was still afraid, and Wu Zhangkong folded his arms and said, "That is the second thing today I don't recognise."

"That's a lot of not recognising."

"Yes."

"You said you were going to write to somebody."

"I am."

"Have you written yet."

Wu Zhangkong looked at him.

"No," he said. "Because I have been standing in a forest for two hours and every time I think I have something to write down, something else happens that makes the last thing irrelevant." He started walking. "Come along. We are not finished, and your spirit soul is at one thousand two hundred and eighty-eight and I said two thousand."

---

## Part 6: What Lin Hao Wrote

He wrote it that night, above the forge, with the window open, and he wrote the number first because the record came before the thinking now.

*two hundred and eighteen.* It had been two hundred and fifty-eight. Six points, and he had not meditated.

*One thousand two hundred and eighty-eight.* Forty-seven of it from an ape that was not in any record.

Then the rest.

*Wu Zhangkong used the Ghost Shadow Perplexing Track on me. He said it is a Tang Sect technique and that they have had it for eight thousand years and that I have been in the organisation for four days and that he did it anyway and he would like me to notice that.*

*I noticed.*

*I have spent six days being a category error and four days being something nobody can name, and tonight a man who has said I don't know twice put his hand on my shoulder and moved me out of the way of something that was going to kill me, using a thing his organisation has kept secret for eight thousand years, and he did not explain it and he did not apologise and he did not ask me anything.*

*That is not a teacher. That is a person who has decided.*

Then the line at the bottom.

*I read the undergrowth and found a ten-thousand-year beast and Wu Zhangkong trusted me and cut where I pointed, and afterwards he said nothing about it at all, which is how I know it mattered.*

*I am not the largest thing in a forest. I have never been the largest thing in anything except a classroom.*

*I am going to learn the Track.*

*Zhou Zhangxi came to the training field tonight and stood at the edge for twenty minutes and did not say anything, and Yun Xiao came with him and wrote something down, and neither of them asked me anything, and not-asking has become the thing they do, and that I have not earned the asking.*

He closed the book.

And then he went downstairs, because there was a light on in the room along the hall and he had been in a forest for six hours and had not thought about anybody in it except a man in white and a thing with gold hair.

Xie Xie was awake. He was sitting on the end of his bed with his daggers across his knees and he was *cleaning them*, which he did when he was working something out, and he did not look up.

"Six hours," he said.

"Yes."

"He took you and not me."

"Yes."

"I'm not going to say I'm fine about it." Xie Xie ran the cloth along the flat of the blade. "I said that in the corridor four days ago and you told me that was the longest he'd ever spoken to me, and I've thought about it, and I've decided I'd rather say the true thing, which is that I sat in this room for six hours and worked out fourteen ways I would have handled the needles."

"How many of them worked."

Xie Xie stopped cleaning.

"None of them," he said. "But I'd have looked better dying."

"That's the most honest thing you've said to me."

"I'm exhausted." Xie Xie put the dagger down. "Go to bed. And tomorrow you're going to teach me whatever that moving thing was, because he gave it to you and not to me, and the only thing I can do about that is learn it off you, and I have made my peace with that, and it took six hours."

Lin Hao stood in the doorway.

"I don't know it yet," he said. "He used it *near* me. He said in four months I'd understand how large that difference is."

"Then in four months," Xie Xie said, "you're teaching me."

"Yes."

"Good." Xie Xie picked the dagger back up. "Go to bed. You've got forest on you."



The *hawk-gold lock* at his temple lay across his cheekbone, and the *marks on his forearms* were raised and had not gone down in two days, and the hawk turned over in him — *one thousand two hundred and eighty-eight* — and did not pull toward anything, because it had found what it was looking for and was, apparently, willing to wait.

Lin Hao lay on his back in a city with no skyscrapers, and thought about eight thousand years, and about four days, and about a hand on his shoulder.

He slept.

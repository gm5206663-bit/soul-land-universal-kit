## Part 1: Xie Xie's Location

Xie Xie chose the location the way a general chooses a battlefield: the old training yard behind the dining hall, the same dirt he had been flattened in twice, because — and he explained this to anyone who would listen, which was everyone — *a debt is cleanest where it was made.*

He had also, it turned out, invited an audience. Half of class five stood in a loose ring under the afternoon light with the bored excitement of people who have been told there will be violence. Zhou Zhangxi had brought food. Zhou Zhangxi always brought food.

"Rules!" Xie Xie announced, standing in the center with his Light Dragon Dagger already surfacing into his grip, pale as a held breath. "One fight each. No teachers. No yielding until someone's flat or someone's blade says so. Tang Wulin first."

"You're going to lose first," Wulin observed, setting down his tray.

"I've *analyzed* you," Xie Xie said, with enormous dignity.

That should have been the warning. Wulin walked into the circle anyway, because the alternative was letting him finish the sentence, and the last two times he'd ended Xie Xie's fights in two strikes — quick, clean, and, judging by the boy's face, deeply unsatisfying for everyone involved.

The Light Dragon Dagger arrived the way it always did: first the tightening in the air, then light where light had no business being. But this time the first strike was not at Wulin's chest. It flicked low — at his footing — and the second came high while his weight was still answering the first, and Wulin, who had spent four years being hit by hammers and had opinions about momentum, simply ate the feint and let the dagger's second pass skim his forearm, close enough to part sleeve and skin in one thin bright line.

The yard made a sound. Xie Xie's eyes went wide — *I touched him* — and that was, in retrospect, the mistake. Because the line on Wulin's arm had already stopped bleeding, the furnace behind his sternum having glanced at the wound the way a blacksmith glances at a spark, and while Xie Xie was still enjoying his achievement, Wulin stepped inside the light.

Two strikes again. It was becoming a tradition.

Xie Xie lay in the dirt contemplating the sky and the specific unfairness of the universe, and then rolled onto his elbow. "The grass," he said. "My dagger can't cut your grass. I've been practicing on trees. I cut a *boat* in half yesterday. And your grass just — folds around it and springs back."

"Bloodline things are like that," Wulin said, offering a hand. He had no idea why his grass did that. Old Tang was silent on the subject, which was its own kind of answer.

Xie Xie took the hand, got upright, dusted himself with offended precision — and turned to the other boy at the ring's edge.

"You," he said. "Now. And I have questions."

---

The second fight lasted longer, and everyone who watched it understood, in the wordless way yards understand things, that they were seeing something they would describe inaccurately for years.

Xie Xie was fast. He was also, Lin Hao could tell within four exchanges, *smarter than Tuesday* — he had stopped leading with the dagger, stopped letting his light gather where eyes could track it, was arriving from angles he had plainly drawn on paper the night before. Three of those angles would have finished most first-graders. Lin Hao simply was not standing in them.

It was not speed, and the yard kept trying to call it speed. It was that the air told Lin Hao first. Light Dragon Dagger drew its light from somewhere, and before the light moved, the air above Xie Xie's leading shoulder went *taut*, the way a drum skin goes taut a half-breath before the strike — and hawk-eyes, fed by seven hundred and thirty-five years of a soul that had spent its whole first life reading weather for prey, saw the tautness the way other people saw the blade.

So Lin Hao redirected. A wrist turned here, a flat blade there, Xie Xie's own speed doing all the work of Xie Xie's defeat, until the dagger-boy was breathing hard in the middle of the yard with his hair coming loose and his fury — and this was the part the yard would remember — completely gone.

"How," Xie Xie said. Not *fight me.* How. "How do you see my first step?"

Lin Hao considered lying. He considered the truth. He split the difference the way his father split kindling — cleanly, along the grain.

"You tell the air you're coming before you move," he said. "Everyone does. You just tell it louder than most, because your soul has to gather before it shines." He shrugged. "My eyes are from further away than your feet. That's all."

Xie Xie stared at him. The yard stared at both of them. Somewhere in the distance a dining-hall bell rang, and the spell broke into noise and arguments, and Xie Xie sheathed his light with a snap.

"First window," he announced. "All of you. My treat." He pointed at Wulin. "You too. Especially you. I'm not letting you insult my character *and* skip dinner."

---

The first window served spirit-nutrient meals to first-graders in silk jackets, and it had never in its institutional memory served four boys who ordered like a harbor crew. The woman behind the counter confirmed their money twice. Zhou Zhangxi — who had attached himself to the procession the way weather attaches itself to mountains — confirmed the arrangement by eating his own pyramid and then looking, with genuine scholarly interest, at Lin Hao's second plate.

"You're small," he said. It was not an insult. Zhou Zhangxi did not do insults; he did observations, at volume.

"Eat less, forge less," Lin Hao agreed, and Zhou Zhangxi, who had heard about the Association badge and the ten-year-old second-rank smith from four separate witnesses, nodded slowly as if a theory had been confirmed.

Xie Xie, halfway through his fourth course, slammed a hand on the table. "The class match. Friday. Wu Zhangkong's putting our whole year against each other in joint training, class against class, and class one has been *smirking* at me in the corridors all week." He looked around the table with the grave air of a man proposing something reasonable. "We're going to win all of it."

"All of it?" Wulin asked, around a mouthful.

"Every. Match." Xie Xie's dagger-light flickered over his knuckles, then settled, controlled — controlled, Lin Hao noted, in a way it had not been a week ago. Loss, it turned out, was an excellent teacher, provided the student was too stubborn to quit. "They call us the worst class. So let's be the worst class that beats them, and then let's watch their *faces.*"

Nobody at the table argued. It was, everyone privately agreed, an extremely good reason.

## Part 2: Victory Streak

Friday's joint training filled the academy's main yard — a hundred and eight first-graders, five classes, one ice-cold teacher walking the lines with his hands behind his back, and Director Long Hengxu observing from the gallery with the expression of a man expecting to be bored.

The format was simple. Class against class, matched pairs, elimination. Wu Zhangkong judged. No rules as to how you won.

Class five went last, the way the smallest wave goes last against the shore, and the shore had opinions. Class one's file was silk jackets and family tutors and a girl with a mirror-polished shield soul who had been smirking, per Xie Xie's report, all week.

The first match went to class one — a tool-soul boy from class five who lasted eleven seconds and walked off red-faced. The second went to class five: Yun Xiao, quiet Yun Xiao, whom half the grade had forgotten was in the class at all, stood in the circle against a bigger boy with a rock-boar soul and simply… declined to be where the charges landed. Not fast, not flashy — *patient*. Three charges, three empty circles, and on the fourth the rock-boar boy ran into a foot placed, very gently, exactly where his own momentum wanted to put him down. The yard went quiet, then loud. In class five's corner, Wulin nodded at Yun Xiao like one accountant nodding at another's ledger.

The third match was Zhou Zhangxi, and Zhou Zhangxi did not decline anything. He had watched Wulin for two weeks — watched how the smaller boy let force arrive and then *went with it* — and he had built, on top of his mountain of a body, the beginning of an idea. The class one boy hit him like a siege engine. Zhou Zhangxi took it on his guard, staggered — and turned the stagger into a step, the step into a shoulder, and the class one boy into a horizontal person.

"My turn," Xie Xie said, and the smirking girl from the corridor got her match, and it is the considered opinion of everyone present that she did not smirk afterward.

Then class one sent their best — a rank-21 Soul Grandmaster with a silver-horned rhino soul and family money in every stitch — and Wu Zhangkong's eyes slid, once, toward the back of class five's file, and the yard understood the assignment.

Lin Hao went, and the match lasted four seconds, and that was the part nobody in the yard had a way to describe afterward, because a rank-21 Soul Grandmaster does not lose in four seconds to a boy three ranks under him and nobody could say what had happened. The rhino soul had come forward. The Stormbringer had been where the rhino soul was going to be. That was all of it.

And the thing that changed because he was standing there — the thing that nobody in that yard would have had any reason to think about if he had not been in the file — was that **Wu Zhangkong did not send Xie Xie next.** Canon's yard would have sent Xie Xie, because Xie Xie was class five's best and the format was class against class. But Wu Zhangkong had watched four seconds of a four-second match and had made a decision about the shape of his class that he did not say out loud, and the shape was no longer the shape it had been that morning.

Xie Xie noticed. Xie Xie always noticed. He did not say anything, which was the loudest thing Xie Xie had ever done.
Tang Wulin took the circle, and the match lasted one exchange, and it was not a fight so much as a *demonstration of the difference between rank and strength.* The rhino charge was magnificent. Wulin met it the way an anvil meets a rumor, and the silver horns went up, and the class one boy went down, and Director Long Hengxu sat forward in the gallery for the first time all afternoon.

"Lin Hao," said Wu Zhangkong, "you're wasting their time. Center."

Lin Hao took the circle against the last standing class-one fighter — a composed boy, careful, with a wind-blade soul and good tutors — and gave the yard sixty seconds of something that looked, to the untrained, like a storm politely declining to touch a very good dancer. He won without drawing blood, without using a soul skill, and — because Wu Zhangkong was watching with that recalculating attention — without using anything his classmates hadn't already seen. The Union stayed folded. Some doors you don't open for an audience.

Class five won the day, four matches to one. It was not, by any arithmetic that mattered, supposed to happen. The worst class — the ground-floor class, the ground-floor-end-of-the-building class — stood in the main yard with the noise of a hundred first-graders rearranging itself around them, and Xie Xie, because Xie Xie could not help himself, bowed.

It began a streak. The next week's joint training went the same way, and the week after that went faster, and by the end of the month the phrase had entered the first grade's vocabulary: *the worst class is winning.* Older students came to watch. Director Long Hengxu came twice more, and left thoughtful. And Wu Zhangkong — who said nothing, ever, about any of it — did one thing that both furnaces in his class noticed.

He made Lin Hao and Wulin each other's permanent sparring partners.

"The rest of the class will learn more from surviving the two of you than from beating each other," he said, which was as close to praise as his face was structurally capable of. It was also, both boys understood without saying, a decision about *them*: two monsters sharpening each other, because nothing else in the yard was whetstone enough.

Their first real session ended with the training yard's fence needing repairs, Wulin grinning through a split lip, Lin Hao's feather-marks glowing faint through his shirt at the shoulders, and neither of them — this was the part that frightened and delighted them in equal measure, though neither would have used either word — anywhere near the other's ceiling.

## Part 3: Ten Thousand Coins

The Association gave Tang Wulin his first solo commission on a Tuesday: a lump of Heavy Silver the size of two fists, and a work order, and Mu Chen's single instruction — "Same as the test. I want the note."

He took four days of evenings. The Association paid on delivery, and the clerk counted out **ten thousand federation coins** — a sum so large the clerk watched Wulin's face as he said it, the way people watch for fainting — and Wulin carried it back to the dormitory in both arms like a sack of grain and sat on his bed holding it for a long time without saying anything.

Ten thousand coins. His father made perhaps four hundred a month at the factory. This was two years of his father's hands, in his own, at ten years old, because a hammer had taught him that metal talks if you hit it honestly.

And the clerk would not have counted that money out in a story without him.

Not because the Association is unkind. **Because the commission would not have existed.** Mu Chen had given the Heavy Silver to Wulin on a Tuesday because Mu Chen had spent a month watching a boy who listened to metal, and the reason Mu Chen had been watching was that **another boy had been standing at the east window of the Association for six weeks, reading grain in metal that nobody else was reading, and Mu Chen had started looking at everything else in the building differently because of it.**

**In a story without him, Wulin is a promising boy with grass and a hammer, and the Association gives him small work for years, and the ten thousand coins never happen.** In a story with him in it, Mu Chen has already decided that this building contains something he did not expect, and Wulin gets Heavy Silver on a Tuesday, and his father's two years of hands come home in a bag.

That is the butterfly, and Wulin never knew. He would have said the money was because he worked. He worked. **But the reason the work was there was a boy at a window who had never once been introduced to him, and who would not have said it was him if anybody had asked.**

Mu Chen's actual teaching came in fragments between commissions, and it was never about the hammer. "Where does the sound go, boy?" he asked once, watching Wulin's sixtieth-something strike sing true. "Into the air means waste. Into the metal means work. Into *you* means you'll be deaf by forty and I'll have to shout at your students instead." He watched the next strike. "Your friend with the hawk-eyes — he listens like the metal owes him money. Bring him to the forge. Not to work. To listen. A smith who can hear what I can hear is worth three who can't."

And there was Mu Xi.

She had been coming to the public forging floor for two weeks — officially to work her own commissions, though her work sat finished and cooling while her eyes sat on the boy at the far anvil. She watched the way the Tungsten hammers moved in his hands. She watched the *silence* of his strikes — the thing her father's whole school was built on, the thing she had spent nine years being told she would eventually earn, and that this commoner's son did like breathing.

She had **first rank at eleven** and second rank a month after her thirteenth birthday, and her father had called her a child prodigy of the blacksmithing world, and she had learned by about the age of twelve to stop correcting people when they said it, because correcting them took longer than letting them.

It was not pride. She had checked. Pride would have been simpler.

What it actually was, and what she had never said out loud to anybody, was this: she had started at **five**, on a box, making small hammers out of offcuts, after her father told her kindly and accurately that forging was **not suitable for girls** — and every rank she held was two years of being thought funny, paid in advance. So when the Association started saying *prodigy*, it felt like somebody taking the nine years and handing back a word.

She was not here to compete with Tang Wulin.

She was here to find out what he had that she had not been able to buy with nine years.

On the fourth day she crossed the floor while Wulin was banking his forge, and stood beside him for long enough that he noticed and wiped his hands and stood up straight, and asked — in the clipped, exact tone of a person carrying a very heavy object — one question.

"The sixty-third strike. In your test. The note." Her chin was high. Her knuckles, at her sides, were not relaxed. "My strikes ring at the fortieth, if I'm perfect. Yours rang at sixty-three, and my father says yours was *purer.* Why sixty-three?"

Wulin thought about it honestly. He was not performing modesty; he genuinely turned the question over the way he turned metal.

"I don't know," he said. "The metal wasn't ready before then. You can hear it getting ready — like bread. I just kept hitting where it told me until the telling turned into the note." He hesitated, then added, because she looked like someone who had never once been told to just *listen*: "Maybe your strikes ring at forty because forty is when your metal is ready. Mine's a different lump every time."

It was, without question, the truest answer he had.

And it was the wrong shape of answer entirely for a girl who had built herself out of *doing it correctly*, because *the metal tells you* is not a thing you can practise. It is not a thing you can start at five on a box. You either hear it or you spend your life becoming good enough that it almost doesn't matter.

*The metal tells him,* her face said, briefly, before nine years of training put the mask back on. *The metal does not tell me. The metal obeys me, or I obey my father's school.*

"Thank you," she said, precisely, and went back to her own anvil.

Her strikes that evening rang at the fortieth, exactly as always, exactly on school-perfect — and she did not go home until midnight, because there was nothing else she knew how to do, and because if the metal was not going to speak to her then she would simply have to become so good that its silence stopped being the point.

Her father had reached **eight stars**. There were **three** men on the Douluo Continent who had reached nine and could be called **Divine Craftsmen**, and she had known their names before she could read properly. She had decided at about the age of seven, in the flat unexamined way children decide things, that she was going to be the fourth, and she had never once revised the plan, and she was not going to start now because a boy from a town nobody visited had a metal that talked to him.

In his office above the floor, **Mu Chen — eight-star Saint Craftsman, President of the Eastsea City branch, the only man at that rank in the entire Association, and one of perhaps forty on the continent who could name all three of the nine-star Divine Craftsmen** — watched his daughter through the window, watched the boy bank his forge and leave with ten thousand coins in a bag over his shoulder, and said nothing at all.

Which, for him, was a kind of grief. He had spent nine years teaching her that effort was the whole of it, because effort was the only thing he had ever been able to honestly promise her.

## Part 4: The List

On the roof that night, there was a letter.

It had come to the academy's post-room in the afternoon — Glorybound City postmark, Chen Long's handwriting, which was swordsmanship in ink: every stroke committed, none wasted. Lin Hao had read it twice and was reading it a third time, which for him was an event.

*Boy. Your forms are clean but you're carrying weight in your left shoulder and compensating right. I can see it from here. Whatever you're carrying, put some of it in the sword, that's what it's for. Your father's letters say you eat him out of the forge. Good. The sword arm is fed by the kitchen arm. Monthly, as agreed. — C.L.*

Below it, in smaller, different ink — his mother had taken the pen from someone, or someone from her: *Write every month. Your father checks the post-road every morning like a fool. We are well. The forge is well. Come home for new year.*

Lin Hao folded the letter into quarters and put it against his skin, under the shirt, where things that mattered went. Beside him, Wulin was doing arithmetic on the back of a work order by soul-lamp light, and the arithmetic had a title at the top of it, in careful characters:

**FIND NA'ER.**

"Ten thousand," Wulin said. "Notices in Glorybound, Eastsea, and the two port towns between — the ones with the ferry landings. The authorities here say no records, but records are for people who *use gates.*" He wrote a number beside *notices.* "Then I start saving for tickets. Coastal runs. Every harbor town north and south." He paused, pen hovering. "Someone took her, Hao. That means somewhere. Roads end. The sea doesn't end, but it has *routes.*"

Lin Hao looked at the list for a long moment. Then he took the pen and, at the top — above Glorybound, above the port towns — wrote two words, and turned the page around.

**Glorybound harbor. The night she left. The wind was off the water.**

"She went toward the sea," he said quietly. "I was there the morning after. I stood where she'd been standing for four years and I looked the wrong way — I looked at the *roads*, because that's where people go." He tapped the words. "But the whole time she lived with you, every evening, she stood facing the harbor. Not the town. The *water.* Whatever she was remembering, it wasn't behind her. It was out there." He handed the pen back. "Start at the water. Ask the night fishermen. Ask the ones who don't talk to authorities."

Wulin stared at the page. The furnace-hum under his ribs — the second seal, patient as ever — seemed, for a moment, to lean toward the same horizon.

"Friday I deliver the next commission," he said. "That's another eight thousand. I can have the harbor covered by the end of the month."

"And I'll write the notices," Lin Hao said, "because your handwriting looks like it was forged, not written."

"*It was* forged, that's the whole point—"

The argument went on for twenty minutes, which is what arguments between brothers sound like when both of them are, underneath, agreeing completely. Above them the Eastsea sky did its slow harbor thing, and the hawk inside Lin Hao's chest turned once in its sleep, heavy with another week of fed cultivation — seven hundred and thirty-five years now, and climbing toward a number he could feel the way sailors feel weather. On his shoulders, under the shirt, the feather-fine wind-marks from the week's sparring still glowed faint, and glowed, he noticed, a little longer each time.

Becoming, not changing. Slowly. The way the sea makes a route.

Down below, in room eleven, the door stood open its careful inch, and on the windowsill the Association badge caught the lamp-light like a small bronze moon, and the worst class in Eastsea Academy slept the sleep of people who have begun, without quite noticing, to carry weight — and to carry it together.

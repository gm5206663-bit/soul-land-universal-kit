## Part 1: Workshop Eighty-Eight

The Blacksmiths' Association turned out to be two different buildings wearing the same door.

Downstairs was the outer foundry where Lin Hao had registered — the public tables, the one-armed master, the roar and coke and student smiths paying by the hour for a place to hit metal. Upstairs, reached by a narrow coiling corridor that wound like a snake past soundproofed door after soundproofed door, was the part of the association that only opened up once your rank stopped being a rumor.

"You get a workshop," Wulin said, standing in the center of room eighty-eight and turning slowly so he could see all four metal walls at once, the way a man turns to take in a house he has been told is his. "Your own. Private. Soundproof. A table that's better than anything Mu Chen ever let me breathe near. And they *pay* you. Two thousand points a month, stipend, ten chunks of metal free, eight percent off anything you buy — for being a fourth-rank blacksmith. I had to fight the academy to get a *job* sweeping stone, and the association gives me a key and a salary and asks nothing but three jobs a month I could do in my sleep."

"That's because the jobs the academy hands out are a trap," Lin Hao said. He was leaning against the doorframe, watching his captain discover the world one beautiful scandal at a time. He had signed his own register that morning, a rank above, and the clerk's face had gone through a very satisfying sequence when the paperwork loaded. His own terms ran better than Wulin's — a larger room when he wanted it, a deeper metal allowance, a higher stipend — and he had taken the key to a workshop of his own with the quiet satisfaction of a man closing a ledger that had been open since Eastsea. "The academy posts thousand-refining jobs for a thousand contribution points. The association buys the same piece of work for two thousand. Every working student who doesn't know that yet is doing their thousand-refining through the administration and getting taxed in ignorance."

Wulin stopped turning. "That's *criminal*."

"That's a ladder with a rung missing, so that the people who haven't found the association yet fund the people who have." He pushed off the doorframe. "First-grade thousand-refined metal sells for two thousand points minimum through the guild. There's a five percent fee if you sell through them, which still leaves you nearly double the academy job. Nobody with a badge takes the admin jobs. The board in the admin building is bait for first-years who don't know the guild exists." He let his mouth tug. "We took the bait for four days."

"We cleaned a plaza very well," Wulin said, with dignity. "It's spotless."

The forging table stood in the center of eighty-eight, standard grade and not extravagant, but to a boy who had learned his craft on a borrowed corner of Mu Chen's bench it might as well have been an altar. The shelves were bare except for the ten free monthly chunks and the less-valuable metal Wulin had tipped out of his storage ring. He was already running a hand along the table's edge with the particular reverence he reserved for things that were about to feed him.

"First customer's on the way," Lin Hao said. "And she'll ask you a question worth more than the metal."

The knock came before he had finished the sentence.

## Part 2: The Selection

Yuanen Yehui came in the way she did everything — unremarkably, without announcement, her ordinary face and ordinary build giving no warning at all of what was underneath them. She nodded once to Lin Hao, the same single working-student nod she had given at the admin counter, and turned to Wulin.

"You're actually set up here." Her eyes went over the room. "I need a full set, eventually. Battle armor. I can pay, and I'll keep paying — I'm a third-rank mecha designer, I've got the points. I just need the metal started. Something ductile. My martial soul makes things *large* when I use it, and the armor has to come with me."

Wulin's eyes went round. "Your martial soul — you never said what it was."

A hesitation. Then: "Titan Giant Ape."

Lin Hao, at the doorframe, did not move, but something in him went very still and attentive, the way it did when a name clicked into a space he had been holding open. *Titan Giant Ape. King of the forest.* The unassuming girl who finished design plans before dawn. The examiner who had walked Xie Xie into the ground. The shoulder under ordinary clothes that moved like it carried far more than the frame suggested.

"You want heavy silver," he said, into Wulin's open mouth. "Most ductile mid-grade metal there is. It'll stretch with the transformation." He said it to Wulin, but he was watching Yuanen as he said it, and he saw the small flicker in her — the second glance, the reassessment, the careful re-filing of *the quiet fifth examinee* into a different slot.

"He's right." Yuanen's gaze came back to him and stayed a beat longer this time. "Heavy silver. Thousand refined."

Wulin made the deal like a boy who cannot quite believe his life — and then, because Wulin was Wulin, he spent the goodwill he had just bought on the one question he could not stop asking. "The price is too cheap for that. I know it is. So — working students. Tell me the secret. Why does this whole academy go quiet and respectful the moment someone says it?"

Yuanen took the finished heavy silver from him and put it away, and answered bluntly, as she answered everything.

"Because working students are freaks. The academy takes people who didn't pass the full exam but are spectacular in one area — or too extraordinary somewhere to turn away even if everything else doesn't fit. Your Elder Feng was one. He graduated the outer court at thirty-four on blacksmithing alone, and the same year he broke through to spirit refining and seventh rank, became the youngest Saint Blacksmith on the continent, and the academy made an exception and took him into the inner court. Six rings to nine in twenty years. The greatest smith Shrek has." She leaned against the soundproofed wall. "Working students have been reaching the top ever since. To an ordinary student, we're unpredictable, and we are *absolutely not to be offended*. A graduating working student's chance at the inner court is thirty percent — much higher than theirs. The pressure's worse too. Elder Cai wasn't punishing you when she made you working students. She was sorting you into the stream that goes somewhere."

The room was quiet a moment. Xie Xie, who had drifted in behind Xiaoyan and spent his whole time at Shrek nursing his humiliation at the exam, made a small strangled sound in the doorway. The examinee who had beaten him into the ground was a *freak admit*, like them.

"Thirty percent," Xiaoyan said slowly, doing the arithmetic on her fingers and arriving, as she always did, at the thing the others missed. "So it's not a charity class. It's a *bet*. The academy's wagering that a monster who's overwhelming in one thing will drag the rest up to match. That's why they tolerate the whole arrangement. The ones who make it are worth more than twenty ordinary graduates."

Lin Hao said nothing for a long moment. He had worked this out alone in the drink shop, watching a girl with no name fold her wings and trust the sky to answer. *A selection. Not a guild, a selection.* He had been right. He had been right in every particular — the waitress, the examiner, the girl at the counter, all the same careful monster the academy was growing in the margin where ordinary students never looked.

"One thing," he said, quietly, into the gap. "If it's thirty percent in, and you're fifteen and a third-rank designer and this strong — why aren't you inner court already?"

The loneliness crossed her face so fast it was almost not there. "That," she said, "is a secret." She straightened. "Treat me to dinner for the answers, glutton."

Wulin protested the name the whole way out the door.

## Part 3: The Ram

They walked back toward the working-student dormitory as the Shrek light went amber, Wulin and Yuanen ahead, the other four strung out behind. The forest edge came first, as it always did on that path — and at the edge, sitting on a low wall facing the dormitory with the patience of a man who has decided to wait all night, was a figure in green with golden hair.

Lin Hao knew him before he stood up. The drink shop. The white suit, swapped for an academy uniform. The overbearingness that even Wulin could not out-look.

"Have either of you seen a red-haired girl?" Yue Zhengyu strode over, golden eyes scanning, arrogant as gravity. "Actually, does a red-haired girl even live here?"

Wulin's heart-rate picked up — Lin Hao saw it in the way his captain's shoulders went. *The Fallen Angel waitress. She said she was a working student. She's in this dorm block. He's hunting her.* And beside him, at his elbow, walking exactly the same line home, was Yuanen.

Lin Hao's hand did not move. He stayed where he was, a half-step back, the margin watching two people walk toward a collision he had been expecting since the night the sky came down.

"I don't know anyone like that." Yuanen's voice was flat. "Please move out of the way." She did not stop walking.

"I waited half a day," Yue Zhengyu said, "and I've only met you two working students. Now you say you don't know anything?" He reached out — not a strike, just the lazy push of a man used to clearing space, a hand toward her chest to move her off his line.

Yuanen's whole body went dark with an aura that hit the amber twilight like a wall dropping.

Lin Hao read it the instant it came. The unassuming frame did not shrink into itself. It *grew* — not visibly, not yet, but the *potential* of growth filled the air around her, the way the air over a forge fills with heat before the iron comes out. King of the forest. A soul beast so far above the ordinary ladder that the second-grade Angel boy three paces ahead of her had no idea what was standing in front of him.

She dropped her shoulder and rammed him.

It was not a martial technique. It was the single, insulting, contemptuous thing a stronger body does to a weaker one when it has decided the confrontation does not deserve a soul ring — she checked him like a tide checks a fence post. Yue Zhengyu was lifted off his feet and thrown backward through the air, three meters, five, and only caught himself by flaring his white Holy Angel wings wide and beating the air, hanging there with his dress shoes dangling and an expression of total, spiritual astonishment, his right shoulder visibly trembling with the recoil.

"Holy Angel's a spirit of light and judgement," Lin Hao said, very low, to no one in particular — to Xie Xie, whose jaw was hanging. "It isn't built to win a shoving match with the forest king. He's strong and he's got three purple rings, and she just told him, with one shoulder, that strength isn't the same as *weight*."

"She didn't even light a ring," Xie Xie whispered. "She just *pushed* him."

"That's the lesson." Lin Hao watched the golden-haired boy hang in his own wings and glare. "The person who beat you in the exam and the girl he's hunting for having black wings are standing in the same body, and he's too proud to see it." He held up a hand before Xie Xie could blunder into the middle of it. "Nobody says anything. This isn't our secret to hand him, and she doesn't need us. Watch. She'll handle it the way she handles everything — by being ten times stronger than the situation requires and declining to discuss it afterward."

He was right. Yuanen did not pursue. She stood on the path with her arms loose and her face unchanged, the aura sinking back into the ordinary frame, and looked up at the hovering Angel with the mild, weary contempt of a person who has had this conversation before and does not expect this telling to be better.

"The working students aren't hiding her," Lin Hao said, as the group — with Wulin, wisely, looking nowhere but at his own shoes — began to move past the standoff toward the dorm. "She's been standing in front of him the entire time. He's hunting for something behind the wall while he's talking to the wall."

## Part 4: The Next Rung

They left Yue Zhengyu shaking dust off his wings at the forest edge, and nobody spoke of it for the rest of the walk, the way nobody steps on a thing they have just seen sleep.

But that night, after the curtain was hung and the others had settled, Lin Hao did not take out his notebook. He took out the culling board instead — the memory of it, the layout he had carried out of the administration hall and turned over in his head through the whole of dinner.

The secret had not changed anything about the path. It had clarified it.

He was a freak admit, same as Wulin, same as Yuanen, same as the president who went from six rings to nine. The academy did not bet on well-rounded. It bet on the thing that was overwhelming and trusted the rest to catch. His overwhelming thing — the thing Shen Yi's sixty-percent compatibility rule couldn't touch, the thing a Saint Blacksmith's association had issued a key for, the thing the working-student stream was built to feed — was not going to be sharpened by sweeping plazas or forging other people's thousand-refinements at a comfortable rank. It was going to be sharpened by the only thing that had ever sharpened anything in him.

A thing that hit back.

He had priced the ten-thousand-year rung in a single afternoon and walked away with it intact, his sealed doors unstirred. The beast in the valley had been real and it had killed a team, and it had still not made him *reach*. The freaks did not graduate at thirty percent by staying in the band where they were already comfortable. Feng Wuyu had not become the youngest Saint on the continent by working metal he could forge in his sleep.

So he lay in the dark with his hands behind his head and made the decision the way he made every decision about work — plainly, by listening for the note.

The next ticket he pulled would not be a ten-thousand-year.

There was a higher mark on that board. He had seen it the first time, the way his eye always found the most expensive thing on a bench — a ticket flagged past the danger band his year was supposed to take, something in the country beyond even the outer ranges that the academy had stopped sending ordinary students to. A beast nobody had a contract out on because nobody who could take it needed the points. He had walked past it the first time because walking past it had been the correct, careful, *first-week* decision, and the careful decision was what a working student did until he knew his own reach.

He knew his reach now. He knew it down to the inch, the way he knew the weight of a hammer before he swung. He did not know the reach *above* him — the one the sealed rooms existed for. That was the whole point. That was the only thing worth buying with the points and the metal and the key.

And the other thing, the one he did not say out loud because there was no one in the dorm who could hold it: the academy had not been wrong about him. He *was* a freak admit, in exactly the sense Yuanen meant — he had not come through Shrek on the clean total score, he had come through because there were things in him the exam could not grade and had not dared to refuse. The adaptation. The sword. The thing the honest instrument had printed five signatures and no bracket for. The academy had looked at all of it, bet on it, and put him in the margin stream where they put the monsters who were overwhelming in one direction and unclassifiable in every other. Yuanen carried that in a plain frame and a design badge. Feng Wuyu had carried it in a hammer. He carried it in the thing he refused to spend in front of people. Which meant the academy was, somewhere in its frost-composed head, already waiting to see what he became when the margin finally ran out.

"Tomorrow," he said, into the dark of the boys' room, too low for the captain's curtain to catch, "I take the expensive one."

Somewhere outside the Shrek night, past the training ranges and the wild country where a Thousand-Plate had owned its valley, there was a thing that might actually be big enough to knock on one of the doors.

He was looking forward to finding out if it could.

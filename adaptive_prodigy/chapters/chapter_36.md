## Part 1: What the Plate Could Not Do

Mu Chen said it on the Saturday, at the end of the shift, while he was banking the far forge, which was how he said things he had been holding.

"The machine you used in the examination," he said. "Do you know what it is."

"An endurance plate."

"It's a *child's* endurance plate." Mu Chen put the rake down. "The Association fits every branch school with them, and they are built to read a hundred and twenty kilograms comfortably and two hundred and fifty at the top of the scale, because that is the range a child is supposed to be in and there is no reason to build an instrument for numbers that do not occur." He looked at Lin Hao. "The Association's machine put **six hundred and twelve** on you last month. This plate would not have shown you that. It would have shown the end of its own travel and called that your number."

"That's what the examination said."

"The examination said two hundred and fifty, because two hundred and fifty is where it stops." Mu Chen said it without emphasis, which was how he said the important things. "The plate was at the end of its travel. You were not."

Lin Hao stood there with his coat half on and understood, with a small cold feeling that was not fear exactly, that he had known this for a while and had not looked at it.

"How do you know."

"Because the plate came back with a bent arm." Mu Chen picked up the rake again. "They sent a man out on the Tuesday to look at it, and he wrote in his report that the plate had been over-driven, and the Branch has had six of those plates for eleven years and has never once had to send for a man." He banked the last of the coals. "There is a press in the sub-level. It is not a child's instrument. It is what we use to test forged work for the Association's own records, and it has a dial with marks on it up to a number I am not going to say out loud, and I would like you to come down there with me on Wednesday when the building is empty."

"Why."

"Because I have been a third-rank smith for nineteen years and I have never once been curious about a boy before," Mu Chen said. "And because if you are what the plate says you are not, then somebody ought to know, and it should be somebody who will not write it down."

## Part 2: Wednesday

Wulin came, because Lin Hao asked him, and because asking him had stopped being difficult about a week ago.

The sub-level was cold and smelled of oil and hot scale, and the press stood in the middle of it under a single lamp like something that had been left there by people in a hurry. It was not beautiful. It was a column of black iron with a screw the width of a barrel and a plate at the bottom the size of a table, and the dial was brass and as wide as a shield, and it had marks on it, and the marks stopped at a number that Mu Chen did not say out loud and Lin Hao did not read, because he had decided before he came down the stairs that he was not going to know it in advance.

"Put your fist on the plate," Mu Chen said. "Not a strike. A *push.* Slowly. I want the whole of it and not the fastest part."

"What if it doesn't stop."

"It will stop." Mu Chen was already at the dial. "Everything stops."

Lin Hao put his fist on the plate.

The iron was cold enough to bite. He could feel the marks on his forearms — the faint feather-patterns that ran from wrist to elbow — go slightly more present against the metal, the way they always did against metal, and the hair on his arm lift, and somewhere above him the lamp buzzed, and he thought about the fact that he had never in his life pushed at anything as hard as he could push, because there had never been a reason to, and every person he had ever fought had been somebody he was trying not to hurt.

So he did not push hard.

He pushed *all of it,* which was a different thing, and he had never done that either.

## Part 3: The Marks

The needle went up.

It went up smoothly, which was the strange part, because Lin Hao had expected a jump and there was none — the needle simply climbed the way a tide climbs, without deciding, and it passed the mark that the child's plate had at its top and did not slow, and Mu Chen said nothing, and Wulin, who had been leaning against the column with his arms folded, unfolded them.

It passed the second mark.

"Mu Chen," Wulin said.

"I can see it."

"I know you can see it, I'm saying it because I'd like somebody else to say it too."

It passed the third mark. And Lin Hao, who was watching the dial over his own arm, noticed two things at once: that his shoulder had begun to shake, which had never happened to him in his life, and that the needle was still climbing, and that these two facts were both true at the same time.

And then the needle went past the last mark on the dial.

Not to the end of the dial. **Past the last mark, into the brass with nothing printed on it.**

Mu Chen took his hand off the release and stood very still for a moment, and then he walked around the press and put his own hand on Lin Hao's shoulder and said, "Stop," in a voice that was not loud, and Lin Hao stopped, and the needle came down, and the sub-level was quiet except for the lamp.

"What was it," Wulin said.

"I don't know," Mu Chen said.

"You were reading the dial."

"I was reading a needle that was in a place with no number on it." Mu Chen looked at the brass. "The marks stop at a figure I am not going to say, and it went past it, and the difference between those two things is the only answer I can give you." He turned to Lin Hao. "Take your hand off."

Lin Hao took his hand off. His arm was shaking so badly he had to put it against his leg to steady it, and he stood there in the cold and understood that the shaking was not the effort. The effort had been nothing. The shaking was the four years of never having done it.

---

Gu Yue was in the yard when they came back up, sitting on the low wall with her arms folded, which was where she sat when she had been waiting a while and did not intend to admit it.

"You went down there," she said.

"Yes."

"Without telling anyone."

"I told Wulin."

"That isn't anyone, that's Wulin." She unfolded her arms. "The needle went past the marks. Mu Chen's journeyman has been talking about it all week and he does not know what he saw, and there are now three people in this building with a version of a story about you and none of them have asked you, because you have made it extremely clear that you would rather not." She stood up. "So I am asking. What are you."

"I don't know."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." Lin Hao put his hands in his pockets. "And I'd rather give you that than make one up, because you'd know, and then you'd have to decide whether I was lying or stupid, and I'd rather you didn't have to decide either."

Gu Yue looked at him for a moment.

"That's the second honest thing you've said to me," she said, "and I'd like it noted that I've been keeping count."

"Am I at two."

"You're at two." She went past him toward the dormitory, and stopped. "Three," she said, without turning around. "That was the third."

## Part 4: What Mu Chen Did Not Write Down

They sat on the cold floor with their backs against the press, because nobody suggested going upstairs, and Mu Chen produced a flask from inside his coat in a way that suggested it had been there for a while.

"I'm not going to write it down," he said.

"There's nothing to write."

"There's the fact." Mu Chen drank, and offered the flask to Wulin, who took it, and did not offer it to Lin Hao, which was correct. "And I've been thinking about that fact for eleven days, since the examination, and I want to tell you what I've decided, and I want you to argue with it if you can." He looked at the brass dial. "The Association believes that a body is what its rank says it is. That's not stupidity, it's bookkeeping — a rank-30 has two rings, a rank-30 has a known range, and everything in the world is filed by rank because rank is the only thing we can count. And the reason your plate bent is that you are two rings' worth of *age* in a rank-30 body, and there is no column in any ledger for that."

"How old are my rings."

Mu Chen looked at him.

"You don't know," Lin Hao said.

"I know that a hundred-year soul spirit bestows two rings," Mu Chen said carefully, "and I know that spirit souls age, and I know that the Association does not measure a member's spirit soul age because there has never been a reason to, and I have watched you for four years and I have never once seen you do anything that a hundred years would explain." He put the flask away. "I don't know how old they are. I know they are not a hundred. And I have decided not to find out, and I want to be clear that this is a decision and not an oversight, because I would rather be the man who didn't look than the man who looked and then had to decide who to tell."

Wulin said, "Why not tell Wu Zhangkong."

"Because he already knows," Mu Chen said. "He's known since before the tournament, and he has told nobody, and I have watched a man keep a secret for four years and I have decided that whatever he is protecting is not the boy's number." He stood up and brushed the scale off his coat. "It's the boy."

---

## Part 5: The Walk Back

They walked back through the yard at about ten, and neither of them said anything for two streets, and then Wulin said the thing he had plainly been holding since the sub-level.

"It's more than mine."

"Yes."

"I'm not upset." Wulin said it honestly, in the way he said things when he had already worked out how he felt about them. "I want to say that properly, because you've been waiting all night for me to be. I've had the Golden Dragon King in my blood since before I was born, and everybody who has ever looked at me has seen a weed, and I have spent ten years being told what I am by people who could not see. And you have had everybody look at you and see exactly what's there." He put his hands in his pockets. "So I don't mind. I'd rather have been seen wrong and be catching up, than be seen right and be where everybody already put me."

"That's a better way to feel about it than I would have managed."

"I've had more practice at it." Wulin glanced at him. "Your arm's still shaking."

"I've noticed."

"Is that bad."

"No." Lin Hao looked at his own hand, and at the faint feather-marks on it, which had gone slightly bright during the press and had not entirely gone back. "It's the first time I've found out what I am and it turned out to be more than I thought, and it turns out that's frightening in exactly the same way as finding out it's less. I'd have bet you either way it would feel like relief."

"Does it."

"No." He laughed once. "It feels like somebody handed me a bar I don't know how to forge yet."

Wulin was quiet for a bit.

"You'll work it out," he said. "You always do. You'll put it on the anvil and you'll hit it until it tells you what it is, and then you'll tell me, and I'll say something unhelpful, and that'll be that."

"That's exactly what will happen."

"Good." Wulin turned in at the dormitory door. "Wednesday again?"

"Wednesday."

"And Lin Hao." Wulin stopped with his hand on the door. "Next time bring Xie Xie. He'll be unbearable about not having been there, and I'd rather he be unbearable at the time than for a fortnight afterwards."

---

## Part 6: What Was Not Said

Lin Hao stood in the yard for a while after the door closed.

The marks on his forearms were still faintly bright. He put his sleeve down over them out of habit, and then stopped, and left it up, because there was nobody in the yard and because he had spent four years deciding what to hide and he was beginning to suspect that the deciding was costing him more than the hiding.

He thought about the dial. Not the number — there was no number — but the brass past the last mark, where there was nothing printed, and the fact that a needle had been there, and that the only three people who knew it had all separately decided not to say so.

Mu Chen had decided not to look.
Wu Zhangkong had known for four years and told nobody.
Wulin had watched the needle pass his own strength and said *I'm not upset.*

Three men, one week, three different kinds of not saying it.

*So that's what I am,* Lin Hao thought. *I'm the thing that makes honest men keep secrets.*

He stood with that for a while, and then he went inside, because it was late, and because there was a bar of spirit steel on his bench that he had not finished, and because whatever he was, he was still a smith, and the bar did not care.

The hawk, nine hundred and forty-two years old, did not comment.

But when he lay down, the hair at his temple was still moving, and it went on moving for a long time after he stopped noticing it.

## Timeline: Age 10, weeks 7-9 at Eastsea Academy — tournament in three months

## Part 1: Did You Win?

For three days after the enrollment test, the first grade asked Xie Xie one question, in every corridor, at every meal, with the tireless repetition of a grade that had found its new favorite subject.

*Did you win?*

He had developed his answer by the second afternoon. To the silk jackets of class one, who asked with smirk-edges: "No." Flat, final, offered with the same expression a duke might use to decline a dance. To the lower classes, who asked with hope: "No," plus a shrug. To Zhou Zhangxi, at breakfast, who asked with a mouth full: "Zhou. Zhangxi. No."

It should have cost him standing. Losing the test in front of the whole grade should have put him back where his arrogance used to live — alone at the top of a very small hill. Instead something stranger happened. The grade had watched him *take* the loss — the three breaths on one knee, the laugh, the offered hand, the "you're going to have to teach me that last one." And the grade, which was ten and eleven years old and therefore merciless, decided without holding a meeting that Xie Xie of class five was, somehow, *all right.*

He noticed this himself on the third day, standing in the dining line behind a fourth-grader who turned around, recognized him, and said — not mockingly, but the way you'd confirm weather — "You're the one who fought the Elementalist."

"Everyone says that like it's my name now," Xie Xie said.

"It kind of is," said the fourth-grader, and moved on.

## Part 2: Elementalist?

The word itself had arrived the way words do in academies: nobody knew who said it first, and by the end of the week nobody needed to.

*Elementalist.*

The upper grades knew the term from theory lectures; the theory lectures knew it from footnotes; the footnotes knew it from older books. A soul that carried not one element but the command of elements — fire and water and wind and earth and light and, if the oldest books were to be believed, *space* — was not a martial soul in any register the Academy kept. Martial souls were sword or beast or tool or plant. They were *one thing, deeply.* The six-thing soul was what scholars called a classification error, and what older scholars, quietly, called a legend.

Wu Zhangkong, asked point-blank by a brave second-grader during joint instruction, had answered in full: "I have never heard of such a martial soul." Then, after a pause that the grade would analyze for weeks: "Her file is sealed. Train for the tournament."

So the grade did what grades do. It made the legend into a girl. The back-left desk under the bad window acquired a gravity that rearranged the classroom's weather — students volunteering to sweep near it, questions asked in her direction at rates no other first-grader in living memory had drawn, and Gu Yue answering precisely the number of questions that were asked, in the fewest words the questions deserved, and then returning to a stillness so complete that Yun Xiao had murmured — audibly, at lunch, to general awe — "I think she's furniture. Extremely dangerous furniture."

Wu Zhangkong worked it out on the ninth day, alone, in the training ground, watching her take a hit.

She had not blocked it. She had *redistributed* it — taken the force on a wall of moving air, bled the heat into the flagstones, and stepped sideways through the gap she had made, and she had done all three at once without a soul skill, because she had no soul skill for any of it, because her ring would not attach to any element she commanded.

He stood there for a long time after she had gone in.

Then he went to the theory library and took down a book nobody in the intermediate division had opened in eleven years, and he read a passage about **spiritual-type martial souls**, and he closed the book, and he understood.

It was not six martial souls. It was **one**, and it was not an element at all.

She was a **Spirit System Battle Soul Master** — a soul whose substance was not fire or wind but the *command* of them, held together by spiritual power so large that it could afford to spend itself six ways at once. That was why no element of hers was ever strong. That was why her ring would not sit on any of them. That was why her spiritual power was a number that did not belong to children.

And that was why, Wu Zhangkong understood, sitting in a library at four in the afternoon with a book on his knee, the Spirit Pagoda had sealed a ten-year-old's file.

**Spirit System Battle Soul Masters were among the rarest Soul Masters alive, and the most sought after — because they combined with mechas better than anything else a body could carry.** A strength-type soul made a man strong. A spirit-type soul made a *machine* an extension of a man.

He put the book back. He did not take it out again. He did not tell anyone, including the Director, and when Long Hengxu asked him a week later whether he had identified the girl's martial soul, Wu Zhangkong said, "It's not a bad type of martial soul. You'll see it in the tournament," and changed the subject, and Long Hengxu — who was a Director and not a fool — let him.

## Part 3: Your Opponent is Me

On the seventh day, Wu Zhangkong kept class five after the bell — all of them, the whole twenty-two, for the length of one sentence.

"The class promotion tournament begins in three months." His pale green eyes crossed the room and came to rest, one by one, on four faces. "Tang Wulin. Xie Xie. Gu Yue. Lin Hao. The rest of you will compete in the roster matches. These four will additionally train as the class's combat team — and their training starts tonight, after dinner." A beat. "Dismissed."

The training ground behind the dining hall was circular, warded at its rim by a soul-guidance shield that hummed like a held note. Wu Zhangkong stood at its center with his hands behind his back, and when the four arrived he did not greet them. He checked things.

"Spiritual power. Hands forward." He went down the line with a flat gray crystal — the Federation's standard measure — the way a harbor officer checks manifests. Xie Xie: a good number, noble-good, Spirit Origin advanced; Wu Zhangkong logged it without comment. Gu Yue: the crystal glowed to a value so *even*, so precisely mid-range, that the officer's eye — trained on thirty years of crooked human numbers — narrowed at its perfection by one millimeter. He logged it. Lin Hao: the crystal climbed, and climbed, and settled at a number that belonged to grown men and seasoned ones — Spirit Connection, a hundred and twenty deep — and Wu Zhangkong looked at the small ten-year-old holding out his hand like a boy offering a library card, and logged it, and said nothing at all, which the four of them were learning was the loudest thing he did.

"Your team training," he said, "has one subject. Not victory. *Function.* The tournament is won by classes, not by monsters, and I have four monsters and no class." He let that settle. "Tang Wulin — you fight like a forge that walks. Good. Stop using only the forge: combine the hammer and the Bluesilver Grass; the grass is a hand and you use it like a pocket. Xie Xie — your speed currently serves Xie Xie. Retire it; the team needs your speed to serve four. Gu Yue —" and here he paused, because he had prepared nothing for a soul he had never heard of, and Wu Zhangkong did not pretend — "your martial soul is powerful and comprehensive. Use less of it. A team drowns in a flood; it drinks from a cup." And finally, the pale green eyes came to rest — not on the boy, but on the sword at his hip: the only weapon on the field that was also a soul. When Wu Zhangkong spoke again, the register had changed. It was no longer a teacher sorting students. It was a swordsman recognizing one.

"Lin Hao. Your blade reads the world and waits for the world to finish speaking. That is a servant's sword." A beat, cold as his namesake. "A true sword speaks *first* — one voice, one edge, everything you are behind a single word. That is sword intent. Your eyes are not the problem; they are the crutch your blade is too polite to refuse. Find what your sword wants to say before the world finishes its sentence." He paused, and the temperature of his voice dropped the last degree, into something almost like respect. "I have spent my life on one edge. Do not make me watch you spend yours on everything else."

"Teacher," Xie Xie said, bouncing on the balls of his feet with the specific eagerness of a boy who had been promised combat and intended to collect, "who are we fighting tonight?"

Wu Zhangkong stepped to the center of the warded circle. He put his hands down at his sides — the first time any of them had seen his hands not behind his back — and the hum of the shield around the field rose half a note, the way a kettle's does.

"Your opponent," he said, "is me."

He did not release his martial soul.

He walked to the rack at the edge of the circle and took down a **wooden sword** — plain ash, worn smooth at the grip, no soul guidance in it at all, the sort of thing a first-year uses so that they stop hurting each other — and he weighed it once in his hand the way a man weighs a letter he has read before.

"Xie Xie. Come here."

Xie Xie came, because he was Xie Xie, and he went in at full speed with the Light Dragon Dagger out and his hundred-year ring up, and for eleven seconds the training ground was a blur of him.

And Wu Zhangkong did not move his feet.

He moved the wooden sword. Four times. Each time it arrived at the exact place Xie Xie was about to be, and each time it did not strike him — it simply *was there*, so that continuing the attack meant walking onto it. On the fourth, Xie Xie's dagger was resting on the ash and his wrist was being held at an angle that he had not agreed to, and Wu Zhangkong had not released a martial soul, or used a soul skill, or taken a single step.

"Again tomorrow," he said, and let him go.

Then, to all four of them, because he was a teacher and this was the lesson and the beating was only the punctuation:

"My martial soul is a sword. I am working toward a realm called **One Sword Cleaves All Techniques**." He turned the ash blade once in his hand. "It means that whatever comes at me — whatever it is, whoever is holding it — I answer with the same thing. One sword. That is the whole of it." A pause. "Not because the sword is the strongest answer. Because it is *mine*, and I have made it strong enough."

Lin Hao, who had been holding his own blade at a polite angle for eleven minutes, understood something in that sentence that he would be turning over for the next three years.

"You're not teaching us four weapons," he said.

"No."

"You're teaching us to have one answer."

Wu Zhangkong looked at him for a moment. "You are the second person this year to hear that correctly," he said, "and the first to say it out loud before I had to." He set the wooden sword back on the rack. "Your eyes are not the problem, Lin Hao. Your eyes are an excellent instrument attached to a boy who keeps asking them what to do. **The sword decides.** Until it does, you have a very good eye and a very polite blade, and you will lose to worse swordsmen than me who simply decided sooner."

The first night, they lost in forty seconds. The second night, thirty-one. It was not that he was fast — he was not fast; he was a teacher, and teachers do not run, they *arrive*. It was that every attack the four built arrived at a place he had already stopped standing, and every defense they raised was gently, almost kindly, taken apart at its seam — the seam he had *told them about that afternoon* — and by the ninth night, bruised and breathing hard in a circle that had stopped being four circles, something happened that Wu Zhangkong had been waiting for.

Lin Hao, flat on his back with his hawk-eyes full of stars, did not call it out — he *answered* it. He rolled, came up on one knee, and the Stormbringer was in his hand and *moving* before Wu Zhangkong's next strike had finished arriving; the blade met the incoming force not to block it but to *turn* it, the flat of the steel catching the strike's line and redirecting it past his shoulder into the floor, and in the same motion he was up and forward, the sword speaking the counter his body had already read. Xie Xie's light-step carried him clear of the redirected strike *because Lin Hao's blade had already moved it*, and Gu Yue's cup of earth rose exactly where the low thing needed to break, and Wulin's grass caught the break — and Wu Zhangkong, for the first time in nine nights, had to *move twice.*

He called the night there. Walking out of the circle, he paused beside the four of them, and said the longest sentence of the term.

"A team is not four students who win together. It is four students who have agreed, in advance, on what each of them is for." He looked at each of them — the forge, the light, the flood learning to be a cup, and the sword that had not yet spoken. "You began agreeing tonight. Three months is sufficient. Go eat."

They went eat. And afterward, in room eleven, while Xie Xie bragged at the ceiling and Lin Hao lay on the other bed listening to the harbor, Wulin sat down into his spiral meditation the way he sat down into everything — fully — and somewhere under the ninth night's bruises, the rank that had been leaning on its door for two weeks quietly came through.

No spectacle. No light. Just a settling, like a casting cooling true in the mold — Soul Master rank fourteen, arrived the way forged things arrive: because the heat and the hammering and the hours had finally added up to it. The Goldsong under his sleeve stirred a warm hello against his wrist. The first seal, that furnace behind his sternum, banked itself a degree hotter, satisfied. And Wulin, who had been rank thirteen since before enrollment — rank thirteen while sparring a monster three evenings a week, while his hammer and his grass learned each other's names, while a teacher who praised no one told him to combine them — opened his eyes in the dark and grinned at nothing, and said nothing, because some things you keep until they're worth a sentence.

Lin Hao, across the room, heard the settling the way he heard everything metal: as a note. He didn't turn his head. "*Fourteen,*" he said, into the dark, the way you'd read a temperature.

"How did you—"

"Your spiral changed pitch. It's deeper." A pause, and the grin in his voice. "Dinner's on you tomorrow, rank fourteen."

"Ranks aren't *ranks,* they're— Wulin. *Wulin.* That's not even a real word—" But Xie Xie was already asleep, and the harbor kept its lights, and room eleven held two boys and one very quiet Goldsong, and the grade's worst class had, without anyone filing it anywhere, gotten quietly, measurably *stronger* — which is the only kind of stronger that has ever counted.

## Part 4: Forge for Me Then!

The dining hall's third window did its post-training trade, and the combat team of class five claimed its usual corner with the bone-tired silence of people whose bodies had been disassembled and returned.

It was Xie Xie, refueled and therefore verbal, who broke it. "Question," he said, pointing a spoon at Wulin. "Something's been eating at me since Mu Chen's name came up in your mouth — and I notice, I notice everything now, it's my whole personality — you come back from the Association smelling like a forge three nights a week. In forge clothes. With forge hands." He sat up straighter. "Tang Wulin. Are you a *blacksmith?*"

Wulin, who had learned that lying to Xie Xie was like hiding a hammer from a forge, nodded. "The Blacksmith's Association. My martial soul's innate talent wasn't high enough, so I began to learn forging young."

"A blacksmith," Yun Xiao repeated, thoughtfully. "The kind that purifies metal?"

Another nod. "I'm second rank. Certified." He hesitated a moment — and then, because they were his team now, and the night's forty seconds lived in all of them equally, he gave them the whole of it. "Officially second. But I can complete the Thousand Refinements. Barely."

The silence that followed was Xie Xie-shaped. He had gone very still, in the particular way of a boy doing arithmetic on a dream.

"And you?" said Xie Xie, turning the spoon on Lin Hao. "You go too. You come back the same nights."

"I'm second rank as well."

"You're— " Xie Xie put the spoon down. "Both of you? Since when?"

"Since before we knew you," said Lin Hao.

Which was the moment Wu Zhangkong, who had come into the dining hall for a bowl of soup and who had absolutely no reason to stop at the corner table of four exhausted ten-year-olds, stopped at the corner table of four exhausted ten-year-olds.

"Forging," he said.

Nobody had seen him arrive. That was, by now, a normal part of the evening.

"You're Soul Masters," he said. "You learned how to *forge*." He looked at Wulin, then at Lin Hao, with an expression that was not angry, which somehow made it worse. "**The clumsy bird flies early into the forest.** You two are the clumsy birds of this class — you have the least to work with and the most ground to make up — and you spent it on a *pointless thing.*"

"Teacher—" Wulin began.

"So you decided to become blacksmiths? **What a farce.**" Wu Zhangkong set his bowl down. He did not raise his voice; he had never once raised his voice. "Remember this. **You are my students. Unless I deem you to be useless, you will put all of your effort into becoming Soul Masters.** Not most of it. All of it. The forge does not care whether you live."

Wulin looked at the table. He did not argue. He had, Lin Hao noticed, decided something privately some time ago and was not going to reopen it in public, which was the most Wulin thing there was.

Lin Hao put his chopsticks down.

"I'm keeping it," he said.

"Then you'll be a worse swordsman."

"Probably," said Lin Hao. "And I'll be the only swordsman in this academy who knows what his blade is made of." He met Wu Zhangkong's eyes, which was not a comfortable thing to do and which he did anyway. "You told me last week my blade is too polite. You told me the sword has to decide. You can't tell a boy to make his own weapon and then be surprised when he wants to know how it's made. That isn't a distraction, Teacher. That's the same instruction."

The dining hall noise went on around them. Xie Xie had stopped eating, which was the highest available compliment.

Wu Zhangkong looked at him for a long time.

"You are arguing with your teacher," he said at last, "in a dining hall, in front of your team, on the ninth day."

"Yes."

"Good." He picked up his bowl. "Argue better next time — you conceded 'probably,' and you should not have, because you were right." And he went to get his soup.

Gu Yue, who had said nothing at all through the whole of it, moved her tray two centimetres closer to Lin Hao's, which Xie Xie noticed, and filed, and did not comment on, because he was learning.


"The Thousand Refinements," he said slowly. "That's — that's *Grandmaster* territory. That's third rank work. Do you know what my father paid for a mecha core component that had been Thousand Refined *once?*" His voice climbed. "It was the price of a house. A *house*, Wulin. Do you understand what you're saying? In a few years you'll be a Grandmaster for certain — be my personal blacksmith! Forge mecha components for me! I'll pay market price — no, better than market —"

"You want to be a Mecha Master," Wulin said. It was not a question; the dream was sitting on Xie Xie's face like a crown.

"The symbol of a true noble," Xie Xie said simply, and for once there was no performance in it at all. "A mecha made for you, by you, inscribed by your own arrays — nothing off the rack. And if the metal under it was refined by your own blacksmith—" He caught himself, grinned, unashamed. "Fine. I'm saying I want the best. Is that so evil?"

At the end of the table, Lin Hao had been watching with his chin on his fist, and now he laughed — not at Xie Xie, but with the particular pleasure of a boy watching something true happen. When Xie Xie's recruitment gaze swung on him — *and you, the Listener, the President's Friday boy, your hands are surely—* — Lin Hao held up both of his, palms out, in surrender.

"Spoken for," he said. "My father's forge gets my hands, the President's door gets my Fridays, and whatever's left belongs to a hawk with opinions." He nodded at Wulin. "You want the man who makes metal *sing.* Ask him about the sixty-third strike sometime." He picked up his fourth bun. "I'll be right here enjoying the show."

"Fine!" Xie Xie wheeled back, and Wulin — looking at the three faces around the table, at Zhou Zhangxi who had stopped eating to listen, at Yun Xiao who was pretending not to have leaned in, at the boy who had shared forty seconds of ground with him nine nights running — found that the answer had already happened somewhere under his ribs.

"Fine," he said. "When I reach that rank, I'll forge for you. All of you."

It was, in retrospect, a mistake to say it within arm's reach of Xie Xie. He was hugged — briefly, fiercely, in the manner of a boy raised by servants who had read about hugs — and then Xie Xie stood on the bench, glass of milk raised, and announced to the third window in a voice that reached the second:

"From this day forth, Tang Wulin is under the protection of I, Xie Xie! Whoever dares to offend him will answer to my Light Dragon Dagger!"

The hall applauded, because it was ten and eleven years old. And from the middle of the applause, dry as a ledger page, came Yun Xiao's voice — not loud, but perfectly carried:

"You should wait until you can defeat Gu Yue before saying such words."

Two tables away, Gu Yue was leaving with her tray, alone as always, and she did not turn around — but her step, for exactly one pace, was not quite the still water it always was. Whether that was because of the applause, or the declaration, or the word *Grandmaster* hanging in the air above the buns, nobody at the third window could have said.

Nobody at the third window noticed, either, that she had paused — half a breath, unremarkable — at the word *Thousand Refinements.*

## Part 5: Three Months

On the roof that night, the wind came in off the harbor with its long argument, and Wulin counted on his fingers: three months to the tournament; second rank on the board and third rank visible from here; a team that had agreed, tonight, on forty seconds; a promise about a mecha that did not exist yet, made by a boy who shone like a promise himself.

"Three months," he said.

"Three months," Lin Hao agreed. He was lying back on the tiles, and if anyone had watched closely — nobody was — they would have seen that his eyes were on the far window of the girls' dormitory, where the Elementalist's lamp burned steady as a filed report, and that in his chest the hawk sat facing it, patient as weather, exactly as it had every night since the fence.

He had stopped asking it why. Some instincts, like some metals, only show their grain under the hammer — and the hammer, he was beginning to understand, was three months away.

*All right,* he thought at the city, at the wind, at the lamp, at the tide of the whole coming thing. *Come on, then.*

The harbor answered the way harbors do: with all its lights, and none of its secrets.

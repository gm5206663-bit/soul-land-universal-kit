## Part 1: The Call from Eastsea

The eleven hundred points from the ridge-boar job settled into Lin Hao's ledger like good iron into a forge, and for a few days the five of them ate properly and stopped counting the cost of every bowl, which was its own kind of happiness. The workshop rent was paid a season in advance. The ridge-plates he'd carried down sat in his rented space at the Blacksmiths' Association waiting to be cut into something worth cutting, and his forearms had forgotten there had ever been a wound, which was the jiao's quiet opinion about wounds and a good thing, since a smith could not afford to be precious about his hands.

It was Wulin who came back from the administration building two evenings later with an expression Lin Hao had learned to read across a great many years and a great many shared meals — the particular brightness Wulin wore when he had hold of news too large to keep and was trying, badly, not to spend it all in the first sentence.

"Teacher Mu," Wulin said, and then stopped, and laughed at himself, and started over. "I called him. To tell him. About the academy, and the association here, and — and the spirit-refining. I did it again, Hao. I spirit-refined. At thirteen."

Lin Hao set down the practice blade he was oiling. "You told him that."

"I told him that." Wulin's laugh went a little thin around the edges. "He didn't say anything for a long while. Then he told me if I so much as thought about spirit-refining again before I reached rank thirty, he'd come all the way to Shrek and break my hands for my own good. Far too dangerous, he said. The one success doesn't mean I understand it."

"He's right," Lin Hao said. It was not what Wulin expected him to say, and the boy blinked. "A successful first refinement is the luckiest thing that ever happens to a young smith. You treat it as luck, not skill, until the metal can't surprise you anymore. Rank thirty. Listen to him."

Wulin nodded, and then the brightness won, because Wulin could not keep a lid on joy the way some boys couldn't keep a lid on a kettle. "And then he said — he said he's been at Eastsea too long. That Shrek is a pretty nice place. That he'd come for a visit in a few days." Wulin was nearly bouncing. "Hao. He's coming here. He's leaving the association. I think — I didn't hear it like a visit. I heard it like he's moving. You don't book two train tickets for a visit, do you? You book two tickets when you're bringing your hammers and not coming back."

Lin Hao was quiet a moment, turning the cloth over the blade in his hands. Mu Chen. The square, patient man who had set a child the test of a grown apprentice and then spent six months not quite believing what had come back to him; the teacher who had never once praised him to his face and had never once, either, failed to put a harder piece of metal in front of him when the last one was mastered. He had not seen Mu Chen since the train to Shrek. He had expected to be an adult the next time they shared a forge — or close enough to one.

"Good," Lin Hao said, and meant it. "The metal's all in this city now. A teacher who follows the metal is a teacher worth following. We'll set a place for him at the table."

## Part 2: The Working Students' Table

There was, that same week, the matter of dinner, which in the working-student dormitory was less a meal than a small rolling parliament.

The working students were a shrinking and strange population, which was the first thing you learned sitting among them. Xie Xie, who counted everything, had counted. "Last year there were six of us in this dorm," he said around a mouthful of cheap food, pointing his chopsticks at the row of tables. "Two graduated, two went up to the inner court, one dropped out. So of the old lot —"

"It's just me," Yuanen Yehui said. The strongest of the working students sat a little apart, eating with the economical steadiness of someone who had been paying for their own education for a long while. "One of the old batch. Five, if you're counting the new."

"Five," Xie Xie agreed. "Five of us now." He did not say, the way a less careful boy might, that the five of them had already done more in their first weeks than some students did in a year. Wulin was the youngest fourth-rank blacksmith Shrek had ever registered. Gu Yue was the element-user the intake instruments had refused to settle on. Xu Xiaoyan had crossed to her second ring over the summer. Xie Xie himself was three rings and entirely too pleased about it. And Lin Hao — Lin Hao was Lin Hao, which the table understood meant more than anyone at the table was allowed to say.

There was a girl who worked the dining hall on the supper shift, red-haired, quick, with the particular closed watchfulness of someone running two lives at once. Wulin had been trying, without success, to make the arithmetic about her close — a working student's badge that did not match the shift she worked, rooms that looked lived-in when the roster said they were empty, the way she and Yuanen were never, ever seen in the same breath. He had not asked. A boy with his own family's sealed doors learned early not to pry at other people's. But his eyes followed the red-haired waitress the way they followed a piece of metal with a hidden seam, and Lin Hao saw him seeing it and said nothing, the way he said nothing about most of the things Wulin was too kind to say aloud.

The peace of the table did not last the meal, because Yue Zhengyu was incapable of letting peace last.

The Holy Angel boy — fifteen years old, second grade, just out of a year of clan seclusion, with the serene, unshakable certainty of someone whose family had told him every morning of his life that he was one of the elect — sat himself down uninvited at the working-student table with his tray, and beamed around at them all like a missionary arriving among grateful heathens.

"Once I catch her," he said, by way of opening, addressing no one and everyone, "it'll be done. To eradicate evil is the duty of every Holy Angel soul master. A Fallen Angel soul master is an evil soul master — there's no such thing as a Fallen one that isn't. She's on this campus. I'll find her."

The table went the particular kind of quiet that comes right before someone is rude. It was Xie Xie who went first. "The enforcer already told you she's not an evil soul master. How are you not embarrassed? You shoulder-rammed a working student in the hall and you've been chasing a girl around the academy for days on top of it."

"Then I'll dig up proof," Yue said, unruffled, and reached for his purse with the easy confidence of a boy who had never once in his life worried about the price of anything. "Here. Let me pay for tonight's meal. All of you. It embarrasses me to think of you scrimping — please. Take care of me in the future, won't you? I'll be seeing a lot of this table."

"This has nothing to do with backbone," Wulin said, with the mild firmness Lin Hao knew from a hundred such moments. "The meal's already paid for."

Gu Yue, who had said nothing through all of it, set her chopsticks down with a small, precise click and favoured Yue Zhengyu with a look that would have frosted a lesser boy. "We won't accept food from someone as dishonest as you."

Yue Zhengyu blinked, then grinned, entirely charmed and entirely unchastened, and Lin Hao — watching it over his own bowl with the weary affection of an older brother watching a cat knock over someone else's cup — concluded that the Holy Angel boy was going to be a permanent feature of their lives whether they wanted him or not, and that the cheapest thing to do about it was exactly what Wulin did a few breaths later: Wulin tilted his head, looked at Yue Zhengyu the way he looked at a useful piece of scrap, and asked, very politely, whether a Holy Angel clan with businesses all over the campus had any need of a blacksmith who could thousand-refine, because he was a fourth-rank smith and he was, in so many words, poor.

Lin Hao kept his eyes on his bowl and let his brother do the haggling. Some smiths mined the mountain; Wulin mined the people sitting next to him.

## Part 3: The Plates in the Workshop

He went down to his rented workshop after the meal, because he could not sleep when there was good metal in the room with him, and the ridge-boar plates were good metal.

The Blacksmiths' Association at Shrek let its working smiths rent a bench and a furnace by the hour, the price coming out of the same contribution points as everything else on the academy grounds; it was a better arrangement than Eastsea's, in that the fire was hotter and the man in the bay beside you might be a Master Craftsman twice your age, and a worse one, in that nothing on the premises came cheap. He had paid a season's rent out of the cull bounty, which felt exactly right: the beast had bought the place where its own hide would be worked. Ten thousand years of horn plate did not turn into battle armour by sitting in a cupboard, and he had learned at Mu Chen's elbow that a smith who hoarded material instead of cutting it was only a man pretending at the trade.

He laid the best of the ridge plates across the anvil and read it the way you read anything worth making. Too hard on the surface, where the beast had grown it against blows; soft and fibrous at the seam, the same fault line he had driven the Frost Abyss sword through on the mountain. A piece of metal that had lived on a charging animal's back carried its whole history in its grain, and the grain told him it wanted to be drawn, not hammered flat — opened slowly, the way you opened a stubborn seam in a character, by following where it already wanted to go. Two-word-grade foundation stock, He Qiao had called it, and He Qiao was not a man who gave praise past what a thing had earned.

He did not start the piece that night. He was a fifth-rank smith, and five ranks in the blacksmith's trade carried with it the particular discipline of knowing exactly which jobs were yours to begin and which were not; the foundation of a two-word suit wanted hands steadier than his at the opening, and he had already quietly, in the way he did most things, begun looking forward to the day he could carry it up the stairs of this same building to the man at the top who could. That was a project for the coming weeks and the Saint Blacksmith's open ear, not for a single tired evening. What he did instead was the work that was exactly his rank: he cut a practice piece off the edge of a lesser plate, a clean first-grade thousand-refinement, and he put the hours into it until the rhythm was back in his shoulders after the long trip up the mountain.

The hammer made its steady noise alone in the late workshop, and he thought about Mu Chen while it fell. Two hammers coming to Shrek. A teacher following the metal. He set the practice piece to cool and let himself, for the space of a few honest strokes, look forward to it — the way you looked forward to a door opening that you had not, until that moment, realized you'd been standing outside of.

## Part 4: The Boy Who Counts

After dinner, Lin Hao walked the perimeter path with Gu Yue, which was not a thing they announced or arranged and was therefore always exactly what happened.

The autumn wind off the training grounds was ordinary. He made a point of noticing that it was ordinary, because Gu Yue had a way of turning her small unasked questions into weather, and he had learned to answer the weather honestly and leave the rest alone.

"Where did you go last week?" she said. She did not look at him. "For two days. You came back with your sleeves bloody and the blood gone the day after, and you bought all of us dinner and didn't tell anyone the job."

"A suppression ticket," he said. There was no reason to lie, and Gu Yue was the one person in the academy to whom he never gave the careful half-truth he gave the rest of the world. "Black class, up in the iron country. A wounded ten-thousand beast. It was a hard job. It's done."

"Alone."

"Alone."

She walked in silence for a while, the wind lifting the edge of her hair, and then she said the thing she had actually been turning over, the way she always did — coming at the true question from the side. "You don't have to do everything that hard by yourself. There are five of us."

"There are five of you," Lin Hao said gently, "and I am one of the five, and that's the whole answer. When the job needs five, you'll have me. When the job needs one man alone on a mountain for three days setting traps so nothing gets near his family — that one's mine. You can't come on every ticket, Gu Yue. Some of them, I carry so the table stays warm."

She did not answer that directly. She never did. The wind went briefly, oddly still between them for a breath — not cold, not strange, just the particular hush that always came when she felt something about him that she had not yet decided she was allowed to name — and then it moved again, ordinary and loud, full of the academy's evening noise.

"Teacher Mu is coming," she said instead, which was not a non-sequitur; with Gu Yue it never was. She had heard Wulin too. "You're glad."

"I'm glad." He said it plainly. "He taught me the trade before anyone knew I had it in me. A boy gets few enough of those. You keep them when they come back within reach."

They walked the rest of the circuit in the comfortable quiet of two people who had decided, long ago, that the things they did not say to each other were being kept for the right time and not thrown away. In the dormitory ahead of them Wulin was already loudly explaining to Xie Xie exactly how much work a Holy Angel commission was going to pay for, and Xie Xie was already demanding a cut, and Xu Xiaoyan was laughing, and even Yuanen's corner of the room looked a degree less lonely with the table that full.

Somewhere to the east, a man was packing two hammers and booking two train tickets, and the metal that mattered was beginning, slowly, to move toward Shrek.

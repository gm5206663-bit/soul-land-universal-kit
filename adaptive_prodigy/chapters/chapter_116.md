## Part 1: Make the Tier You Stand On Flawless

The glimpse of the far water had not made him reach upward. It had done the opposite.

On the mountain he had felt, for one cold breath, how deep the sword went beyond him — the place Wu Zhangkong had never pretended to teach him to, the mortal-sword shore the old legends pointed at. A stupider boy might have come down from that trip and started clawing after it, trying to drag himself across a gap measured in decades before he'd earned the ground he already stood on. Lin Hao came down and did the smith's thing instead. You did not forge a better blade by staring at the steel you didn't yet own. You took the blade in your hand and made it flawless, edge by edge, until the flaws ran out and the next steel came within reach on its own.

He was at Sword Consciousness. Man and sword as one; the weapon moving on the reflex before the thought finished; the purpose wearing every object he picked up. That was a grown master's tier, and he had reached it years before he should have — and it was still, when he was honest with himself in the cold before dawn, full of the small drags and unevennesses of a boy who had climbed fast. There were depths inside Consciousness he had not yet worn smooth, the way there are depths inside a single form that take a thousand repetitions to stop fighting you. The far shore would be there for years. The job in front of him was to stop wasting a single inch of the shore he was on.

So he sat before dawn with the Stormbringer across his knees and ran the Mysterious Heaven Method the way he had every dawn since Eastsea, the long slow circuit of spirit energy through the body, and he did not point that circuit at any distant gate. He poured it, evenly, into the ground he held — carrying the soul power through the forms, through the deep water, through the storm-sky of the Inner Country, feeding the whole machine rather than chasing one bright horizon. Spiritual power grew the way a smith's shoulders grew: not on the day you lifted something impossible, but on the hundred ordinary days you lifted the right weight the right way. When he opened his eyes the light had changed and his sea was a degree deeper than it had been — not a realm, just a true, measurable inch, the kind that stayed.

## Part 2: Every Tool Back on the Bench

A tool left in the drawer forgot its weight. He had spent enough chapters staring at a far horizon, and now he took down the whole kit and put each piece back to work against the tier he actually held.

He ran the six strokes of Still Water on the practice field at dawn, the foundational forms Wu Zhangkong had given him, and the man-sword-as-one reflex deepened under him: the third form, the one Xinglan had been hunting, stopped needing the decision behind it — the edge was true before he knew he'd committed, the depth within Consciousness that he had shown her by example now owned by his own hand instead of borrowed. He ran the Disorder Splitting Wind Hammer at the anvil in the afternoons, the forty-nine-strike rhythm climbing its own mountain; with the Consciousness depth in him, the hammer met the metal a half-breath truer, the strike landing where the grain was already willing, and he turned out a clean first-grade thousand-refinement that afternoon and logged it for points the way he hadn't in weeks of looking inward.

He worked the Frost Abyss Domain in the cold evenings out on the range, alone. On the cull he'd called it up only as a thin shield-brace at the moment of need; the truth was he had never made peace with the thing, had treated it as a door you flung open in an emergency and slammed shut after. Now he walked it out a breath at a time and taught it the difference between a battlefield and a practice yard — he let the deep cold spread from the Frost Abyss sword until the grass under his boots went white and the air went still, and he HELD it there, ten breaths, twenty, a full minute, a domain that knew how to be a tool and not only a war. By the fourth evening the frost no longer bit his own hands when he spread it.

He ran the Purple Demon Eyes against the sunrise until the light stopped hurting, the third stage he had reached at his own pace and never drilled past; he read the wind off the ranges with Storm Reading, the perception skill that had sat idle too long, and on a clear morning he could taste a storm building a day's march away; he called up Domineer, the black-edged Talon, the deep-water body that healed overnight and walked cold water unburned, and he put each through its forms the way a smith oiled every tool in the box and not only the newest. By the week's end he was not a boy chasing a realm above him. He was a craftsman who owned, completely, the tier he stood on, with every tool in practice and no gaps between the ones he trained and the ones he used.

## Part 3: The Hawk and the Fusion

The hawk was not a thing you climbed toward; it was a thing you flew until it stopped being an effort.

The Hawk-Soul Union — the perfect fusion that let him wear the second body of storm and feather, the wings of wind-light and the hawk-eyes — had been a door he opened in extremity on the dangerous jobs, never at will. The dragon-fusion that rode in its wake, the deep-water joining that burned him every second he sustained it, he had never held for long at all. These were not realms to cross; they were skills to practice, like any stroke, and he had been treating them like trumps to be hoarded.

He took them out to the high ridge above Shrek at dawn, where there was no one to see and no job riding on success, and he drilled the thing the way it should have been drilled from the start: low, short, and often. He opened the Union for ten seconds and folded it; opened it for fifteen and folded it; let it come a dozen times in a morning until the door's hinges wore smooth and the second body answered the way his own hand answered. Then, when that was easy, he let the dragon-fusion ride behind it for a breath — two — three — and folded down before the burn began to eat him, the careful repetitions of a smith testing how long the iron would take the heat before he committed it to the quench.

He did not stabilize the dragon-fusion in a week. Only a fool would have claimed that; the deep-water joining would take months, maybe years, before it held without cost. But the door that had opened only at the death-edge now opened on a dawn ridge for the asking, and he could hold the dragon's edge of it for the count of a slow breath where before it had scalded him immediately. That was the shape of true progress: not the crossing announced in a moment, but the seam widening a hair each day until, one day, you did not have to force it. The hawk's plumage was a season deeper in the cold thread of its long mutation, and he left the ridge that week no higher in rank than he'd come up it, and a great deal more the master of the strength he already had.

## Part 4: The Others Are Not Frozen

He was not the only one at the grindstone. The whole five were being put to it, because Shrek did not let a working student rest, and because Wulin led by the simple method of being the hardest-working boy in any room he entered.

Wulin was in the foundry every afternoon without fail, his two carved-out hours of forging, and every spare second cultivating — the third ring, rank thirty, the gate Mu Chen had told him he had to reach before he so much as thought about spirit-refining again. He hadn't broken through. What he had done was put on the invisible, honest swell of soul power that preceded a breakthrough and was always mistaken, by anyone not watching, for standing still. Mu Chen himself was set up in Shrek now, two hammers and a hard eye, and he worked Wulin's strokes the way he had in Eastsea, and when the boy's forty-nine strikes ended in a clean refinement the new master's face did a thing Lin Hao recognized — the same wonder he had never quite managed to hide when a child handed back the impossible.

Xie Xie, ordered into the mecha-makers' association, spent his evenings swearing at his own clumsy hands and came back the next evening anyway. Xu Xiaoyan and Gu Yue bent over designs at the library tables, the two mecha designers, Gu Yue a rank ahead and pulling the other two toward her; the second profession was no longer a footnote for any of them, because the academy — and the math of battle armour — did not let it be one. Lin Hao worked at their side, refining practice blades at cost, showing the association smiths the trueing trick for the dragging sixth gate he'd shown the exam hall; a tool not used for the people around him was a tool left in the drawer.

It was into the middle of this ordinary, grinding week that Shen Yi walked into the lecture hall, with Elder Cai absent as she always was and Wu Zhangkong a silent cold column at the wall, and turned every head in the room.

Seven positions in the class. One president, two vice-presidents, four profession-council seats, all of them settled in a single battle on the practice field — survive to the end, and the last standing took the president's chair. The points were real, the inner-court advantage was real, and as Shen Yi listed the monthly stipend — a thousand contribution points to the president — she swept the room of assembled monsters with a calm eye and began with the easy one. "First," she said, "I'll declare Tang Wulin the blacksmith representative. That seat doesn't get contested." When the enormous slit-eyed boy in the back stood to object — Yang Nianxia, a third-rank smith himself who felt himself the obvious choice — she gave him the flat answer that settled the room: no one competed against a fourth-rank blacksmith who was not yet fifteen. Wulin, who had omitted mentioning the fifth rank on the sensible grounds that no one would believe it, sat down looking modest, and did not, Lin Hao noted, correct her.

The rest of it would be decided on the field. Fifteen minutes to choose allies. Survive; or touch the ground with four limbs or a torso and be eliminated.

The fifteen minutes were, in their way, more instructive than the battle that would follow. Shen Yi gave them the room and the clock and stood back, because at Shrek the test had already begun before anyone reached the field. The lecture hall erupted into the particular scrambling of talented children who had just learned that there were three seats worth having and a field's worth of bodies to be eliminated for them. Strangers became best friends in under a minute; control-type soul masters who had never spoken to anyone were suddenly very popular; one nervous boy announced to no one in particular that he had no wish to be president and would happily carry a strong team's water, which got him three offers at once.

Wulin caught the moment early and bent his head to his own people. "Xie Xie, Xiaoyan — you two go make another group," he said, low enough that only the five of them caught it, and when Xie Xie made a wounded face Wulin shook his head once. "We've been seated together since day one. We're already a clique to them, and now I'm the declared blacksmith rep with every eye on me. If we all stand together we draw every team in the field at once. Split. Be four problems instead of one." He didn't turn to Gu Yue; he didn't have to. She had already chosen her place half a step behind his shoulder, and telling her to go make allies elsewhere would have been the kind of order Wulin was wise enough never to give.

Lin Hao watched the arithmetic settle and said nothing, because the arithmetic was Wulin's particular genius and not his to improve. Xie Xie and Xu Xiaoyan let themselves be gathered up by two different scrambling teams with practised ease; Gu Yue stayed with Wulin; Lin Hao found himself, a few minutes later, calmly attached to a third group of nervous classmates who had correctly counted him as one of the quiet strong ones and therefore worth standing near. None of them knew what they were actually getting, which was, he reflected, exactly how he preferred it. He would matter on the field, when the field came, the way he mattered at everything — without a word wasted, from the inside of the crowd.

The fifth seat, in the end, suited him. The loud seats went to the loud people. He had spent the week making the ground under his own feet flawless; the field in two days would show whether flawless, at the modest tier a first-year was allowed to show, was enough to be the last team standing.

Lin Hao, in his fifth seat, said nothing. He was a fifth-rank smith himself, and the blacksmith seat could as easily have been his; but a declared representative sat in the light with the whole class reading him, and he had spent the week making himself quietly, completely master of the tier he held. He let the loud seat belong to Wulin, where it was earned and where it didn't cost him anything he wasn't prepared to spend, and he turned his attention to the field coming in two days' time — a tournament of thirteen-year-olds, fought among peers, where the grade of the man-and-sword-as-one he had just spent the week sharpening would be more than enough to matter, and where there was no reason, and no excuse, to reach for anything beyond the shore he stood on.

## Part 5: The Measure of a Week

That night before the field he sat a last quiet dawn in the workshop and took stock the way a smith measured a finished piece — not by its shine, but by its grain.

A week before he had come down from a black-class cull having glimpsed, once, the true depth the sword could go. He had spent the week correctly. He had not chased the depth. He had made the ground under his feet flawless: every stroke truer, the frost a tool and not an emergency, the fusion a door that opened on command, the spiritual sea an honest inch deeper, the ledger fed by real work. He was not a step closer to the far shore in the way a man is not a mile closer to a mountain until he has walked the miles in between — but he was no longer wasting the step he was on, and that was the whole secret of every strong person he had ever met, from Mu Chen to Wu Zhangkong to the Saint Blacksmith four flights up.

The numbers of him told the truth to anything that could read them. His spiritual power had climbed a real, earned increment under the steady Method — still well within the Spirit Sea, nowhere near any far gate, but up where a week of serious work ought to put him. The hawk had climbed with him as the fusion came on call. His ledger had grown, point by point, from the honest refining he had gone back to. And the things no instrument measured — the frost that didn't bite him, the fusion that answered, the third form without a decision — those were the week's true product, built quietly below the surface of any rank-up, the way a foundation was laid where no one cheered.

He set the Stormbringer across his knees and the steel sat quiet and familiar, an extension of the hand, which was exactly what a sword was supposed to be at the tier he owned. Outside, the academy slept. In two days the field opened on a tournament of children, and the whole class would be watching to see who stood last. He had not reached for a single thing beyond his station. He had simply, at last, become flawless at it.

On a field of geniuses, he suspected that would be plenty.

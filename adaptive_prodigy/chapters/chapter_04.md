## Part 1: The Year of the Sword and the Hammer

The year after the Spirit Pagoda was the year the two boys stopped being children who trained, and started being cultivators who lived it.

For Lin Hao, the year measured itself in dawns. Master Chen Long's courtyard, cold stone underfoot, the old sword master's breath fogging in the early dark.

"Again."

Lin Hao reset his stance, the Stormbringer Sword steady in his grip. His arms ached. They ached every morning, and every morning the ache was a little less than the day before — his body rewriting itself overnight to meet the demand, the way it always did.

"What did you change?" Chen Long asked.

"The last form. My weight was forward on the transition. I'm carrying it back now, through the hips."

Chen Long grunted. Two months ago, the answer would have been *I don't know, it just felt wrong.* Now the boy named his own flaws before the correction was given. The old man had stopped testing him for it.

"You're rank nineteen now," Chen Long said one morning, watching the wind curl along his student's blade like it belonged there. "Twenty before the snow. Do you know what that means?"

"That the climb gets steeper?"

"It means the climb *changes*." Chen Long sheathed his own practice blade. "Every rank past ten is worth three of the ones below it, and the ones past twenty are worth three of those. And there's the other matter — that hawk of yours. A hundred-year soul doesn't pour itself into one ring, boy. When you hit the wall at twenty, it will have a gift waiting on the other side." The old man's eyes crinkled. "Talent is a horse. Discipline is the road. The horse goes nowhere without the road."

"Yes, Master."

The wall came in the first week of winter, exactly where the old man promised — rank twenty, soul power piled against a ceiling it could not pass. Lin Hao sat with it the way he sat with everything: breathing, letting his Awareness take the measure of the obstacle. And on the third night, the Gale Hawk — which had grown fat on a year and a half of fed-back cultivation, seven hundred years of condensed wind riding quietly inside him — stirred, unfolded, and laid a **second ring of yellow light** around his soul like a promise keeping itself.

Rank twenty-one. Then twenty-two before the thaw. A Soul Grandmaster at ten years old, two rings from a single spirit soul, and his spiritual power pressing up past the far shore of Spirit Origin into territory the textbooks said belonged to grown men.

"You'll carry all of that to Eastsea," Chen Long said at their last lesson, "and you'll meet boys and girls whose families have been feeding them spirit resources since the cradle. Find out what the road is made of." A pause. "I expect a letter every month. If your forms get sloppy, I'll know."

Across the city, Wulin's year measured itself in heat.

Mang Tian's forge, the hammer rising and falling, the ring of steel from dawn to dusk. The Thousand Refinements — the old blacksmith's word for the work that turned iron into something worthy of a soul master's hands. Wulin's arms had changed; the thin boy from the awakening ceremony was gone, replaced by corded muscle built one hammer strike at a time. His appetite had grown to legendary proportions — the "Rice Bucket" nickname had followed him out of the ordinary class and somehow become, over the years, a term of grudging respect.

Two streets over, the Lin family's rice bin emptied at a rate that defied its single teenager. Lin Hao ate like his training burned — which was exactly the case; something inside him rebuilt itself a little more every night, and rebuilding was hungry work. Lin Mei had stopped counting meals and started counting pots. "Two blacksmiths in this house," she told the neighbors, "and one of them is a furnace with legs." Lin Hao, mouth full, would only grin. He'd noticed the hunger himself — noticed that on the weeks the family money ran thin and the bowls got shallower, his meditation crawled. Whatever his talent was, it ate first and optimized second. So he never complained about the food, and he never skipped a meal, and Mang Tian's workshop — where the boys' paths crossed most evenings — fed him with the resigned generosity of a man already feeding one bottomless pit.

His cultivation had changed too. The first seal's power hummed under everything — a furnace banked low, warming his meridians while the spiral technique carried soul power through them in slow, golden-edged turns. Rank eleven had become twelve, then thirteen, and each rank had come slower than the last, the way his father's forge work came: not by force, but by repetition.

Some nights, deep in meditation, he felt the second seal — a knot of sealed fire somewhere behind his sternum, pulling at its chains.

*Not yet,* Old Tang's voice would come, gentle as always. *A vessel that fills too fast cracks. You're building the vessel, Wulin. Every hammer strike. Every turn of the spiral. Let it be slow.*

And Na'er — Na'er had grown too. Nine going on ten, taller, her silver hair long past her shoulders now, her quiet ways settling into the Tang household like a held note finally allowed to ring. She still followed Wulin everywhere. She still said little. But she had started leaving small things on the workbench at Mang Tian's workshop — a wrapped bun, a cup of warm water — for a boy who forgot to eat until his stomach roared.

By autumn's end, the letters came.

Eastsea Academy. Intermediate division. Both of them.

## Part 2: Silver

The strangeness began in the winter, and — this was the part Lin Hao never stopped turning over, afterward — he had noticed it first. Not because he was watching. Because his body always noticed.

It was small things. Na'er standing at the end of the street after dusk, face turned toward the sea, so still that the snow gathered on her shoulders. Once, murmuring in her sleep — not words, exactly; a sound like a language made of bells, beautiful and wrong. Once, a stray dog that had snarled at every child on the street took one look at her and lay down in the slush, whining, on its belly.

Wulin noticed too, the way you notice weather. "She's dreaming about home," he said once. "Wherever it is. She'll remember someday."

He said it warmly. He had always wanted her to remember.

She remembered in the last week before departure.

Lin Hao was at his own house, packing, when the shouting started. He was across the street before his mother could say his name — and found Wulin in the middle of the Tang family's front room, turning in slow circles, as if the room might have somewhere to hide a person.

The window was open. The winter air stood in the room like floodwater. On the sill, where the latch should have been frost-bitten shut, the metal was warm.

"Na'er?" Wulin said, to the room, to the street, to the sky. "Na'er!"

They searched until past midnight — every alley, the market, the harbor wall, the shrine road, the clearing behind the Lin house where the three of them had watched a thousand sunsets. Lang Yue searched with them, calling until her voice cracked. Tang Ziran went to the authorities, and the authorities, who kept careful records of every child with soul power in the city, found no record of a silver-haired girl leaving by any gate, any road, any train.

Because there had never been any record of her at all.

Toward dawn, sitting on the curb outside the empty house while Wulin stared at nothing, Lin Hao let himself feel what he had been refusing to feel all night. His head had a habit he had never entirely made peace with: it remembered everything, and it compared everything, and it did both without being asked. And what it laid out now, quietly, in the dark, like evidence on a table, was this —

In four years, his perception had never once read Na'er as *only* a girl.

There had always been something under the smallness. A depth his senses went quiet beside, the way a room goes quiet beside a sleeping predator. He had told himself it was mystery. Everyone has one.

It was not mystery. It was the surface of something vast, held carefully still — the way the sea is still, over things.

*What are you,* he thought, *and who took you home?*

He said none of it. Not then. Not for a long time. Because Wulin was sitting beside him with his hands around a cup of tea he wasn't drinking, and grief was the only thing on the table that belonged to anyone.

"I'll find her," Wulin said, when the sky went gray. His voice wasn't loud. It had the flat, forged quality of hot metal laid on an anvil. "I don't know where she went. I don't know who she was. But I'll get strong enough to look everywhere, and I'll find her."

Lin Hao looked at his friend — at the jaw, the hands, the terrible calm — and felt the old protective knot in his chest pull tight, and then, strangely, settle. There was nothing to protect Wulin from here. This was a fire Wulin needed.

"Okay," Lin Hao said. "Then we'd better not miss the train."

## Part 3: The Soul Train

They left Glorybound City on a morning so cold the harbor steam stood in pillars.

And neither of them said the thing that was sitting on the platform between the two families, which was that one boy had a sword that made the Spirit Master's eyebrows go up, and the other had grass.

Lin Wei had looked at his son's blade on the testing floor and had not said anything for a long time, because a man who makes horseshoes does not have a word for what his son had been given. And Wulin's father had looked at a handful of grass and had said *that's all right, son, that's all right,* in the voice of a man who had already worked out what he was going to do about it.

**And Lin Hao had looked at both of them, and had chosen the worst class, and had not told anybody why.**

That is the butterfly, and it is the first one, and everything else in this story grows out of it. **In a story without him, Wulin goes to the worst class because the worst class is where a boy with grass goes, and the worst class stays the worst class, and the grade learns nothing.** In a story with him in it, the worst class has a boy in it with a sword that made a Spirit Master's eyebrows go up, and the worst class stops being the worst class within a month, and the grade learns a thing it would not otherwise have had any reason to learn.

He did not choose it for Wulin. He would have said that if anybody had asked, and it would have been a lie, and he knew it was a lie, and he chose it anyway, and he has never once explained it to anybody, and he does not intend to.

Both families came to the platform. Lin Mei cried and made Lin Hao promise, again, to write every month. Lin Wei said nothing, only gripped his son's shoulder once, hard, in the way of men who had already said everything important the night before, over a sword. Tang Ziran pressed a letter into Wulin's hand — thick, sealed, addressed in Mang Tian's blocky writing.

"Master Mang's own hand," Tang Ziran said. "His workshop in Eastsea City, and the Blacksmith's Association besides. He says: *the boy eats like a furnace, so someone had better keep him employed.*"

Wulin managed a smile that didn't reach the rest of his face. Behind his father, Lang Yue was looking at the empty space beside them — the small space, exactly Na'er-shaped, that no one had mentioned all morning and everyone had carried to the station.

The soul train drank its charged soul stones and pulled the world flat. Countryside became streaks of white and gray; Wulin's forehead found the window and stayed there. Lin Hao, across the aisle, closed his eyes and let his Awareness settle into the train — the rhythm of the rails was not noise to his body; it was information, a pulse to breathe against. He slowed his circulation to match, and his meditation deepened past anything the courtyard had given him. He filed the trick away. *Any repeated rhythm is a resource.*

He opened his eyes to find Wulin watching him.

"You're doing it again," Wulin said. "The face. Where you look asleep but you're taking everything apart."

Lin Hao opened one amber eye. "I resemble that remark." A pause. "What are you taking apart?"

Wulin looked back out at the white country. "How strong I have to be," he said, "to look everywhere."

Eastsea City arrived the way oceans do — gradually, then all at once. Flat country wrinkled into hills, hills into suburbs, suburbs into a skyline of glass and stone threaded with soul-powered transit lines. Wulin had thought Glorybound City large. Eastsea City made Glorybound City a neighborhood.

It was the **second largest seaside city in the Sun-Moon Federation**, and everything about it followed from that — the port first, a nexus for the shipping lanes and for whatever came up out of the ocean, and then three million people arranged around it.

Lin Hao had read the shape of the country on the train, in a free pamphlet he had taken because it was free. **Eighteen first-class cities, in five regions** — north, east, south, west and centre. The centre had only two, **Heaven Dou City and Shrek City**, and the pamphlet said this was fewer than the others and then spent two paragraphs explaining that it did not matter, which Lin Hao took to mean it mattered a great deal. The east had **five first-class coastal cities that made up something called the Skysea Alliance**, and Eastsea City was **second of the five**.

"Second," Wulin said, when Lin Hao told him.

"Second of five. In the east."

"That's still second."

"It's second of five *first-class* cities in a region, in a federation of eighteen," Lin Hao said. "Which is a sentence I did not know existed this morning." He put the pamphlet in his bag, because he kept things. "There's a tournament. Every three years — the Alliance one. And a bigger one every five, for the whole continent."

"Where's the big one held?"

"**Shrek City.**"

Neither of them said anything after that for a while, because they were ten, and Glorybound City had been the whole world until Tuesday, and the word *Shrek* had arrived in the middle of a free pamphlet next to an advertisement for soul-lamp repairs. And old. Older than the glass. For several hundred years the Federation had taken particular care of the ancient buildings here, so the skyline was a city of towers with a much older, plainer, unadorned city still standing intact between their feet — and the soul trains, when they came in, were nearly all dark blue, every one of them, which was the sort of detail Lin Hao noticed and would have been unable to explain.

"Look at the size of it," Wulin whispered.

Lin Hao, who was gawking the hardest, didn't even deny it.

## Part 4: Reporting In

There was a sign at the end of the platform — white letters on blue, **EASTSEA ACADEMY** — and a chair behind it, and a row of seventeen-year-olds in blue sportswear who had been given this job as a punishment and had decided to enjoy it.

The girl who stood up when she saw them had black hair and a pair of red phoenix eyes and the moderate, unhurried build of somebody who was not in a hurry and did not need to be. She had the sort of face that made people feel they had arrived somewhere.

"Little brother," she said, "are you here to report?"

"Yes," said Wulin. "I'm Tang Wulin."

She looked at him, and then looked again, in the particular way Lin Hao had already learned to recognise — the second look was the one that meant somebody had worked something out and was deciding how polite to be about it.

"I'm **Liu Yuxin**," she said. "First grader, class one of the **advanced** academy. I'm receiving new students this year, which means I'm your senior sister whether you like it or not." She held out a clipboard. "Fill this in. Then show me the recommendation letter from your elementary academy."

Wulin dug it out of his rucksack, where it had been folded three times by his mother and pressed flat again by his father.

Liu Yuxin read the form aloud as he wrote, which Lin Hao suspected she did for everyone and which nonetheless made Wulin's ears go red. "Tang Wulin. Ten years old. Graduated from Glorybound City's Red Mountain Elementary Academy." Her voice changed register on the next line, only slightly, the way a good clerk's does. "**Rank thirteen. Plant system. Martial soul: Bluesilver Grass.**"

She looked up.

"You know what that is?" said Wulin, flatly.

"Of course I know what it is." She said it briskly, without pity, which was the kindest thing anybody had done for him all week. "Our academy recognises it. Soul Masters have been developing for tens of thousands of years — the martial soul isn't nearly as important as it used to be. And to reach rank thirteen at your age with Bluesilver Grass, honestly, that isn't easy at all."

Wulin, who had braced himself, found he had braced against nothing.

Then Lin Hao stepped up and put his own form on the clipboard.

Liu Yuxin read it.

She did not say anything for a moment, which with her was more informative than anything she might have said.

"Rank twenty-two," she said. "*Soul Grandmaster.* Two rings." She turned the page to the spirit-soul assessment and her eyebrows came together. "One spirit soul. Gale Hawk, wind attribute, purchased at ten-year classification — and this gauge reads its cultivation at somewhere near **seven hundred years**." She set the clipboard down on the chair, carefully, the way you set down something you have decided not to be holding. "That gauge is not wrong, is it."

"The gauge is fine," said Lin Hao.

"You bought a ten-year hawk and it is seven hundred years old."

"I bought a ten-year hawk," said Lin Hao, "and then a great deal happened to it, and I'd rather not go into it on a platform, because there's a man in a black suit eleven metres away who has been pretending to read a timetable for four minutes."

Liu Yuxin's eyes went to the man in the black suit. Then back.

Then she laughed — a short, surprised sound — and picked up the clipboard again.

"Right," she said. "Well. You'll want to know how this works, because you're both going to be disappointed. **Classes are assigned at the opening ceremony, tomorrow, by the Director.** Not by me, not by that form, not by anything you can argue with. Martial soul quality and soul power. There are five classes and the smaller the number the better the class, and there are a hundred and eight of you this year."

"One request," said Lin Hao. "Whatever class he's in, I'm in it."

"That's not how assignment works."

"I understand. I'm making the request anyway, so that it's written down." He smiled at her, and Liu Yuxin — who was eleven months his senior, a first grader in class one of the advanced academy, and entirely capable of handling a ten-year-old — had the distinct and unfamiliar sensation of being *managed*. "You'll pass the forms up. Somebody will read them. And when they get to that line they'll wonder whether the boy who wrote it meant it."

"Did you mean it?"

"Completely."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she stamped both forms, took two brass keys out of a box, and handed them over with the metal placard that let them through the gate.

"**Dormitory is behind the school building. Twelve floors. You're in room two-oh-five, second floor.**" She glanced at the key tag. "Four to a room, two bunk beds. You'll find out who the other two are when you get there, and my advice, as your senior sister, is to be extremely pleasant to them for the first forty-eight hours and then reassess."

"Why forty-eight hours?"

"Because that's how long it takes."

---

They found the dormitory without difficulty, because it was nearly as tall as the school building and there were eleven-year-olds going in and out of it carrying suitcases.

**Room 205, second floor.** The door was already open and there was already somebody inside.

Two bunk beds, enough for four. Two square desks. Four chairs. Two cabinets. One roof lamp. That was the whole of it.

The two bottom bunks were taken. The one on the left was occupied by a boy who was taller than Wulin by half a head and thicker than him by more than that, with short hair and eyes that bulged slightly and, at ten years old, an air of ferocity he was clearly working at. The one on the right held a thin, frail-looking boy wearing glasses and holding a book, who looked up with the mild interest of somebody who had already worked out how this was going to go.

"Hello," said Wulin. "I'm Tang Wulin. I've just arrived."

The frail one nodded. "**Yun Xiao.** The Yun from cloud. The Xiao from big and small."

The tall one rolled his eyes.

"Newcomer," he said. "Clean the room first."

Lin Hao, who had been watching this with great interest from the doorway, put his bag down and said, "Before we start — is this the bit where you tell us you're in charge, or have you already done that?"

"**Zhou Zhangxi,**" said the tall boy, and grinned, and got off the bed.

## Part 5: The Worst Class

The opening ceremony filled Eastsea Academy's great plaza — the only day of the year the intermediate and advanced divisions stood on the same stones. Wulin, tall for his age, was pushed toward the back by the sheer mass of students, which suited him; from the back he could watch all of it, the way you watch a forge you don't yet understand.

Director Long Hengxu spoke from the high platform — a broad man with a voice trained to reach the last row without soul power, welcoming the one hundred and eight new students of the intermediate first grade, congratulating them, reminding them that Eastsea Academy had produced Titled Douluo, that excellence here was expected and mediocrity was merely tolerated.

"And now," he said, "class assignment."

A rustle went through the first-graders. Beside Wulin, Lin Hao murmured, "Here we go."

"It is known within Eastsea Academy that the smaller the class number, the more prestigious the class," Long Hengxu announced. "One hundred and eight students, divided among five classes. Those called first are assigned to class one…"

Names rose and fell like tide. The front of the plaza filled with the chosen — children in fine coats, children whose martial souls had glowed on the assessment platforms, children whose families had clearly been feeding them spirit resources since the cradle, exactly as Chen Long had promised. Wulin watched his own name's absence stretch, class by class, and felt the old familiar weight settling onto his shoulders — the weight he had carried since the testing stone at six years old.

It landed. It didn't crush. Three years of hammer work had changed what he was made of.

The names rose and fell like tide — and Lin Hao's came early, where the silk names were.

"…class one: Mu Tiancheng, Wei Lili, *Lin Hao*…"

He heard it the way you hear a stone skip off water. Around him, a few first-graders turned — the reporting-in clerk's gossip had traveled, apparently; the rank-twenty-two boy with the two rings was not exactly anonymous — and he felt the whole bright front of the plaza tilt toward him expectantly, a hundred silk jackets making room.

Lin Hao did not move toward them.

"Director," he said. Not loudly. He simply said it in the direction of the high platform, the way you'd continue a conversation — and the plaza's acoustics, built for announcements, carried it everywhere. "Lin Hao. I'd like to be in class five."

The silence that followed had texture. Director Long Hengxu looked down from the platform at the small, neat boy standing still in the middle of the tide, and something in his expression suggested that Liu Yuxin's paperwork had, in fact, mentioned this. "You understand," the director said, in his carrying voice, "that class five is the last class."

"Yes, Director."

"It is the worst class in the grade."

"I've heard that too," Lin Hao said. "I'd still like to be in it. My brother's in it."

He did not point. He did not have to. At the back of the plaza, in the smallest and quietest knot on the whole stone, Tang Wulin had gone very still — and beside him, a pale-haired boy in fine clothes was staring at Lin Hao with an expression like a held breath.

Director Long Hengxu regarded him for three long seconds. "…Noted," he said at last, and there was something in his tone that might have been the ghost of amusement, or the ghost of a warning. "Class five. The *whole* of it, young man — the walking and the teaching both. Continue."

"…class five: Tang Wulin, Zhou Zhangxi, Xie Xie, Yun Xiao, *and Lin Hao*…"

Five names, at the very end. The last class. The class at the back of the plaza, twenty-odd students with listless expressions, the smallest and quietest knot on the whole stone — which was quiet for an entirely different reason now, because the whole plaza had watched the rank-twenty-two boy choose to walk to the back of it, and knots, even listless ones, remember things like that.

Wulin stepped out of the crowd toward them. Halfway there, a boy fell into step beside him — pale hair, fine clothes, a face as striking as his own but worn differently, like a blade someone kept polished for display. The boy's eyes flicked over Wulin's plain coat, his patched sleeve cuffs, and stopped, briefly, on the calluses on his hands — forge calluses, which were not the same as any other kind.

Xie Xie said nothing. But when they reached the back, he chose the spot next to Wulin, and stood there with his chin up and his shoulders squared, as if daring the plaza to have opinions.

"Class teachers," Long Hengxu called, "receive your students."

Five teachers descended from the platform. Four were middle-aged and unremarkable. The fifth, heading for class five, made the listless students sit up straighter.

He was young — twenty-seven, twenty-eight at most — and tall, a full head and more above everyone near him, over a hundred and ninety centimeters of lanky arms and thin waist, white trousers accentuating legs that went on unreasonably. His hair was swept back from a face that was simply, inconveniently *handsome*: well-set nose, thin lips, bright eyes of a smoky green that matched — matched — the strange blue of his hair. His expression was a closed door.

One of the girls in class five breathed, too quietly to mean to, "The teacher is so handsome…"

The teacher's icy gaze swept the class, and the whisper died mid-flight. He turned and walked. Class five trailed after him, to the ground floor, to the innermost corner of the building, to a classroom that took longer to reach than any other — thirty desks in a room built for thirty, which told its own story about how the class had been sized.

"Take your seats." Every word came out cold. "My name is Wu Zhangkong. For the next six years, you will be under my tutelage." He let the silence ring. "And I would emphasize one point. You may disregard whatever Director Long said at the ceremony. Even if you are a bunch of trash, I will train you to be the strongest students in your cohort. That is your aim for the next six years. Unless you choose to drop out."

It should have been an insult. Wulin, listening, heard something else entirely — the same thing the hammer hears from the anvil: *something here intends to hit back.*

"Introductions," Wu Zhangkong said. "Name. Martial soul. Soul power rank. Aspiration."

The class went around, voices small. Zhou Zhangxi, shield soul, rank nine. Yun Xiao, rank ten, aspiration mumbled. Then the pale boy beside Wulin stood, and the temperature of the room changed.

"Xie Xie. Light Dragon Dagger — tool soul, speed type. Rank twenty — one soul ring, second coming this year." He sat down as if the stunned silence were furniture he owned. Rank twenty. In the worst class. Someone whispered the obvious question — *then why is he here?* — and Xie Xie's smile answered that whatever story lay behind it, he did not intend to tell it.

"Tang Wulin."

Wulin stood. He felt the whole class turn — not at his name, but at the grass. It had the reputation it had; four years had not improved it.

"Tang Wulin. Martial soul: Bluesilver Grass. Rank thirteen. One soul ring."

The murmur was immediate and not kind. *Bluesilver Grass — here? — with a ring? — someone's counting wrong—* A boy two rows over laughed outright. "Trash spirit. What's it doing past the gate?"

The laughter did not get the chance to spread.

"There are no trash martial souls in this world." Wu Zhangkong's voice was not loud. The classroom went dead anyway. The icy green eyes moved across the class, touching each face like a blade checking an edge, resting last on the boy who had laughed. "There are only trash people. Consider your own morals before calling others trash — if you aren't trash yourself, would you have landed in class five?"

Nobody moved. The boy who had laughed turned the color of old ash.

Wulin stood very still. Something hot and unfamiliar pushed up behind his ribs — he had spent four years being defended, by a friend, by a father, by a nickname that turned into respect. This was the first time a stranger had stood between him and the word, and the stranger was the coldest man in the room.

"Your aspiration," Wu Zhangkong said to him, flatly. Not unkindly. "You paused."

Wulin's hand closed, under the desk, around nothing — around the memory of a small hand that used to fit in it. For one heartbeat the answer stood right behind his teeth: *to find someone.*

"I don't have one yet," he said.

Wu Zhangkong looked at him — a long, flat look that seemed to read the lie and file it without comment. "Then find one," he said. "Next."

"Lin Hao."

Lin Hao stood, and the class's attention swung with the obvious pleasure of an audience given better seats.

"Lin Hao. Martial soul: Stormbringer Sword — wind and lightning. Rank twenty-two. Two soul rings."

The classroom's silence was a different species now. Rank twenty had been a held breath. Rank twenty-two — a Soul Grandmaster's standing, two rings, in a first-year's body — was arithmetic none of them could complete, and none of the answers put him here.

Wu Zhangkong's expression did not change by a single degree. "Rank twenty-two. Two rings. A soul like that." The green eyes narrowed, fractionally. "Why are you in my class?"

"Because he's in it," Lin Hao said, and nodded at Wulin. "I asked at registration. The director said a straight request deserved a straight answer, and the answer was yes."

Two rows over, Xie Xie's polished composure cracked into open staring. Wu Zhangkong looked at Lin Hao for three full seconds — the first genuine interest anyone had seen on that face — and said, "Sentiment is a poor cultivation method."

"It's not a method," Lin Hao agreed cheerfully. "It's a promise."

"Sit down," Wu Zhangkong said. But the corner of the closed-door mouth had moved. It might have been nothing. It was not nothing.

## Part 6: The Roof

That night, past curfew, the two of them sat on the roof of dormitory three, above the eastern training ground. Eastsea City glowed below in avenues of amber soul-light, and the harbor wind came in cold off the water, carrying salt — almost, from far enough away, like home.

Wulin unfolded Mang Tian's letter for the fourth time, though he had memorized it on the train. The Blacksmith's Association. The workshop. The note in the old man's blocky hand: *show this to Mu Chen. He is an eight-star Saint Craftsman and the finest smith this side of Heaven Dou City. He owes me a drink, which makes you his problem now.*

"Chimneys are over there," Lin Hao said, pointing down the hill toward the harbor end of the city, where a cluster of squat roofs breathed thin fire into the night, working late. "First thing tomorrow?"

"First thing," Wulin said.

Lin Hao leaned back on his hands, letting his eyes close — and immediately, the way it always did, his awareness went to work on the night. The harbor bells. The lamp-lines humming. Wulin's breathing beside him.

And under Wulin's breathing, something else.

It had been there all year — a density in his friend's soul power, like a river running higher behind a dam. But tonight, with grief and a promise and a rank-thirteen body full of forge-built strength, the water was different. Tighter. The second seal — the knot behind the sternum that Wulin had described to him in fragments — sat in that river like a stone, and the river had risen another finger's width since spring.

*It's coming,* Lin Hao thought. *Not this month. But this year.*

He said nothing. Some stones you did not point at. You just stayed close when the water took them — the way you stayed in the worst class in the academy, because that was where your brother was.

"You know what Na'er would say?" Wulin asked suddenly, at the city. "If she could see us now. Two trash from Glorybound City, sitting on a roof in Eastsea."

Lin Hao considered it. "She'd say: *be careful.*"

Wulin's laugh was short and wet and real. "Yeah. She would." He folded the letter carefully, along its old creases, and put it back inside his coat — over the ring, against the ribs, where important things went. Below them, in the dormitory corridor, room eleven's door stood open a careful inch, the way it had stood every night at home, in case a small silver-haired girl came back from a nightmare.

Wulin had not closed it.

Lin Hao looked at the open door, and at the Blacksmith's Association chimneys breathing fire in the dark, and at his friend's face turned toward a city full of places to look.

"First thing tomorrow," he said.

"First thing," Wulin agreed.

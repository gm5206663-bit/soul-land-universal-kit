## Part 1: The Gate Opens

It took the metal a year and four months to say yes, and it said it on a Tuesday, which Lin Hao considered typical of metal.

He had been carrying his own spirit-refined piece since spring — a chisel-length bar of closed grain, the twelfth refining asked and answered clean. On a Tuesday morning in the second winter Mu Chen turned it in the light for a long time, set it down, and said, "Saturday."

"Saturday what?"

"The examination is on Saturday. You will take it on Saturday, because the metal told me you will, and I am done being careful about it."

He passed. There is not much more to say about the passing, and Mu Chen said less than that. He took the fifth-rank stamp down from the shelf where it had sat for eleven years waiting to matter, and pressed the book. "Master Craftsman," he said. Then he ruined the ceremony in the way only he could, by being practical: "The examination was the door. The craft is the room. You are standing in the doorway of the room."

"Where do I stand in the room?"

"Nowhere, yet. Fifth rank is the licence to be bad at spirit refinement for years. That is what a licence is for."

Five years old when he started at the bench. Ten when the road was named. Eleven, almost twelve, when the stamp came down. Second-youngest Master Craftsman in the Branch's recorded ledgers. He wrote *second-youngest* in the book himself, because the record was somebody else's, and records were things you respected at exactly arm's length.

Wulin found out at supper and stood up and could not be gotten to sit down for a while.

## Part 2: What the Others Did With Their Winters

The Tang Sect took Xie Xie first.

He went in the ordinary way, the year he turned eleven — outer sect, associate, a screen and a mission list. The mecha bay's Saturdays had a ceiling and he had hit it. And the Tang Sect, he discovered, *made parts*. Not machines. Parts. The class-four catalogue had a mission that paid two hundred points for a month of filings and dimensional checks on actuator stock. Xie Xie — who had held a lamp over an open shoulder for two hours at ten and never recovered — took that mission, and every mission like it.

"Design, machining, and the licence," he said, in the spring of the first winter, at the yard. "The Sect has all three under one roof. It's disgraceful. I'm never leaving."

Xu Xiaoyan came in a year after them. Middle test, passed, inducted. Then, because her Starwheel Ice Staff was what it was, she went up the associate classes faster than any of them — class five, class four, **class three**. The Sect's clerks learned to stop being surprised by the small girl who read payout tables the way other children read dessert menus. By the third winter she was cultivating more than she was missioning. Rank **29**, peak, and holding, and the holding was on purpose: rank 30 was a door, and doors got chosen, not bumped.

Wulin ran two ledgers.

Everyone knew about the first one — the Association's, the commissions, the money, the tin. The second one lived on a Tang Sect screen and paid in points. Missions came back as metal went out; that was the whole arrangement of his life, and had been since Mang Tian's lane. In the third winter the screen's electronic voice said, over the counter, in front of everybody:

*"Three pieces of first-grade thousand-refined metal: six hundred points. Available contribution points: eight thousand four hundred and sixty-three."*

And Wulin, thirteen years old, stood at the screen and spent it nearly all. **Thousand-year Land Dragon Tendon — 3,900.** Legendary spirit medicine, refines the body, no side effects. **Thousand-year Sea Dragon Marrow — 4,500.** The same, and the qi and blood besides. He had the **Dragonscale Fruit** already — bought in Heaven Dou City three years before, on the trip with Wu Zhangkong, wrapped and kept. With the tendon and the marrow beside it, he was one item short of the set.

One item: the **Azure-veined Vine**. Under sixteen, the missions that paid enough were forbidden him. The Sect did not let its fledglings die for points — it was practically the Sect's whole personality. So the vine could wait for an auction, and the auction could wait for him.

"What's the set for?" Xie Xie asked, on the way out.

"You'll see."

"It's the seal, isn't it. The second one."

"You'll see," Wulin said, and jogged, because it was in him to jog, and the electronic voice chose that moment to clear its throat, in the manner of electronic voices, and say:

*"Tang Wulin, congratulations. You have surpassed fifteen thousand total contribution points and will now be promoted to class five."*

Class five. Before his third ring. He stood very still in front of the screen with the receipt in his hand. Xie Xie watched him do it, and had the decency — learned at the bay, at eleven — to say nothing at all.

Gu Yue did not join the Tang Sect.

This had surprised exactly nobody who had ever shared a classroom with her, and it had surprised everybody anyway. The Sect took the others; she took the **Spirit Pagoda** — a membership, a floor pass, a silence. There was a version of these three years — the one where the tournament had gone the other way, where he had been carried off that platform hurt — in which there was a reason with a wound in it: a life force spent to mend him, and a gap that opened afterward and never fully closed. **Not this one. In this one there was no wound to spend.** Class zero had won its tournament with everyone standing, and so her road into the Pagoda was not a retreat from anyone — it was the dawn slots, kept, hour after hour at five-forty, until the morning she crossed five hundred points of spiritual power and the Spirit Sea opened under her like a floor giving way to a bigger floor.

A year later she passed seven hundred, and the machine that said it had to be reset by an attendant who had never reset one before.

She told nobody the number. The others knew it anyway; these things travel, in a class of five. The custom of the class became that nobody said it. Which is the closest thing to a crown that Eastsea Academy had.

## Part 3: What the Winters Taught Him

The rank was the slow line, because the rank was gated at the rings. Everything else moved, and most of it moved through the Saturdays and the yard.

Wu Zhangkong came to the yard on the first Saturday of the first winter with nothing in his hands, which was how he carried the important things, and said, "Sit. The Sect's method. You've earned the quiet version of it."

The Mysterious Heaven Method was the Tang Sect's own way of turning breath into territory. The first lesson was four hours long and contained eleven words, which was the Sect's idea of a generous introduction. It added nothing. It removed the waste — the leak every cultivator carries in the spine, the sigh at the top of the breath, the half of every cycle that goes to noise. The same gains for less noise, and then gains on the gains, and by the second winter his cultivation had a floor under it that had not been there before — stone where there had been boards. He could feel the difference at the bench within a month: the same hammer, the same metal, and more of him arriving in the strike.

The Purple Demon Eyes came next, and they came because he was a reader.

Dawn work, at the sea horizon, in the cold half of the year, when the light comes up out of the water like something being unbolted. The discipline was simple and cruel: face the first light and do not flinch, do not blink past the burn, come back the next morning, and do it again, for years. His eyes watered for most of the first winter. Then they stopped watering. Then — the second winter — the light stopped being light and started being information.

Wulin reached the second stage at thirteen, and the whole class knew it; it was a Tang Sect thing, honored, and Wulin had earned it at the pace the Sect expects of anyone. Lin Hao reached the second stage in the second winter and the third before the fourth ring, and told nobody, and the class knew it anyway.

What they changed was the reading. When he read now, a violet light stood in the gold of his eyes — you had to be told to look for it once, and after that nobody had to be told again. He had read grain like a smith. Now he read it like weather. The seam showed sooner, showed deeper, and showed in more things — metal first, then matches, then people: where a man's weight had already decided to go before the man announced it, where a bout carried a flaw the width of a hair. Wu Zhangkong watched him watch a bout for four minutes and said, to nobody, "That's the last time that's fair."

Xu Xiaoyan asked the hawk question in the second winter, because she was the one who kept the numbers.

"It's older again," she said. The hawk was on the fence rail, mantle deep, doing its impression of architecture. "It was nine hundred and something when we were ten. What is it now?"

"Older than that."

"Rings don't do that," she said. "You take a ring at a thousand years and it is a thousand years forever. It's fixed. It's a record."

"Records don't moult," Xie Xie said, from the fence, where he had been not-pretending not to listen. "That thing has moulted twice since spring."

Wu Zhangkong had come to the fence. He looked at the hawk for a while, the way he looked at anything that kept succeeding.

"Nobody has written the book on him," he said at last. "When someone does, it will be a short book, and most of it will be measurements." He turned back to the yard. "Keep your numbers, Xu Xiaoyan. They are the only part of this that anyone will believe later."

The hawk turned its head and considered Xu Xiaoyan with one eye that had a great deal in it.

"Write it down anyway," she told herself, and did.

So the hawk aged with him, and it showed the way weather shows. The grey-brown bird they had known at nine was gone into a hawk of deep storm-slate, the feathers carrying the lightning the sword had fed them since the hawk's nine-hundredth year, so that in some lights its wings held weather in them. Each winter it sat heavier on the rail. Each winter its pressure arrived earlier: the yard dogs stopped coming near the fence in the first winter; the journeymen stopped offering to spar under its flight line in the second; by the third, when it crossed the yard, bouts paused the way conversations pause for thunder. And the marks on his forearms deepened in step with it, the raised lines edging a shade further each winter — one ladder, climbed from two sides. Gu Yue watched the hawk the way she watched everything that kept growing: without comment, without blinking, and as if filing something.

The hammer art came from the Sect too, and it came for the smith, not the swordsman: the Disorder Splitting Wind Hammer, the inner sect's rhythm, forging and fighting in the same motion, a thing no book could teach because the book would lie about when to breathe. He learned it as the Sect teaches it, with a hammer and an audience of one, on Saturday mornings, until the rhythm stopped being a thing he kept and became a thing he was. It rearranged his benchwork inside a season — the same strikes he had made for six years, all of them suddenly in time with one another, and with him — and spirit refinement bent toward him after that, the way wood bends toward the year's light.

The first time the rhythm crossed over — the bench's cadence arriving whole inside a bout, strike and step and strike with no seam between the two skills — Wu Zhangkong said nothing at all. The next Saturday the yardwork was hammer forms for both of them, master and student, two hammers, one rhythm, and the frost never settled on that grass.

His own style grew its sixth stroke in the third winter, and it was born at the bench, on an afternoon he would keep for the rest of his life, out of the quiet scale's old lesson.

It was the nothing hour — the forge banked, the light going, the shop full of the tick of cooling metal — and he was not working, only standing. A bead of quench-water ran down the hammer's cheek and hung at the corner of the peen and would not fall. He watched it gather and not fall. Everything in the shop was loud: the tick, the sea, his own blood. The bead hung in the middle of all of it, being still.

He did not strike it. He understood, in a way that arrived finished, that every stroke he owned was a question — five kinds of asking, five winds before the blow — and that there was room in the style for a stroke that was not a question. A stroke that waited until the opponent had already answered. Answered what? Anything. A feint, a habit, a fear, a question nobody had asked. Everyone answers something. The stroke lived in the instant after the answering, and nowhere else.

The bead fell. He moved the hammer through the place where it had been, slow, and the air did not move at all.

Still Water. A stroke with no wind before it. He wrote it into the book without explanation, because he had none, and Mu Chen read the page and said, "That one isn't a sword's. Keep it anyway."

And the body was the headline, because the body had always been the truth of him. Adaptation goes to the body the way rain goes to ground. Seven years of hammer and anvil. The hawk's vitality, banked into his blood a year at a time. The Sect's method refining all of it. The Association measured him every midwinter — the same machines, the same clerk, the same small room with its one mirror, which nobody was supposed to use for anything and everybody used for everything.

He was ten the first time, then eleven, then twelve, then thirteen: fist 2,612 at ten; 2,940 at eleven; 3,215 at twelve; 3,436 at thirteen — under the dragon claw's famous number, and climbing, and the clerk initialed each line the way clerks initial weather. But it was the mirror that kept the true ledger. Each winter the boy in it was less boy. At eleven his shoulders had gone first, the smith's build arriving years before his voice had decided anything. At twelve the last of the round went out of his face and the jaw came in, and the hawk-gold at his temple — the color of the sword — had deepened past fashion and into fact. At thirteen he stood at the glass and saw what strangers had been seeing for months: a youth the eye read as two years older than the register, tall for his age and heavier through the chest and arm than tall accounts for, with a stillness that was not slowness, marks on his forearms like weather systems, and eyes that noticed you noticing them.

"Height," the clerk said, the third winter, reading off the rod. "Up again. You'll want to tell your tailor. Or your teacher."

"He cuts the practice swords down himself," Lin Hao said. "He's done it twice."

The clerk looked at him — sixteen in the face, thirteen in the book — and wrote thirteen, and underlined it, as though the underline would help.

The class noticed the way families notice, which is late, and then all at once. Wulin's version was to lay his own arm alongside Lin Hao's at the bench one day, consider the difference in the forearms with the gravity of a man reading scales, and deliver his verdict: "This is offensive. You know that? This is offensive to smiths." Xie Xie's version was worse, because it was public. He presented Lin Hao to the yard, side-on, with the sweep of a man showing prize livestock: "One of those. He's going to be one of those whose face is a personality. I said it first — remember I said it first." And Mu Xi, at the members' forge, said nothing at all. She moved the second apron up the peg row two holes one Friday, without comment — that wall keeps every smith's apron at the shoulder of whoever it serves, a row of heights pretending to be storage, and his had climbed past where it hung — and being Mu Xi, she adjusted the equipment and told no one what the adjustment measured.

The lines no machine weighed moved too. The walks kept their day, all three winters, unspent, one button. Fridays kept theirs. Mu Chen's bench got a sixth chair that nobody called anything. Four letters went north and four came south. And the cards from Shen Yi came quarterly, two lines each, until Wu Zhangkong's correspondence became, without anyone naming it, the longest conversation of his life.

## Part 4: The Wall at Forty

Lin Hao's winters were Sundays, and the Sundays were a room with five men in it, and by the third winter he was the sixth.

Not a boy who stood and carried. A smith. Fifth-rank work has a bench and the bench had a place, and the place was his, and Mu Chen never said so out loud, which was how it was said. He learned spirit refinement the way the room taught it: by watching what the metal was asked, for two winters, before he was allowed to ask it anything himself. The asking, when it came, was smaller than he had spent three years imagining. A thread of what a man is, laid along grain that is already willing, and then the ordinary hammer, and then the note.

The note. His first one — his own, out of his own bar — rang for four seconds and set itself in his hands like a bird landing, and five men who had worked in silence for years said nothing about it in voices he could hear for a week.

The rest of the lines moved the way lines move when a person is ten, then eleven, then twelve, then thirteen, and everything he does is cultivation he keeps. The rank climbed — **37, 38, 39**, each one entered with its cause named — and the ledger thickened. Fifty-four lines in three winters. Most of them were Saturdays' work, floor bouts with journeymen who wanted to know whether the Master Craftsman was anything at the anvil besides a stamp. Two were Tang Sect mission escorts, a winter apart. One, entered in different ink, was a boy from the intermediate academy who had read about the tournament and did not believe it, and believed it. Line **164**.

And then, in the spring of the thirteenth year, the rank stopped, and it stopped the way a corridor stops: at a wall, with a door in it.

**Rank 40.** The ceiling of three purple rings. Beyond the door was a fourth ring, and the law of what he was left nothing to negotiate with: *ten thousand years, and not one year under.* A ten-thousand-year soul ring was black. Black rings belonged to soul masters of fifty and sixty. And the law of him — the monster law, the one Wu Zhangkong had said out loud in a Hall of Heroes with the displays running — did not care what belonged to whom.

He stood at the bench with the book open and did not write for a while.

*Rank forty,* he wrote, finally. *The door is a ring, the ring is black, and what waits behind it has been waiting since I was nine. Next time I write in this book I will be on the other side of a month that changes everything, or I will be able to say why not. Those are the terms. They have always been the terms.*

He showed the page to no one. He did show the wall to Wu Zhangkong, at the yard, on a Thursday, by saying, "Forty."

Wu Zhangkong looked at him for a long moment, and there was something in the look that Lin Hao had never had directed at him before, and it took him most of the afternoon to name it. Not pride. Not the assessment. **Appetite.** His teacher wanted to see what came through that door the way a man wants to see a coast.

"Say it," Lin Hao said.

"I was going to wait until you were fourteen," Wu Zhangkong said. "So was the calendar. Neither of us is going to get the chance."

## Part 5: The President's Office

The conversation that decided everything happened without Lin Hao in the room, which was fitting, because most of the important ones had.

Eastsea Academy's president was a patient man named Yu Zhen, and for three years his school had drunk the attendance that Wu Zhangkong's name poured into it — a battle armor master, teaching, at an intermediate academy; the Skysea Alliance had never quite gotten over it — and for three years Yu Zhen had asked for nothing, and now Wu Zhangkong had come to his office and asked for something first, and Yu Zhen's brow was creased before the door shut.

"Teacher Wu, are you sure about this?"

"From the academy's perspective, raising outstanding students who achieve great things is the highest priority," Wu Zhangkong said. "We have been nourishing class zero for three and a half years. Don't you want them to contribute? Being admitted to that academy is a better achievement than anything else. The academy can announce it. Many will be drawn here."

"Isn't it too early?"

"Shrek Academy only holds its entrance exams once every three years. They're already thirteen. If they don't go now, they'll miss their only chance."

Yu Zhen frowned, and played the card he had been holding for three years face down, the way patient men do. "But didn't you come from Shrek Academy, Teacher Wu? In my opinion, you should be more than enough to teach them."

The room went still in the specific way that room did.

"I can, to a degree," Wu Zhangkong said. "Only that much. They need a proper foundation laid down for the future. What I can give them ends."

"The future? Teacher Wu — how high can they reach?"

And Wu Zhangkong, without any doubt in his voice at all:

"Battle armor master."

Yu Zhen stood up. Sweat arrived on him in the honest way it arrives on a man doing arithmetic about giants. He breathed in, and breathed out, and made the only request of Wu Zhangkong he had ever made:

"Then I ask one thing. When they are — that — have them come back and give a talk. To our students. One talk."

"They'll give it," Wu Zhangkong said. "I'll promise it in their stead."

He did not answer the other question, the one from three years back, the one Yu Zhen had never actually asked out loud because he knew the answer would be engraved somewhere he couldn't read it. *Will you return?*

The answer was in the corridor he walked on the way out. It had been engraved for years. If his students made it in, the odds of his return were minute — and he had never once, in three winters, tried to raise them.

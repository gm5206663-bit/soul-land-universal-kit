## Part 1: The Slum

The second morning showed them the room, and the room explained the rent.

They came back with the key in full daylight — the visitor beds of admin had held them for one night, which was precisely long enough for the porter's "best wall in the outer court" to sound like a promise. The gate opened on the courtyard they had swept at dusk: warm flue wall, old pine, a hand-span of frost still on the branch in spite of a clear September sunrise, and Lao Zhou somewhere behind his tree pretending not to be their first neighbor. But the *room* — the room behind the warm wall, the single key the sheet had issued to all five of them — was the thing that stopped them in the threshold.

Thirty square meters. Two metal bunk frames and nothing else: no mattresses, no basin, a single string hanging from the lightless bulb like a question nobody had answered. Dust lay on everything in the settled way dust lies when it has been somewhere for years. Two of the windows were broken outright, and the wind off the trees came through the gaps with the comfortable familiarity of a thing that had been visiting for a long time.

"This…" Xie Xie stood in the doorway with his bag over his shoulder. "Isn't this just a bit cruel? Are we certain this is Shrek Academy and not the slum that lives next door to it? At Eastsea we had — we had *mattresses.* We had the concept of a mattress."

"Quit complaining," Wulin said, stepping past him, because Wulin had already begun the inventory. "If you have spare time, spend it tidying."

"Captain, how are all of us supposed to live in a single room?" Xu Xiaoyan said, with the mild worry of a surveyor noting a boundary dispute. "Men and women in one room — there aren't even walls to be improper across."

"How old do you think you all are," Xie Xie said, "nobody here has even—"

"Hmph," said Xiaoyan, and turned to Gu Yue for the support one woman is supposed to lend another in the matter of curtains.

Gu Yue was watching Wulin decide where the curtains would go.

Xiaoyan closed her mouth with the serene finality of a surveyor confirming a border nobody was disputing. *Noted,* said the set of her shoulders. *I am surrounded by forces of nature, and at the trial for this curtain, not one of you will be called as a witness.*

From the bunk frame where he was conspicuously not spirit-refining the table leg, Lin Hao watched it without watching it — the particular arithmetic of being in a room with Gu Yue, two years learned now: she was the constant, Wulin was the direction she pointed, and he was the carpenter's mark on the floor. Precise. Useful. Never the thing the compass loved. It hurt the way a ledger column hurts when the total is correct and merely not the total you had hoped for — exactly as much as it ought to, and quiet.

Wulin stood in the middle of the room with his captain's face on — the face he wore when a thing was broken and standing in front of him, which was his favorite kind of thing. "Since we've been assigned the room, all we can do is make the most of it. Cloth for the broken windows first, curtains down the middle after — boys one half, girls the other. We'll improve as we go." He looked at the warm north wall, and at the courtyard pine beyond the gate, and did the arithmetic aloud. "Listen. This is the condemned end of a barracks full of empty rooms. Down the flank it's cold, dark, and honest poverty. Ours breathes."

The wall breathed, sure enough — under the birds and the distant plaza bells, the low sigh of the forge flue on the other side of the stone, holding the heat of a hundred mornings.

"I'm starting to think," Xie Xie said slowly, "that being the mistake on the paperwork has an up side."

"Five on the key sheet," said Lin Hao, who had been turning the key over in his hand the way he turned a bar before the first strike. "The register had four names. The steward who roomed us had a wing he couldn't fill and a flue that made it *worse* for anyone normal — nobody wants to sleep against a furnace that breathes all night. He gave the unfillable room to the unfillable students. It wasn't kindness. It was filing."

"I'm choosing to hear it as kindness," Wulin said. "Xiaoyan, measure the window gaps. Gu Yue, wind for the dust — carefully, the room is the one thing we own. Xie Xie, your selves are on plank and mattress duty, all three of them, and if night-Xie objects I'll explain it to him at dawn personally." He hesitated, and his eye fell on the single desk-like table the room possessed, one leg of which was already looking at him thoughtfully. "Also. Nobody spirit-refines the furniture. I see you both looking at that leg. The leg is a civilian."

"That's fair," said Xie Xie. "That is, in fact, completely fair."

The room came clean fast — soul masters make short work of dust, when the dust has no points riding on it — but cloth they did not have, nor hot water, nor anything to eat the dust with except their sleeves. The dining hall and the bathhouse were both across the enormous plaza; the only tap the slum quarter owned was a single cold faucet behind the barracks, shared by every working student who lived in the trees.

So they went for water. The faucet, when they reached it, had company.

## Part 2: The Faucet
## Part 2: The Faucet

A boy stood at the spigot with a towel over one shoulder and a bucket under the stream.

He was frail. That was the first word for him — narrow shoulders, wrists like a girl's, the kind of frame the medic's stones would have insulted with their very politeness. He watched the water rise in his bucket with the absent patience of a person doing a thing he had done ten thousand times.

Xie Xie gulped. It was a sound Lin Hao had never heard him make.

"That's number two," Xie Xie breathed, without taking his eyes off the boy. "The exam. The second examinee. He — all right, I need everyone to understand that the following is said by a man who was *there*: he hit me once. I have been hit by a lot of people, including, yesterday, a syllabus of senior students in my imagination. He hit me the way weather hits you. I woke up with my three selves agreeing on something for the first time, and the thing they agreed on was *that man.*"

The boy's bucket filled. He turned the spigot with an economical wrist, bent, and lifted it.

Lin Hao watched the lift the way he watched everything — from the margin, where the readings were truest. And the reading was wrong, and the wrongness was the whole point. The bucket came up full, maybe thirty kilograms of sloshing water, and the boy's shoulders did not change. Not "didn't strain" — *did not change.* The load went somewhere that was not on display. The frail frame was clothing, worn over something the faucet didn't deserve to see. The boy balanced the bucket the way Wulin balanced anything, the way a balance scale balances — with the certainty of a mechanism that had never once in its life considered tipping.

Wulin walked over, bucket in hand, and put on the radiant smile.

"Hi," he said. "We meet again. I'm Tang Wulin."

The boy gave him an apathetic look, acknowledged him with the smallest nod, and — because his bucket was full — picked it up, nodded once more, and carried it off toward the windowless middle rooms without spending a single word.

Five sets of eyes followed him until the trees took him. (The count was five now; the margin watched too.)

"That guy's a working student," Xie Xie said, awed. "He's *that* and he's a *working student.* I had him filed as inner court. I had him filed as *faculty.*"

"Remember what the administrator told us," Wulin said slowly. "'Don't bring shame to the working students.'" He stood there with his empty bucket, and they could see him doing the arithmetic — the face he made when the price of a thing turned out to be lower than its value. "A boy like that, three rings, power enough to fold Xie Xie's argument in one motion — and he sleeps in the slum and draws his own water. That's not punishment. Nobody punishes a boy like that into a bucket line."

"It's a selection," Lin Hao said.

They looked at him.

"The building's a filter," he said, keeping his voice down, the way you kept a reading down when the metal was still hot. "Normal students pay twenty percent and get beds. Working students pay the whole fee in hours and get — *this.* Every comfort removed. Every minute bought. Now ask what kind of student *chooses* that, or what kind of student the Academy is willing to let do it." He nodded at the trees where the frail boy had gone. "The strongest three-ring power type we've measured. The boy the faucets are for. The administrator didn't say 'don't bring shame to the working students' as a warning about *us.* He said it the way you'd say it about a guild. The working students have a *name* in this place, and the name means something, and I don't think we're supposed to find out what by asking."

Gu Yue, who had been watching the trees with her head tilted the way a cat watches a closed door, said: "Then we find out by being ones." She turned back toward the plaza, and a faint, dangerous smile came onto her face, the smile she wore right before spending a spirit skill on housework. "Also. That plaza took us until dusk with brooms. The steward will find something else for us to clean by tomorrow. And nobody has yet told me we're not allowed to use our martial souls."

Wulin's eyes lit up. He grinned.

"All right," he said.

## Part 3: Vortex

She went out onto the plaza edge the way a smith walks to a known anvil: without ceremony, but with everyone in earshot quietly stopping what they were doing.

Her three rings rose — two purple and the yellow that the five of them no longer needed explained to each other. The first ring lit. Elemental Tide: green light gathering in her cupped palms, specks of wind-light, and then blue answering it, water drawn out of the morning air itself, two equal lights beginning to turn around each other in the smallest possible spiral.

The second ring. Elemental Control. The spiral took feed — the morning mist, the damp off the flags, the air above the plaza giving up its water the way ore gives its metal when the temperature is honest — and grew. A meter tall. Two meters. A standing whirlpool of fused wind and water, and Gu Yue standing inside the calm at its center, eyes narrow, moving a tenth as fast as she moved in a fight, because this wasn't combat, this was *craft*, and craft, as Lin Hao had been telling every bar of metal he owned since he was seven, is what power looks like when it refuses to hurry.

The third ring lit. Elemental Fusion.

The green and the blue stopped being two colors. The vortex became one thing — a ribbon of moving sky that went down the plaza in a sweeping pass, dust and water and ten years of working-student grime going up into it the way filings go to a magnet, leaving wet, gleaming flagstone behind it in a broad, absurd stripe.

"Do the slum end," Wulin called, laughing. "The whole barracks! We live there!"

She turned the vortex on the barracks — and that was when Lin Hao stopped watching the technique and started watching the courtyard.

The fused ribbon swept the flank of the condemned end block. It took dust. It took the leaf-fall. It took the rot-damp off the plank windows in a single glittering pass. It reached the old pine.

It went around the frost the way a river goes around a stone.

The hand-span of white on the September branch sat through a fused wind-and-water element storm without changing by a single crystal. The vortex moved on — Gu Yue was too controlled to force anything, and the element went where it was permitted — and left the rime exactly as it had been: white, patient, glittering at noon.

Lao Zhou was standing under the tree with his broom and his string-bound notebook, and he had stopped breathing.

Gu Yue lowered her hands. The vortex unwound itself into the air, two meters of impossible sky going back to being ordinary weather. The plaza and the barracks gleamed behind her. The works steward, coming to measure how five brooms were progressing against the largest plaza in the outer court, arrived at the far end of the wet stripe and stopped with his clipboard held at the angle of a man re-reading his own opinion.

Gu Yue was not looking at the steward.

She was looking at the branch. For exactly one heartbeat, her eyes moved from the rime to Lin Hao — the flat, measuring look she had given the spring that fed him once, back in the exam, the look that filed a thing without asking about it — and then the look was gone and she was smiling at Wulin's praise like a girl who had simply cleaned a plaza very well.

Lin Hao gave nothing back. Neither did the branch.

Under him, very far inside, in the dark water at the bottom of the country he carried, the vein of iron that ran through the sea rang once, very softly — because two hundred meters away the forge pavilion had just breathed in, and iron knows iron, even iron that is a soul and iron that is a fence.

"She didn't melt it," said Lao Zhou, to his tree, in a voice of discovery. "Winds from a three-ringed element fusion, and she didn't melt it." He opened the string book and wrote in it for a long time.

## Part 4: The Failure Wall

The scrap gate was open when he got there.

He had followed the breathing sound. The pavilion's furnace door stood wide to the afternoon, a smith working inside with his back to the gate, soot to the elbows, and the hand-painted sign over the lintel reading its soot-colored rule to anyone tall enough to read it: **SCRAP IS NOT FREE. NEITHER IS READING IT.**

"Reading's a skill," Lin Hao said, stopping at the threshold. "I've got the second one. I'm here about the first."

The smith did not turn. He was watching a pour — a small one, a workshop pour, the kind nobody famous ever watches — and he said, without looking away: "Then read me this."

So Lin Hao came off the threshold and stood where the heat first becomes a thing you have to *decide* about, and he read the pour the way his father's hands had taught him, the way Mu Chen's room had confirmed: the color of the stream as it left the lip, the speed of the skin forming on the flow, the note the mold made when the metal first touched in. He did not say anything until the stream ended and the smith tilted the crucible back.

"Sixty degrees too cold for the section it's going into," he said. "The flow will set before the mold fills. It's not your fire — it's the scrap you're running. That ingot's been poured before, twice I'd guess, and it carries the memory of it. Reforged metal cools honest but cools *fast.*"

The smith set the crucible down. He turned.

He was forty and nothing — no title on his chest, no rank-band on his sleeve, just a man who ran the pavilion's scrap and furnaces for a wage and the deep private satisfaction of being correct. He had the face of a hinge: useful, plain, built to swing the same direction for thirty years.

"Two things in that answer," he said. "The first is right. The pour's dead — I knew it before I started; I was testing the *scrap* to see what it would tell me about the batch. The second is why you're standing at my gate instead of in a classroom." He wiped his hands on his apron. "Most children who can read a pour were taught to read *good* metal. You read the trash like it owed you money. Where'd a working student learn to do that?"

"I'm a fifth-rank smith," Lin Hao said. "With no calling card in this city."

The hinge of a face didn't change. But the man's eyes went to the gate and back, the way a buyer's eyes go to a bar he hadn't expected in the lot.

"Fifth rank." He said it flatly. "At thirteen."

"The Association knows. The paperwork hasn't caught up to Shrek."

"The paperwork never catches up to anything." The smith considered him for a moment, then jerked his head at the corner of the pavilion — the scrap corner, where the off-cuts and failed pieces lived in sorted piles, and along the back wall of which ran a long, dark rack of things that were not sorted at all. Lin Hao looked at the rack. Every piece on it was a failure. Miscast fittings, cracked bars, joints that had folded, pieces whose shapes he couldn't even name because they had stopped being shapes. None of it was salvage stock. All of it was *kept.*

"The failure wall," the smith said, following his eyes. "Every smith on the pavilion hangs his firsts there. First pour. First fold. First piece he believed in and shouldn't have. The scrap pile is metal. The wall is *lessons.*" He paused. "I'm Tie Jin. I don't take apprentices — I'm not anybody's master, and I'm not on anyone's staff. I run what gets burned and what gets kept. A working student with trade-hours can buy scrap time: hours of work for hours at the pile. The reading's free, like the sign says — but free reading means I get to test you whenever I feel like it."

Lin Hao looked at the failure wall, and at the scrap pile, and past the pile, at the good metal locked up for the academy's proper students.

"Hours for hours," he said. "And for firsts?"

Tie Jin looked at him. "What're you making, fifth-rank-with-no-card?"

"Armor," Lin Hao said. "Two words. Eventually. Starting with the part it is impossible to get wrong."

A sound came out of Tie Jin that might have been a laugh, if a hinge laughed. "Nothing's impossible to get wrong," he said. "That's the wall. But the simplest piece is a wrist guard, and a wrist guard is the right place to learn that. Deal's this: you work my hours, you get the scrap and the fire. You hang your failures with the rest of us, and you do not, *ever*, melt one back down because it embarrassed you. The wall keeps them. The wall keeps all of them."

That evening, at the smallest fire in the pavilion, Lin Hao made his first piece of the two-word dream out of a piece of scrap heavy silver that no one had claimed — a wrist guard, the simplest form there is, a cuff with a seam.

He read the metal. He heated it honest. He struck it the way his father struck everything — with the margin held in reserve. And at the fourth strike, the seam folded. Not catastrophically. Quietly. A small, sickening curl, the way a lip curls when a bar is telling you the temperature was a lie.

The piece cooled on the anvil, wrong and done and unmistakable.

Wulin found him there an hour later, walking in from the barracks end with a half-mended curtain rail over his shoulder and the look of a captain checking on his whole roster one last time before sleep. He saw the failed cuff on the anvil. He did not say anything about it being a good try. He set the curtain rail down and picked the cuff up, turned it over in his big, furnace-calloused hands, and read it the way only one other smith in the city could.

"You knew at the third strike," Wulin said.

"Second," said Lin Hao. "I took two more to be sure."

"Good." Wulin put the cuff back down. "The ones you don't take the extra strikes on are the ones that teach you nothing. May I?" He held up his own hands, and his eyes went to the good-metal rack and then, correctly, to the scrap pile. "I've got a two-word dream of the same length and the same first word. If you're buying hours here, captain's order: buy two sets. I'll pay for mine with whatever the pavilion needs heaviest."

Tie Jin, who had been listening from the furnace door in the dark, said without showing himself: "You're the sixteen-bun boy. I heard about you from the island. You can have hours too. Bring the other small smith's reading and I'll test the both of you at once." He came out into the firelight long enough to nod at the cuff. "Hang it."

So Lin Hao took his first failed piece to the failure wall, and Tie Jin gave him an iron hook, and he hung it among the strangers' catastrophes — the cracked fittings, the folded joints, the shapes that had stopped being shapes — and put no name on it, because the wall did not ask for names, only that the piece stay.

On the way back through the gate he stopped under the sign, and read it again, and said, to Wulin, the last thing he planned to say about the matter for a while:

"The metal of the armor starts tonight. Both of us. Not one word. Two."

"Two," Wulin agreed.

Behind them, in the pavilion's small fire, a cuff-shaped gap of nothing was worth more than any piece of armor either of them had ever finished — because finished armor tells you what you can do, and the wall tells you what you're allowed to learn.

Somewhere over the condemned end block, Lao Zhou's tree branch caught the last of the light, and held it, the way it held everything: the frost, the secret, and the first night of two smiths buying their own fire one failed wrist guard at a time.

## Part 1: What He Said

He said yes on the fourth day.

Not on the first. He had said *not yet* on the first day and he had meant it, and the not-yet had been true for three days, and on the fourth it stopped being true, and he wanted that kept straight — the not-yet had been a real thing, not a stall with the serial filed off.

He said it to the man with the thick folder, in the room where they had put him. The man wrote it down.

"I'm not surprised," the man said.

"I am," said Lin Hao.

The man looked up. "That's the first interesting thing you've said."

"I've said six interesting things in four weeks. Nobody wrote any of them down."

"That's because they weren't answers."

"No," Lin Hao agreed. "They weren't."

---

## Part 2: What Shen Yi Said

She had come to Skysea City for the tournament and stayed three days, and she watched, and in a corridor where Lin Hao was not standing she said something to Wu Zhangkong that he did not hear.

He heard it anyway, later, from Mu Xi, because Mu Xi had become the person who told him things.

"His first ring is purple and his physique is excellent," Shen Yi had said. "Did you raise him to be like this?"

"No."

"If he has three rings by the time he's fifteen, his body will likely transform again." She had said it the way you say a thing you have worked out and are not pleased by. "You have an eye for disciples."

"I have an eye for one disciple," Wu Zhangkong had said. "And I did not have an eye for this."

Mu Xi told him all of this on the seawall, eating dried fish, because Xu Lizhi had given her dried fish and she had accepted it, and because she had resented him for four days and then started telling him things, and neither of those had been planned.

"Why does that matter," Lin Hao said.

"Because she's saying your body's going to change," Mu Xi said. "And she's saying Wu Zhangkong didn't do it. And she's saying she's got an eye for disciples and he's got an eye for one disciple, and neither of them had an eye for *this*, which means whatever you are isn't a thing anybody raised." She pulled a flake off the dried fish. "I've been a fourth rank five months and I've never once been a thing nobody raised."

## Part 3: What Wu Zhangkong Said

He said it in the smithy, because the smithy was where Lin Hao knew what he was doing, and because Wu Zhangkong had begun to understand that a thing said in the smithy landed differently from the same thing said anywhere else.

"Guide rather than oppose," he said.

"I don't follow."

"It's what I'm going to do." Wu Zhangkong turned a bar a quarter-turn on the anvil and did not look at him. "For eight months I've been correcting you, and correcting is opposing, and I've decided I've been doing the wrong thing. I'm telling you rather than letting you work it out, because you work things out fast and I'd rather say it first."

Lin Hao set the hammer down.

"You've been guiding me."

"No. I've been opposing you. I told you your blade was a servant's blade. I told you the Track was the only test I had. I said *I don't know* twice about what you are, and saying *I don't know* is opposing too — it leaves you nothing to hold." He straightened. "I'm going to stop. I'm going to guide, and I don't know how to do that yet either, and we'll work it out, and that's the first time I've said *I don't know* about something I'm going to do instead of something you are."

Lin Hao looked at him.

"That's the seventh person who's said I don't know."

"I know."

"You've said it three times yourself."

"Yes."

"I noticed."

"I know you did," Wu Zhangkong said. "That's why I said it in here."

## Part 4: What Wulin Said

They left on the ninth day.

Wulin said it on the seawall, because the seawall was where they said things, and because Lin Hao had started to understand that places carried weight and he had not used to notice which ones.

"I've been thinking about what you said," Wulin said. "About not-yet not being no."

"Yeah."

"I've decided not-yet is a thing you say when you're waiting for a reason," Wulin said, "and the reason comes after. You waited four weeks. The reason came on the fourth day and you said yes. And I want you to know I noticed you waited instead of just saying no, because that's the thing about you I understand that nobody else does."

Lin Hao looked at him.

"That's the second thing you've told me I didn't know."

"I know."

"How'd you get there."

"Because I've known you since we were three," Wulin said, "and you taught me that saying a thing is the same act as thinking it, so I thought it and then I said it, and I didn't know I was going to say it until it was out. That's twice now. I've stopped being surprised, and the not-being-surprised is the part that scares me a little, and I'm telling you that too."

Lin Hao sat down on the seawall.

"I'm glad you're coming," he said.

"That's the eleventh time."

"I know."

## Part 5: What Lin Hao Wrote

That night, in the room that smelled of the sea, he took the book down and wrote the number first, because the number never asked anything of him.

*Two hundred and eighty-one.* It had been two hundred and ninety-eight. Eight points had gone somewhere between four days of not-yet and one day of yes, and he could not say which of them had done it, and he had stopped needing to.

He looked at that sentence. He had written some version of it five times now — *I cannot say which, and it no longer matters that I can't* — and each time it had come out a little longer, and the getting-longer was the only honest part of it.

*One thousand three hundred and ninety-nine.*

*Eight years.* Eight years to the next threshold. He had written that number four times, and writing a number four times was a way of not looking at it, and this was the first time he had caught himself doing it.

Because the crossing he had been afraid of was behind him now. It had happened. One thousand one hundred and sixty-four — both rings gone purple, a third ring arrived — and he had been carrying three thousand-year rings for so long that he had started writing the number the way a man taps a pocket to check for a purse he has not actually lost. The thousand-year line was not ahead of him. He was standing on it.

Then the rest of it.

*Shen Yi says my body will change again if I have three rings by fifteen. I have three at ten. Wu Zhangkong says he didn't have an eye for this. Mu Xi says she's never once been a thing nobody raised.*

*Wu Zhangkong says he's been opposing me and is going to start guiding, and doesn't know how to do that either.*

And then the line at the bottom, which he wrote last because it was the one he had been circling all week.

*I said yes on the fourth day. The reason came after the offer. This is how most of the things that have mattered to me have happened — I have never once said yes to a thing for the reason it was offered — and that has stopped troubling me.*

He closed the book.

Then he opened it again, because there were three things in it he had not written down in a long while, and noticing that you have stopped writing something is the same skill as noticing that you have stopped looking at something, and it was the only skill he was sure he had.

*One hundred and seven. That's the ledger. I haven't written the number in a long time — longer than I've gone without any other — and it isn't that I stopped counting. It's that the number stopped being the interesting part. It used to be the whole of it. Seventy-one lines and every one an answer I could use. Now the interesting thing is that there's one person in this city I can't use the answers on, and I've stopped minding, and I don't know when I stopped.*

*The hawk hasn't asked me anything since the forest and the question is still open. I've written that before. I'm writing it again because it's the oldest open thing I have, and because every time I write it I notice I've been waiting to be asked instead of asking, and that's a thing I do, and it isn't a virtue.*

*Wulin didn't say Na'er's name today. He hasn't said it in front of me in a long time and I haven't asked, because I know where the money goes and I know what the Fridays are for, and because there's a way of asking that's a kindness and a way that's a knife, and I haven't been sure which one I'd be holding. I've been sure about a great many things this year. This isn't one of them. Not being sure hasn't stopped me doing anything, which is either growing up or being a coward, and I can't tell which yet.*

The lock at his temple lay gold across his cheekbone, and the marks on his forearms were raised and had been raised for thirty-eight days, and the hawk turned over in him — *one thousand three hundred and ninety-nine* — and did not pull toward anything.

Eight years.

Lin Hao lay on his back in a city that smelled of salt and hot metal and thought about eight years, and about the thing he had been counting toward that had already come and gone while he wasn't looking, and about the fact that he was frightened. He did not write the frightened part down. He had written *it no longer matters that I don't know* so many times it had stopped being true, and he was not going to add to the pile.

He did not sleep well.

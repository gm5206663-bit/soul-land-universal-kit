## Part 1: The Quiet After

The news died in nine days.

Lin Hao counted, because that was what his head did, and because he had learned that a story dying was a thing with a shape if you watched it. On the first day two battle-armor masters were on every tongue in Skysea City. On the third they were on three tongues and a notice-board by the east gate. On the ninth a man at the smithy called a kettle a battle armor, because it had a handle on each side, and nobody corrected him, and that was how Lin Hao knew it was over.

Wu Zhangkong had named it once, in the room, the way he named everything, which was without weight. "A blossom from a night-blooming cactus," he had said. "The petals wither before dawn." He had not been disappointed by it. Lin Hao watched him not be disappointed and understood it was the same not-being-disappointed as the Sky Ice and the not-upset and all the other nots, and that Wu Zhangkong had been watching stories die for a long time and had stopped mistaking the dying for a loss.

Eastsea Academy sealed the records. Long Hengxu did it himself, which Mu Xi said was the most exercise his signing hand had had in a decade. When Long Huantian and Zhang Zhenpeng sent people to ask after class zero, the people came back with nothing, because there was nothing to hand them. The class had gone quiet. Not gone. Quiet — the way a room goes quiet when the people in it stop performing and start working.

They stopped competing. That was the part nobody outside understood. Five students who had breached the top sixteen of the Skysea Alliance tournament stopped entering tournaments, and the reason was not that they had lost and not that they had won, and it was not a reason any of them said out loud except Xie Xie, who said it to a plate of food.

"There's nothing left to prove in here," he said. "I've beaten everyone in this city who'll stand still for it, and the ones who won't stand still for it I've beaten anyway, and now I'm bored, and bored is the worst thing a person can be."

"You're ten," Tang Wulin said.

"I'm fourteen in here," Xie Xie said, and tapped his head, and then his plate, "and fourteen in here, and both of them are bored."

## Part 2: What Wu Zhangkong Decided

Wu Zhangkong watched them at the break of dawn, because that was when Tang Wulin ran, and because he had decided — though he had not told anyone — that the best time to understand a student was the time the student thought nobody was looking.

Tang Wulin ran at the break of dawn. He had run at the break of dawn for four years, since before the roosters, since before the sky had the colour it was going to have, and he ran alone because he had decided a long time ago that the running was his and the sleeping was everybody else's.

Wu Zhangkong had seen it for four years and had said nothing, because the running produced results, and because he had made a mistake, which he understood now, standing in the cold, which was that he had let the results stand in for the boy.

Tang Wulin was the most hardworking of them. Wu Zhangkong had known that the way he knew the weight of his own sword, and he had used it, the way a man uses a thing that is not going to break, and Tang Wulin had a spirit ascension platform behind him where a lapse had nearly turned into a cultivation deviation, and a tournament behind him where he had been hurt badly and recovered in a day, and another challenge after that which had left him where he was now. And through all of it he had not complained. Not once.

*He is painfully aware of how much talent he lacks,* Wu Zhangkong thought, *compared to Gu Yue, and Xie Xie, and Xu Xiaoyan. That is what drives him. That is why he invests twice the effort of all of them combined. He is fatigued in body and in mind. And I have been overlooking it.*

He turned to go inside and make the short-term revisions he had been putting off, because they were still children after all.

And then he stopped, because there was a light on in the forge.

Lin Hao did not run at the break of dawn. Lin Hao was at the forge at the break of dawn, because the forge was hot at dawn and cool by noon, and because Lin Hao had decided — though he had not told anyone either — that the best metal was the metal you hit while the world was still deciding whether to wake up.

Wu Zhangkong stood in the cold with two students who both rose before the sun, and understood that he had been making one mistake and not the other, and that the two boys needed opposite things, and that nobody had told him that would be the hard part.

Tang Wulin ran because he was afraid he was not enough. Lin Hao was at the forge because he was afraid he was too much, and did not yet know what the ceiling of it was, and had been shown the ceiling nine days ago in the form of his own teacher turning into a knight of ice in front of four thousand people.

Tang Wulin needed easing.

Lin Hao needed a wall.

Wu Zhangkong went inside, and he did not make the revisions that morning, because he understood that he had been about to ease one boy and had no idea how to wall the other, and that guiding — the thing he had promised in the smithy, the thing he had said he did not know how to do yet — was going to mean doing two opposite things at once and not letting either boy see that the other was getting something different.

He was not sure he could do it.

He did it anyway, because that was the part of guiding nobody told you about, which was that you did not wait until you could.

## Part 3: The Metal

Mu Chen came three days later, ostensibly to confirm Tang Wulin's state, and he confirmed it, and then he did not leave, which was how Lin Hao knew the visit was not about Tang Wulin.

Mu Chen found him at the forge, because Mu Chen always found him at the forge, and stood watching him work a bar of ordinary iron that was not going to be anything, because not every bar was going to be anything and a smith who could not make a nothing was not a smith.

"You saw it," Mu Chen said.

"I saw it."

"The Sky Ice."

"I saw it."

Mu Chen watched the bar. "And you have been thinking about it for nine days."

"Eleven."

"Eleven," Mu Chen agreed. "And."

Lin Hao set the hammer down, because Mu Chen had said *and* the way a man sets a cup on a table when he means to stay, and because Lin Hao had learned that Mu Chen's *and* was a door and you were supposed to walk through it.

"It's metal," Lin Hao said.

"Yes."

"The Sky Ice is metal. All of it. Every piece. The crown, the bands, the sword that isn't a sword anymore. It's metal that a person made, and then wore, and then became." He picked the hammer back up. "Xie Xie said I'm going to make the metal. He said it out loud, on the seawall, where Gu Yue could hear, which with Xie Xie is the same as saying it to the whole city."

"Xie Xie is not wrong."

"I know he's not wrong." Lin Hao hit the bar. It was not going to be anything. "I'm a fourth-rank Grandmaster. A one-word battle armor is thousand-refined metal and needs five rings. I have three rings and I have never thousand-refined anything that wasn't going to be a competition piece, and the Sky Ice is two words, which is spirit-refined, which is a rank of metal I have never touched and cannot name and do not know how to make." He hit the bar. "So Xie Xie is not wrong, and Xie Xie is also about eleven years early, and I have been trying for nine days to work out whether that's a problem or a schedule."

Mu Chen was quiet for a while.

"Do you know what the foundation of a battle-armor master is," he said at last.

"Metal."

"A first-rank blacksmith," Mu Chen said. "Not a fourth. A first. The foundation is not the rank you reach, it is the rank you start from, because a battle armor is not forged, it is *grown*, and you cannot grow a thing you do not understand from the bottom." He looked at Lin Hao then, properly. "You are eleven years early and you are also already past the foundation, and those are the same fact, and you have been treating them as a problem and a schedule when they are one thing."

Lin Hao did not hit the bar.

"Say it again."

"You are early," Mu Chen said, "and you are ready, and you have been trying to make those two things into a contradiction because a contradiction is a thing you can work on, and a fact that is simply true is a thing you have to wait for, and you have never been good at waiting."

Lin Hao looked at the bar, which was not going to be anything, and understood that Mu Chen had just handed him the second ceiling in nine days, and that the first one had been ice and the second one was time, and that neither of them was a wall he could hit.

## Part 4: What Gu Yue Did Not Say

Gu Yue had been holding it for nine days.

She had not told anyone, because she did not tell anyone anything, and because the holding was not the kind of thing you put down by saying it, and because she had watched him say the whole of it to her on a seawall and then vanish before the last word, and she had known the last word before he got to it, and she had not said it then and she had not said it since.

She found him at the forge, because that was where he was, and because she had decided — though she had not told anyone either, and would not have — that the forge was the only place he was not performing.

"You're making nothing," she said.

"I'm making nothing."

"That bar is not going to be anything."

"No."

She stood in the doorway, the way she stood in doorways, which was like a person who had arrived but had not decided to come in.

"Mu Chen told me something," Lin Hao said, and hit the bar. "He told me I'm early and I'm ready and those are the same fact. I've been trying for nine days to make them into a problem and a schedule."

"And."

"And they're not."

Gu Yue was quiet. She had a way of being quiet that was not empty, the way a held note is not silence.

"You told me something," she said.

Lin Hao's hand stopped on the bar.

"On the seawall," Gu Yue said. "You told me the whole of it. And then you said *or I* and you left, and you have not said the rest, and you are not going to, and I have known the rest for nine days." She did not look at him. She looked at the bar, which was not going to be anything. "And I have not said it either."

Lin Hao set the hammer down.

"Why not."

"Because you left," Gu Yue said. "You said the whole of it and you left, and the leaving was the part that was true, and if I say the rest then I am finishing a sentence you decided not to finish, and I do not finish people's sentences." She turned then, in the doorway, the way she turned, which was a few degrees and was the equivalent of anybody else turning around. "I do not want the version where I say it. I want the version where you do."

And she left, because that was the thing they did now, the leaving, and because she had decided that the leaving was the part that was true for her too.

Lin Hao stood at the forge with a bar that was not going to be anything, and understood that the silence had changed hands nine days ago and had just been handed back, and that he was holding it again, and that it was heavier than it had been, and that he was not going to say it today.

He picked the hammer back up.

## Part 5: What Lin Hao Wrote

He wrote it that night, above the forge, with the window open, because the forge was cool by then and the writing was the part of the day where he found out what he thought.

*Two hundred and ninety-six. One thousand four hundred and eighteen. Thirty-seven.*

*The news died in nine days. Wu Zhangkong called it a blossom from a night-blooming cactus and was not disappointed, and I watched him not be disappointed and understood he has been watching stories die for a long time.*

*Mu Chen told me I am early and I am ready and those are the same fact. The Sky Ice is metal. Xie Xie said I will make the metal and he is not wrong and he is eleven years early, and a first-rank blacksmith is the foundation and I am past the foundation, so early and ready are one fact and I have been treating them as a problem and a schedule.*

*Gu Yue gave the silence back. She has known the rest of my sentence for nine days and did not say it, because she does not finish people's sentences, and she wants the version where I say it. I am holding it again. I am not going to say it today. I do not know when I will, and I am not going to dress that up as peace, because I have dressed it up as peace before and it was a lie I told the page.*

He set the pen down, because there was one more thing, and because he had learned that the last line was the line that mattered.

*Two ceilings in nine days. The first was ice, in the shape of my teacher. The second is time, in the shape of a first-rank blacksmith being the foundation of a thing I am not ready to make yet even though I am ready to make it. Neither is a wall I can hit. I have spent eleven days wanting one of them to be a wall, and tonight I understand that wanting a ceiling to be a wall is the last thing a person does before they grow, and that I am doing it, and that it is almost over.*

He closed the book.

The lock at his temple lay gold across his cheekbone, and the marks on his forearms were raised, and the hawk turned over in him — *one thousand four hundred and eighteen* — and did not pull toward anything, and somewhere under the wanting-a-wall there was a thing that was not wanting, which was the beginning of the month he did not yet know was coming.

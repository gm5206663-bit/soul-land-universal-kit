## Part 1: The Haggle

"Stop bullshitting," Elder Cai said. "You're an old hermit without a wife. Where did you get a granddaughter?"

"The girl is right, that's where." Zhuo Shi was still riding the wheels XXY's tears had lifted off the ground. "I don't care what you say. Today I want all of these children in Shrek Academy. The final score is decided by the three of us *together*, if you recall. You say Gu Yue deserves one point. I say she's earned at least four — and four puts her in the outer court with room to spare."

"We agreed on this earlier. What are you trying to pull now? No. I refuse."

Wulin, head lowered, was having his own quiet crisis: *the grandteacher changed sides awfully fast.* The old man had gone from *get out, all of you* to *four points* in the time it took a girl to wipe her face.

"Then what will it take for you to accept?" Zhuo Shi demanded.

And Elder Cai looked at him the way winter looks at a late flower. "Have you forgotten *why* we're having Tang Wulin take a makeup trial at all?"

There was a pause — the pause of a man finding a stair in the dark. Then Zhuo Shi swerved to Wulin so fast his grey hair moved. "The makeup trial! Of course! Shrek's rules, boy — a student who scores full marks on *every* trial is owed one request. Anything within the academy's power. You're already perfect on the seven scored trials. One more full score, and you can stand there and spend your request on an extra point for that girl."

"But the fourth trial—" Wulin said. "I walked out of the fourth trial."

"Fine." Cai's voice could have iced a pond. "I'll give you ten for the fourth. Score perfect on the makeup, and the tenth goes to full marks as well. Full marks everywhere. One request."

Wulin didn't understand the shape of it yet, but he understood the hand being held out. Behind his back, where the elders couldn't see, a finger rose and made one slow, level warning motion in Gu Yue's direction. *Calm. Whatever it costs you, calm.*

She saw it. She stayed silent — not because she was told to. Because she was no fool: she wanted in, wanted it more than she had ever wanted a point of anything; and because if she were driven out, these four idiots would follow her, and their futures would follow them off the cliff. So she stood still, and bit down on the one point, and waited.

"What is your second profession?" Zhuo Shi asked. "Are you confident?"

"I'm certain I'll ace this test," Tang Wulin said.

Elder Cai snorted. "Don't be so confident, brat. Do you even know what a full score requires now?"

"Please advise me, Elder Cai."

"It is impossible to score full marks," she said, "without reaching the fifth rank of your second profession."

Zhuo Shi's eyes popped. Shen Yi's popped. The looks they traded were almost comical — *when did the fifth trial start demanding fifth-rank professionals? Most outer court GRADUATES don't carry that. That's an inner court requirement. He's an examinee. He's thirteen.*

Wulin stood very still.

Fifth rank. For a blacksmith, fifth rank meant Spirit Refinement — the great divider, the line between the grandmasters and the master craftsmen, the chickens and the phoenixes, the earth and the heavens. Thousand-refined metals went into one-word battle armor; but two-word armor and beyond — *real* armor, the kind that rewrote a soul master's ceiling — drank only spirit-refined metal. A thousand refinements opened everything a metal had. Spirit refinement *changed what it was*: soul power pressed into the metal until the metal carried spirit, until at the moment of completion the smith did not decorate the metal but woke it. Mu Chen had said it exactly once, in the shop, with the dust settling: *to spirit refine is to create life.*

He was at the peak of the fourth rank. He'd been there a while — the techniques were his, Mu Chen had seen to that; what he lacked was the raw soul power, and no amount of wanting corrected an arithmetic. Spirit refinement was supposed to demand four rings. Mu Chen had told him his innate divine strength might let him cross on three — *might.*

He looked at Gu Yue. Sparks of worry must have shown; what came back was a gentleness like nothing else the trial had produced. It settled him the way a coal settles a forge.

"I'll do it," he said. "Blacksmith!"

"And the other one," Elder Cai said, without turning her head. "You didn't imagine I'd forgotten you."

The fifth examinee stepped forward, unhurried, the way a man steps up to a counter he already knows the price at.

"Same terms," Cai said. "Full marks everywhere, one request. Yours is also held on the makeup. And before you enjoy the symmetry, blacksmith — a certification is paper. You hold a fifth-rank stamp issued by people who have never watched you work under a debt of a hundred spent points and a night with no sleep in it. Tonight, the paper gets tested. Both of you, same bar."

"Finally," Lin Hao said. "Someone here grades properly."

Zhuo Shi made the not-quite-laugh sound again.

## Part 2: The Design, Whispered

"Follow me," said the grey-haired elder.

The three of them — Zhuo Shi, Elder Cai, and Elder Li, the old man from the first trial — led the class down and into a long room at the academy's root, where staff were already rolling out a forging table and wheeling up a shelf of rare metals that made Xie Xie's eyebrows attempt to leave his face. The companions gathered behind the line painted on the floor. Two stations had been set out. Whatever else the night was, it was not improvised.

While Wulin stood at the table with his eyes shut — and stood, and stood, a full quarter of an hour, and no one hurried him — the two old monsters put their heads together at the room's edge.

"What is the matter with you?" Zhuo Shi whispered. "Why are you acting crazy? Are you calm yet?"

"Aren't you the one who taught Zhangkong to be stubborn?" Cai whispered back. "So what does that make you?"

"Fifth *rank*," Zhuo Shi pressed. "The rule was a third-rank second profession for full marks. A thirteen-year-old fifth-rank professional — when have you ever heard of such a person? Name one."

"You used a flimsy pretense yourself to set this whole stage," Cai said, and there was a chill in it that was almost warmth. "Why not one step further? Afterward you need only produce some explanations — and these children are far more mature than their age. They need to be *challenged*, Zhou. The more pressure they carry young, the stronger they grow. That Tang Wulin is the most resilient of the lot. He showed a golden soul ring in the fourth trial. He fought with a bear's strength in the ninth. What, exactly, can't he accomplish — if nobody forces him to prove it?"

She turned her page, softly, in the room where sound was record.

"And the other one," she added, so low it was almost to herself, "has the Blacksmith Association's inquiry on my desk, three pages long, because a fifth-rank stamp at his age is either the find of the generation or a clerical error, and Mu Chen's letter says *find* and I intend to know which by morning. Pressure, Zhou. It is the only teacher that never lies."

## Part 3: The Table

Wulin opened his eyes. There was a purple light in their depths that hadn't been there at the start of the quarter hour — the color of a decision that has finished deciding.

He went to the shelf and came back with the ingot his hands already knew: heavy silver. The first metal he had ever thousand-refined, in a shop in Eastsea with his teacher's arms aching in memory beside him. Some choices are arguments, and some are homecomings. He set it on the table, pressed the stud, and let the furnace take it — the bloom of heat rolling up the room like a remembered season.

At the second station, Lin Hao had already unrolled his tool-wrap. From it, wrapped in oilcloth, he took a bar of his own — not from the academy's shelf. Grey-silver, dense as an argument, a private weight.

"The Yaluo lot," he said, to nobody and everybody, answering the looks. "Bought at auction by the kilo, read through the crate." He set it in his calipers and turned it once, reading the grain the way other people read a face. "It's been waiting for a forge. It found one."

The two of them stood at their tables in the same firelight, and the room slowly understood it was looking at one profession at two altitudes. The captain was attempting the leap — fourth rank's peak to fifth rank's country, on three rings, under a night's debt, for a girl's one point. The fifth was doing something almost harder to watch: proving a rank he already held, with nothing to gain that he wanted, in front of three people qualified to see every flaw a master's hand hides. The leap and the testimony. The same hammers would tell both truths.

Lin Hao checked his own pulse the way he'd been taught — not the heart's beat; the deeper one. The joining, when he'd measured it that morning in the still dark, had held its seconds, patient as an apprenticeship. The body at a hundred points down was a body that had learned to work tired. Every smith's boy learns that early or learns it never: the forge does not care what your night was.

He looked across at his captain, and gave him the only gift the profession has.

"Rows breathe in threes," Lin Hao said. "Be somewhere it was."

Wulin's hammers came out of his soul in a flash — the heavy silver pair, worn to the shapes of his own grip. He shut his eyes again and walked the vein-patterns with his fingertips until his blood forgot where it ended. Lin Hao's hammer was already moving.

The first strikes rang, and the second strikes answered, and somewhere in the swing of it the two rhythms found each other and locked, the way they had in a yard in Eastsea once — the metronome and the storm around it, hammer and hammer, anvil and answer.

The elders watched two boys breathe life into metal, and did not breathe much themselves.

And at the door, unnoticed by everyone who mattered noticing, a man the color of winter had arrived and stood without one word — Wu Zhangkong, who had been sent for the moment the roof went loud, watching his students hammer, his face as still as the day he'd told them the price of things. His eyes went from the leap to the testimony, from his captain to his fifth, and stayed there, at the second table, where a boy was reading grain by firelight with a hundred-point debt on his shoulders and no ring lit at all.

The metal glowed. The night was long. Nobody left.

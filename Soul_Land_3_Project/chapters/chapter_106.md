## Part 1: The Late Celebration

The toast came after the ice cream, which was the wrong order and therefore exactly the order Wulin would choose.

"Congratulations, everyone," he said, raising his cup of juice like it was a goblet at a banquet and he was not, ten minutes earlier, a boy who had eaten a mountain. "We made it into Shrek Academy. Let's call this a late celebration."

Gu Yue and Xu Xiaoyan perked up at that, their ice cream forgotten on the table. The five of them — six, now, with Xu Lizhi folded happily into the wreckage of Wulin's dessert, and Ye Xinglan in the chair she had told herself she had decided to take — exchanged the particular smile of people who had survived an exam that failed most of a continent. Strength, potential, secondary profession, character, patience: a single failed trial and none of them would be sitting in this warm orange light. They were sitting in it.

"Here's to not having failed anything," Xie Xie said, and drank.

"Here's to food that doesn't cost contribution points," Lizhi said, around a spoon he had not been offered.

"Here's to the door that doesn't close," said Xiaoyan, which made Lin Hao glance at her, because Xiaoyan usually said the light thing and that had not been the light thing.

They drank. The shop was loud and full and warm. And then Xie Xie, who had been scanning the room all night with the focused industry of a boy preparing for adulthood, leaned in with the air of a man about to share state secrets.

"Haven't you heard of Shrek's Sea God's Fated Date Festival?"

Wulin blinked. "The what?"

"The Fated Date Festival. If you're inner court and a certain age, you qualify. It's a grand occasion for the whole inner court — even outer court students catch glimpses of it later. Ten thousand years it's been held, straight. Countless couples sprang up there." Xie Xie's eyes were shining. "Legend says the Spirit Ice Douluo and the Dragon Butterfly Douluo started dating at that very festival."

"You're already thinking about those things at your age?" Wulin said, with the genuine distress of a boy whose only romance was with his next meal. "We're too young for that. Besides, you have to get into the inner court first. We've a long way to go. Think about student number two — the one who beat you into a pulp in the trials. He's that strong and he isn't even inner court."

"I'll figure it out," Xie Xie said, bouncing, "once I have a target."

It was at the word *target* that Lin Hao, who had been watching the door the way he always did, became aware that the temperature on his left had dropped.

Not by much. Not enough for anyone else. The juice shop was warm, and full of bodies, and the orange lights threw heat-colored light over everything. But the side of him closest to Gu Yue had gone the way a window goes cold when the night wind finds the seam in the frame.

He looked at her.

She was not looking at him. She was looking at her ice cream, which she had been eating with the focused joy of a girl who had found one thing in the world that asked nothing of her — and which was now, untouched for the length of the toast and the festival speech, melting. A slow silver bead of it was running down the side of the glass. Gu Yue, who could count the elements in a room and name the wind's direction by the ache in her teeth, had not noticed her dessert turning to soup.

"Ten thousand years," Gu Yue said, in the flat, level voice she used for things she had already judged, "of a festival, and the best story they can tell is that two people who were going to end up together anyway ended up together." She did not look up. "That isn't fate. That's arithmetic taking its time."

Xie Xie opened his mouth. "That's — that's romantic—"

"It's inefficient," said Gu Yue.

And Lin Hao did the thing he had trained himself to do across a hundred rooms where her mood was weather: he did not read it. He watched the frost crawl a finger's width up the side of her melting glass — the cold answering her the way it always did, the elements leaning toward her like reeds toward water — and he filed it under *she's practicing*, under *the elements answer her and it is not addressed to me*, under the careful, locked heading he had built the day she said nothing and he decided that a nothing was a no and he would not say the thing twice.

He did not see, because he was not looking for it, that the frost was not on her glass.

It had started on her glass. It was climbing toward his.

## Part 2: The Boy Who Bought a Shop

The commotion at the next table saved him from having to decide what any of that was.

"Waitress! Over here!" someone shouted, and then, with an arrogance you could have stood a spoon in: "Give me your number."

The whole shop turned. A young man sat at the neighboring table in a white suit — no Shrek uniform, which was its own statement in a room three-quarters full of people who had killed to wear one. He was a year or two older than them, with hair like spun gold combed back and held against any wind, and eyes the color of the gold in his hair, and there was a strange air to him as though his existence gave off light. Of everyone in the shop, Lin Hao thought, only Wulin could rival the looks — and even Wulin, who was presently wiping ice cream off his chin, could not rival the overbearingness.

The waitress standing in front of him had jade-white skin and short fiery-red hair and large black eyes, and the kind of beauty that made men want to stand between her and things. She was not standing the way a person stands when they want to be looked at.

"I'm sorry," she said politely. "That is out of the scope of my job responsibilities."

"Then what will it take for me to get your number?"

She shook her head and turned to leave.

"What kind of service is this?" The young man's palm hit the table, loud as a slap. "Get me your manager." And here was the thing Lin Hao watched with the cold interest of a man reading a ledger: the students in Shrek uniforms, the ones who had fought spirit beasts in the Great Star Dou for the right to be arrogant themselves, looked over, saw who it was, and *looked away*.

"Who is he," Xiaoyan murmured, "that even here, people avert their eyes?"

"Someone the uniforms have been told not to touch," Lin Hao said quietly. "Watch. The room will tell us before he does."

The manager came, polite and firm — could not divulge staff information. The owner came, polite and firmer — would not divulge employee privacy no matter what. The young man narrowed his golden eyes.

"So you won't agree no matter what."

The owner nodded.

"Fine." He rose. He snapped his fingers. A finely dressed man stood from a nearby table, approached, and bowed slightly. "Young master. Your command?"

"Negotiate with the owner. I want to buy this drink shop."

Xie Xie's mouth corner twitched. Wulin sighed the sigh of a boy from a town where money was for flour and forge-coal. "These city people sure know how to play."

The butler — Sun Bo, he introduced himself, with a smile and a golden card passed low across the owner's palm — led Boss Yang aside. One look at the card and the owner's eyes had gone wide. They were gone perhaps two minutes. When they came back the butler spoke with the measured disinterest of a man ordering water: "Young master, I've negotiated a price with Boss Yang, and payment has already been sent. From now on, this drink shop is your property."

The shops around Shrek Academy had property values that did not bear thinking about. The young man did not bear thinking about them. He flicked his gaze back to Boss Yang.

"Give me her number."

Across the table, Gu Yue did something she rarely did. She made a sound — a single, cold, perfectly audible *snort*, the kind that came out of the nose and said more than a paragraph.

"Money," she said, to no one and therefore to everyone, "is the laziest kind of wanting. He doesn't want her. He wants the thing she said no to, to not be allowed to say no." She watched the golden-haired boy with eyes the color of winter sky. "If he'd crossed a single thing in his life instead of buying it, he'd know that a no is not a wall. It's a door that opens inward. And it is *never* for sale."

Lin Hao looked at her then, fully, because that had not been a comment about the boy in the white suit, and they both knew it, and she had built it so carefully that no one else at the table could have proved it. It was a thing said about a stranger, in the abstract, about the futility of buying what had to be given — and it landed a hand's breadth from the locked heading in his own ledger, and he did not let himself look at where it landed.

Wulin only shrugged. "It seems money can sway anyone."

Xiaoyan, who had been watching not the farce but the people at her own table, said nothing at all, and her eyes went once, slowly, from Gu Yue's profile to Ye Xinglan's back, and then to Lin Hao, and then down into her juice.

## Part 3: The Pitcher

The red-haired waitress came over to the young man's table a moment later, while Boss Yang stood aside with the face of a man who had just sold a year of his life and was trying to believe the price.

"What do you want?" she said.

He smiled. "Nothing much. I just thought you were pretty, and wanted to get to know you better."

Without a word, she began unbuttoning her apron.

"I didn't tell you to undress!" he yelped, genuinely flabbergasted.

She snorted, and threw the apron down on the table in front of him. "I quit. So I'm not your employee anymore." And she turned and walked toward the door.

The young man's smile widened. He stood, mirroring her. "Even better!" — and went after her shadow.

The table watched them go. And at Lin Hao's elbow, Ye Xinglan leaned forward the way she leaned into everything: all at once, having already decided.

"He spent a fortune," she said, half to Lin Hao, half to the room, "and she walked out anyway, and the only thing he bought was a drink shop he doesn't want." Her Stargod Sword was dim at her hip, but her voice had the edge on it that her blade did. "People who can't hear a *no* are the same in a shop or on a sparring ground. They keep buying another attempt at the same wall." She turned her head and looked right at him. "I told you I'm not the same swordswoman I was four months ago. I'd be happy to show you. Tomorrow, if you like — the practice yards are empty at dawn."

Lin Hao opened his mouth to answer.

He did not get to answer.

His juice cup was empty — it had been empty for a while; he had been doing most of the listening. Ye Xinglan, sitting on his right, was already reaching for the pitcher, her hand halfway there with the easy readiness of a girl who decided things and then did them. It was a small courtesy, nothing, a neighbor refilling a neighbor's cup.

A thread of frost moved first.

Nobody saw it move. It simply happened: the pitcher on the table was in Gu Yue's hand — she had not stood, she had barely shifted — and Lin Hao's cup was filling, the juice rising steady to the rim, poured by a girl who was not looking at him, who was looking at Ye Xinglan with the mild, pleasant expression of someone discussing the weather.

The water that went into his cup came out a degree too cold. By the time it reached the halfway mark, a lace of ice had bloomed across the pitcher's rim and was climbing down its side. By the time she set it down, the surface of his juice had a thin skin of silver across it, cracking softly as it settled.

"Thank you," Lin Hao said.

Gu Yue said nothing. Which was normal. Gu Yue said nothing most of the time; her silences were her sentences, and he had spent two years learning to read them like a man reads tides.

Except that the pitcher was rimed white with frost. Except that she had never once, in two years, poured him anything. She sat *beside* him, she put her hand on his arm when the moment was nine days old, she told him the truth about himself in three words and left him to assemble the rest — but she did not pour. She did not do small, visible, claiming things in front of other people.

She had done it now. She had done it with the frost on, and put the pitcher down between him and Ye Xinglan like a fence-post driven into the table, and then she looked back at her melting ice cream and did not say a word about any of it.

Lin Hao looked at the silver skin over his cup.

He looked at Gu Yue's profile — the black hair, the winter-sky eyes fixed on a dessert she had not eaten, the faintest line between her brows that meant she was angry at something she refused to name.

And he did not read it.

He *would not*. He had built the heading; he had honored the nothing; he was not going to be the man who watches a girl who once said nothing and decides, in the warm light of a drink shop with another girl's chair pulled up beside him, that perhaps the silence had not meant what he had decided it meant. To read the frost as a message addressed to him would be to hope. He had hoped once, plainly, six strokes of a sentence with no wind before it — *I love you* — and she had answered it with nothing, and a nothing honored was a no he could live with, and a no reopened was a wound he would not hand her the power to give him twice.

So he watched the frost climb the pitcher, and he thought: *the elements answer her. It is not addressed to me. I will not be the person who assumes every cold thing in her vicinity is a letter with my name on it.*

It was the most disciplined thing he had ever failed to see.

Ye Xinglan looked at the pitcher. Then she looked at Gu Yue. The Stargod Sword at her hip was not quiet now — it was wary, the way it was wary in the presence of an elementalist it could not measure; her own three rings, even the thousand-year purple she had fought so hard for, meant nothing against a girl whose mere mood was freezing glassware without a single ring lit. Ye Xinglan was a swordsman; swordsmen understood claims in the way other people understood weather, because a blade in a room *was* a claim. She read the frost-rimed pitcher, the melting ice cream, the winter eyes. She read it all in about three seconds, because she decided things quickly.

She decided that the boy she had walked across a room for had someone. She also decided that she did not, in fact, know that, because he had not looked at the silver-haired girl once during any of it — not with the look she was watching for. And she decided a third thing: that this was not a wall she had any interest in buying attempts at, regardless.

She settled back in her chair and left the dawn invitation on the table, unpressed, the way a real swordsman leaves a strike offered and not forced.

"Dawn's fine," she said. "If you're there."

"I'll be there," Lin Hao said. He said it the way he said things about sword-work, plainly, because a yard at dawn was the one thing in this room that did not need a cipher. "Bring the sword you used in thirty-one seconds. I'd like to meet it again — it's had four months to decide things."

Across the table, very quietly, the ice on Lin Hao's juice cup cracked.

## Part 4: The Ripple at the Door

"I can't believe something like that happens in Shrek City," Xie Xie said, already on his feet, because Xie Xie's body consistently made decisions about justice roughly two seconds before his brain had the paperwork ready. "If that's tolerated, then what isn't?"

He went after the waitress and the golden boy, out the door.

"Xie Xie — wait—" Wulin, captain to his bones, was up and moving too, pausing only to throw bills down on the table with the pained efficiency of a man who knew, in his soul, that even justice should settle its tab. "At least let me—" and he was out the door after Xie Xie.

Lin Hao was standing. He had not decided to stand; the margin stood when the captain moved, the way a compass needle moves north without being consulted. Lizhi was already peering anxiously after Wulin, the food bond thrumming. Xiaoyan had one hand on the table, ready to go.

It was at the door that it happened.

Tang Wulin, half a step outside, stopped dead — and Lin Hao, right behind him, felt it pass over all of them like the shadow of a bird the size of a weather system: a wave of soul power, enormous, the air going thick and then electric, and outside in the street the night itself seemed to flinch. Golden light climbed the sky over the orange-lit town, rising slow and inexorable, and where it touched the dark it *dispelled* the shadows — not lighting them, not fighting them, but erasing the night the way daylight erases a fog, as though something with a holy disregard for darkness had simply opened a door on the far end of the street.

The drink shop went quiet.

"Someone," Lin Hao said, very softly, into the golden-lit hush, "just hit the ground in the outer court with a soul power that turns shadows off."

He could not feel the rank of it. It was above his instrument the way the sky was above a weather vane — the vane spun, it did not measure. Beside him, Gu Yue had gone perfectly still, and for the first time all evening her face was not a winter he was refusing to read; it was a blank page turned toward the light, and he could feel her doing the thing she did when she met something that was not in her catalog: going quiet, and deep, and vast, the way the sea goes quiet before it decides whether it is going to be weather.

The frost was gone from the table. She had pulled every thread of it back into herself the way a swordsman sheathes a blade.

"We should see," said Xiaoyan, who was already moving.

Lin Hao reached the door in two long steps, Wulin and Xie Xie already visible out on the street, the golden light pouring down over both of them, over the orange lights, over the whole outer-court town that had been so warm and ordinary one breath ago and was now a stage.

He went. He went the way he always went — in the margin, a half-step behind the captain, watching where the entrances were. And as he crossed the threshold of the drink shop, over his shoulder, the frost-rimed pitcher sat on the table in the emptying booth, and Ye Xinglan sat beside it, watching the door the tall boy had gone out of, and the silver-haired girl had gone through it half a step ahead of him, so that she was the first one into the golden light.

She had not poured him anything else. She had not needed to.

The pitcher had said it.

*She put frost in his cup and I told myself it was the weather in her. I have spent two years teaching myself not to read the silences of the one person I cannot read, because I read the one that mattered and she gave it back to me empty. I will not say it twice. I will not open it twice. But the pitcher came out of her hand before she decided to move it, and the frost in this family does not do anything she does not mean. I have written that down in the book she will never read, and I have written it as a question about weather, because that is the only category I am allowed to put it in, and I have put a line under it, and I am not going to look at the line.*

*Outside the door the night is gold. I will deal with the golden light first.*

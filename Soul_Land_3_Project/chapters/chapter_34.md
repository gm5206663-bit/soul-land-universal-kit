## Part 1: The Letter From the Association

Wu Zhangkong had left him with a stroke he could not name and no instruction at all about what to do next, which was, Lin Hao had begun to understand, the instruction.

It came to the academy, which was unusual, and it was addressed to Lin Hao, which was more unusual, and Xie Xie intercepted it, which was the most unusual thing about it and the reason he knew the contents before Lin Hao did.

"It's from the Association," Xie Xie said, at breakfast, holding it at arm's length like something that might be warm.

"Open it."

"I *opened* it. That's what I'm telling you. I opened it and I read it and then I closed it again and I have been having feelings about it for eleven minutes." He put it on the table. "They want you to sit the third-rank examination. On Saturday. And there's a sentence at the bottom I've read six times and I still don't like."

Lin Hao read it.

The body of the letter was ordinary — the Eastsea Branch invited him to sit the examination for the third rank, Grandmaster, on the coming Saturday, and would provide material, forge and tooling. The sentence at the bottom was not ordinary.

*The Branch has been advised that your recent work has attracted attention beyond the Branch, and the examination will be observed by a visitor from the head office. This is a formality.*

"That's not a formality," Lin Hao said.

"That's what I've been *feeling,*" Xie Xie said. "For eleven minutes."

"It says 'a formality' the way a man says 'it's nothing' while he's holding his arm." Lin Hao put the letter down. "Somebody at the head office has read something about me, and they're not going to tell me what, and they've put a man in a room on Saturday to find out."

"So don't go."

"I'm going."

"Why?"

"Because I want the third rank," Lin Hao said, simply. "And because if I don't go, the man from the head office still finds out whatever he's finding out, and I'd rather be in the room holding a hammer when he does."

## Part 2

He kept his sleeves down in the examination room, not because the marks were a secret but because nine candidates and a man from the head office did not need one more thing to look at while he worked.
: What Mu Chen Said

Mu Chen was in the side forge, and had been expecting him, and had two bars of spirit steel on the anvil and a cup of tea that had gone cold, which meant he had been standing there a while.

"You got the letter."

"I got the letter."

"Sit down." Mu Chen did not sit down. "I'm going to tell you what the third-rank examination is, because you have never asked and I have been waiting four months for you to ask, and I've decided you're not going to."

"I wasn't going to."

"You weren't going to." Mu Chen picked up one of the bars and turned it in his hands the way Lin Hao turned things in his hands, and Lin Hao noticed that, and Mu Chen noticed him noticing, and neither of them said anything. "Second rank is: can you do it. Third rank is: can you do it *when it goes wrong.* Every smith alive can refine a bar that behaves. The examination gives you one that doesn't."

"Deliberately?"

"Deliberately. They hand you material with a fault in it and they don't tell you where, and you have four hours, and what they are actually examining is the moment you find out. Some men find it in ten minutes and some find it at three hours and fifty and some never find it and hit it, and the ones who hit it are the ones we send home." He put the bar down. "You will find it immediately. That's not the test."

"Then what's the test."

"The test," Mu Chen said, "is what you do in the four hours *after* you've found it, when you already know the answer and you still have to do the work. Because a man who knows where the fault is and rushes it makes a worse bar than a man who doesn't know and goes carefully. I have failed eleven third-rank candidates and nine of them failed because they were *clever.*"

Lin Hao thought about that for a while, and recognised it, because it was the same shape as everything else that had happened to him this month.

"So it's the audience thing again," he said.

"I have no idea what that means."

"Neither do I, properly. But I've been told it twice this week by two different men and I'm starting to think it's the only thing anybody's actually trying to teach me." Lin Hao picked up the second bar. "Can I practise on this."

"You can practise on both." Mu Chen finally sat down. "And Lin Hao. There's a man coming from the head office, and I've known you since you were nine and could barely lift a hammer, and I want you to hear this from me rather than work it out on Saturday. **You are better than me.** You have been for about a year. I asked to strike your edge in front of the whole Branch because I wanted them to see it before I had to admit it, and I have never once said it out loud, so I'm saying it now, and I'd like you to not make a face."

Lin Hao made a face.

"I told you."

"You *told* me and I still made it."

## Part 3: Saturday

The examination room had three forges, nine candidates and one observer, and the observer sat at the back with a board on his knee and did not speak to anybody, and was perhaps forty, and had the specific stillness of a man who had watched a great many people fail at things.

They gave Lin Hao a bar.

He knew where the fault was in about four seconds, because he always knew, and it was a sulphur pocket sitting deep on the far side of the centre, and the interesting thing — the thing he would think about afterwards — was that he felt a small, ugly, entirely recognisable impulse to *show* that he knew. To find it fast. To be seen finding it fast. There was a man at the back with a board, and part of him wanted the board to have something to write.

He put that part down, the way he had been putting it down all month, and started at the wrong end of the bar on purpose.

Four hours is a long time when you already know the answer.

He worked the near third properly. He worked the middle third properly, and slowly, and it cost him nothing except time, and the time was the entire point. And somewhere around the second hour he understood what Mu Chen had been trying to say, which was not that clever men rush, exactly, but that **knowing removes the reason to be careful, and care is the only thing that makes the work good.** He had spent four years being the person who knows. He had never once had to consider that knowing might be a *liability* rather than an advantage, and here it was, in a room with three forges, in the form of an impulse to be seen.

At three hours he reached the pocket.

He opened it the way he had opened a hundred of them — along the grain, following the seam, letting the metal decide — and the sulphur came out clean and the bar closed over itself without a mark, and he had ninety minutes left, and he used every one of them on the finish, because the finish was the part nobody examined and the part he actually cared about.

And then, at about three hours and forty, the lightning came.

## Part 4: What the Lightning Did

It was not a decision. That was the thing he noticed first and the thing he was still turning over a week later.

He was on the last pass, drawing the edge down, and the hawk — nine hundred and thirty-four years old and entirely unconsulted — did something, and the thin blue line that had been running along his sword since the crossing ran along the *hammer* instead, and went into the bar, and the edge did not so much sharpen as **arrive.**

He had not laid a Gale Talon. He had not done anything at all. He had been hammering, and the lightning had been there because the lightning lived in him now, and the steel had taken it the way steel takes heat.

The edge came out wrong.

Not bad. *Wrong* — in a way he had never seen and could not immediately name. It was too keen. The geometry was the geometry he had drawn and the finish was the finish he had planned, and the edge would hold an edge the way a rumour holds, and there was a faint blue in it that caught the forge light and gave it back a half-second late.

Lin Hao stood there with a hammer in his hand and a bar on the anvil and understood, with total clarity, that he had just done something in front of nine witnesses and a man from the head office that he could not explain, and could not repeat, and had not intended.

*Well,* he thought. *That's the month I've been having.*

He did not hide it. That was the decision he did make, and he made it in about a second, and he made it because hiding a bar was not possible and because he had decided in a corridor eleven days ago that the truth was cheaper than the alternative and would still be cheap in ten years.

So he put the bar on the examination table with the other eight, and it sat there among bars that were merely good, and it was very slightly blue, and the man at the back stopped writing.

---

## Part 5: The Third Rank

They called them in one at a time in the evening, which was theatre, and Lin Hao knew it was theatre, and he went in last because he had gone in first at everything else for four years and somebody had decided to change it.

"Third rank," the examiner said, before he had sat down. "Grandmaster. Your bar was the only one of the nine that the panel could not agree about, and I want to be precise about what that means. Six of us said it was the best bar in the room. Two said it was defective and should be remade. One said it was the best bar he had seen in thirty years and that he wanted to know how, and I was that one." He closed a folder. "So: how."

"I don't know," Lin Hao said.

"That's not an answer I can put in a file."

"It's the only answer I've got." Lin Hao looked at the table, because looking at the man felt like performing, and he had been given a great deal of advice about performing this month. "I was drawing the edge and something came out of my hands and went into the steel, and the edge came out keener than I drew it, and I've been trying for eleven days to do it again on a sword and I can't, and the only thing I can tell you is that it happens when I'm not doing it on purpose."

The examiner looked at him for a long moment.

"That," he said, "is either the most honest answer I've had in this room or the least useful, and I've decided it's both." He wrote something. "You're third rank. The Branch will register it this week and it'll go to the head office with the rest, and I want you to understand that I argued for you on the bar and not on the answer, because the answer would get you thrown out of some rooms." He stood up. "And for what it's worth — I've been examining for nineteen years and I've never passed a bar I couldn't explain. You're the first. I'd like to think that means something good."

---

Wulin came with him, which Lin Hao had not asked for and had wanted.

He sat on the bench outside the examination room for four hours with his back against the wall and his eyes closed, and did not come in, and did not say anything through the door, and when Lin Hao came out with a third-rank certificate in his hand and a great deal to think about, Wulin opened one eye and said, "Well?"

"Third rank."

"I know. I could hear the hammers." Wulin stood up and stretched. "You went quiet at the end. About forty minutes of going quiet. That's the part I was waiting for."

"I did something I can't explain."

"I know." Wulin started walking, and Lin Hao fell into step beside him, which was the arrangement and had been since they were six. "You always do. And then you explain it three weeks later and it turns out to be obvious and I never would have got there. It's extremely annoying and I've made my peace with it." He glanced sideways. "Your hair's standing up."

"It does that."

"I know it does that." Wulin said it easily, the way he said everything, and Lin Hao understood that this was the whole of what the last fortnight had been missing — not a conversation about the lightning, not a speech about the file, just somebody walking next to him who had known what his hair did since it started doing it.

Gu Yue was at the gate when they got back, sitting on the stone with her knees drawn up, which she never did, and she did not get up when they arrived.

"Third rank," Wulin said.

"I heard the hammers too," Gu Yue said. "I came to see whether he'd come out looking like he'd won something." She looked at Lin Hao for a moment. "You don't. You look like somebody who's been given a bill."

"That's exactly what it felt like."

"Good." She got up. "Then you'll pay it, and you'll stop looking at yourself from the outside, which is what you've been doing for a fortnight, and which I have found extremely tiring to watch."

## Part 6: The Man From the Head Office

He was waiting in the corridor, which was rude, and Lin Hao noticed that it was rude, and then noticed that he had noticed, and put that down too.

"Lin Hao."

"Yes."

"I'm not going to talk to you about the bar." The man from the head office had a voice like a closed drawer. "I watched four hours of a ten-year-old doing third-rank work and I have put down what I saw and it will go in a file, and the file will be read by people whose job is to read files, and that is the whole of it, and I want you to understand that nothing is going to happen to you on Saturday's account."

"Then why are you in the corridor."

"Because of the other file." He said it without any inflection at all. "There is a file about you that has nothing to do with the Association. It came to us as a courtesy copy, which is not a thing that happens, and I have read it twice, and I have come down here on a Saturday to watch a child hit metal because I wanted to see whether the file was describing a person or a rumour." He looked at Lin Hao for the first time with anything like interest. "It's describing a person."

"What does it say."

"It says a great many things, and I'm not going to repeat any of them, and I'm going to tell you why, and it's the same reason your teacher won't name the city he came from." He buttoned his coat. "Because the moment you know exactly what people believe about you, you start managing it, and the moment you start managing it you stop being the thing they were interested in. So: there's a file, it's accurate, it's not going to hurt you, and I'd rather you didn't know the contents." He went past, and stopped. "One thing. Not from the file. From me, having watched you for four hours."

"Yes."

"You spent ninety minutes on a finish nobody was going to examine." The drawer of a voice opened a fraction. "In nineteen years I have never seen a candidate do that. Everybody else in that room was making a bar to pass an examination. You were making a bar. That's the part I'd keep, if I were you, because it's the only part of you that isn't in anybody's file."

He went out into the street, and Lin Hao stood in the corridor of the Eastsea Branch with a third-rank certificate in his hand that he had not been given yet and a great deal to think about, and thought: *that's three men in a month telling me the same thing in three different ways.*

Wu Zhangkong: you perform with your eyes.

Mu Chen: nine of eleven candidates failed because they were clever.

And now a man with a voice like a drawer: you were making a bar.

"Right," Lin Hao said, to the corridor. "I've got it. Stop being watched. Including by me."

## Part 1: The Weld

The idea came to Lin Hao the way forge ideas came — not as a thought, but as an itch in the hands.

He found Wulin still awake, because Wulin was always still awake, the slate on his knees glowing with class-one arithmetic: *the dragon goes spectral a quarter at a time. The eagle folds over him. The fusion made a sound. Do not let them touch.* And Lin Hao sat down on the other bed and said, "That last line's wrong."

"It's the whole plan."

"It's half a plan. 'Don't let them touch' assumes we can keep three drilled two-rings apart for a whole match on a platform the size of a dinner table." He turned the slate around with one finger and drew, over Wulin's careful lines, the thing his hands had been itching to say since the gate — since the seven notes, since the two strokes, since a teacher had told him his sword needed to learn certainty. "They have a fusion skill. We've *seen* it eat a three-ring combination. Tomorrow we fight it. And the grade's entire strategy is *don't let them touch.*" He tapped the slate. "So I say we stop guessing what a fusion is, and go find out what one *feels* like. From the inside."

Wulin stared at him. "You want to *test* for fusion compatibility. Us. Tonight."

"Martial soul fusion happens when two souls are compatible enough to speak with one voice — Xie Xie told us that himself, the night of the scales; the boy reads a battle book like other people eat candy." Lin Hao shrugged. "Tests cost nothing. Two souls released, close, soul power circulated — the books say compatible souls *resonate.* And if we feel what resonance is — the seam where two voices become one — then tomorrow, when the eagle folds over the dragon—"

"—you want to know where the weld is," Wulin said slowly, "so you can break it."

"A weld is just two pieces of metal that agreed to be one piece." Lin Hao held up his hand, thumb and forefinger a hair apart. "Father taught me to break them the same way you make them: heat, timing, and the seam. You don't pry a weld apart. You *interrupt* it — before it cools." He let the silence hold a moment, and then said the other half, the quieter half, in the voice he kept for the file Wulin was the only other keeper of: "And there's something else. My soul *adapts,* Wulin. It adapted to a ninety-year hawk until the compatibility wasn't a number anymore — it was a *person,* living in my spiritual sea, thinking with me. If it can do that with a soul spirit…" He spread his hands. "Maybe it lifts numbers that are supposed to be fixed. Maybe any of us — you and me, me and Xie Xie — maybe we're standing on compatibility we've never measured. Or maybe—"

"Or maybe?"

"Or maybe it gives results the books don't have a name for."

Wulin looked at him for a long moment — the look of a boy who had watched that particular soul do unnamed things to a scale of gold — and swung his legs off the bed. "Let's wake the dagger."

Xie Xie did not need waking; Xie Xie had been lying awake *hoping.* He arrived in four seconds flat with his dagger already out and the fusion-skill speech already assembled ("—do you know what it *means* if we have one? There is NO ONE in Eastsea City—"), and Gu Yue arrived in the doorway behind him with her robe over her arm and her expression packed for refusal.

"What," she said, "is happening."

"Testing!" Xie Xie sang. "For fusion! You and me first—"

"No."

The flatness of it stopped even Xie Xie. Gu Yue did not refuse things. Gu Yue *selected* things, and refusal was simply the sound a selection made. "I'm watching," she said, and sat on the windowsill like a cat that had chosen its evening, "but I am not releasing my soul into a — experiment." Her eyes went, for one unreadable instant, to Lin Hao, and away.

Lin Hao filed the look without examining it. Examining was for later; the shelf was for now.

They went down to the training yard because rooms had ceilings and ceilings had opinions, and the yard was empty except for the lamps and, it developed, their teacher — because Wu Zhangkong lived on their floor, and Wu Zhangkong heard everything on their floor, and the white robes were already standing under the far lamp with the expression of a man deciding between punishment and curiosity and arriving, as always, at neither.

"Fusion compatibility testing," he said, coldly, before anyone had spoken. "The night before the largest match of your lives."

"The night *before* is exactly right," Lin Hao said. "Tomorrow we fight a fusion. Tonight I'd like my sword to have met one."

Something moved behind the ice — not warmth; *interest,* which was colder. "Proceed," said Wu Zhangkong, and folded his arms under the lamp, and watched the way a man watches metal he intends to judge.

Xie Xie went first, because Xie Xie went first at everything. The Light Dragon Dagger came out singing — that thin bright note — and the Stormbringer answered it the way the Stormbringer answered everything, wind rising along the blade in a lazy silver ribbon, and the two boys stood a meter apart circulating soul power the way the books said, and for six breaths nothing happened at all.

And then the notes *met.*

It was not loud. It was the opposite of loud — a thinning, a clarification, the way a room goes quiet when two people start speaking the same language. The dagger's light leaned. The sword's lightning leaned back. Where the two leaned, the air between the blades developed a *grain,* fine as hair, running from hilt to hilt, and Xie Xie's eyes went enormous.

"I feel that," he whispered. "Wulin, I *feel* that—"

"I can see it," Wu Zhangkong said from under the lamp, and his voice had lost its tribunal tone entirely. He crossed to them in two strides and stood inside the resonance the way a smith stands close to a forge, reading. "Sword and light-attribute tool soul. Non-kin, non-elemental-match." A pause with ice in it. "You two are standing in a doorway that should not exist. Children your age, souls your age — this grain should not be possible."

"Is it a fusion?" Xie Xie breathed.

"It is a *door,*" Wu Zhangkong said. "Not the room. Your soul power couldn't open it if you both burned it to the wick — a fusion skill takes years of joint cultivation and a spiritual power you do not have." His smoky green eyes went from Xie Xie to Lin Hao and stopped, and stayed a beat too long. "Compatibility is supposed to be fixed at awakening. It is a number written in the soul." Another beat. "Some souls, apparently, edit."

The blade went back into the air before the sentence finished landing, and the wind went back to being wind, and Lin Hao stood very still with his heart doing forge-work, because the teacher had just said *edit* in the tone of a man filing something away forever.

Wulin went next.

The Bluesilver Grass rose gold-veined in the lamplight, and the Stormbringer came up beside it, and Lin Hao — who had expected the grass to lean, because wind and grass were the oldest conversation there was — felt instead the sword go *heavy* in his hand, the way a compass goes heavy over a buried keel.

The resonance did not form. Something else did.

The hawk-eyes, running as they always ran, saw the grain appear — but not between the blades. It ran *lower,* along the ground between their feet, and where it crossed under Wulin it did not thin or clarify; it *spiked,* bright and sudden, in four places up the other boy's body — wrist, shoulder, spine, sternum — like nails of light driven where no martial soul grew. The grass was silent. The *seals* were answering. And under Lin Hao's sleeve the Goldsong writhed against Wulin's wrist in a panic its owner caught and smothered with a fist, and on the far water inside Lin Hao the seven-hundred-and-sixty-year hawk went absolutely, completely still — the stillness of a bird that has seen the shape of something the sky does not have a name for.

"*Enough.*"

Wu Zhangkong's voice came down like a portcullis, and the test died, and the yard was just a yard. The teacher stood between the two of them now, facing neither, his expression the closed door it had been all year. "Testing is concluded for tonight. All of you. Dormitories." And then, to the yard at large, or to the night, or to whatever he kept his own files in: "A compatibility reading is a measure of two martial souls." A silence with frost on it. "I did not just take a measure of two martial souls."

Nobody answered that, because there was nothing to answer it with. On the walk back, in the corridor's last dark stretch, Wulin's hand found Lin Hao's shoulder and squeezed once, hard — the brother-signal, the *I saw you see it* — and neither of them said a word, because the file was full and the words didn't fit, and tomorrow there was a dragon to break.

"You and Xie Xie," Wulin said at his door, very quietly. "That was real."

"That was real," Lin Hao agreed.

"And whatever mine was—"

"Is yours," Lin Hao said. "And it answered me. That's all I'm saying, and all I'm filing." He met his brother's eyes. "The books don't have a name for it. So we'll keep it until they do."

## Part 2: The Dark Eagle Dragon

The stands on the final morning were not stands. They were a city that had taken the day off.

The grade had done its arithmetic all week — the ten seconds, the twenty-one, the sky that had been walked on, the gate that had been held — and the grade had decided, in the way grades decide, that the worst class in the building was going to climb the last rung in front of everyone. The teachers' platform had a visitor row. Long Hengxu stood at the rail like a man hosting weather. And at the near end of it, alone by a full chair's width on either side, stood Ye Yingluo — pale, immaculate, patient as a drawn blade — watching Wu Zhangkong with an expression she had not bothered to disguise since the gate.

*I'll take my price on the platform, where he has to watch.*

Class one walked out to silence, the way they always did. Wang Jinxi at the point, dark and heavy-jawed, the light leaning away from him in the morning sun like water off oil. Zhang Yangzi at his shoulder, blond, watchful, a boy built like a held breath. And Wei Xiaofeng, the third two-ring, whose martial soul none of class five had ever managed to name and whose face, this morning, had the set of a boy who had been told exactly what he was for.

Class five walked out to noise.

"Begin," said Long Hengxu.

And class one did the thing Ye Yingluo had spent a week preparing, which was not a formation and not a probe. It was an execution schedule.

Wei Xiaofeng went straight at Xie Xie — his soul unnamed, its work plain: something heavy and light-swallowing around his forearms that turned the dagger-boy's speed into a debate he kept losing on points. Zhang Yangzi rose, the Shadow Phantasm Eagle folding out of him in a spill of dark feathers that were somehow also *shadow,* and the sky over the platform became his property. And Wang Jinxi walked toward Wulin the way a decision walks toward an argument, the Bone Dragon King moving under his skin like something under dark water, two yellow rings turning slow beneath him.

"They're not feeling us out," Lin Hao said, at walking volume, the Stormbringer already in his hand, the wind already leaning. "They're spending us. Watch the eagle — he's not hunting, he's *herding.*"

Because that was the shape of it, and it took him three exchanges to be sure: Zhang Yangzi's dives were not attacks, they were walls — each one closing a lane, each one pushing class five's pieces two steps closer together, and Wulin two steps closer to Wang Jinxi, and Xie Xie two steps deeper into Wei Xiaofeng's heavy light, and Gu Yue — Gu Yue standing in her cup of a corner, her hands still, her eyes running the whole geometry—

"Now," she said. "Break their line. Any line. They need us *grouped.*"

Lin Hao broke a line. The Stormbringer took Wei Xiaofeng's next heavy strike on a Gale Talon seam and turned it *up,* into the sky, and the light-swallowing gauntlets came apart in a scatter of startled dark — and in the half-second Wei Xiaofeng spent reassembling, the herding geometry lost its corner, and Zhang Yangzi banked wide to rebuild it—

—and Wang Jinxi and Zhang Yangzi touched.

It happened the way the reference books said and the reference books did not say. The eagle folded over the dragon like a wing over a bone. The two souls *met* — and the sound came, the sound Lin Hao's hollow bones remembered from the scouting morning, less a noise than a *chord struck under the world.* The platform's ward hummed. The stands went white-faced and silent. And where two boys had stood, something long and dark and doubled stood — a dragon with an eagle's architecture, shadow running through its scales like current through water, and two rings and two rings becoming a single turning light that made the four of class five's rings look like candles at a funeral.

The Dark Eagle Dragon moved, and the world's temperature went with it.

"LIN HAO!" Wulin roared, from somewhere under the thing's first sweep.

He was already going.

## Part 3: Breaking the Weld

He had met it before, in a way — he had spent the whole night meeting it. Resonance was a grain. A fusion was a *weld:* two voices agreeing, mid-stride, to be one voice; a seam running the length of the thing where the agreement lived. The hawk-eyes found the seam the way they had found it in the yard — thinner than hair, brighter than the souls on either side of it, running from the eagle's folded wing down through the dragon's shoulder, the place where two souls had shaken hands—

—and the Stormbringer was already speaking, because that was the homework, and the homework was *certainty,* and a true sword did not wait for the world to finish its sentence.

One stroke. No feint. No second thought hedging the first. The Gale Talon laid along the full length of the blade, wind and lightning braided into the edge the way they braided into everything he carried, and the sword went *into the seam* — not into flesh, not into scale; into the handshake.

The world *rang.*

And here is the thing that would not have happened in a yard without him. Class five's plan that morning had been to survive the fusion and lose well — that was the plan Wu Zhangkong had not said and everybody had understood, because class one's trio was the top of the ladder and class five was the worst class in the grade, and arithmetic is arithmetic. **Nobody had planned to break the weld, because nobody in that yard thought in welds.** Lin Hao had spent the whole night thinking in welds, because thinking in the shape of a thing is what he does instead of being afraid of it, and the yard had no way to know that until the seam opened.

That is the whole of it. The butterfly is not that he won. It is that **a fight everybody had already scored became a fight nobody had scored,** and the reason was one boy who reads seams, and the yard did not have him in the arithmetic.

The backlash came up the blade like a struck anvil's voice — soul-fire, the two-ring agreement burning where it had been interrupted — and it bit through the wind-guard into Lin Hao's palm, searing a line from thumb to wrist, and his whole right arm went numb to the elbow with the echo of a strength that was not his and had never been his, and the Stormbringer screamed in a register swords do not have—

—and the Dark Eagle Dragon *stuttered.*

The single voice cracked down the middle. For one heartbeat — one long, shining, bought heartbeat — the fusion was two souls again, an eagle stumbling out of a dragon's outline, a boy and a boy with their hands still touching and their faces wearing the identical expression of men who have felt a conversation they were having be *interrupted in a language neither knew existed.*

"NOW!" Lin Hao's voice broke across the platform, walking volume gone, and class five — drilled by nine weeks, sharpened by three matches, agreed in advance on what each of them was for — spent itself all at once.

Xie Xie took Wei Xiaofeng, and the dagger-boy's speed, freed of the herding, became the thing the grade had been calling weather all term — three touches in the time the gauntlets took to rise, and the third two-ring was on one knee with the Light Dragon Dagger's flat against his ear, hearing his own heartbeat negotiated. Gu Yue's hands finally moved — ice under the eagle-boy's landing, the ground declining exactly three degrees, exactly where a shadow-soul's footing mattered most, and Zhang Yangzi's next step arrived somewhere his body had not agreed to go. And Wulin took Wang Jinxi.

The Bone Dragon King converted — a quarter of himself going spectral, the shoulder and the arm and the ribs turning to dark water so the hammer-fist passed through him like breath through a gate — and Wulin, who had watched him do it twice now and had filed it the way his brother filed things, did not check the swing. He *continued* it, the Bluesilver Grass already woven through the spectral quarter's re-materialization window, the golden-veined vines arriving exactly where solid bone was about to be, and Wang Jinxi reformed *into* a fistful of grass and a hammer's momentum—

—and threw his own counter, the Bone Dragon Claw raking up out of the tangle, dark energy sheeting the talons—

—and Wulin's other fist met it.

Stopped. One inch from the dragon-claw. Held there — not blocked, *held,* the way a man holds a door he has decided about — and in the space of that inch, something passed between the two of them that neither had a file for. Wang Jinxi felt his martial soul — the Bone Dragon King, the top-tier dark dragon that drank light and shrugged hammers — *hum.* Answer. Lean toward the fist the way a compass leans north. And Wulin felt it too, the answering heat surging unbidden up from under his second seal, the gold wanting to rise, the bones in his right arm beginning, faintly, to *sing*—

He closed his fist. Shut the heat down like a furnace door. And let the stopped punch stay stopped, one inch from a dragon's claw, in front of the whole plaza.

"Yield," Wulin said. Quietly. The way you say it to someone you might, in some other arithmetic, have been glad to meet.

Wang Jinxi knelt in the grass with his claw still extended and his soul still humming and his face doing something the Bone Dragon King had never once asked it to do — which was wonder. He looked at the stopped fist. He looked at his own trembling hand. And then he laughed — once, short, bewildered — and lowered the claw.

"Yield," he said. "Class one yields."

## Part 4: The Top of the Ladder

"Class five wins."

Long Hengxu's voice did not need the slate this time. He looked at the platform — at the dark dragon boy kneeling in golden grass, at the eagle boy sitting down in a puddle of his own element, at the third two-ring with a dagger's flat still whispering at his ear, at the four small figures in the middle of the largest silence Eastsea Academy had ever produced — and the director of the academy consulted nothing but the morning, and said it again, slower, as if the words needed settling:

"The tournament is concluded. Class five has defeated every class in the first grade." A pause with the ghost of something in it that forty years of assemblies had never once contained. "As of this moment, you are the first grade's *class one.*"

And the grade came apart. The sound that went up off those stands had no arithmetic in it at all — the worst class, the joke, the back of the building, *on top.* The noise rolled over the platform until even the ward hummed along. Zhou Zhangxi began a song that twenty-two throats finished for him, and somewhere in it Xie Xie was hugging people who had not consented, and Gu Yue was being looked at by four separate teachers with four separate expressions, and Wulin stood very still in the middle of it flexing a right hand that had not, he noticed, entirely stopped singing.

On the teachers' platform, Ye Yingluo did not move for a long time. Her price had been sitting on that platform in the shape of a dark-haired boy and an eagle and a fusion, and a small swordsman with a burned palm had walked into the middle of it and *interrupted* — and the two-year wound had learned, this morning, something new about itself, which was that it could wait. It had waited two years. It was, she decided, turning from the rail with her composure intact and her eyes on the white robes one last time, extremely good at waiting. The price would keep. So would she.

Wu Zhangkong watched her go, and said nothing to anyone, and gave his class — his *first* class now, though no one would ever call it that — no words at all on the walk back. Only, at the dormitory door, as four very loud boys and one very quiet one filed past him into the rest of their lives: one finger, stopping Lin Hao's shoulder for exactly as long as a sentence takes.

"The seam," he said. "You read it last night."

"I read a weld," Lin Hao said. "Last night I just learned what it feels like when two souls decide to be one piece." A beat. "The strike was the homework, teacher. You assigned it."

The ice considered him. "A sword that can interrupt a fusion skill is not a sword," Wu Zhangkong said. "It is an argument." And the faintest possible movement at the corner of the cold mouth. "Make yours better than theirs. Go eat."

And later, on the roof, with the harbor doing its nightly accounting and the whole dormitory still singing two floors down, Lin Hao lay back on the tiles with his burned palm open to the stars — the sear already closing, the AT's hungry furnace eating the wound the way it ate everything — and thought about doors.

A door that should not exist, between a sword and a dagger, standing shut and waiting for years neither of them had yet. A reading with no name in it, four nails of light where no martial soul grew, and a brother's hand on his shoulder in a dark corridor. A dragon's soul that had *leaned* toward a stopped fist. A shelf so crowded the files had begun leaning on each other.

And on the far water, the hawk — who had gone still yesterday before something the sky had no name for, and who tonight was doing something it had never done in the whole of their shared life — was, very slowly, very deliberately, preening. Not the wind. Not the dark. *Him:* the turn of an old bird's head following the boy's breathing, the way a teacher watches a student's handwriting finally, finally come out level.

The question lay under all of it, unchanged, unasked, and tonight — for the first time since it had been asked — Lin Hao did not feel the need to answer it. Because somewhere between the yard and the seam, the wind had begun arriving a half-breath *before* he called it. Not much. Not yet. Just enough that the line between asking and answering had gone, like the weld's seam, too thin to see — and a boy lay on a roof at the top of a ladder with his whole life still ahead of him, and did not, tonight, know which side of the line he was on.

He slept like a room where someone had pulled up a chair.

And the chair, tonight, was not watching him sleep.

It was watching the *door.*

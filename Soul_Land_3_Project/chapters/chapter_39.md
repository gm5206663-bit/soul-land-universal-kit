## Part 1: The Hour

"All we need to do in this first hour is survive," Wulin said.

He said it calmly, the way he said most things now, and Lin Hao noticed — because Lin Hao noticed — that he said it without looking at anybody, which was the tell. Wulin looked at people when he was asking. He looked at the trees when he had already decided.

"Captain," Zhang Yangzi said, "should we take the initiative?"

"No."

"There's a hundred-year something forty metres east."

"I know."

Lin Hao knew too. He had known for about ninety seconds, because the hawk had gone still in a particular direction, and he had learned to treat that stillness the way other people treated a shout.

He did not say it, though, because Wulin had already said it, and because there was a thing he had been working out since the dormitory floor three days ago, which was that a formation only works if the shape decides and not if six people do.

So he stayed in his place, at the front, with the sword down, and he let the hour be boring.

It was boring for eleven minutes.

---

## Part 2: What Came Down

The first thing Lin Hao knew was that Little Black went up.

Zhang Yangzi's eagle had been circling at about forty metres — not hunting, just watching, the way Lin Hao had asked it to, because the eagle could see a thing nine seconds before the rest of them could and nine seconds was the entire margin the formation ran on.

Then Zhang Yangzi's face changed.

He did not shout. He went pale in a single movement, the way a lamp goes out, and his body swayed, and he said — very quietly, as though he were apologising — "**Not good. Little Black…**"

They all looked up at the same time, because his voice had done something to them, and they saw the **yellow-green net** coming down out of the trees, and they saw the eagle in it.

It was not a fight. There was nothing to see of a fight. There were a great many limbs in one place, and the eagle was among them, and then the eagle was not.

Lin Hao had seen a spirit soul die exactly once before, in a book, described by a man in an orange robe who had used the word *recoil* and then moved on to the next subject.

He understood the word now.

Zhang Yangzi made a sound. It was not a scream. It was smaller than a scream and much worse, and it came out of a boy who had been, eleven minutes earlier, complaining about a hundred-year something forty metres east.

And his rings went dim. One of the three simply stopped being bright, and Lin Hao watched it happen and understood, with the flat horrible clarity he had come to hate, that **a third of him had just gone.**

"**RETREAT!**" Wulin shouted, and the grass went up.

Gu Yue was already moving. Her spiritual power went out in a gale that Lin Hao felt in his teeth, a wall of moving air forty metres wide, and it was — he understood this instantly, and he understood it the way you understand weather — it was *enough.* It was enough for anything that had ever been in the ledger.

The net came through it.

It did not slow. It did not sway. The wind simply was not a thing that applied.

And Wulin's Bluesilver Grass — the grass that had held a thirteen-year-old with a purple ring, the grass that had owned ground in eleven separate platform runs — touched the net and **began to smoke.**

It smoked the way meat smokes. It curled. The strands that had been golden-threaded and tough as wire went black at the point of contact and the blackness ran up them faster than Lin Hao could count.

"*It's not a net,*" he said, and heard himself say it, and did not remember deciding to.

Eight legs came down out of the trees like eight spears.

## Part 3: The Shape Held

What Lin Hao did next was not brave, and he would have argued with anyone who said it was.

The **marks on his forearm** had come up while he was running, the way they did now whenever the hawk went still — fine **storm-light** lines under the skin, **gold-flecked**, running the length of the bone from wrist to elbow, and they were **glowing** faintly in the shade under the trees. He had learned not to look at them in front of people. He looked now, because the only person who could see them was Gu Yue, and Gu Yue was not going to say anything.

He had three seconds, and in three seconds he did not have time to be frightened, or heroic, or clever, and so he did the thing he had actually practised, which was to say one word to the right person.

"**Door — closed, not open!**"

Zhang Yangzi could not have heard him. Zhang Yangzi was on his knees with his hands in the leaf litter and a third of his cultivation gone out of him like heat out of a room.

But **Wang Jinxi** heard him, and Wang Jinxi had spent three days being told that the door was two boys and not a fusion skill, and Wang Jinxi went low and took Zhang Yangzi by the collar of his uniform with one hand and dragged him backwards eleven metres without ever standing up.

The spear-leg came down where Zhang Yangzi had been.

It went into the ground to the joint.

"**Gu Yue — not the net, the *ground!***"

She understood him before he finished, which was the thing about Gu Yue that nobody in the class had ever said out loud: she was not fast because she was quick, she was fast because she had already decided. The ground under the spider's two front legs went soft, and then went hard, and the legs that had been about to come out of the earth found that the earth had changed its mind about them.

It bought four seconds. It did not buy more, because the spider was a hundred years old and had been doing this since before any of them were born, and it simply pulled harder.

"**Xie Xie,**" Wulin said, and his voice had gone into the register Lin Hao had only heard twice before, "**if I'm unable to break free, you're the acting captain.**"

"Wulin—"

"**Say it.**"

"I'd be the acting captain," Xie Xie said. He did not make a joke. Lin Hao filed that, because Xie Xie not making a joke was itself a fact about the world.

Wulin went in.

---

## Part 4: What It Cost

Lin Hao would remember the next ninety seconds for the rest of his life, and he would never be able to describe them properly, because they happened in a shape that language does not have.

Wulin's grass went into the spider and the spider's blood went onto the grass, and the grass **smoked**, and Wulin kept going anyway, and at some point — Lin Hao could not afterwards say when — **Goldlight came out of him** and went into the grass, and the grass stopped smoking.

It stopped smoking because it was no longer only grass. The strands went gold and scaled and thick, and they held, and the cost of holding was written on Wulin's face in a way Lin Hao had never seen written on anybody.

He was spending himself. Not soul power. *Himself.*

And then Wulin recalled Goldlight — pulled the snake back out of the grass with an effort Lin Hao could see in his shoulders — because he needed the soul power for his hand, and his hand was going to be the part that ended it.

The Golden Dragon Claw came up.

It was three inches of mirror-smooth claw under a hand that had stopped being a ten-year-old's hand, and it went into the Man-Faced Demon Spider, and the spider's yellow-green blood went everywhere.

Lin Hao was four metres away and it did not touch him, because Gu Yue had put a wall of moving air between them at some point in the last ninety seconds and had not told anybody, and because Wulin's own arm had gone gold and scaled and the blood ran off it instead of into it.

The spider came apart.

Not dramatically. It came apart the way a thing comes apart when the part holding it together stops being there.

---

## Part 5: No Room For Luck

Wang Jinxi hugged Wulin.

He did it with both arms and no dignity at all, and he was still holding Zhang Yangzi's collar with one hand, so all three of them went over sideways into the leaf litter, and Wang Jinxi did not let go.

"That was an incredible victory," Zhang Yangzi said, from the ground, in the voice of a boy who had been somewhere none of them had been and had come back with less than he left with.

It echoed what everybody was thinking, and nobody said anything, because there was a body eight metres away and a boy in the middle of them who was not going to be the same for a while.

Lin Hao sat down with his back against a tree, because his legs had decided the fight was over before his head had, and he did the thing he always did, which was to go through it in order and find the seam.

**The net came down on Little Black because Little Black was in the sky.**

That was the whole of it. The eagle was the one part of the formation that went *up*, and the spider had put a net in the trees, and the net had been waiting for the one thing that flew.

He had asked the eagle to circle.

He had asked it, by name, three days ago, in a dormitory, and he had thought it was the cleverest part of the shape, because nine seconds was the entire margin the formation ran on — and the cleverest part of the shape had been the part with a net in it.

"Wulin," he said.

Wulin was sitting with his head between his knees and his scaled hand going slowly back to being a hand. "Mn."

"Little Black died because I put it in the sky."

"No."

"I put it in the sky."

"You put it in the sky," Wulin agreed, tiredly, "and it saw the net, and it told us, and we're all alive. That's what it was *for.*"

"That's not—"

"That is exactly what it was for." Wulin lifted his head. His face was the colour of old paper and his eyes were entirely steady. "Lin Hao. You built the shape. The shape had a hole in it. The hole cost him a third of himself and it cost the rest of us nothing, because the hole was in the part that sees. If the hole had been anywhere else we'd be dead." He looked at Zhang Yangzi, who had stopped pretending not to listen. "Ask him whether he'd rather have had the nine seconds."

Linhao looked at Zhang Yangzi.

Zhang Yangzi looked back at him for a long time.

"I'd rather have had the nine seconds," he said.

---

## Part 6: What Lin Hao Wrote

They passed. They had been in for seventy-one minutes when they came out, and the hour had been the whole of what the exam asked for, and the man at the door checked a board and nodded.

Nobody celebrated. Wang Jinxi was holding Zhang Yangzi up on the steps outside, and Zhang Yangzi was letting him, which was the most surprising thing that had happened all day.

Lin Hao sat down on the lowest step and opened the ledger.

He went to the column marked *WHAT THEY WANT*, and he found that his hand did not want to write in it, and he wrote anyway, because that was the whole discipline and the discipline was the only thing he had.

*A net waits for the thing that flies. I asked the eagle to fly. Nine seconds is not worth a third of anybody.*

Then he stopped, and looked at it, and crossed out the last line.

Not because it was wrong. Because it was incomplete, and an incomplete line in the ledger was worse than no line at all.

He wrote it again.

*A net waits for the thing that flies. I asked the eagle to fly. Nine seconds bought five lives and cost one boy a third of himself, and he says he would do it again, and I believe him, and I am not going to ask him to.*

He closed the book.

The hawk turned over in him — *nine hundred and sixty-four years* of something that had, forty minutes ago, gone very still in a particular direction and been right — and Lin Hao sat on a step outside the Spirit Pagoda with the sun going down over a city that was second of five in an alliance he had learned about from a free pamphlet, and thought about a boy who had said *I'd rather have had the nine seconds,* and about the fact that he had just learned something that had cost somebody else a third of his cultivation to teach him.

That was the part he did not like.

He liked the learning. He had liked it since he was four years old and his father had let him hold a bar that was too hot. He had never once, in his life, minded the price of knowing something.

He minded this.

"You're doing the face," Wulin said, sitting down next to him.

"What face."

"The one where you've learned something and you're deciding whether to be pleased."

Lin Hao considered lying, and did not, because they had an arrangement.

"I'm not pleased."

"Good," Wulin said. He leaned back on his hands and looked at the sky, where there was nothing, because the eagle was not up there any more. "That's the first time you haven't been."

## Part 1: The Walk Back

The warmth started in Wulin's spine about halfway down the hill.

Lin Hao noticed it before Wulin did, because he had spent four years noticing things slightly before the people they were happening to, and because the sign was not on Wulin's face. It was in his walk. Wulin had a way of going down a slope that involved his shoulders arriving a half-beat before the rest of him, and the shoulders had stopped arriving.

"Wulin."

"Mm."

"You're swaying."

"I'm not—" and then he was, badly, and Gu Yue caught him, which was the second time in two days she had put her arms under him and held up something heavier than she looked able to.

"Wulin, what's going on?"

"I don't know," Wulin said, in the voice of a boy whose mouth was already elsewhere. "I just feel really sleepy."

And then he went, completely and without dignity, into her arms.

Wu Zhangkong was in front of him before the second breath, with two fingers on his wrist, and his face did the thing it did when something had arrived that he had not scheduled. Long Hengxu came up behind and looked, and his gaze went sharp and attentive in a way Lin Hao had only seen once before, at an assembly, when a number had been read out that nobody had expected.

"He's fine," Wu Zhangkong said, and hefted Wulin onto his back like a sack of coal. "It's just that his spirit soul is evolving."

"Ah?" said four people at once.

## Part 2

The lock of storm-gray at his temple was long enough now that it fell across his cheekbone when he looked down, and he had started pushing it back without thinking, forty times a day, the way other boys pushed back ordinary hair.
: One-Tenth

They walked, and Wu Zhangkong explained, and Lin Hao listened the way he listened to everything — with the part of his mind that kept ledgers running whether he wanted it to or not.

"His spirit soul is naturally weak," Wu Zhangkong said, "so it's easier for it to evolve."

He went through it slowly, because Xie Xie had already told them the shape of it on the way in and had called it a possibility, and because a possibility that had happened to somebody standing next to you stopped being a possibility.

"For every soul beast you kill in the spirit ascension platform, you obtain one-tenth of its cultivation energy. That energy is spread evenly among your soul rings." He said *evenly* the way a man says a word he wants you to keep. "Kill a ten-year beast, your spirit soul gains a year. A hundred and ten years becomes a hundred and eleven. It accumulates, and when it reaches a thousand, it evolves. Wulin's spirit soul is ten years old. He took at least twenty years from the hundred-year beast and a hundred from the thousand-year one, and he has *one ring,* so all of it went into that one ring. Ten years became a hundred."

He shifted Wulin on his back.

"The rest of you will need to kill eighteen or nineteen thousand-year soul beasts."

Nobody said anything for a while, because that was a number you had to sit down inside.

"Because we've got two rings," Xie Xie said, at last.

"Because you have two rings. The energy splits. Each ring takes five percent." Wu Zhangkong did not soften it, which was why they all believed him. "And finding that many thousand-year beasts in the elementary platform is not a matter of effort. So. There are benefits to having one ring."

Gu Yue was looking straight ahead.

Lin Hao was looking at the road, and doing a sum, and wishing — for the first time in his life — that the part of his head that did sums would shut up.

## Part 3: The Arithmetic He Could Not Stop

The Horned Dragon was a hundred years. He had killed it. Two rings. Five percent each.

**Five years.**

The Crystal Bear was a thousand, and he had hit it eleven times with everything he had and never once landed the killing blow, so by Wu Zhangkong's rule he would have taken whatever share the platform gave to a participant who did not finish — and even at the full ten percent, split two ways, that was **fifty years.**

Call it fifty-five. Be generous. Call it sixty.

The hawk had gone into the platform at eight hundred and sixty-two years and come out at eight hundred and eighty-five.

**Twenty-three.**

Lin Hao walked down the hill behind a man carrying an unconscious boy and did the arithmetic three separate times, because the first two answers had been wrong in the direction he wanted, and he did not trust himself to want the right one.

Twenty-three years from the platform, where the rule said he should have taken five, or at an absolute stretch sixty if he claimed a share of a kill that was not his.

Which meant the platform had not done it.

Which meant the thing that had taken the hawk from seven hundred years to nine hundred and six in four months was not the Spirit Ascension Platform, and was not the cultivation energy of slain soul beasts, and was not any of the mechanisms Wu Zhangkong had just spent five minutes explaining to four children as though they were the whole of how the world worked.

It was the other thing. The thing he had never been able to name to anyone's satisfaction, including his own. The thing his father called *the boy's way of it* and the Association called *an unusual affinity* and Wu Zhangkong had never once asked about directly, in four months, which was starting to look less like an oversight and more like a decision.

His soul ate the world. That was the honest version. Not one-tenth of what it killed — **all of what it experienced**, continuously, whether he was fighting or forging or lying in a dormitory with his eyes shut, and it had been doing it since before he was born, and it was roughly a hundred times faster than the rule that applied to everybody else.

Lin Hao stopped walking.

Not dramatically. He just stopped, on a public road, in the late afternoon, because a thought had arrived that deserved to be stood still for.

Wu Zhangkong had told him, at five in the morning, two days ago, not to become a curiosity. *You are not a curiosity yet. I would prefer you stayed that way until you are too large to be one.*

And then, this afternoon, in front of four students and a director, Wu Zhangkong had explained exactly how much cultivation energy a soul should take from a slain beast.

Lin Hao put the two sentences next to each other and looked at them.

*He knows,* Lin Hao thought. *He's known the whole time. He explained the rule to everybody in order to find out whether I would do the sum.*

And then, because the next part followed whether he wanted it to or not: *And I did the sum. And I stopped walking. And he is standing thirty metres ahead of me with his back turned, and he has not turned around, and he is waiting to see whether I catch up.*

Lin Hao caught up.

## Part 4: What Wu Zhangkong Did Not Ask

They walked the last part of the way with Wulin's breathing on Wu Zhangkong's neck and the others ahead, and neither of them said anything for four streets.

Then Wu Zhangkong said, "You stopped."

"Yes."

"Why."

Lin Hao looked at the back of the man's head, and made a decision, and it was the same decision he had made in the forge with Xie Xie, which was that the truth was cheaper than the alternative and would still be cheap in ten years.

"Because I did the arithmetic," he said. "The Horned Dragon was a hundred years and I killed it and I have two rings, so I should have taken five years from it. The hawk took twenty-three in one session, and it had already taken a hundred and sixty-two in the four months before that, and I never killed anything in those four months except a bar of spirit steel and a couple of wolves in a training ground." He kept his voice level, because level was the only way to say it. "So either your rule is wrong, or I'm wrong, or the thing that's growing my soul isn't the thing you just told everybody about."

Wu Zhangkong walked on.

"Which do you think it is," he said, at last.

"The third."

"I agree."

They walked further.

"Are you going to ask me what it is."

"No," Wu Zhangkong said. And then, because he was Wu Zhangkong, and because he could not let a thing sit without putting a fence around it: "I am not going to ask you, and I am going to tell you why, and you will listen. A boy of ten who can explain his own anomaly is a boy who has been *told* his anomaly, by somebody with a reason to tell him. You have not been told. You have noticed. Noticing is survivable. Being told is not, because then there is a second person who knows, and second people talk, and there is a man in this city with a card in his pocket who is not stupid." He shifted Wulin again. "So I am not going to ask. And you are not going to find out on purpose. And when it starts doing things you cannot account for, you will come to me, and I will decide then whether we are dealing with a gift or a debt."

"And if it's a debt."

"Then we pay it," Wu Zhangkong said, "and you will not enjoy the manner of the paying, and you will survive it, and that is the most anybody can promise anybody."

## Part 5: The Warm Pond

Wu Zhangkong stayed in the dormitory with Wulin, which he would not have done for anyone else, and Lin Hao knew this because Xie Xie told him, and Xie Xie knew it because Xie Xie had asked a second-year, and the second-year had laughed.

He stayed because Wulin's martial soul was mutated, and because a spirit soul evolving in a body like that was not a thing you left alone.

Wulin soaked in it.

That was the only word for it and he would use it himself afterwards, with some embarrassment: it felt like a warm pond. The warmth came up out of his spine and went into his bones and stayed there, and his body was beaten and his spirit was spent and the tiredness simply dissolved off him the way mud comes off in hot water. He did not want to open his eyes. He had never, in ten years of being told he had a trash martial soul, felt anything so entirely without cost.

The golden halo around him went blue.

Bluesilver Grass came out of his palms — not the thin weak stuff, but strands, and then more strands, until they surrounded him — and Wu Zhangkong took one and twirled it around his finger and looked at it for a long time.

There were channels in it.

Fine, faint, gold, running the length of the grass like veins, and the veins threw light, and the light was the same gold-blue that was on the boy, and under that light the natural lines in the grass grew more distinct, as though something were being *imprinted* rather than grown.

*Just as I thought,* Wu Zhangkong thought. *His bloodline is mutating the martial soul again. And this one is beneficial.*

He sat with it for a while longer, and did not write anything down, because he had decided some weeks ago — without telling anybody, including the director — that there were certain observations about Tang Wulin that were going to live in his head and nowhere else.

---

## Part 6: The Ledger Gets a Line It Shouldn't Have

Lin Hao went back to his own room and sat on the bunk with a piece of paper, because he had told himself for two years that he did not keep a ledger on paper, and because tonight was the night that stopped being true.

He wrote three lines.

*The hawk: 906 years. Wind and lightning. Grew 206 years in four months without a single qualifying kill.*

*The rule: one-tenth of a slain beast, split across rings. Eighteen or nineteen thousand-year beasts to evolve. I have two rings. Wulin has one. Wulin evolved today. I will not, by that rule, for a very long time.*

And then the third line, which he wrote slowly, and which he looked at for a long time before he folded the paper and put it inside the lining of his sword bag where nobody would think to look.

*Whatever is growing my soul does not care about the rule. Wu Zhangkong knows and has chosen not to ask. There is a man with a card. So: be too large to be a curiosity, and be that before the card is read.*

He blew out the lamp.

In the dark, the hawk turned over in him — nine hundred and twelve years of condensed wind and, since three nights ago, something else that had no name yet and did not answer to being called — and Lin Hao lay on his back and thought about one ring, and two rings, and the fact that he had been handed the worse arrangement twice now, once by the world and once by his own talent, and that the second time it had come with an interest rate.

"Wulin gets the pond," he said, to the ceiling, "and I get the arithmetic."

The hawk did not answer. It never did.

But the air above his palm had that texture again — the forge floor before the bellows — and it stayed there all night, and in the morning the nail on the windowsill was standing on its end.

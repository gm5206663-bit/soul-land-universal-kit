## Part 1: The Third Morning

The stands were full before the sun cleared the harbor, which had not happened for any match in the memory of the posting-master, and the faculty row on the reviewing platform wore an expression the students had never seen on teachers: the look of people who had read the same reports and arrived at the same arithmetic.

Long Hengxu read the sanction in his weather-report voice. *Class one versus class two — formerly class one, demoted by the ladder they lost. Full roster. Rules of the Class Promotion Tournament. Referee: the Director.* And then, because the ink on the second page had been pressed hard enough to bruise the fiber, he read the attendance line, and did not look at anyone while he did: *Faculty in attendance. Wu Zhangkong. Ye Yingluo.*

They stood at opposite ends of the reviewing platform the way two weather systems stand in one sky. Ye Yingluo in her best coat, composed as a sealed letter, two years of patience folded into her sleeves. Wu Zhangkong in white, hands behind his back, watching his students the way he watched everything: entirely, and without asking them to notice.

Class two walked out first — formerly class one, wearing the demotion the way some men wear insults: openly, and with interest — and the grade saw three weeks of a teacher's price had been spent on them. Wang Jinxi at the point, both arms whole, the right one moving heavier than the tournament, the way an arm moves when its owner has been thinking with it for three weeks. Zhang Yangzi at his shoulder, watchful, the eagle's stillness in his posture. Wei Xiaofeng third, and the stands that remembered the tournament noticed the smugness was gone from him, replaced by something drilled.

Class five — class one — walked out to a noise that was not cheering. It was the sound of a city holding a breath it had been holding for three weeks.

"They studied us," Lin Hao said quietly, at the line, his eyes walking the other formation the way they walked a weld. "All of them. Wei Xiaofeng is standing a full step behind where his arrogance used to put him. Zhang Yangzi's weight is on his back foot — he's not planning to fly first. And Wang Jinxi—" he paused, because Wang Jinxi was looking exactly where the sentence said, straight at Wulin, with an expression that was not the hate the stands were hoping for and not the wonder of the tournament platform either, but something in between that had been sleeping badly. "Wang Jinxi's been thinking about you for three weeks."

"Good," Wulin said. "So have I."

"Here's the doctrine." Lin Hao's voice dropped to the forge register, and three heads came in, because nine months of being read correctly had built the habit of listening. "They lost to the seam-break. They've had three weeks with that fact. So the seam will be the bait — something about that fold will be a lie, and I will *want* to strike it, because wanting is what I do. So: nobody trusts the fold. Not even me. Xie Xie — Wei Xiaofeng is yours, and his clones are a curtain; curtains hide doors. Gu Yue — Wulin's back, until the arithmetic says otherwise, and *you* decide when it says otherwise. Wulin — Wang Jinxi. The mountain's yours."

"And you?" Xie Xie asked.

"I'll be where the lie is," Lin Hao said, and drew.

"Begin," said Long Hengxu.

## Part 2

There was a lock of storm-gray at his left temple now, and it had a sheen to it that the rest of his hair did not have, and when he walked the hair in it moved a half-second after the rest. Xie Xie had noticed it a week ago and said nothing, which was how Lin Hao knew he had noticed.
: The Lie

Wei Xiaofeng moved first, and the stands learned what three weeks of humiliation teaches.

His first ring lit and his body went *unreal* — the Green Shadow Snake's skill unfolding like a hand of cards: phantoms pouring off him in a revolving crown, each one a perfect Wei Xiaofeng, illusory air shimmering at the edges, the real body lost among his own copies the way a fish is lost in a river of itself. He did not attack Xie Xie. He *enveloped* him — the curtain of himself rolling across the platform's west side, taking the dagger-boy's fight exactly where the geometry wanted it: far.

"Wu Zhangkong taught me," Xie Xie had said once, bragging, that if you can't analyze a trick you hit it with everything you have — and he did, the golden blade of the two-ring Light Dragon Dagger carving arcs through the phantoms, scattering them like starteled birds. But the curtain did not thin. It herded. And on the east side, under the covering noise of a hundred fake footfalls, the real fight opened.

Wulin took Wang Jinxi head-on, because that was the mountain's assignment and because neither of them knew any other way to have this conversation. The Bone Dragon Claw came down in a wash of black gas, the dark-attribute soul drinking the morning light off its own path — and Wulin's fist met it, and *the ground under Wang Jinxi gave.* He went back three steps. Four. The stands remembered this from the tournament — the impossible strength of the one-ring boy — but this time it was worse, because this time Wulin's open palm flashed *gold* as it turned the second claw, a scale-ridge rising through the knuckles and vanishing inside a heartbeat, and Long Hengxu half-rose from the reviewing platform before he caught himself.

The Bluesilver Whip answered the eagle — Zhang Yangzi's shadow-dive meeting a lash of golden-veined grass that cracked like a mast in a storm and bent his flight path a full body-length off the line — and Gu Yue stood at Wulin's back, the cup that did not spill, fire and ice alternating in her hands with an economy that made the faculty row lean forward without deciding to.

And Lin Hao watched the fold.

It came in the seventh minute. He could not have said afterward what the seam-reader saw first — the eagle banking wide, the dragon's shoulders opening, the two rings and the two rings beginning their slow rotation toward each other, the dark feather-light spilling toward the bone the way it had spilled in the tournament, the way it had spilled in the yard that night the four of them learned what a weld was—

The certainty rose in him like a struck note. *There. The seam. Now.*

The Stormbringer was already moving — the Wind-Step already firing, the Gale Talon already braiding along the edge, the whole unhedged grammar of the homework pouring into one stroke aimed at the handshake between the two souls—

—and the eagle's wings closed on *nothing.*

The dark light guttered. The fold collapsed like a tent without a pole — Zhang Yangzi's shadow snapping back to his shoulders, Wang Jinxi's rings settling, the two of them standing a full platform-width apart, untouched, *unfused,* and Lin Hao's sword arrived at the seam of a weld that had never been lit. A practiced decoy. A three-week lie, rehearsed until it could stand in front of a boy who had never been lied to successfully and *hold his eyes.*

For one half-breath, the seam-reader stood committed in empty air. It was the first time in his life his certainty had been used against him — not beaten, not outrun: *aimed.* And in that half-breath, forty feet west, the curtain of Wei Xiaofeng's phantoms parted like a door.

The Dark Eagle Dragon came through it.

The real fold had happened behind the curtain — hidden by the one place on the platform where eyes were busy — and it did not seek Wulin, or Gu Yue, or the swordsman recovering from his stroke. It crossed the west side in one long dark glide, and it arrived at Xie Xie — mid-swing, weight committed, dagger extended into a phantom — with the multiplied strength of two Soul Grandmasters' souls speaking in a single voice.

Xie Xie saw it. That was the thing the stands never forgot, and the thing Xie Xie himself never let anyone forget: he *saw* it, one step too late to dodge and exactly on time to decide, and he brought both arms up in front of his face with the Light Dragon Dagger still in his right hand, because a dagger-boy blocks with what he has.

The sound was not loud. That was the other thing nobody forgot.

He went out of the air the way the persimmon stall had gone out of the air, and landed against the west rail, and did not get up, and his arms lay at angles that the whole first grade could read, because everyone in the first grade had been taught by the same three weeks.

## Part 3: Eruption

Gu Yue moved before Lin Hao did.

She had been holding Wulin's back, and the arithmetic — *her* arithmetic, the one she decided — said otherwise now, and what she crossed the platform doing was not running. It was *unfolding.* The elements came out of her the way the sea comes out of a cup when the cup stops pretending: fire and ice side by side in a revolving ring, wind braiding them, earth rising under her feet in stepping-stones that were not there and then were. Under it all — under the fire and the frost, so fast that only hawk-eyes forty feet away caught it at all — one flash of a light that was neither, silver-white and cold as a river under ice, gone before the mind could keep it.

The eruption met the Dark Eagle Dragon broadside.

It did not defeat it. Two rings times two rings, multiplied, was not a thing any one first-grader defeated — the stands would understand that later, in the retelling, when they had calmer hearts. But it *moved* it. The fusion recoiled out of the west rail in a wash of steam and scorched shadow, the single voice staggering into two notes, and Gu Yue stood between it and the broken boy with her ring of elements still turning around her like the dial of something far larger than a girl. Even through the rage and the steam, Ye Yingluo on the reviewing platform went white to the lips — because she was a teacher, and she was watching a student *hold back* inside an eruption, spending exactly as much as revealed exactly as little as she intended.

And Wulin roared.

There is no other word the stands used, afterward, for the sound that came out of the forge-boy. He had seen the arms. He had seen the angle. He had known Xie Xie for a year and heard him brag through every meal of it, and the furnace behind his sternum — the nested heat, the thing the storm week had made and the letter had warned about and the brother had promised to hear coming — took the one key it had ever needed. Was the sight of his friend lying wrong, and *opened.*

A faintly discernible golden ray of light appeared from his body for a split second — and forty feet away, Wang Jinxi's Bone Dragon Claw, rising to guard, immediately slowed, the dark dragon in it flinching from something older than itself. The gold ran up Wulin's right arm: the veins coming alive, the scales rising through the skin in a climbing armor of hammered light, the fingers lengthening into claws, the pupils going to slits, the whole of him suddenly *more* in a way that made the platform's wards hum a note the bones of everyone present felt individually.

The Golden Dragon Claw came down at Wang Jinxi's head.

"STOP!" Long Hengxu and Ye Yingluo shouted out simultaneously — and the shout was arithmetic, not authority: the claw was at full strength, aimed past stopping, and both of them knew in the same instant that neither of them would reach.

A slender arm noiselessly appeared and touched Wulin's right arm, softly pushing it aside.

Gu Yue had already been moving — the eruption's own momentum, the crossing of the platform, the body that had placed itself between a dragon and its second target and was simply *there,* the way she was simply there — and the Golden Dragon Claw that had been heading for Wang Jinxi's head was deflected a hand's width and came down instead on his right claw.

Bang.

Despite being several times bigger than Tang Wulin at that moment, Wang Jinxi was smashed into the ground, while Wulin landed softly on the other side from the counterforce. The black light separated from the transformed body. The two boys inside came back to themselves and released miserable shrieks of pain — Zhang Yangzi on the ground with both legs broken, Wang Jinxi's right arm fractured and bent at an impossible angle, the tail of the fusion's ghost still flickering over them like something reluctant to leave.

"ENOUGH!"

The cold voice rang like the morning bell and the evening drum, and Wulin woke out of the rage the way a man wakes out of a flood — standing, gold dying down his arm, Wu Zhangkong beside him with the injured Xie Xie already gathered in his arms, and a platform full of silence and steam and three broken children.

"Teacher Wu, how can you allow your students to be so *violent?*" Ye Yingluo flew onto the stage, finger pointed, the two-year grudge finally given its sentence—

Wu Zhangkong said to Wulin, "Go." And from beginning to end he did not spare her a glance. She took one step to pursue, and he released a sharp ice-cold aura that froze her whole body where she stood and put the sensation of death in her mouth, and the reviewing platform's other teachers looked suddenly very interested in the horizon.

---

And the thing that changed because he was in the yard — the thing nobody would have had any reason to learn if he had not been standing at the edge of it — was that **Gu Yue erupted at all.** In a yard without him, Wei Xiaofeng's clones get broken by Xie Xie's speed and the morning ends with a lesson about clones. In a yard with him in it, Xie Xie's speed is not enough, because Lin Hao had already told Xie Xie which clone was real two seconds before Xie Xie would have found it himself, and Xie Xie had used the answer instead of finding it, and had therefore never learned the thing he was about to need. **Gu Yue erupted because a boy who reads too well took the lesson away from the person who needed it.**

That is not a small thing and it is not a flattering one, and Lin Hao understood it in the yard and did not say so, and would think about it for a week.

## Part 4: What It Cost

Lin Hao was one breath late, and the one breath lived in his hands.

He had seen it — that was the promise, and the promise had kept its half: he had seen the gold rising under Wulin's sleeve a half-second before the ray, from forty feet, mid-recovery, and he had gone, the Wind-Step firing with everything the Spirit Connection had left — and his right hand had arrived exactly one hand's width behind Gu Yue's, which was already there, which had been closer, which was why the world still had a Wang Jinxi with a head. He knelt now beside Xie Xie with his scabbard and his belt and the splinting hands of a smith who had set his father's broken fingers at eight years old, and his hands would not stop trembling — not with fear, never with fear; with *arithmetic.* The sum of one breath, added and re-added. *You'll know before I will.* He had known. Knowing, it turned out, was the easy half of the promise, and nobody had told him the second half was called *reaching.*

Both forearms, fractured clean. The healer-teacher said it four times like an apology. Xie Xie heard it from under a face the color of paper, grinned with the parts of him that still worked, and said, to the whole infirmary, at full volume:

"Did you *see* me block? Both arms. I blocked a *dragon* with both arms."

"You did," Lin Hao said, tying the sling with steady-enough hands, because the hands had decided that Xie Xie got to have this. "You're the only person in the grade who can say that."

"I'm going to say it at *everyone,*" Xie Xie promised, and fainted gently into the painkillers, mid-brag, which everyone agreed afterward was the most Xie Xie way to have fainted.

Wu Zhangkong stayed. The ice-cold teacher who had carried him off the platform like something breakable and valuable sat in the infirmary chair with his arms folded. "Teacher Wu, I—" Wulin began, because the sentence had been standing in his throat since the platform.

"You don't need to say anymore," Wu Zhangkong said, "as you didn't do anything wrong." A beat. "I will be here."

And Wulin — who had watched that man freeze a teacher and terrify a brigade — discovered that two sentences could rearrange a boy's whole understanding of the word *cold.*

Later, walking back through the dark yard, Gu Yue said the things she had been assembling since the platform. "Zhang Yangzi and Wang Jinxi used a soul fusion skill. They are nearly impossible in this era — it can only appear when two martial souls are exceptionally compatible. It multiplies, it doesn't add." A pause, and then, quieter, the second assembly: "You must learn to control your power. It seems to be very formidable — but such great power requires great control. It seems that during the week you were away, a change occurred in your martial soul."

Wulin was quiet a long moment. "Gu Yue. We're friends, so I don't want to lie to you. But I really can't tell you about it. So — please don't ask. Okay?"

She pondered for a moment, and gave him her reply: "Fine. I won't ask." And then, because she was Gu Yue, and because a girl who held back inside her own eruptions recognized the shape of holding back: "But you must learn to control it. *Before* it decides for you."

In room eleven, with the door shut and the harbor doing its nightly accounting, Wulin sat on the bed with his golden-veined right hand open on his knee, and said the true thing to the only person the true thing belonged to.

"I wasn't me, Hao. For those ten seconds, I wasn't me. If her arm hadn't been there—" his voice cracked on the arithmetic, the same arithmetic, the one breath, "—I would have killed him. A boy who yielded to me three weeks ago. I would have—"

"I know," Lin Hao said. "I saw it coming. That's what I'm *for,* remember? I knew." He sat down on the other bed, facing his brother, and said the rest of it, because a shelf is not a brother: "I ran. I was one breath late. My hands still haven't decided to forgive me." A pause. "So here's the file, and it's the only one that matters: her arm was there *this time.* Next time, one of us has to be faster — you at stopping yourself, or me at reaching you. Both. We train both. Every day. That's the whole plan."

Wulin looked at his hand. Closed it. Opened it. "Every day," he agreed.

On the far water inside him, the hawk had risen when the gold rose — not cowering, not still: *standing,* wings half-open on the wind of its own making, watching the golden thing across the water the way a bird watches weather it knows it will one day have to fly through. And when the gold went down, the great bird folded, slowly, and turned its head toward the boy, and the question between them — *can you see without me?* — lay under everything, unanswered, and for the first time felt less like a question about seeing than a question about *what was coming that they would both need to see.*

In the other wing of the infirmary, Wang Jinxi lay with his arm in a frame of healer's wood and stared at the ceiling. His right hand — the broken one — kept flexing against the splint, because the dragon in him would not stop replaying two things: the hammer-fist it had *answered* on the tournament platform, and the golden claw that its own kind had flinched from. Pride said hate. The bone-deep thing under the pride said *follow,* and had no idea what it meant, and would not stop saying it.

And in the director's office, Long Hengxu sat with the incident reports stacked like a wall — three students badly injured, a fusion skill witnessed, an unnamed golden power witnessed, an elementalist witnessed exceeding every file the academy held on her — and across from him, unreadable as ever, Wu Zhangkong folded his hands and said the sentence that had been waiting all evening:

"Director. Seven children like these should not be kept in separate rooms."

Third morning's post, on the board by the gate, under the results nobody had posted:

**FIRST GRADE — SPECIAL ARRANGEMENT, FORTHCOMING. DETAILS TO FOLLOW. — OFFICE OF THE DIRECTOR.**

The worst class in the grade read it on the way to breakfast, and none of them — not the boy with the sling, not the boy with the golden hand, not the girl who said *fine,* not the swordsman whose hands had finally, at breakfast, decided to forgive him — none of them knew yet that the wall behind that sentence had a number on it, and the number was zero.

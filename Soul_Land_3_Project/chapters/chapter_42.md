## Part 1: What Came Out Of The Trees

Gu Yue had not come back up the slope with the rest of them, and nobody had asked her to, and Lin Hao noticed that he had wanted her to see the thing at the bottom of it and had not examined why.

Lin Hao recognised it.

That was the part he would keep going back to for years — not that it came, but that he looked at it and knew what it was, the way you know a tool by its handle, because he had read about it at the age of nine in a library book with a cracked spine and a coloured plate on page forty-one.

**Duskgold Dreadclaw Bear.**

Dark-gold fur. Over three metres at the shoulder. Shoulders like a wall that had decided to walk. And from each paw, **four golden claws a metre long**, which caught the light under the trees in a way that made them look, from a distance, like somebody had laid four swords down in the leaf litter.

*Reputed to be the strongest soul beast in the world.*

*When mature, it would not need to fear any predator. Not even true dragons.*

He had read that sentence at nine years old and thought it was the kind of sentence books put in to make you turn the page.

It was thirty metres away and it was looking at him.

Not at the six of them. **At him.**

And Lin Hao understood why in about one second, because he had three rings and the other five had one or two, and whatever the bear could smell hanging off them, it could smell most of it on him.

"**Ejection,**" he said. "Everyone. Now."

"It hasn't moved," Wang Jinxi said.

"It doesn't have to."

## Part 2: What Xie Xie Did

Xie Xie did not hit the ejection.

Lin Hao would think about that for a very long time and would never arrive at a version of it that he was comfortable with, because Xie Xie had looked at the bear, and then at Lin Hao, and then at the trees to the left, and Lin Hao had known — in the flat clear way he knew things when there was no time — exactly what the calculation was.

The bear wanted him. The bear was going to come for him. And if something else moved fast enough in a different direction, the bear might turn.

Xie Xie went left.

He was very fast. He had always been very fast, and he was faster now than he had been in the spring, and he crossed forty metres of forest in the time it took Lin Hao to say his name.

The bear turned.

It did not turn quickly. That was the worst part of it — it turned the way a hillside turns, without hurry, because it had never in its life needed to hurry, and when it was facing Xie Xie it went through him.

Lin Hao did not see it. He saw the beginning and he saw the end, and the middle was something he did not have, and would never have, and would spend the rest of his life not having.

Xie Xie's energy came up out of the trees in two halves, because that was what the platform did, and one half went toward Wulin and the other half went somewhere else, and the half that went toward Wulin went into him and did not come back out.

## Part 3: What The Wind Did

Lin Hao made the circle.

Nine metres. He had made it eleven seconds before, and he had made it with the bear thirty metres away and looking at somebody else, and he made it now with the bear four metres away and looking at him.

The wind went up.

The bear walked into it.

It did not stop. It did not slow. The leaf litter and the loose bark and the small branches that Lin Hao had put into the air went around it and over it and past it, and the bear came through the circle the way a man comes through a curtain, and Lin Hao stood in the middle of his own weather with a sword in his hand and understood, with a clarity that he would have given a great deal not to have, that **the wind was not a wall and had never been a wall.**

It had been a wall eleven seconds ago. It had been a wall against a lion.

This was not a lion.

He did not die, and afterwards he could not entirely explain why, because the bear's claw went past his shoulder and took the sleeve of his uniform and the skin under it, and something in his chest went very quiet — and the hawk, which had been leaning toward things for two days, went still in a way he had never felt it go still before.

Not still like calm.

Still like **deciding.**

And then the light took him, because Gu Yue had come around from behind the bear with her hands already moving and had put her hand on his arm, and she hit the ejection for him.

## Part 4: The Box

He came up out of the metal box feeling fine.

That was the strange part, and the staff thought it was strange too, and said so, in front of him, the way adults say things in front of children.

"He's awake and has feeling." A man with a clipboard prodded him with a thing that looked like a popsicle. "This is odd. He recovered this quickly?"

"Are you conscious?" another one said. "Do you feel cold?"

"Yes," Lin Hao said, because that was the answer and because he was aware, in a distant way, that he was sitting in a metal box with his sleeve gone and his shoulder bleeding and he felt **fine**, and that this was not what was supposed to happen.

**Wu Zhangkong's face** appeared above the box.

He did not say anything for a moment. He looked at the shoulder, and then at the face, and then at the three purple rings that had not gone away, because they had stopped going away two days ago and Lin Hao had not yet decided how he felt about that.

"You recognised it," Wu Zhangkong said. It was not a question.

"I read about it when I was nine."

"And you did not hit the ejection."

"Xie Xie didn't hit the ejection."

Wu Zhangkong was quiet for a moment. "No," he said. "He didn't."

**Gu Yue** was standing beside the box. She had been standing beside it, Lin Hao understood, for the whole of the time he had been in it, and she let out one breath when he sat up, and then her face went back to being her face.

"Where's the other box," Lin Hao said.

---

## Part 5: The Backlash

Xie Xie was in the next box, and the next box was full of people.

He came up out of it about four minutes after Lin Hao, and he was not fine, and everybody in the room could see that he was not fine, and nobody said anything about it, because there was not a thing to say.

He had been killed. Not ejected, not beaten, not knocked out — **killed**, in a way that the platform had rendered faithfully and completely, because that was what the platform did, and he had come back through it in about ninety seconds and he was sitting on the edge of a metal box with his hands between his knees.

"It's not real," Wang Jinxi said. He said it quickly, the way you say a thing you have been told.

"I know it's not real," Xie Xie said.

"It's the backlash. It passes."

"I *know* it's not real." Xie Xie looked up. His face was the colour of the metal. "That's the thing. I know it's not real, and I still felt it, and the part I keep thinking about isn't the dying." He stopped. "It's that I was **wrong.** I thought it would turn. I did the arithmetic and I thought it would turn and it didn't turn, and it went through me, and the arithmetic was mine."

Nobody said anything.

Lin Hao sat down on the floor next to the box, because his legs had opinions and because it was the closest place.

"The arithmetic was mine," he said. "I'm the one who reads things. You ran left because you'd watched me read things for three days and you trusted that I'd seen something."

"You hadn't."

"I hadn't."

"So it's mine."

"It's **ours,**" Xie Xie said, and his voice did something on the word, and he cleared his throat and did not do it again. "That's the whole point of the stupid shape, isn't it. It's ours."

---

## Part 6: What Wu Zhangkong Said Afterwards

He said it in the corridor, with the six of them on the benches and the sun going down through the high windows, and he said it in the flat voice he used for the things that mattered.

"You all scored highly," he said. "Which is attributable to each of you doing exactly what was necessary." He let that sit. "At the very least, you are now a **functional team.**"

They could not work out whether to be pleased or ashamed, and Wu Zhangkong watched them not working it out, and did not help.

"Tang Wulin. Your control has improved past what I expected. Gu Yue." He looked at her. "You took the decision to eject him. That was the only correct decision available and you made it in under a second." A pause. "Xie Xie."

Xie Xie looked up.

"You ran left."

"Yes."

"It was wrong."

"Yes."

"It was wrong," Wu Zhangkong said, "and it was also the second-fastest thing anybody in that forest did all day, and I want you to understand that both of those are true and that the second one does not excuse the first." He folded his arms. "And Lin Hao."

Lin Hao looked at him.

"You made a circle and a bear walked through it."

"Yes."

"Before the rest of it." Wu Zhangkong put the stick under his arm, which he did when he was going to say a number. "Rank thirty-one. Three rings, all purple, all of them compatible with you in a way I have not seen in any other person I have ever stood in front of. A spirit soul at eleven hundred and seventy-one years that has given you a skill a perfectly bonded soul gives **once.** A sword that has an opinion. And the marks." He looked at Lin Hao's forearms for a beat longer than was polite. "Rank is a measurement of how much soul power you are holding. It is not a measurement of **what you are.** Do you understand the difference."

"I think so."

"Then say it."

"Two people can hold the same amount and be different things."

"Say the other half."

Lin Hao said it, because Wu Zhangkong had the stick under his arm and because he had been counting without meaning to, the way he counted everything. "A normal three-ring Soul Elder is fourteen to eighteen years old and has two yellow rings and one purple and has never had a skill that cost him anything he hadn't planned for." He stopped. "I'm ten. And when I use everything at once I am not fighting at rank thirty-one."

"No," Wu Zhangkong said. "You are not. If you spent it all today you would be fighting at the top of what a man without a black ring can do, and there are Soul **Kings** in this continent's armies who would lose to you and would not understand why afterwards." He took the stick out from under his arm. "That is not a compliment. That is a description of a problem, and the problem is that you are the only person in this room who does not yet know how to be that, and I have four months."

Then he said the rest of it, without heat, which was the only way he said anything.

"You have three rings, and there is a full realm between you and every other child in this room, and you found out today that **being a realm above them is not the same thing as being strong.** You are not going to be able to protect them by being larger than them. You are going to have to be **smarter than the thing that comes for them.** You had one day of that and you did not manage it."

"No."

"Good," Wu Zhangkong said. "Now you know what the next four months are for."

He was about to leave, and then he stopped, because Lin Hao had said something.

"Teacher."

"Mn."

"The Spirit Pagoda." Lin Hao said it carefully. "The man with the card. He saw the crossing."

Wu Zhangkong was quiet for a moment.

"You're still young," he said, "and there are things you needn't know yet. Remember my words and you'll be fine." He looked at the high windows. "**The Spirit Pagoda does not think the way you do.** They have so much power that even the Federation is afraid of them. And they span across to the other continents."

There was a silence with a great deal in it.

"The other continents," Lin Hao said.

"You'll know what they're like when you visit them," Wu Zhangkong said. "Not now."

And he went, and left six children on a bench in a corridor with the sun going down, and one of them had three rings and a shoulder that had stopped bleeding, and another had come back through a thing that was not real and was still sitting with his hands between his knees, and none of them said anything for a while.

Then Wulin said, "Functional team."

"Functional team," Xie Xie agreed.

"That's the nicest thing he's ever said to us."

"It's the nicest thing he's *capable* of," Gu Yue said, from the end of the bench, without turning her head.

And Lin Hao, who had been quiet, laughed — one short surprised sound, because it was the first time he had laughed in two days and he had not been planning to.

---

He wrote it in the ledger that night.

*The wind is not a wall. I made it into a wall and a bear walked through it. Being the largest of the six is not the same as being useful.*

Then he looked at it, and he added the line he had not wanted to write.

**Xie Xie ran left because he trusted me to have seen something. That is the cost of being the one who reads. It is not a cost I can stop paying, only a cost I can try to earn.**

He closed the book.

The **hawk-gold lock** at his temple caught the lamplight, and the **marks on his forearms** were still raised, faint, following the bone, and they had not gone back under since the crossing and he had stopped expecting them to.

The hawk turned over in him — **one thousand one hundred and seventy-one** — and did not lean toward anything, because there was nothing to lean toward.

Lin Hao lay on his back in a room that was too small, in a city that was second of five, and thought about a bear that would not need to fear a dragon, and about four months, and about the fact that he had one day of being smarter than the thing that comes and he had not managed it.

*Right,* he thought.

And went to sleep.

## Part 1: The Room

The Heaven Dou Imperial Auction House had a door you had to be let in through, and a room behind it that was quiet in a way Lin Hao had only previously encountered in cemeteries.

He had been to an auction before. Once, in Eastsea City, standing at the back with Wulin, looking at things that other people were going to buy. That room had been loud.

This one was not loud.

There were perhaps forty people in it, and they were all sitting, and none of them were talking, and the man at the front was speaking in the voice of a person who had no need to raise it because everybody in the room was already listening with their whole attention.

Lin Hao looked at the screen.

**Intermediate spirit ascension platform entry card. 5,000,000 Federation Coins.**

He read it, and then he read it again, because he had done the arithmetic once, in a dormitory, about a hundred-year spirit soul costing a million, and he had thought at the time that a million was the largest number that had ever been said in front of him.

Five million.

Three cards.

Fifteen million Federation Coins, sitting on a screen in a quiet room, for three pieces of card stock that let you walk into a forest that did not exist.

He did the second arithmetic without meaning to, because that was the thing his head did, and it was: **his father had made, in the best year Lin Hao could remember, perhaps nine thousand.**

Five hundred and fifty-five of his father's best years.

For one card.

## Part 2: What Wu Zhangkong Had Already Paid

"He's already paid three million," Xie Xie said, very quietly, into Lin Hao's ear.

"What."

"Of his own. He said it in the corridor. Three million of his own, and the Tang Sect is covering the rest." Xie Xie's voice had gone strange. "Do you know what three million is."

"I've just worked it out."

"I haven't worked out anything, I've just been told a number and I can't feel my hands." Xie Xie looked at the screen. "He's a teacher. He's a *teacher.* Where does a teacher have three million."

Lin Hao looked at Wu Zhangkong, who was sitting two rows ahead with his hands folded and his white sleeves falling straight, and who had, four days ago, cleaned a stone that was not dirty.

He did not say what he was thinking, because he had learned in four days that not saying it was a skill.

What he was thinking was: a man who has three million and spends it on two children's entry into a forest, and who travels in white and carries nothing, and who does not own a house that anybody has ever seen.

That was not a man who had money.

That was a man who had **converted** it.

---

## Part 3: What Lin Hao Saw

The miscellaneous treasures went first, and Lin Hao stopped listening at the fourth item.

Not because he was bored. Because he had started **reading** them, and he could not stop, and it was the first time in six days that his head had done the thing it was actually for.

A bar of **Cloud Titanium**, forty centimetres, offered at four hundred thousand. The screen showed it from three angles and Lin Hao looked at the grain and knew — with the flat certainty of a person who had spent four years with his hands in the same material — that it had been **Thousand Refined**, and well, and by somebody in a hurry, and that the last two hundred strikes had been rushed and the bar would carry that for the rest of its life.

*Four hundred thousand,* he thought, *and it's a three-hundred-and-fifty-thousand bar.*

He said it out loud.

He had not meant to. It came out at about the volume of a person thinking, which in a quiet room is the volume of a person speaking, and three heads turned, and Wu Zhangkong's did not, because Wu Zhangkong had already known he was going to say it.

"Continue," Wu Zhangkong said, without turning around.

So Lin Hao continued.

He read the Cloud Titanium. He read a set of soul-conductive fittings that the auctioneer described as *exquisite* and that Lin Hao could see had been ground rather than forged, which meant they would hold and would not sing. He read a **battle armor** — his first, a two-word one, on a stand under glass — and he understood immediately why Wu Zhangkong had said the words he had said in a classroom four months ago, because the armor's plates were **metal**, and the metal had been worked by somebody, and the whole of the thing was a blacksmith's work wearing a shape.

"*From a purple battle armor onward, the creation process becomes inseparable from forging.*"

He had been told that. He had written it in a ledger. He had not **understood** it until he saw a two-word battle armor under glass in Heaven Dou City and worked out, in about four seconds, roughly what it was worth in metal and roughly what it was worth in the man who had made it.

The metal was perhaps a tenth.

---

## Part 4: The Fruit

Then the spirit fruit came, and Wulin stopped breathing.

Every one of them was **thousand-year.**

Not some. All. The screen went through them one after another and every single one had four characters in front of it, and Lin Hao watched Wulin's face as Wulin worked out what that meant.

"This auction doesn't sell hundred-year fruit," Wulin said. It was not a question.

"It's a high-class auction," Lin Hao said.

"That's not what I mean." Wulin's voice had gone careful, the way it went when he was working out something he did not want to be true. "I mean there isn't a single item in this room that anybody I know could buy. Not one. The cheapest thing on that screen is four hundred thousand and my father makes nine thousand a year and I have been thinking about money for four months and I thought I understood it."

"You understood your money," Lin Hao said. "You didn't understand this room."

And then the **Dragonscale Fruit** came up, and Wulin stopped being able to speak at all.

---

## Part 5: The Golden Veins

It was **azure.**

The auctioneer explained — Dragonscale Fruits are usually light blue; this one is azure; it was **plucked too early**, so it will never become a priceless **purple ten-thousand-year** Dragonscale Fruit; starting price, **one million.**

"One million," Xie Xie said, in the voice he used when he had decided to be the person who said the thing everybody was doing arithmetic about. "For a fruit that was picked wrong."

"Yes, sir."

"And if it had been picked right."

"It would not be here, sir."

Xie Xie sat back. "Right," he said. "Right. Okay. That's — that's a whole *economy* and I would like somebody to notice that I have just understood it, and that I understood it in about four seconds, and that I have never in my life thought about fruit."

Wulin had not spoken since they sat down. He was looking at the entry cards, which were in a case, under glass, with a man beside the case who was not doing anything at all and was doing it very expensively.

"How much," Wulin said.

"Five million each, young master."

Wulin nodded once, the way he nodded when a number arrived that he had already guessed and had been hoping not to be right about, and then he said, "Thank you," and looked at Lin Hao, and did not say anything else, and Lin Hao watched him put a number somewhere inside himself where he kept the numbers, and understood that he was not going to talk about it in front of a stranger, and that he was probably not going to talk about it at all.

**Five million is four hundred and sixteen Fridays. I did that sum without deciding to, and then I did it again in tin — because Wulin's tin is not a number to him, it is a thing he opens on a particular night and counts by hand — and the second sum took longer, and the reason it took longer is that I had to think about what it would be like to be him, and that I have known him for four years and that was the first time.**

Lin Hao was not looking at the colour.

He was looking at the **veins.**

Faint, jagged, gold, running along the surface of the fruit in a pattern that he had seen before. Not on a fruit. Not in a book.

On **Wulin's right hand**, when the scales came up.

He looked at Wulin. Wulin was looking at the screen with an expression Lin Hao had never seen on him, which was the expression of a boy who has been told his whole life that the thing inside him is a secret, and has just seen it for sale.

"Wulin," Lin Hao said, quietly.

"I know."

"Did you know."

"No." Wulin did not look away from the screen. "I've never seen it. I've never seen a picture of it. I've only ever seen *mine.*" His voice was very steady, which was how Lin Hao knew it was not steady at all. "And there's a fruit with my hand on it, and it costs a million, and somebody in this room is going to buy it and eat it."

Lin Hao looked back at the screen.

He did not say anything clever, which was three times in three days now, and he was beginning to understand that this was not a failure of cleverness at all.

It was that some things were not clever-shaped.

## Part 6: What He Wrote

He wrote it that night, above the forge, with the window open.

*Five million for a card. Fifteen million for three. My father's best year, five hundred and fifty-five times over.*

*I thought about Wulin's tin when I wrote that, which I have not done before, and I did it. Five million is what a card costs. Wulin has been putting money in a tin for four hundred Fridays and the tin is for Na'er, and I have known that for two years and never once written it down, and I think the reason is that writing it down would have meant having an opinion about whether the tin was going to work, and I did not want to have that opinion. I still do not know whether it is going to work. Not knowing is not the same as not caring, and that I have been using one for the other.*

*Wu Zhangkong has already paid three million of his own and he travels in white and carries nothing and I do not think he has money at all. I think he converted it, and that a man who converts three million into two children's entry into a forest is not a man with money, he is a man with a purpose, and I have never met one before and I did not know they were real.*

*I read the metal in that room. I read four hundred thousand down to three hundred and fifty in four seconds and three people turned around and Wu Zhangkong said continue, and that was the first time in six days that my head did the thing it is for.*

*A two-word battle armor under glass is a tenth metal and nine tenths the man who made it.*

Then he looked at the page and added the line at the bottom.

*Wulin saw his own hand for sale for a million and he had never seen a picture of it and nobody in that room knew what he was looking at except me.*

*I am the only person in the world who can read that room in two directions at once — what the metal is worth, and what the fruit means.*

*That is not a small thing. I have spent six days thinking I was a category error, and tonight I found out what the category is for.*

He closed the book.

The *hawk-gold lock* at his temple lay across his cheekbone, and the *marks on his forearms* were faint under his sleeves, and the hawk turned over in him — *one thousand one hundred and ninety-three* — and did not lean toward anything.

But tomorrow there was going to be a forest.

And it had recognised the last one.

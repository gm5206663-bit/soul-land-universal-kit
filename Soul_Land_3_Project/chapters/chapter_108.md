## Part 1: One Breath

"Your teachers will show you the door," Elder Cai said. "It is up to you to step through it." She folded her hands. "That is enough for today. The rest is yours."

Then she rose.

Lin Hao watched her come down from the lectern the way he watched everything — from the margin, which in this room was a seat five meters from the front, dead center, where not a breath could be missed. He had been placed there on purpose. He knew it; she knew it; the frost on her composure had not flickered once while Shen Yi read the five names that put all five working students in arm's reach of the desk. A marked boy is a watched boy. She had kept him not on a shelf but open on the table, and the morning had not yet produced a single reason to think she was wrong to.

She reached the end of the front aisle. Her back was to him.

And it was there — in the one second between her turning and her leaving, with the twenty-year bar still lying across all their shoulders and the front-row captivity warm on his neck and the ledger she kept on him open under her hand — that Lin Hao, who had spent thirteen years learning to say nothing the room did not need to hear, let one breath go that the room did not need to hear.

It was barely shaped. It did not move his lips enough for anyone past his own shoulder to read. It was the smallest, driest, most private fragment of the thing the morning had built in him, and it slipped out the way a tool slips off a bench — not because he chose it but because his hands were full.

"Old woman."

The elbow hit his ribs before the breath had finished.

Xu Xiaoyan, in seat four, beside him, had heard. Her eyes were very large and very round, and she had gone the particular pale she went when five seconds showed her something she was not supposed to have seen. She did not say a word; she only stared at him with the full force of a girl who had just watched a boy mutter an insult at a headmaster who could, by every law of cultivation anyone in the room believed in, hear heartbeats through walls.

Across her, Xie Xie had frozen with his mouth open. Wulin, center, did not move at all — Wulin's gift in a crisis was to become furniture. Gu Yue, in seat two, did not turn around, but Lin Hao saw the line of her shoulders go still in the particular way water goes still before it freezes solid.

Elder Cai did not turn around.

She walked the aisle. She reached the door. She went through it. The door closed behind her with a soft, ordinary sound, and at no point in any of it did her head move, her step change, or the frost-composure on her face alter by a single degree.

Nothing happened.

Xiaoyan exhaled a full lungful of air she had been holding since the elbow. Xie Xie's mouth closed. The lecture theatre began, very slowly, to breathe again, and Shen Yi turned at the lectern to take the class over.

Lin Hao looked down at his open notebook.

The page was the one he had written on during the seating — the careful line in the hand he used for the book nobody else read: *The teacher shows the door. The question is which side of it she's standing on.*

Under the last half of the line there was frost.

It was thin as a sword-stroke, a single perfect underline running from the word *door* to the word *on*, white and cold and already sweating in the warm air of the hall. It had not touched anything else — not the words above, not the margin, not the next page. And at the right-hand end of it, where an underline simply stops, the frost made one tiny, precise little turn, a downward tick, like the mark a person makes at the bottom of a column of figures to say *counted*.

Then it was gone. Shen Yi had not yet finished walking to the lectern. The line of frostwater sank into the paper and vanished, leaving only a faint raised trace, like a vein under skin.

Lin Hao did not move for two full seconds.

Then, very gently, he closed the notebook.

He did not need to ask whether she had heard. That frost was not a guess and it was not a warning; it was a *receipt*. She had been at the door with her back to him, across five meters and a hundred rustling students, and she had caught a breath his own neighbor only caught because she was sitting closer than the notebook — and she had not turned, and she had not spoken, and she had not punished him. She had done the one thing that was worse and better than all of those. She had answered in the language of her own ledgers: she had written down that she heard, and ticked it, and moved on to the next column.

*She heard every word,* he thought. *She has heard every word I have written in this room, and she put her hand on the margin of my own book to tell me so.*

It should have frightened him. It did frighten him, in the small cold place where he kept the things that were true whether he liked them or not. But underneath the fright, to his complete lack of surprise, there was something else. The front row was not a cage, then. It was a conversation. He had said *old woman*; she had said *I heard*, and she had said it without spending a single breath on it, the way a far stronger swordsman ends a bout by letting the student feel the point and not the wound.

Xiaoyan was still looking at him, round-eyed. She mouthed a single word at him, soundless and aghast.

Lin Hao mouthed one back, perfectly calm, because there was no point in pretending the account was anything but open.

"Noted."

## Part 2: The Rules

"On behalf of Shrek Academy," Shen Yi said from the lectern, "I welcome you. I will say each of these things once, because they concern your survival here, and I do not repeat myself."

She told them the shape of the place, and Lin Hao wrote each rule in the book with a number already attached to it, because that was how he heard rules — not as warnings, as prices.

Shrek made its own law. The Federation had no jurisdiction here; punishment was set by the academy and would have nothing to do with whatever the Federation decided. He filed that beside the sky-enforcer who had pressed an Angel Clan prodigy to his knees the night before and let the girl walk on the strength of a card. The points were everything: they bought resources, spirit souls, time in the special facilities, a teacher's private guidance, entry to the spirit ascension platform, and — for working students — food. They could not be bought with money. They were earned, job by job, and they could be moved between students, and if a student ran himself to minus one thousand, he was expelled.

"Classes are held in the mornings only," Shen Yi said. "Your afternoons and evenings are your own, and so is falling behind if you waste them. Tardiness counts as an absence. Three absences, and you are gone. Nothing bought with Federation money comes onto this campus; bring any and you are fined ten times its price in points." Her gaze passed over the first row without pausing. "Working students buy their own meals and their own optional courses. And there is one thing Elder Cai did not say at the ceremony. In the yearly minor examination, the bottom five of each year are expelled."

She let that sit.

"Shrek Academy does not raise students. It raises monsters. You may spend your year at leisure if you like. The year after it will simply not include you."

Xiaoyan had gone very still beside him. Lin Hao watched the class take it — the hundred one-time geniuses of a hundred lesser academies, discovering that the thing they had all been first at was, here, the bottom edge of a machine that ground the lowest five out of every batch annually. He priced it himself, the way he priced everything. He was not at risk of the bottom five. Wulin was not. None of the five were, on merit. But the rule was not aimed at merit; it was aimed at *ease*, and it meant that the margin he lived in was not optional here — it was the air.

He thought about the frost receipt on the page behind the notebook's cover. *Bottom five,* he wrote, and then, in a smaller hand that was entirely for himself: *She is not going to let me be near the bottom five. That is not what the front row is for. She wants to see where I go when the bottom five are behind me.*

Gu Yue, two seats away, did not look at him once during the rules. She sat straight and listened with the particular focus she gave to anything that resembled a system being explained, and Lin Hao knew she was not hearing rules — she was hearing architecture. She was mapping the machine the way he mapped a room's entrances. She had not seen the frost. She had only seen him close his notebook with an expression she had learned to read, and he felt her file that without comment, the way water files the shape of a stone it means to come back to.

Shen Yi finished the rules and did not pause for breath.

"Now," she said, "this morning's topic. Battle armor masters."

The lecture theatre came alert, the way a room does when the word that all of them crossed a continent for is finally spoken aloud.

"I expect some of you are wondering why the very first class is about battle armor masters." Shen Yi's eyes moved over them. "It is because the basics were supposed to be learned before you arrived, or you would not have passed through the gate. So I will not spend the year on basics." She stopped. "What is a battle armor master? Most of you, if you are honest, will say: a more powerful kind of mecha pilot."

A small, subconscious wave of nodding moved through the seats.

"Then," Shen Yi said, "you are all gravely mistaken."

## Part 3: Clothes For The Martial Soul

She took them apart with the precision of a smith laying out a broken mechanism, piece by piece.

A mecha pilot put clothes on a *person*. A battle armor master put clothes on a *martial soul* — because at the core, the master was still a soul master, and the armor was not worn so much as married, fused, made to resonate and attract and become whole with the soul it served. One-word armor was the beginning, not the achievement; it was only at two words, when a master was one with the armor and it moved as an extension of body and soul together, that the word *battle armor master* actually meant anything.

The professions, then. A student had to choose a second craft that built armor — mecha maker, carving the circuits that let armor and soul fuse; mecha designer, drawing armor that fit a martial soul the way a hand fits a glove; or the harder paths, mechanic or blacksmith, the smith's road the most strenuous and the slowest, the one the academy admitted with its own mouth was the most appropriate craft for *powerful* armor. No one mastered all four. You chose. You would also need the other crafts to finish what you started — you would pay for them in contribution points, and you would be wise to befriend the people who did them well.

"Personal relationships," Shen Yi said, "are part of one's strength. Remember that."

And the number that made the whole room go quiet: armor and master in perfect unity could amplify a soul master's power by twenty soul ranks.

Lin Hao wrote it all. Under *blacksmith — hardest, most appropriate for powerful armor* he put a small mark of his own, the mark of a boy who already was a fifth-rank smith with a failed wrist-guard hung on a failure wall and a name for it waiting. Under *relationships are part of strength* he underlined twice, because he had believed that before Shrek named it — it was the wall with the cuff, the tree, the curtain, the captain, the five hands stacked on one another; it was the whole reason a margin was worth keeping. Under *twenty soul ranks* he paused, and thought about the one-word-armor law he had already priced at the lake with Wu Zhangkong — the twenty ranks a suit lent an ordinary master — and about how little it changed the math of a boy who had been standing in the Emperor margin on no armor at all.

Then Shen Yi gave them the wall.

"To become a one-word battle armor master," she said, "you require five soul rings and the third rank of your secondary profession. Your metal must be thousand refined. Your design must come from a grandmaster designer, your assembly from a grandmaster maker. Your compatibility with the finished armor must be at least sixty percent, and you will need at least nine pieces for it to be called armor at all." The room was very still. "Of outer-court students, one third — thirty-three percent — ever graduate at all. A one-word master by twenty-five may sit for the inner court. By thirty-five, if you are not yet a one-word master, you leave without graduating."

She let the numbers stand where they were.

Lin Hao watched Wulin, three seats off. Wulin's hands had closed slowly on the edge of the desk. Twenty-five for everyone else. Twenty for the five of them — Elder Cai's bar, the thing she had set them back at the assessment, which made a different kind of sense now that they knew she owned the outer court. Five rings, third-rank craft, nine pieces, sixty percent — and five fewer years to carry all of it than any other student in the room.

*Time waits for no one,* Lin Hao thought, and saw the same sentence pass through Wulin's shoulders. He did not lean over to say anything; this was not a thing that was helped by being said. He only made sure his own notebook had the whole of the wall written down cleanly, because the difference between thirty-three percent and the five of them was going to come down to who kept the clearest ledger of the work.

He had, he noted without any particular pride, a head start on the metal. He was the only thirteen-year-old fifth-rank smith the academy had admitted since it stopped admitting people like him. That was not arrogance; it was inventory. The armor was a circuit carved into the soul — he had known that in a midnight meditation before Shrek ever said it — and he knew the names of the failures on his wall, and the one piece of heavy silver that had folded at the fourth strike and hung nameless, waiting to be given its word.

Morning classes had no breaks. One lecture followed the next until the bells let them out, and the five of them walked to the dining hall in the particular silence of people who have just been shown a wall and are estimating its height.

## Part 4: Arithmetic, Not Charity

The crisis was not spiritual. It was financial, and it had Wulin's stomach in it, which made it approximately a force of nature.

"One hundred contribution points," Wulin said at the dining-hall door, staring at the windows the way a drought farmer stares at rain. "It feeds twenty normal people. It does not feed me."

He had been paid the day before for the plaza work, and a hundred points was a comfortable day for any boy in the outer court who was not Tang Wulin. He had taken the first-grade thousand-refining job at the administration counter that morning to build against exactly this, but a job completed and a job paid were not the same hour, and his stomach had never once in its life respected a processing delay.

Lin Hao watched him set his jaw. Then Wulin did the Wulin thing — he took a board from his storage device, wrote on it, and planted himself in the doorway with it held up where the whole lunch crowd could read:

*Fourth-rank blacksmith, capable of first-grade thousand refining. Short on points. Will trade first-grade thousand-refining for a year's worth of meals. Anyone interested, treat me to a meal.*

He had modestly left off that he was fifth-rank. Lin Hao noted the modesty and respected it; at his age, claiming fourth in the doorway was already asking the room to disbelieve him.

The room disbelieved him. Students flowed past in an unending stream, glanced at the board, glanced at the boy holding it, and did the math on their faces — *a year's worth of meals, on that?* — and kept walking. Not one stopped.

"Boss really will do anything to eat." Xie Xie had come to look and then bolted back to the table, mortified on his captain's behalf. "But isn't that a little too embarrassing? Why is he selling his skills at the door like that?"

"Captain doesn't care about anything he can't eat," Xiaoyan said, and giggled, then picked at her own lunch. "Anyway, after the mountain yesterday, nobody's going to take him. Imagine feeding him for a year! A month might work, but his stomach is just a bottomless pit."

Gu Yue said nothing. She stood near the door watching Wulin with a small frown between her brows, then looked down at her own tray and went back to eating with the focused, ruffled energy of a girl who did not have a place to put a feeling and so was putting it into food.

Lin Hao did not pick up a board.

He stood in the margin and watched the stream of students, and he did the arithmetic out of the same clear cold place he did everything in. His own contribution account was not large — the smith's Association registration still waited on its morning, the pavilion work paid slow, and he kept the Yaluo metals in reserve rather than points. But it did not need to be large for this. Wulin's thousand-refining job would pay a thousand points on completion, which meant the points existed, in the near future, as surely as ore exists in a vein. What was missing was the present tense of them — the food in front of the captain today.

You could not, by Shen Yi's own rule, buy points with money. You could move them between students.

He went to the window himself. He did not announce it. He paid for a tray — two trays, the second one arranged with the kind of absent thoroughness that produced, without commentary, a small honest mountain — and carried it back through the crowd and set it down at Wulin's elbow where the board stood.

"You can put the sign away," Lin Hao said. "Today's points are mine."

Wulin stared at the tray, then at him. "You don't have to —"

"I know I don't. That's why it's not a favor." He took the board out of Wulin's unresisting hand and turned it face-down against the wall. "When your thousand-refining pays, you feed the table for a week and we call the ledger square. You fed the whole of Eastsea when there was nothing in it for you; I have a good memory for debts. This is arithmetic." He paused. "Also, if you stand in that doorway one more minute, Gu Yue is going to start icing the floor, and I would rather not explain that to an administrator on our second day."

Wulin looked at the tray. Then he looked at Lin Hao the way he looked at things he had decided not to put words on, and sat down, and ate, and did not argue, because arguing with a debt stated as arithmetic was the one kind of argument Wulin had never won in his life.

Xiaoyan saw it. Xie Xie saw it and, for once, did not make the joke, which was how Lin Hao knew it had landed properly. Gu Yue saw it — she had the angle from the door — and her fork stopped for half a beat over her tray before she went back to eating, and the small ruffled frown between her brows did not go away, but it turned into something else, something Lin Hao, already turned back toward his own seat, did not see.

He did not need to see it. He was not, today, in the business of reading what was written in frost.

That night, in the working students' dormitory, after the curtain was hung and the others meditated, Lin Hao opened his notebook to the page with the receipt.

The frost had dried hours ago. The underline and the little downward tick remained, a faint raised trace in the paper, permanent as a scar. He sat with the pen over it for a long moment.

Then, under her tick, in the same careful hand, he wrote one word.

*Noted.*

He should have closed the book there. He nearly did. But it had been a long day, and the front row was a conversation, and he had never in his life been able to leave a column with an empty last line. So in the smallest hand he owned, underneath the *Noted*, almost too small to read, he added two more words for the ledger's other keeper, with the reckless good cheer of a boy who absolutely, mathematically, could not be heard from this far.

*…old woman.*

He held his breath for half a second. Nothing happened. The ceiling did not frost. The curtain did not stir. The room stayed warm and ordinary and quiet.

He stared at the ceiling a moment longer, not quite able to fully believe the nothing, the way you never quite believe a very strong person hasn't heard something.

Then he closed the book, put it under his mattress with the third one she would never read, and put the matter in the same column he put every receipt he could not verify.

Open.

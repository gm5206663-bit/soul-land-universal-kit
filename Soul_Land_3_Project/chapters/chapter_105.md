## Part 1: The Mountain Cleared

The bet was lost, in the end, not at any single plate but at the moment Xie Peichen stopped counting them.

He had been back three times. The third time he carried ten orders of the dish that looked like sea cucumber and did not, on the return walk, look at the dishes at all; he looked at the floor in front of his feet, the way a man carries something he has agreed to carry and wishes only to put down. He set the ten plates down in the gap that Lin Hao had been quietly clearing for them — moving empty stacks one centimeter at a time, no gesture, no eye contact, the margin doing the work of a busboy — and Tang Wulin beamed at him.

"Senior," Wulin said, "can I trouble you for —"

"No," said Xie Peichen. It was the first complete sentence he had managed in twenty minutes. "No. I resign. You finished. You finished it all. I don't know what the second round was even for, because you appear to be on a fourth."

The discipline-committee armband was very red against his sleeve. His ledger, Lin Hao noted, had come up entirely empty; there was a particular look a man got when the column he had been so confident about simply had no number in it, and Xie Peichen had that look the way other men had a face.

The crowd had gathered like moths. A ring of Shrek students stood three deep around the table for ten, watching the mountain of empty plates grow the way people watch weather. "Did this guy starve to death in a past life?" somebody said, with real wonder. "Heavens, don't tell me he cleared all those dishes himself." And then, with the deep respect of the young for appetite: "Awesome. I've always admired people who can eat a lot."

It was into this that the manager arrived — a middle-aged man with a grim, pinched face, who had been pulled from his office by a kitchen reporting, impossibly, a shortage. They overstocked every day. They had never once run short. He came in trembling with the outrage of a man whose ledgers have been insulted, saw the staggering pile of dishes, and understood his shortage in the flesh.

"Hey. You. Student." His voice came out thin. "Your stomach is beyond the realm of normal. The Academy cannot feed someone like you. If you ate this much every meal, I will petition to have your right to a free meal revoked."

Wulin blinked. He had a bun halfway to his mouth.

"Teacher," he said, "you don't have to worry. I'm a working student — I can't eat this much for free starting tomorrow anyway. This is my last free meal. So can't I eat my fill?"

The ring of students changed expression all at once.

Lin Hao watched it go through them like a wind over a field — the leaning-in, the delight in spectacle, draining out to be replaced by something else entirely. *Working student.* The moths remembered they had somewhere else to be. The peanut gallery dissolved backward into the hall, minding its own business with sudden, devout attention to its own trays.

"So you're a working student," the manager said. His grimness did not leave, but it turned itself around, the way a man turns a blade he has decided not to use. "Alright then. Have a good meal." He left.

"Why," Xie Peichen said, with the hollow precision of a man reconstructing a disaster, "didn't you tell me you were a working student earlier?"

"What about being a working student?" Wulin asked, genuinely curious.

Xie Peichen made a sound in his throat, refused to pay him any further attention, and disappeared into the crowd — a committee man returning to his committee with, Lin Hao suspected, a brand-new column in his ledger labeled with a single name and no number at all.

The buns came after that. An older girl set a steamed bun on Wulin's plate and went off, muttering over her shoulder that it was pitiful, a boy that hungry still not full. Then another student. Then another. Wulin's eye corner twitched, and Lin Hao saw the captain's mind do the thing it did — turn charity immediately, and without shame, into logistics. *One hundred points is five or six ordinary meals. The cheapest dish is ten. Today cost over a thousand. Come tomorrow —*

"Come tomorrow," Lin Hao said quietly, across the wreckage of the table, "we price it properly. You're not starving on a dial while I hold a smith's wage. That's arithmetic, not charity."

Wulin looked at him. Then he nodded, once, and put the next bun in his mouth.

He staggered out of the hall at last, walking the way a man walks when each step brings him closer to bursting. The four of them fell in around him in the dusk light of Spirit Ice Plaza.

"Captain," Xie Xie said, wearing an odd expression, "can't you restrain yourself a bit? Everyone in the outer court recognizes you by now."

"How will restraint feed me?" Wulin said, wide-eyed. "Do you have any idea how painful it is to be hungry?"

"Fine. Pretend I didn't say anything."

"You know, I seem to remember someone saying he'd treat us all to dinner. Let's go."

"You're — *still* hungry?"

"I'll be done digesting by the time we get there," Wulin said, and herded them toward the outer-court street. Lin Hao went with them, because witness was the one thing he was unfailingly willing to provide for this captain, and because the smith's summons in his bag said *morning*, and the evening was otherwise unclaimed.

## Part 2: The Street of Orange Lights

The outer court at night was a town. It had shops. It had strings of warm orange lights over the street, and the lights were full of people in Shrek uniform moving in every direction with the particular confidence of people who had passed a gate most people could not pass. Xie Xie herded them through it like a tour guide who had been somewhere once.

"The outer court is practically a town itself," he announced. "We've got to explore. This place sells *drinks* — look, Shrek has this kind of thing too. Juice. Ice cream. Come on, I'm treating."

"You lost the bet," Gu Yue observed. "You are treating because you lost."

"I'm treating because I'm generous. There's a difference."

"There is not," said Xu Xiaoyan, already making for the door of the busiest shop on the street.

The drink shop was near capacity, warm and loud, full of uniforms and the clink of cups. They fought their way to a table the way people fight onto a raft — Xie Xie spotting it, Gu Yue freezing the legs of the chair across the gap with a thread of ice so nobody could pull it out, Xiaoyan sliding into it sideways like an eel — and a server dashed over. Juice for the boys; ice cream for the girls, who dug in with the focused joy that, the canon of the entire world suggested, no girl could resist, not even the detached Gu Yue.

Xie Xie sat with his back to the wall and his eyes moving over the room.

"What are you looking at?" Wulin nudged him.

Xie Xie smirked. "Scouting. I'm seeing whether there are any pretty senior sisters here. A man has to prepare for adulthood."

"How," Wulin said, with honest doubt, "does looking prepare you?"

Lin Hao did not answer this. He had taken the seat that let him see the door — not out of caution, there was nothing in this room to be cautious of, but out of the same quiet reflex that made him clear plates one centimeter at a time: he liked to know where the entrances were, the way other people liked to know where the exits were. It meant he saw the door open.

It opened twice in the next minute. The first time it let in a wall of a boy in Shrek uniform — an outer-court senior like the girl behind him, round and pink and beaming with the particular radiance of a person who has smelled food from a great distance and followed it home. The second time it let in the girl behind him.

Lin Hao's eyes went past the boy — who had stopped dead in the doorway, nostrils flaring, his whole being arrested by the sight of Wulin across a crowded room with the remnants of ice cream at his corner — and landed on the girl, and stayed there, in the margin, for one held half-second.

He knew her. He would have known her in a darker room than this. Stargod Sword at her hip, hilt worn the way a tool wears that is used every single day; three rings under the skin of her, kept dimmed in a shop — two yellow and the third a thousand-year purple that had not been there at the exhibition, the mark of a Soul Elder who had not spent the last four months standing still; the set of her shoulders like she was always half a breath from deciding something. Ye Xinglan. A senior of the outer court now, an inner-court blade sent down where the real forging happened. Four months on the Track, to his nine days. Thirty-one seconds of sparring, in which her confidence had not moved at all. The girl who had decided, once, that she would like to be the one who said something first.

She had not seen him yet.

He watched her look around the shop — and here is what he watched, because it was not what he expected.

## Part 3: What The Sword Did

Ye Xinglan had come in for ice cream, which she would never have admitted to Lizhi, who had already abandoned her in the doorway and was now moving toward a table of strangers with the unstoppable momentum of a meatball rolling downhill. She let him go. She did her own scan of the room, the way she always did, the way she did everything — quickly, decisively, forming an opinion and then telling herself about it later.

Her scan passed over the room and snagged.

There was a boy at a table by the wall.

That was wrong as a first thought, because "boy" was not the right word and she knew it the instant her eyes landed. He was tall — taller than she remembered people being, though she couldn't have said who she was comparing him to — and he was sitting down, which made the tallness worse, because it meant most of him was still not on display. He was broad across the shoulders in the particular way of someone who had stopped being fed and started being *built*, and he had a jaw that had finished whatever argument a jaw has with a face and won it. Black hair, a little long, pushed back behind one ear. Hands around a cup of juice that were too big for the cup and still somehow gentle with it — smith's hands, she thought, or swordsman's, the kind of hands that knew their own strength so well they never had to show it.

The worst part — and Ye Xinglan, who decided things, was immediately furious about the worst part — was the stillness. The whole shop moved: servers dashed, uniforms leaned and laughed, Lizhi was even now descending on the fat boy at the table with a cry of recognition. And this one sat in the middle of all of it like a stone in a stream, the water going around him. Not cold. Not aloof. *Still.* The stillness of something that had already decided where it was going to be and had gotten there ahead of everyone else.

She realized, with a slow and total horror, that she was still looking.

Ye Xinglan did not do this. She was the girl who decided things and told Lizhi about them later, usually while he was eating. She had looked at the three-purple-ring boy at the exhibition four months ago and her confidence had not *moved* — she had measured a prodigy in thirty-one seconds and filed him under *fast, will be interesting later*, and gone back to her sword. She did not stand in the doorways of drink shops being snagged on strangers like a cloak on a nail. It was inefficient. It was undignified. She decided, with great firmness, that she would not look again.

She looked again.

And something happened that she did not understand at all.

The Stargod Sword at her hip — the martial soul that lived half in her and half in the steel, that sparked like fallen stars when it stood in the presence of sword-force worth the name — did not spark.

It *quieted*.

Ye Xinglan's hand went to the hilt before she decided to move it. The blade under her palm was still, in a way she had only ever felt it once or twice in her life: not empty, not dormant. *Attentive.* The way a dog goes still when a wolf walks past the fire. It was the sword's version, she realized with a cold, electric jolt up her wrist, of holding its breath.

She looked at the stranger's face again — the handsome, grown, impossible face of a boy she was absolutely certain she had never met — and her sword, which had never once been wrong about a swordsman in her life, told her in the language of perfect silence that she was in the presence of something her blade considered, deeply and without argument, its better.

That was impossible. She was a Soul Elder now — three rings, the third a thousand years old — and one of the most diligent sword-consciousness students in the outer court; she trained the man-and-sword-as-one state the way other people ate, and there were perhaps four blades in the whole outer court she respected, every one of them a senior she had measured at length. She knew all of their faces, and none of them was this one.

This was not one of them.

This was not —

She was crossing the room. She noticed she was crossing the room approximately three steps into crossing it, which was also not how she operated; she formed opinions *before* she acted, it was her single most reliable trait. She could not, for the life of her, decide what she was going to say. This had been her entire complaint, four months ago — *I would like to be the one who says something first for once* — and here she was, presented with a perfect stranger her sword had knelt to, walking at him without a single word prepared.

She opened her mouth. She would say something first. She would. It was her turn.

"You," she said, and her voice came out, to her complete and withering fury, not the way she had decided it would come out at all.

## Part 4: The Boy Under The Man

The tall stranger looked up at her.

And Ye Xinglan's prepared sentence — whatever it was, she still did not know — died entirely, because when he looked up she saw his eyes.

They were dark and steady and far, far too old for the handsome young face they were set in. And she knew them.

She knew them the way you know a doorframe you walked past as a child and come back to find the house rebuilt around it. The face was a stranger's. The eyes were not. She had looked into those eyes for thirty-one seconds while her Stargod Sword threw stars at a boy who was not where he had been standing four times out of five. She had watched those eyes stop tracking her body and start tracking, with a calm that had genuinely unsettled her, where she was *going to be*.

"Oh," Ye Xinglan said.

The stranger set down his juice cup, gently, with the too-big hands.

"Ye Xinglan," he said.

It was not a question. He had known her since the door opened — she could see that now, read it in the margin of him the way she read sword-force: he had watched her scan the room, watched her snag, watched her walk over, and said nothing, and let her arrive. He had been watching her the entire time, and she, the girl who decided things, had decided nothing at all.

"You," she said again, and this time she heard her own voice do something she did not care for, something thin and off-balance. "You're —" She stopped. She rebuilt. She was Ye Xinglan; she decided things and told people about them later. "You are the boy from the exhibition. The three rings. The Track."

"The same," Lin Hao said. "Though it's four rings now. And you took four months to learn the Track. I took nine days. I believe I owe Lizhi a thank-you for keeping score."

Across the table, Wulin — who had been staring at the round boy bearing down on him with the glazed joy of a man watching his dessert arrive with legs — finally placed the situation. "Xinglan?" he said. "And — is that *Lizhi*?"

"IT IS," the round boy bellowed, crashing into the table like a friendly ship running aground. "Tang Wulin! I smelled you from the street, I mean I smelled the *food*, I mean — are you going to finish that ice cream?" He did not wait for an answer. He turned, beaming, to Lin Hao, and did a small double-take that he visibly powered through with the force of goodwill. "And you! The other one! The quiet one who hit — wait." Lizhi blinked. He looked Lin Hao up and down the way he looked at a banquet table. "Wait. You got *big*. Were you this big? You weren't this big. You were a normal size. I would have remembered this size, I remember sizes."

"Nobody remembers sizes, Lizhi," Ye Xinglan said, without looking at him. Her eyes had not left Lin Hao's face. "They remember whether there was enough food."

"There is never enough food," Lizhi said, with conviction, and sat down, and that was that.

She was still standing. She noticed she was still standing, and that the entire table — the ice-cream girl, the pretty one with the frost on her sleeve, the dagger-boy who had been scouting the room and was now watching with open amusement, the captain, and him — had an empty chair she was not taking.

She took it. She decided to take it. She told herself, firmly, that she had decided to take it.

"Four rings," she said, because rings were safe ground, rings were a thing she could measure. "You said four. What colors."

He tilted his head a fraction. It was the smallest thing, but her sword, under her hand where she had not removed it, went quieter still.

"Three purple," he said. "And one black."

The table did not know what that meant. Xie Xie's scouting smirk stayed on his face. Xu Xiaoyan's spoon paused. But Ye Xinglan felt the Stargod Sword do the thing it had done in the doorway — the held breath, the wolf at the fire — and understood now, in the hush of her own blade, why the handsome stranger in the chair was a stranger at all.

The boy she had sparred for thirty-one seconds had not merely grown into his face. He had crossed some distance she could not see the far side of, in four months — the same four months in which she herself had broken through to a third ring, won a thousand-year spirit, and climbed the sword-consciousness ladder rung by rung, proud of every hard step. He had been a fast child with three purple rings and a movement trick. The thing sitting across from her in a too-tall body with a black ring and a sword that made her sword go silent had gone somewhere she had not even known there was a road to.

And she had walked across a drink shop because she thought he was handsome.

Ye Xinglan, who decided things and told Lizhi about them later while he ate, sat in the warm orange light of the loudest shop in the outer court and discovered, with a slow, burning, furious, completely un-decidable clarity, that she could not have decided to look away even if she had formed the opinion first.

"Thirty-one seconds," she said, at last. It came out level. She was proud of it for coming out level. "Last time. You were fast and you couldn't decide where to stand, so you decided where I would stand instead."

"I remember," Lin Hao said.

"I am not," said Ye Xinglan, "the same swordswoman I was four months ago either."

It was a challenge. It came out as a challenge, because everything she said came out as a decision, and she decided, right there in the drink shop with Lizhi stealing Wulin's ice cream and Xie Xie leaning in to watch like it was the best scouting result of his entire life, that she would not be the only one at this table who had crossed a distance.

The stranger with her sparring-partner's eyes looked at her for a long, steady moment — and smiled, just slightly, the kind of smile that did not show teeth and did not promise anything and made her, to her deep and lasting annoyance, glad she had sat down.

"I'd noticed," he said. "Your sword's been holding its breath since you walked in the door. Mine's been waiting to see if yours would say something about it."

Ye Xinglan's hand tightened on the hilt.

"You have a sword," she said. It was not a question.

"I have several," Lin Hao said. "But I think you already knew that. Yours told you before you did."

Outside, on the street of orange lights, the Shrek night went on around them — full of uniforms and voices and a thousand people who had no idea that in a crowded drink shop two swordswomen, one of them a girl who had just lost every bit of her famous decisiveness and would not admit it for anything in the world, had sat down at the same table for the first time in four months.

Lizhi, his mouth full of stolen ice cream, looked from his oldest friend's rigid back to the tall quiet boy she had planted herself beside, and did the one thing he was genuinely, reliably good at besides eating.

He decided not to say a word about it.

He told himself he would tell her later. Usually while she was eating.

---

*He was the same boy. He was not the same boy at all. I walked across a room because of a face, and my sword walked across it because of something else, and the worst part — the part I will not tell Lizhi, the part I will tell absolutely no one — is that I don't think he was surprised to see me. I think he's been sitting by the door all night. I think he always sits by the door. And I think, when I walked over, he already knew I would.*

*That's the thing I have to decide about. I haven't decided it yet. I, Ye Xinglan, who decides things, have not decided it.*

## Part 1: What The Competition Asked

The metal was blue coppertite, and Lin Hao understood the problem in about four seconds, because he had understood it once before, in Mu Chen's forge, on a bar that had been deliberately badly refined.

Coppertite was not hard. That was the trap. It *changed* — it moved under the hammer in a way that was not the way it had moved a strike earlier, and every strike you made was a strike against a thing that had already decided to be somewhere else.

A hundred strikes would finish a Hundred Refinement if you had the Stacked Hammer effect.

Lin Hao did not have the Stacked Hammer effect.

He had something else, and he had had it about six weeks, and he had never had to use it in front of anybody, and he stood in front of a bar of blue coppertite in a competition hall in Skysea City and understood he was about to find out what it was called.

He started.

## Part 2: What He Did

He did not tell the metal what to do.

That was the thing he had learned in Mu Chen's forge, on the ninth day, with two broken hammer faces, and it had felt like a betrayal of everything he had been taught, because four years of blacksmithing had been four years of *asking* — reading the grain, finding where it was willing, going where it wanted to go.

Coppertite did not want to go anywhere. It wanted to be somewhere else than where it was, constantly, and asking it was useless, because by the time it answered it had moved.

So he told it.

He could not afterwards give a good account of the mechanism, and that troubled him, because he was accurate about everything else and was not going to pretend about this. What he did was stop asking and start *deciding*, and the deciding was not loud, and it was not forceful, and it was the same act that made the Track work, and the same act that had made a blade into a sword, and he had never once connected those three things until he was standing in front of a bar of coppertite in a competition hall.

Eighty-one strikes.

He wrote it in the ledger, because that was the habit, and then noticed he had written it in the ledger, and that the ledger had stopped being a list of the people he had fought and quietly become a list of the things he had counted, and that he could not say when that had happened. One hundred and four lines. Not one of them a person he had not beaten, except the one written in different ink, which was still the most useful line in it.

He set the hammer down.

Wulin was in the fourth row.

Lin Hao had not known that, and understood afterwards that he had not known because he had not looked, which was the thing he would think about for the rest of the day. Wulin sat with his hands on his knees and his body angled slightly forward, the way he sat when he was watching somebody work and had decided in advance not to be a distraction, and he had brought nothing to eat, and Xie Xie was two seats down and had plainly been told to be quiet and was managing it the way Xie Xie managed things, which was loudly.

They had come to watch him hit metal. Neither had said they were coming.

Lin Hao had forged in front of Wulin for four years and had never once noticed him arrive.

The supervising staff member came over, and stumbled over his own feet, which was a thing that happened to that man, and looked at the bar.

"You're finished?"

"Yes."

"Are you sure you've hundred-refined it? If you haven't succeeded, you'll be eliminated."

"I'm certain."

## Part 3: What The Old Man Said

The white-haired old man on the stage said, "Bring that child's product over here."

And then, after a moment, in a voice that carried, because he had been on a stage a long time and had learned which sentences needed carrying:

"If he isn't spouting nonsense, then he's really a terrifying genius."

Lin Hao stood where he was and did not move, and understood that the sentence had not been about the bar.

The bar was eighty-one strikes. Eighty-one strikes was a number that meant something to perhaps nine people in the room, and the old man was one of them, and the old man had not said *eighty-one strikes*. He had said *terrifying*.

Because the old man had been watching the strikes, not the bar.

Lin Hao understood that in about two seconds, and the understanding was the most uncomfortable thing he had experienced in Skysea City, and he had stood in front of a Shrek student with a storage ring and been the largest thing in a room for four months, and none of that had been as uncomfortable as an old man on a stage saying *terrifying* about the way he hit metal.

"Fourth rank," the old man said. "Grandmaster. How old."

"Ten."

There was a silence in the hall that had a great deal in it.

"And the Halo," the old man said. It was not a question. "You produced a Halo. At ten."

"Yes."

"How long have you been a fourth rank."

"Eighteen days."

The old man was quiet a while.

"I've been a fourth rank thirty-one years," he said. "I'm telling you that in front of everybody, and I've decided I'm not upset, and I've been sitting with not being upset about four minutes, which is a personal record."

## Part 4: What Gu Yue Did Not Do

She did not participate.

That was the strategy, and it was Wu Zhangkong's, and Lin Hao understood it before it was explained, because he had been watching the other teams and noticed that the ones who won were not always the strongest, and that the ones who won were the ones who had somebody left at the end.

Gu Yue sat in the stands with her hands in her lap and watched, and Lin Hao sat next to her, because he had started to understand that this was what she did instead of saying something.

"They're conserving me," she said.

"Yes."

"I don't like it."

"I know."

"Tell me whether it's wrong," Gu Yue said. "I'd rather be told than work it out. This is the second time I've asked you."

"It's not wrong," Lin Hao said. "It's the only strategy that works. You're the strongest of us, and the strongest is the one you keep, and I've watched four teams lose because they used their strongest in the second round." He stopped. "You asked, so I'm telling you rather than letting you work it out."

Gu Yue turned her head a few degrees.

"That's the fourth time you've said the saying thing."

"I know."

"I said say it less."

And then Lin Hao asked her for something, which he had never done.

He had told her things. He had answered her. He had sat next to her and been quiet at her, and once, at the bottom of a slope, wanted her to see something without examining why. But he had never once, in the nine months since she walked into class five in the wrong uniform, asked Gu Yue for anything, and he noticed he was about to, and that noticing it was not stopping him, and that this was new.

"I want you to teach me something."

Gu Yue did not turn her head. Her hands stayed in her lap.

"What."

"My sword has two voices," he said. "Wind and lightning. They've both been in it since the forest, and I can use either one, and I can use them one after the other, and I've been doing that three weeks and getting away with it because nobody in this city has been close enough to tell me it's wrong."

"It's wrong."

"I know it's wrong. I don't know *how* it's wrong, which is the part I can't get past, because I can hear that they're two things and I can't hear what one thing would sound like." He turned the Stormbringer a quarter turn in his hand, which was what he did when he was thinking with it. "You have six elements. You've put fire and earth in the same five seconds twice this week. You're the only person I've ever met who made two things into one thing on purpose."

"That isn't the same as what you're asking."

"I know."

"Elemental fusion is a soul skill. Yours is a *sword*." She said it flatly, the way she said the difficult ones. "I can't teach you a sword."

"I'm not asking you to teach me a sword." And then, because he had decided some time in the last four seconds to say the whole of it rather than the safe half: "I'm asking what it feels like from the inside when two things that aren't each other agree to be one thing. You're the only person I know who's been there. I can get the rest from the metal."

Gu Yue was quiet long enough that the crowd two rows down started something and let it die.

"You've never asked me for anything," she said.

"No."

"Why now."

"Because I have three rings and a sword with two voices and a tournament in four days, and because I've watched you make the ground into something that wasn't ground under a man stronger than you, and because—" he stopped, and restarted, because he had heard himself. "Because you're the only person I know who can tell me I'm wrong without being pleased about it."

Gu Yue turned her head then. Only a few degrees, which with her was the equivalent of anybody else standing up.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Before the light. Bring the sword and don't bring the hawk."

"The hawk isn't a thing I bring."

"I know what it is." She looked back at the field. "That's why I said it."

## Part 4-B: Before The Light

They met on the training field behind the dormitory at the hour when the sky was the colour of the inside of a shell, and Gu Yue stood with her hands at her sides and raised nothing at all, and said, "Show me the two."

Lin Hao laid a Gale Talon along the Stormbringer. Wind shoved; the lightning arrived. The bar at forty metres opened along a grain he had already read.

"That's a wind and then a lightning," Gu Yue said.

"Yes."

"Again. Slower. Tell me where each one is."

He did it slower. And slower. And on the fourth time, going so slowly it was barely a stroke at all, he heard it — and the hearing was so simple he nearly laughed, because it was the same thing he had been doing with metal for four years and had never once thought to do with a sword.

The wind was pushing. The lightning was going. They were not two things taking turns. They were two things with different ideas about distance, and the wind had been arriving *behind* the lightning every single time, because the lightning did not wait to be pushed.

"I've been laying the wind along the edge," he said. "And the lightning doesn't need the edge."

"No."

"So the wind isn't carrying it."

"The wind is *in the way*," Gu Yue said. "You've been making the lightning go through something. Take the something out."

He took the something out.

It was not loud. That was the part neither of them had expected, and Lin Hao wrote it down twice that morning for that reason. The stroke left the Stormbringer and there was no shove in it and no crack, and the air in a line forty metres long simply ceased to be one thing and became two, and the bar at the end of it did not open along the grain.

The bar was gone from the waist up, and the top half was still falling when the sound arrived.

Lin Hao stood there with his arm up and his sword in it and his mouth slightly open, and the marks on his forearms were standing up out of his skin and glowing, and he had not decided to do any of it.

"Again," Gu Yue said, from eleven metres away, where she had arrived without him noticing.

"I don't know what I did."

"Yes. That's why I said again." Her hands were still at her sides and her face was her face, and he had known her long enough to know that her face not telling him anything was itself information. "You've spent four years finding the seam in things, Lin Hao. You've never once looked for the seam in *yourself*."

He looked at her.

"That's what you were going to say at the tournament," he said. "When you told me I'd be difficult to fight. You already knew."

"I knew you had two things and one idea." Gu Yue looked at the bar, then back at him. "I didn't know it would do *that*." And then, very flatly, in the voice she used for the things that cost her something: "Nobody's ever done that in front of me before. I want you to know I'm not pleased about it."

"I know."

"And that I noticed you knew."

Wu Zhangkong was standing at the gate. Neither of them had heard him arrive, and Lin Hao understood from the way he was standing — weight on the back foot, arms not folded, the stick held low — that he had been there for the last part and had decided not to interrupt it.

"That isn't a soul skill," Wu Zhangkong said.

"No."

"Say why not."

Lin Hao thought about it, because Wu Zhangkong only asked questions he already had the answer to, and because getting it right was the whole of the exercise. "A soul skill comes out of a ring. That didn't come out of a ring. It came out of me deciding to stop putting the wind in the lightning's way." He turned the Stormbringer over in his hand. "Anybody with a sword could do it. Nobody with a sword has thought of it, because everybody lays the wind along the edge, because that's what laying wind along an edge is *for*."

"So it's a *technique*." Wu Zhangkong said the word the way he said the important ones, which was without any weight at all. "Which means it's yours, and it means it'll grow when you grow rather than when the hawk does, and it means she taught you a thing I couldn't have taught you." He looked at Gu Yue a moment. "I have four rings of frost and I couldn't have told him that."

"You'd have told him something," Gu Yue said.

"Yes. The wrong something." And Wu Zhangkong turned to go, and stopped, and said — without turning back, which was the only way he said the ones that cost him — "He's never asked anybody for anything. Not once, since I've had him. You should know you're the first."

"I know," Gu Yue said.

"You don't," Wu Zhangkong said. "You know that he asked. You don't know what it means that he asked *you*." And he went, down the path, in the shell-coloured light, and left the two of them standing on a training field with a bar missing its top half.

Gu Yue looked at the bar.

"He's wrong about most things," she said.

"Yes."

"That wasn't one of them."

"No."

## Part 5: What Xu Xiaoyan Did

She won the daytime match.

That was the part nobody had expected, because the starwheel was a night thing and everybody had built the shape around the night, and Xu Xiaoyan had spent four days being the person who made five seconds happen and could not move afterward, and in the daytime she had an ice staff and, by her own account, was weak.

She won in forty seconds.

Not with the staff. With awareness — she put two of them in the wrong place in the first six seconds and the third in the wrong place in the next four, and then she used the staff once, on the one who was already in the wrong place, and it was over.

Xie Xie had called her a two-faced little schemer, and Lin Hao had written that down, and he understood now it had not been an insult.

"That's the first time I've won in the daytime," Xu Xiaoyan said, sitting down, because sitting down was what she did. "I want somebody to notice, because I've been the person who makes five seconds happen for four days and nobody's said anything about the other twenty-three hours and fifty-nine minutes and twenty seconds."

"I notice," Lin Hao said.

"That's the third time you've said that to me."

"I know."

"Say it to somebody else."

"No," Lin Hao said. "I've decided noticing the things nobody says is the whole thing, and you're the one nobody says them to, and I'm not going to stop."

## Part 6: What Lin Hao Wrote

That night, in the room that smelled of the sea, he took the book down and wrote the number first, because the number never asked anything of him.

*Two hundred and forty-one.* It had been two hundred and thirty-three. Eight points, somewhere between eighty-one strikes and one daytime match he had watched from the stands. He could not say which had done it. The not-being-able-to-say had become a habit, and he had noticed that too.

*One thousand three hundred and sixty-six.*

*Eighty-one strikes. The old man didn't say eighty-one strikes. He said terrifying, and he was watching the strikes and not the bar, and I've been good at a thing four years and never once been called terrifying about it. The uncomfortable part wasn't being called terrifying. It was being seen.*

*I've been the largest thing in a room four months and a Shrek student was pleasant about a storage ring, and I discovered that was a local condition, and I wasn't upset, and I told myself the not-being-upset was because I'd seen what standing in front of costs.*

*That was wrong and I'm correcting it in the book.*

*The not-being-upset wasn't about standing in front of anything. It was because nobody had ever watched me hit metal. I've been good at a thing four years in a room by myself, and today an old man on a stage watched me do it and said terrifying, and the uncomfortable part was being seen. I hadn't been the largest thing in a room at all. I'd been the only person in it.*

And then the line at the bottom, which he wrote last.

*Eighteen days a fourth rank. Thirty-one years for the old man, and he said he wasn't upset, and that he'd been sitting with not being upset four minutes, which was a personal record.*

*Two men have now told me they're not upset. Wu Zhangkong said I don't know twice. Mu Chen hasn't been upset for eleven years. The old man on the stage hasn't been upset for four minutes.*

*I've started to suspect the thing I am isn't a thing anybody has a word for, and that the people who aren't upset are the ones who've stopped looking for the word, and that this is the closest I've come to being frightened, and that I'm not frightened, and that I'd like to know why I'm not.*

He closed the book.

The lock at his temple lay gold across his cheekbone, and the marks on his forearms were raised and had been raised for thirty days, and an old man on a stage had looked at them and said nothing, and the hawk turned over in him — *one thousand three hundred and sixty-six* — and did not pull toward anything.

Lin Hao lay on his back in a city that smelled of salt and hot metal, and thought about eighty-one strikes, and about the fact that he had been the only person in a room for four years, and about the fact that he was not frightened.

He slept.

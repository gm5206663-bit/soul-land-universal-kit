## Part 1: What Wu Zhangkong Said On The Way In

He said it while they were waiting for the light, which was the only time he had.

"You have seen a battle armor," he said. "Under glass, at an auction, for a number you could not reach."

"Yes."

"Then understand this before you go in, because it is the thing everybody gets wrong." He did not look at Lin Hao. He looked at the door. "Ordinary people can become Mecha Masters now. A support-type soul master with a mecha can be formidable. But **the origin of true strength has not changed in a hundred thousand years.** Only a genuinely talented Soul Master becomes genuinely powerful."

"Battle armor doesn't make you strong."

"**Battle armor has no power at all.** It amplifies. That is the whole of it." He said it flatly. "A six-ringed support-type soul master in a mecha is **cannon fodder** in front of a similarly ranked Soul Master in battle armor. That is the unbridgeable gap. Understood?"

"Yes."

"Say it back."

Lin Hao said it back. "Battle armor amplifies. So a man with nothing gets nothing amplified, and a man with something gets more, and the gap gets wider instead of narrower, and everybody who thinks the armor is the thing has misunderstood the whole of it."

Wu Zhangkong looked at him for the first time.

"Yes," he said. "And that is why I want you in the Tang Sect and not in a mecha factory."

Then the light came, and they went in.

## Part 2: What The Hawk Did

Lin Hao had been in the spirit ascension platform twelve times.

He knew what it did. He knew the wrongness of a forest that had not been a forest, the smell of a place that had never had weather, the way the light came down in a colour you could not name. He had a ledger entry for it. He had stopped being frightened of it around the time of the bear.

The intermediate platform was the same, and it was not the same.

The trees were **bigger.** Not bigger the way a building is bigger — older. The undergrowth was **thicker**, and it moved when there was no wind, and the light came down through it in shafts that had the quality of light in a cathedral, which Lin Hao had never been in but understood immediately.

And the hawk went still.

Not the four-hour stillness of the train window. That had been **recognition**, distant, through glass, at forty kilometres.

This was the hawk being **in the place it recognised.**

And it did not stop.

Lin Hao stood in the undergrowth with his hand on the Stormbringer and felt it — for the first time in two years — begin to **pull.** Not lean. Lean had been a suggestion. This was a direction, and it had a weight to it, and it was pulling him northeast, and he understood with a clarity that was almost insulting that he was going to go that way.

"Teacher," he said.

Wu Zhangkong was already looking northeast.

"I know," he said.

## Part 3: What Was Northeast

They walked for about eleven minutes, and the forest got older, and the pulling got stronger, and Lin Hao understood that he was not the only thing being pulled.

Wu Zhangkong was walking at the same speed he had been walking at, and his face was his face, and his hands were folded — and Lin Hao noticed, because he noticed, that Wu Zhangkong was **not correcting** him.

In twelve platform runs, Wu Zhangkong had corrected him perhaps forty times. Angle. Distance. Breath. Once, memorably, *"you are holding that like a man who is afraid of dropping it, and it is a sword."*

He said nothing for eleven minutes.

And then the trees opened, and there was a clearing, and in the clearing there was a thing that had been dead for a very long time.

It was enormous. It was on its side. It had been there long enough that the undergrowth had grown over part of it, and its ribs were the size of the beams in Lin Hao's father's forge, and its skull was turned toward them, and the sockets were empty, and there was a **sword** in it.

Not a sword-shaped thing. A sword.

Buried to the crossguard, in the place behind the skull where a thing like that would have had whatever it thought with, and it had been there long enough that the wood of the forest had grown around the hilt.

Lin Hao stood in a clearing in a forest that did not exist and looked at a dead thing the size of a house with a sword in its head, and he understood three things at once, and he did not say any of them out loud, because two of them were not his to say.

The hawk in him went **quiet.**

Not still. Quiet. The way an animal goes quiet when it has found the place it was looking for and does not need to look any more.

## Part 4: What Wu Zhangkong Did Not Say

He stood there for a while.

Then he said, "That is not part of the platform."

Lin Hao turned to look at him.

"The platform generates soul beasts. It does not generate **this.**" Wu Zhangkong's voice had not changed. That was the frightening part. It had not changed at all. "This was here before. I did not know that."

"How long."

"Long enough that a tree has grown around a sword." Wu Zhangkong looked at the skull. "This is a **thousand-year** beast at minimum. Probably considerably more. And somebody killed it, and left the sword, and walked away, and this is a place that does not exist, and I would like to know how."

Lin Hao looked at the sword.

He could read it. That was the thing — he had read four hundred thousand down to three hundred and fifty in four seconds in a quiet room two days ago, and he was standing in front of a blade that had been in a skull for long enough to grow a tree, and his head was doing the thing it was for, and what it was telling him was not a number.

It was telling him the blade had been **forged by somebody who was angry.**

That was not a blacksmith's reading. That was not a reading at all. And it was the most certain thing he had ever known about a piece of metal.

"Teacher."

"Mn."

"The hawk isn't pulling me toward the beast." Lin Hao said it slowly, because he was working it out as he said it, and because he had decided four days ago that he was going to find out before Wu Zhangkong did, and it had turned out that finding out was not a thing you did on purpose. "**It's pulling me toward the sword.**"

---

## Part 5: What Happened Next

He did not touch it.

He wants it in the ledger, in the column, in the book, for as long as he keeps the book: **he did not touch it.**

He took the Stormbringer off his back, because that was the thing his hands did, and he held it out toward the buried blade — not at it, toward it, the way you hold a compass — and the Stormbringer went **cold.**

Not the cold of the Skyfrost. Lin Hao had seen the Skyfrost do its cold; it was a *sharp* cold, a cold with an edge, a cold that burst a one-metre fireball on contact and turned it into a half-metre icicle that exploded into a million jagged shards. He had seen Wu Zhangkong do that eleven minutes later, to a thing called an Ice Fire Demon Tiger that had come into the clearing roaring, and he had understood what *sapphire* meant.

This was not that cold.

This was the cold of a thing **recognising** another thing.

And the hawk in him — **one thousand one hundred and ninety-three years of something that had never once answered to being called** — did the thing it had never done.

It **answered.**

Lin Hao did not hear it. That was the part he could never afterwards describe honestly, because he did not hear anything at all. What happened was that the pulling stopped, and the cold stopped, and the Stormbringer in his hand went from being a very good blade to being a blade that had **been somewhere**, and the three rings around his feet went bright at once — all three, including the empty one — and the empty one **took something in.**

He went down onto one knee in the undergrowth.

Wu Zhangkong was beside him in less than a second, which was the fastest Lin Hao had ever seen him move, and faster than he had moved at the bear.

"Do not fight it," Wu Zhangkong said.

"I'm not."

"Then what—"

"It's not fighting me." Lin Hao said it through his teeth, because it was taking a great deal of him. "It's **filling.**"

---

## Part 6: Thirty-Three

He came back to himself with his face in the leaf litter and Wu Zhangkong's hand on the back of his neck, and the clearing was empty except for the dead thing and the sword in it, and the Ice Fire Demon Tiger was somewhere it was not going to be a problem any more.

His rings were around his feet.

Three of them. **Purple, purple, purple.**

The third one was no longer empty.

And he was **thirty-three**, and he had not been thirty-three a moment ago, and the rank had not arrived the way the last one had arrived — quietly, in a room above a forge, at the end of a month of learning. This one had been **given.**

He sat up.

"Teacher."

"I saw."

"What was that."

Wu Zhangkong was quiet for a while. He was looking at the sword in the skull, and his face was doing something Lin Hao had never seen on it, and Lin Hao understood — because he had spent four days learning to read this man, and because he had learned at a grave that the record was not the person — that whatever was on that face was not going to be explained.

"I don't know," Wu Zhangkong said.

It was the second time.

"That's twice," Lin Hao said.

"Yes."

"You've explained a bear, and a legend, and a grave, and eight thousand years, and the price of a battle armor, and the arithmetic of a five-million card." Lin Hao got to his feet, slowly, because his legs had opinions. "And you've said *I don't know* twice in four days, both times about me."

Wu Zhangkong looked at him.

"You are not in either category," he said. "I told you that. And now you have three purple rings and a spirit soul at twelve hundred and forty-one years, and you got forty-eight years of it from a **sword in a skull**, and I have run out of things to compare you to." He folded his arms. "So I am going to stop trying, and I am going to take you home, and I am going to think about it for a long time."

"And then."

"And then I am going to write to somebody," Wu Zhangkong said, "who will know even less than I do, and we are going to be wrong together, and that is the best I have."

## Part 7: What Gu Yue Did

They told her at the inn, because there was no way not to.

Lin Hao had three purple rings around his feet and a spirit soul at twelve hundred and forty-one years, and he had got forty-eight years of it in one moment from a sword in a skull, and he could not put the rings away, because rings do not go away.

Gu Yue looked at them for a while.

She did not look at his face. She looked at the rings, which was the thing Lin Hao noticed, and which he understood, because he had spent four days learning to read her and four months before that failing to.

"How long," she said.

"Forty-eight years."

"In one moment."

"Yes."

She was quiet.

"From a sword."

"Yes."

Gu Yue looked at her own hands, which was the second time in four days, and Lin Hao understood that this was where she put things she was not going to say.

"I need to strengthen my body," she said at last. "That's all it is. My spiritual power is already there — it has been there since I was nine. All I need is a body that can hold it, and with two rings that is now too difficult, and Wu Zhangkong said so in front of all of us four days ago and called it a pity." Her voice did not change. That was the frightening part. "And you walked into a forest and a **sword gave you forty-eight years.**"

"Gu Yue—"

"I'm not angry." She looked up. "I want to be accurate, because you are always accurate, so I will be accurate too. I am not angry. I am doing arithmetic, and the arithmetic is that there are rules, and I have been inside them my whole life, and you are not in them, and nobody has been able to tell me what you are in." She stood up. "That is not anger. That is the first time I have ever been **outside** something."

She went to the door.

"Gu Yue," Lin Hao said.

She stopped.

"If I find out what it is," he said, "I'll tell you. Even if it's not something you can use. I'll tell you, because you're the only person I know who'd want the true thing rather than the useful thing."

Gu Yue stood in the doorway for a while.

"That is the nicest thing anyone has said to me," she said, "and it is also the thing I am least likely to want."

And she went out.

---


He wrote it that night, above the forge, with the window open, and he wrote it in the column, and he did not cross any of it out.

He wrote the number first, because the record came before the thinking now.

**two hundred and twelve.** It had been two hundred and fifty-four. Seven points, and he had not meditated, and he had not cultivated, and he had stood in a clearing and held a sword toward a blade in a skull and something had answered.

**Three purple rings. Twelve hundred and forty-one. Forty-eight of it from a sword in a skull in a forest that does not exist.**

**The hawk answered. I did not hear it. I want to be accurate about that because I have been accurate about everything else: I did not hear anything. What happened was that the pulling stopped.**

*Wu Zhangkong said I don't know twice in four days, both times about me, and then he said he was going to write to somebody who would know even less, and that they would be wrong together, and that was the best he had.*

*I have never been fond of him. I have been careful of him for four months and fond of him for four days, and I did not know the difference until a grave.*

Then the line at the bottom.

**I am not a category error.**

*A category error is a mistake. This is not a mistake. Something put a sword in that skull and walked away, and something in me answered it, and neither of those things was an accident, and Wu Zhangkong has run out of comparisons, and I have not.*

*I know what I am going to do. I am going to find out who forged that blade.*

*Because a man who forges can read a forge, and I read it, and it told me he was angry, and that is not a thing metal tells you, and I am going to find out why it told me.*

He closed the book.

The **hawk-gold lock** at his temple lay across his cheekbone, and the **marks on his forearms** were faint under his sleeves and had been raised all evening and had not gone down, and the hawk turned over in him — **one thousand two hundred and twelve** — and did not pull toward anything, because it had found what it was looking for.

Lin Hao lay on his back in a city with no skyscrapers and did not sleep.

## Part 1: The Ground With No Clipboards

The wild country began where the academy's fences stopped pretending to be fences.

Lin Hao walked out through the outer training ranges at first light with the culling ticket folded in his inner pocket and a borrowed field blade at his hip — plain forge-steel, no name, the kind the quartermaster handed out against a deposit and did not expect back in one piece. He had left the Stormbringer and the good steel in the dorm. There was no sense in taking a named weapon to a job he intended to do up close, and there was a smith's superstition in it too, the old one he had never quite grown out of: you did not bring a thing you loved to a place where things broke.

The ranges thinned out behind him. The marked practice grounds gave way to half-cleared forest, then to forest that had not been cleared at all, the trees going tall and close and unimpressed by the existence of Shrek Academy. The air changed. It stopped smelling of coke and swept stone and started smelling of wet bark and cold water and the particular sharp smell of ground that had not been walked on today by anyone human. He felt the academy's attention slide off his back the way water slides off a pitched roof — the cards, the points, the headmaster's frost, all of it falling behind him until there was only the wood and the job and the thing he had walked out here to do.

No clipboards. No classmates. No one to decide what he was.

He let out a breath he had been holding, in some form, for a year.

The ticket said the beast ranged a valley two hours west, a high-sided cut in the hills with a stream at the bottom and tree cover thick enough to hide anything that did not wish to be seen. He found the first sign before he found the valley: a tree scored at head height, four parallel gashes through bark and into the heartwood, the marks old and half-healed and wide enough apart that he put his own fingers into two of them and did not come close to spanning the set. The ground around the root was churned. Something large had been here, and had stood the way a thing stands when it owns the place it is standing.

He read on. Broken branches at the height of a tall man's shoulder — a charge mark, something low and fast and armoured smashing through cover without bothering to go around. A patch of scrub scoured flat. And then the first thing that made the margin in him sit up: a blade driven into a trunk and left there, snapped at the tang, the steel of a quality that told him its owner had not been a fool. It had bent on something and been abandoned. A good weapon, thrown away. The first team's work.

He found the second team's marker further down the slope — a scorch in a clearing, the blackened earth of a fire-element skill discharged at point-blank range, and at the edge of the scorch, a drag-gouge in the soil where something heavy had been pulled toward the trees and not, by the look of it, under its own power.

Two teams. Both turned back. One had left its dead.

Lin Hao stood at the lip of the valley and looked down at it, and the smith in him — the part that read metal the way other men read faces — looked at the way the trunks were scored, at the height of the broken branches, at the width of the charge-path, and said one quiet thing to the rest of him.

*Armoured. Dense. Low to the ground and fast despite it. Whatever they hit it with, it either bounced or slid off, and then it was on them before they could ready the next.*

He rolled his shoulders, once, and started down into the valley.

## Part 2: The Thousand-Plate

He found it at the stream.

It was drinking, or it had been. It stood the size of a small house, low-slung and slab-shouldered, built along the general plan of a boar if a boar had been designed by someone whose only aesthetic principle was *nothing gets through*. From the crown of its skull to the set of its massive haunches it was covered in plates — great overlapping shelves of a material that was not quite horn and not quite stone, the colour of old iron, each plate a hand's breadth thick at the centre and lapping the one behind it the way shingles lap a roof. A thousand of them, near enough, hence the name the outer rangers had written on the ticket: the Thousand-Plate. Its small eyes were set deep and red and unimpressed under the frontal shelf, and a pair of tusks swept up from its lower jaw, yellowed and scored, each the length of his leg.

Ten-thousand-year tier, minimum. Old. Smart. It had learned the shape of what killed things, which was why it was still here and two teams of students were not.

Lin Hao did not announce himself. He stood in the tree-line at the top of the bank and read the armour the way he would read a failed piece on his own bench.

For that was the thing, and he felt it land in him as clearly as a hammer finding its mark: he had been trying to build this. The heavy-silver wrist-guard that had folded at the fourth strike and hung on the failure wall without a name — that had been his first attempt to make metal carry what he wanted it to carry, and it had broken because he had not yet understood how armour actually worked. And here was armour that worked. Ten thousand years of the world refining the same problem in the dark, and the answer was standing in the stream twenty meters below him, drinking water out of a valley it owned.

He read the plates. The great shelves of the flank and shoulder were dead ground — hit those with anything he carried and it would slide, the way the first team's steel had slid, and spend itself on a surface engineered over a hundred lifetimes to spend attacks. You did not break a roof by hitting a shingle. You looked for where the shingles had to move.

And armour moved. A living thing in plates could not be one solid thing or it could not walk. There had to be seams — places where one shelf ended and the next began, gaps that opened when the beast lowered its head to charge or turned on its forequarters, joints where the thousand plates gave way to tendon and to the body underneath. He watched the beast shift its weight, and he saw the flank-plates slide, and in the sliding the seam showed for a fraction of a breath, behind the jaw, where the great throat-shelf lapped over the junction of the neck and the heart-girdle — the one place the plates could not quite reach without opening, the one place the living animal had to be soft.

The beast finished drinking. It lifted its head.

Its red eyes found him in the tree-line immediately, across twenty meters and up a bank, with the uncanny precision of a thing that had not lived ten thousand years by being surprised in its own valley.

It pawed the stream-bank once. The plates along its spine rose a fraction.

Lin Hao drew the borrowed field blade. He did not light a ring. There was no one to read him but the beast, and the beast did not care about rings; it cared about whether the thing in front of it was prey, threat, or neither.

He came down the bank.

## Part 3: A Fight That Answers

The charge came like a section of hillside deciding to move.

It was faster than anything that size had a right to be — the plates locked flat for the run, the tusks up, the red eyes fixed, and the air in the valley filled with the deep wooden thunder of its hooves. Lin Hao did not meet it. He read the line of it the way he read a descending hammer, the whole beautiful committed weight of the charge running to one inevitable point, and he let the Wind-Step take him a half-stride off that line — not away, *across*, the margin moving lateral while the mountain of plates went past near enough to smell the iron-hot heat of it. The tusk went by his hip with a hand's clearance and took the tree behind him out at the waist.

He struck as it passed. Gale Talon black-edged the borrowed blade, and he put the edge down across the nearest flank-plate, not at the plate's face but along its lapping edge — testing. The steel rang like a bell and the edge slid the way he had known it would, skating off the shelf with no more purchase than a finger on a wet pot, and the force of his own cut turned against him so that he had to flow with it or eat it.

He landed three meters on. The beast's charge carried it past; it wheeled, joints grinding, the front of it coming around with the grinding patience of a siege gate.

*All right,* he thought, with a thing in his chest he had not felt in too long uncoiling like a stretched muscle. *All right. There you are.*

He did not try the plates again. The second pass he took to the inside, moving before the charge had fully turned, and he rode the beast's own rotation — it could not reverse direction, it could only sweep around in its own great arc, and the inside of that arc was the one place the tusks were not and the shelves were busy keeping the spine locked. He was inside its reach with the front quarter of the beast beside him, close enough to set a hand on the iron hide and feel the heat and the huge slow grind of the body under it, and he walked along it for two long heartbeats, reading.

He was not holding back, not the way he had done for a year. He was moving at the speed the fight asked for, striking at the weight the strike required, spending the thousand small disciplines of the margin into an opponent where they were not a secret to keep but a tool to use. It was not full — he knew that even in the white heat of it, the way a man knows there are rooms in his own house he has not walked into; the great sealed things below him stayed sealed because the fight did not reach down to them, did not climb high enough to require them. But it was *real*. He was not waiting to see how much the room could take before he answered. He was answering with the whole of the reach he allowed himself, and against a thing ten thousand years old that had killed a team of soul masters, even that reach was a thing he had to pour out cleanly or die of his own carelessness.

It knocked him once. He mistimed a step on the inside and the beast's wheeling shoulder caught him and threw him across the stream, and he hit the far bank hard enough to feel it through the convergence body's whole frame, the water going up in a sheet around him. He came up out of it laughing once, short and bright and utterly unheard by anyone, because the hit had been *honest*, it had been a real thing landing on him and actually meaning it, and there was a joy in that no instrument had ever measured and no peer had ever been allowed to give him.

It charged again over its own shoulder. He let it come. He read the line.

And this time he did not move lateral.

The plates. He had spent three passes learning them, the way he spent the heats learning metal — asking, not telling. He knew now the one breath in the whole of the beast's engine where the seam showed. It was the moment the head dropped to drive the tusks home. At that exact instant, the great throat-shelf lifted a hair to let the shoulder and neck pull forward — lifting, because muscle had to pull against something — and for the width of a single heartbeat the seam behind the jaw opened, the dark junction where a thousand plates ended and the living thing began.

A blacksmith does not break armour. He waits for the place where it is already about to open, and he is on that place with the strike that belongs there when it does.

He did not waste it on strength. The blade was ordinary; the arm behind it was not, and the arm did not need to be. He guided the point through the opening breath of the seam with the Still-Water stillness on it, no tremor, no flourish, one clean stroke of a swordsman who had spent the whole fight *asking* — and the borrowed steel went in behind the jaw into the heart-girdle the plates could never reach, and Lin Hao put the whole of the convergence body's arm behind the last four inches the way you put a hammer through the final fold, and the thousand-year beast's charge came apart underneath him.

It took three more steps. The plates locked. The red dimmed. The great body went down into the stream with a sound like a foundry wall coming apart, and lay still, and the valley went quiet around it the way valleys do.

## Part 4: Nothing Wasted

He stood over it for a long minute with his breath coming hard and the borrowed blade black along its edge, and he felt the thing settle in him that he had walked out here to find.

It was not the kill. The kill was the smallest part of it, and he did it the honour of treating it as small. He had not come out hating this animal. It had owned its valley and beaten everyone sent against it and lived ten thousand years by being exactly what it was, and he had killed it because a job ticket asked it and because a thing that had dragged a student into the trees could not be left to do it again. He did not enjoy the ending. He put his hand on the cold iron of its crown for a moment, the smith's hand on good material, and said nothing to it because there was nothing a beast needed to hear and he was not a man who made speeches at the dead.

What he had come for was the fight, and he had had it — not the full thing, not the sealed rooms at the bottom of the house, but the largest honest stretch of himself he had been allowed to spend in a year, spent against something that hit back with everything it had and did not care who his father was or what his rings looked like. The itch was scratched. Under the satisfaction, though, there was the loneliness he had known would be there, the thing Gu Yue had not needed to say: no partner here. No captain to thump his shoulder, no Xie Xie to call it impossible, no one at all to tell. He had cut loose for the first time in a year in a valley with two corpses in it and no witness but the trees.

And the other finding, the one he wrote down carefully because it was the point of the whole exercise: he had not touched the ceiling. The beast had been strong enough to kill teams, strong enough to knock him through a stream, strong enough to make him take the fight seriously and spend his whole ordinary reach — and the great sealed reserves had not even stirred. The Union, the fusion, the Domain, the three-fold thunder he had never once tried: they had lain through the whole fight as quiet as they lay through the dinners and the lectures, because nothing in the valley had climbed high enough to knock on their door. He was, he suspected, going to have to go much further than a ten-thousand-year cull before he met the thing that made him *reach*.

He did not let the thought worry him. He had work to do.

A smith does not kill good material and walk away from it. The field knife came out and he butchered the carcass with the same care the fight had had, wasting nothing — the choice plates levered whole along their seams (the irony did not escape him: the armour that had turned every weapon sent against it came apart in his hands almost eagerly once you knew how it joined, and the plates were magnificent, dense and old and full of a strange iron-energy, exactly the foundation-metal for the kind of work the academy taught), the tusks drawn for the bounty proof, the heart-plate that carried the contract's verification mark. It took him the better part of the afternoon. He worked methodically, in the stream-water and the fading light, the way he worked any valuable stock: respectfully, completely, with the old mountain rule running through his hands that a beast you killed owed you nothing and you owed it the use of all of it.

He carried out what he could and marked the rest for the salvage team the job ticket promised, strapped the best plates to his pack with cord, and walked back up out of the valley as the light went gold behind the hills. His soul power, spent thin across the afternoon, was already climbing back toward him the way it always did, steady and certain, the deep sea of his spirit filling to its high-tide line. By the time he reached the outer ranges he was back to whole. Nothing of the crossing kind had happened. Nothing had been asked that his ordinary reserves could not answer.

At the administration counter he laid the heart-plate and the tusks down. The clerk looked at the proof, looked at the danger mark on the ticket — the beast that had come back empty twice — and looked at the thirteen-year-old in grey working-student clothes, and did the same arithmetic the other clerk had done and reached the same decision about saying nothing. He stamped the ticket and logged the bounty to Lin Hao's token, and the contribution points were, by Shrek's quiet accounting, considerable.

"Anything to report?" the clerk asked, because the form had the line.

Lin Hao thought of the score marks and the drag-gouge and the team that had left its dead in that clearing. He thought of the plates on his back.

"It was old," he said. "It fought well. The ticket's cleared." He did not say how long it had taken, or how much of himself he had spent, or how little. He did not mention the seam.

The clerk stamped it closed.

Lin Hao walked back out into the Shrek afternoon with a pack full of armour-in-waiting and a ticket paid and the first real fight in a year behind him — and in him, still, the question the valley had not been big enough to answer: where, on the whole wide continent, was the thing that would make him open the rooms he kept sealed, and when it finally came, would even that be far enough up the ladder to reach the door?

He shifted the heavy plates on his back, the dead armour already planning the shape of the suit it would one day become, and walked toward the foundry. He wanted to show He Qiao the metal. And somewhere in the back of his mind, he was already pricing the next ticket.

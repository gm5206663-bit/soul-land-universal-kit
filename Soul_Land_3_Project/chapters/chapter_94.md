## Part 1: The Rebound

The claw came down.

Wulin rode it in from the height of the silver delivery, full weight, blood essence behind it, aiming to spike the golden beast's skull into its own gem floor — and the blow that should have ended the fight *refused*.

The dragon claw bounced. Clean off. The crash still knocked the cub senseless — it hit the floor with a boom that rattled jewels down the mound — but the crushing weight, the thing his claw did to everything it had ever struck, simply did not happen. His arm went numb to the shoulder, and Wulin stared at his own hand as if it had been overruled.

"It's the luck," Gu Yue said quietly. "Its own legend. The Auspicious Emperor's blood won't let the crushing take it."

"Mind the head, Captain," Xie Xie added, already at his shoulder. "Break the skull and we may lose the bone. Everything we want is *in* the head."

Everything we want. Wulin looked down. The cub lay where it had fallen, out cold, breathing in small hitches. The scales of its crown were smashed to bits from the rebound — golden blood, bright as the floor, trickling from the cracks. Its third eye stayed shut. It looked, in the gem light, like something asleep in a treasure pile: a child in its own bed, in its own house, with the front door broken in behind it.

## Part 2: The Choice She Already Made

"Xiaoyan." Wulin stepped back from it. "It's yours. Like we said."

She came forward with her hammer and the whole cave went with her. This was the shape they had built on the way in — *we'll open the door; you walk through it* — and the door stood wide: the beast unconscious, the defenses broken, the class in a ring around it with their weapons pointed at the floor. There was nothing in her way. That was the thing. In her way there was nothing at all — no rank to cross first, no rival claim, no living defense. Just the walk.

She raised the hammer.

The cub's third eye stayed closed. Not a challenge in it. Not even fear. Asleep.

Xu Xiaoyan stood there one breath longer, and then she set the hammer down against the gem floor with a small clack, the way you set down a cup you've decided not to drink from.

"No," she said.

Nobody moved.

"It's a child." Her voice was small and did not shake. "It hid in its house and we broke the door in. Everything else in this forest came at us — this one was just *home*. And if its mother comes back and finds it dead with our marks on it—" She stopped, and started again, plainer. "We know how to spare things now. We did it for the bear. The ring would have been the best thing I'm ever given, and I would have carried it fine. But I'd have carried *this* too, and I don't want this."

"The bone's worth a province," Xie Xie said — not arguing — saying it out loud so the size of what she was declining would be witnessed. Then he looked at the sleeping cub, and at the golden blood, and put his hands in his pockets. "I don't want it either. Ladies first, and the ladies keep declining."

"I don't want luck," Gu Yue said. "I want strength. Luck is a thing that happens to you. I'd rather be a thing that happens."

Wulin had been quiet a long moment. He had no bear's bone in his own hands to point to — the bear was alive and north of them and knew them by smell; that account was still open. "We're the visitors," he said finally. "That's the whole of it. We're the visitors, and it's the house." And then, because he was the captain and the worries were his: "Also — my arm went numb off a *lucky* bounce. This trial isn't anywhere near as simple as it's pretending to be. Nothing this far in is priced this cheap." He nodded at the cub. "Leave it sleeping. Wulin's rule: take nothing, not even the story."

Something shifted in the cave then, too quiet to name. Xie Xie looked at the little beast a long time. In this forest, until tonight, the killing had been the game the way tag is a game — and here, suddenly, was a girl setting down the best prize of their lives beside a sleeping child, and it fit her hand better than the hammer had. He didn't have a question for it yet. He had the shape of a question, and it would be his for years: *if we are the ones who come in, what does that make everything after?*

## Part 3: What Came Home

The cub had cried out once — when the scales broke. Small. The kind of sound a house makes when a child in it is hurt.

The forest heard it.

The air of the cave thickened like deep water, and at the far bend, where the dark had been merely dark, a gold light arrived — not torch-bright; *arrival*-bright, the light of something coming that had stopped pretending to approach and was simply *closing the distance*. Two golden eyes, high up, higher than a bear's. Above and between them, a third lid slid open, and the light of it was red.

"Out," Wulin snapped. "Now, all of you, the way we came—"

And space *hardened*.

It went down the tunnel like frost up a window, only backwards — the world's own grain tightening — and it caught them mid-stride, four of five, locked like insects in amber: Wulin half-turned, Gu Yue's silver guttering around her fingers, Xie Xie's clones winking out unborn, Xiaoyan's starwheel dying at her wrists. The red eye's pressure lay on them like a column of water. It was not attacking. It was *holding*, the way you hold a door shut with your hand while you look at what's inside.

It reached the fifth.

And broke.

Not loudly. The hardening came to Lin Hao and found the thing it could not freeze — spirit that outranked the beast's whole legend, a soul like deep water that had never once in its life stopped moving — and the space around him simply stayed his. He took one step, unhurried, and planted himself between the red eye and the four statues of his class.

"Go when it lets you," he said, calm as a man giving directions in the street. "Follow the gems out, then the stars. Don't wait, don't watch." He drew the breath of someone about to lift a full crate, and let half of it go. "I'll close the door."

## Part 4: The Fifth Hand Closes the Door

He had lit nothing all exam. Nine trials of edges, of herding, of being the placed stone the flashes died on — the whole academy had watched a fifth examinee farm the margins, and the margins had been the plan, because a sword shown is a price, and Lin Hao does not spend without a reason.

The reason was standing frozen behind him — four statues of his class, and the fifth the only thing still moving.

Lin Hao held out his right hand, and *summoned his sword.*

The Frost Abyss Sword came out of him the way winter comes out of a clear night sky — no warning, total arrival. Black iron, cold past color, a blade with a library in it: four elements asleep down its length, and the iron not a fifth element but the *house they lived in*. The cave went quiet the way his family's shop went quiet on the last cold morning of a year. Even the red pressure paused to look at it.

Then, for the first time since the fourth ring had given it to him — folded at the bear, carried folded through eight trials, through ten doors and a gem floor and a blitz — he *opened his domain.*

The Frost Abyss Domain unfolded, and the cave became the other hall, the one he had been carrying all along: a place where everything is heard. The cub's hitching breath stood in the air like a written line. The hardening of space groaned at the domain's hem and settled, two immensities agreeing to share a corridor. Sound stopped being noise in there. Sound became *record*.

He released his Sword Intent.

The books on his shelf at home had used two names for it, and he had finally understood, on a roof in another city, that they were the same name: *sword intent*, and the older, better words, *sword consciousness*. The intent that knows the cut before the cut. It left him now, unhooded, and the mountain of gold light at the tunnel's end felt a boy of thirteen look at the whole of its advance and *file* it — distance, grain, temper, seam — the way a smith reads a billet before the first hammer ever falls.

And he fused his spiritual power into the stroke. All of it that the stroke could hold: twenty-nine hundred points of deep water forced down the iron's length until the blade sang one flat note, and the four elements woke in their house — lightning, wind, water, ice — braided in the iron the way they had lived there since the day the ring turned black.

One attack. He did not swing at the king. Kings of that size are not killed; they are *answered*.

He wrote one stroke across the tunnel mouth.

Water laid the seam, finding the stone's own grain so the wall would be true. Ice rose out of it a foot thick, then two, gem-bright, the whole height and breadth of the passage. Wind keyed it into the rock like a wedge driven home. And lightning went across the face of it — not thrown, *inscribed*, a white line of script on a black-iron door, which in every language beasts have ever read says the same thing: *this was made by something that was not afraid of you.*

It cost him a hundred points of spirit, spent in a breath — a month of deep water poured out through one seam — and one stroke's worth of Sword Intent, and the domain refolded itself the moment the work was done, tidy as a closed ledger. The gold light arrived at the wall at full charge.

The boom knocked the gems off the mound. The four frozen kids shuddered in their amber. The ice held.

Through its gem-bright face, close now, horribly clear: two golden eyes and one demonic red, lighting the wall from the other side like a lantern held up to a window. The red eye did not blink. Neither did the wall.

On the screen in the metal room, Elder Cai was standing. Nobody had seen her stand up.

"Four in one stroke," she said. "Write it down."

Zhuo Shi did not answer at first. He was watching the domain fold away — watching eight trials of a folded domain, of a boy carrying a closed fist through a showcase of a school, and spending it, in the end, on a *door*. "Second seal," he said, to his own question from the night the grass turned gold. "That's your answer. It was never the ring. The boy's been paying for the door the whole exam."

"And the little one refused a treasure a province would go to war over," Cai said. Her page had a new line on it, and under the new line, room. She looked at the five small figures running north under the stars, one of them trailing the flat cold of a spent stroke like a scent, and at the wall of ice with the unblinking red behind it, and she was done watching. The ninth trial had asked its question and five children had answered it in a language the academy had not heard in a generation.

"End it," she said.

## Part 5: North, Under Stars

They ran the valley under more stars than the sky usually admits to, and the freeze wore off the four of them in pieces — Xie Xie's hands first, then Gu Yue's silver, then their legs, until they were only running, and nobody said the name of what was behind them, and everybody checked anyway: the tunnel mouth, far back, a white seam in the hillside.

Gem-bright. Holding.

The red light in it had not blinked once.

Lin Hao ran last, at the tail of the five, where the cold was. Xiaoyan ran light. The hammer was on her back, and nothing was on her hands — not the ring that would have beaten ten-thousand-year rings, not the bone a province would go to war for. Those had stayed where they belonged, on the brow of a sleeping child in a broken house, and she found, testing the weight of herself as she ran, that she had never once been lighter.

Behind them the ice held. In a metal room under the academy, two old monsters were already moving. Somewhere under the stars, a mother was standing at a sealed door, reading a line of white script.

The night had three keepers now, and none of them were done.

## Part 1: The Thirtieth Day

The ice let go at about four in the morning.

There was no sound. Gu Yue had expected a sound, because she had been imagining it for twenty-nine days, and everything she had imagined had been loud. It was not loud. The ice simply stopped being a solid and became a cold that had opinions, and it came off him the way frost comes off a window, in one piece, and then it was water, and then it was nothing.

The pool was a pool.

He was sitting in it.

---

## Part 2: What Was Standing in the Water

Gu Yue did not move, and afterward she was not able to say whether that was a decision.

He was bigger. That was the first thing, and it was not a subtle thing. The boy who had walked into the platform thirty days ago had come up to her shoulder when she stood straight, and had been small for eleven in the way that all of them were small for their ages. The person sitting in the pool was not small. His shoulders were out of the water and they were broad, and his arms were long, and when he stood up the water came to his waist and he kept going up past the place her eyes had decided he would stop.

He was thin. That was the second thing. Not wasted — the opposite. He looked like something that had been made rather than grown, all of it in the right place, none of it extra.

The third thing was his arms.

The feather-marks were still there. They had been there since he was ten — the fine storm-light lines along both forearms that the hawk had put there and that no healer at Eastsea had been able to explain — and they had not gone. They had gone *further.* They ran past his wrists now, and they ran up past his elbows, and where they crossed the new muscle they lay flat and dark like inlay in a blade, and the storm-gray that had been a single lock at his temple was in his hair properly now, a wide streak through it that caught what little light there was and did not give it back.

There was hawk-gold at his temple. He could feel it, which he had never been able to feel before, the way you can feel a scar in cold weather.

He turned his arm over.

"You did this," he said, to nothing, because the thing that had done it was inside him now and was not a thing he could say *you* to any more.

The fourth thing was the rings.

Four of them were going up around him, slow, the way rings came, and three were purple.

The fourth was black.

Gu Yue had seen a black ring. Wu Zhangkong had two, and she had stood under Sky Ice while they turned, and she knew what black meant the way everybody knew what black meant: ten thousand years. A ten-thousand-year spirit soul was a thing the Spirit Pagoda struggled to make. There were people at this Academy with six rings who did not have one.

An eleven-year-old had four rings and the last one was black, and he was standing in a pool at four in the morning with his eyes open, looking at his own hands.

Then he looked at her.

"You came," Lin Hao said.

His voice was different. It was lower. That was all it was — it was the same voice, saying the same kind of thing, in a register that belonged to somebody older — and Gu Yue found that she had stopped breathing and had to start again.

"Every fifth day," she said.

"I know. I could tell. There was a hand on the ice." He flexed his fingers, one at a time, watching them. "It was cold. Colder than the rest. I used to look for it."

Gu Yue did not say anything, because there was nothing to say that was not going to come out wrong.

"How long?" he said.

"Thirty days."

He thought about that for a second.

"I owe Xie Xie a bearing assembly," he said.

## Part 3: The Sword

He called it up out of habit, the way he had called it up every morning since he was six, and it was not the same sword.

It was longer. It was heavier in a way that had nothing to do with weight. The steel was the colour of deep water, and along the edge there was frost that did not melt, and the guard had a curve to it that had not been there before — a curve like a spine, like something that had been alive.

Wind went along it. Lightning went along it. And under both of those, underneath, the way a river is under a road, there was ice and water, and all four of them were going the same direction.

Lin Hao stood in a pool holding a sword he did not recognise, and he understood something that he had been told at eleven and had not believed until now, which was that the four agreeing was the whole of it. Gu Yue had taught him that in a courtyard in four hours, with two elements and a stick, and she had taught him the shape of a thing that could hold four.

He said the name out loud, to see whether it fitted.

"Frost Abyss."

It fitted.

He had never named a thing in his life that he had not made, and he stood there for a moment with the sword in his hand and the water at his waist and understood that he had not made this one either. He had been holding it while it was made. Somebody else had done the making and had then gone into him and stopped being somebody else, and the least he could do was call the result by a name instead of by a number.

"Stormbringer," he said, to the sword, because it seemed rude not to. "Thank you."

## Part 4: The Door

Tang Wulin was in the doorway of the working-student dormitory at seven in the morning with two black steamed buns, and a stranger was coming up the path.

That was the whole of it. He looked at the stranger, and the stranger was tall, and thin, and moving with the particular economy of a person who has recently been given more body than he has had time to learn, and Tang Wulin thought *who is that* — and then he thought it again, slower, because something in the walk was familiar in a way he could not put next to the face.

He had known Lin Hao since they were three years old.

"Move," the stranger said. "You're in the door."

Tang Wulin did not move.

"You've got buns," the stranger said. "Are those for me?"

"...Lin Hao?"

"Yes."

"That's not —" Tang Wulin held the buns out at arm's length, as though the problem were the buns. "You're — how tall are you?"

"I don't know. I haven't been outside yet." Lin Hao took one of the buns and ate half of it in a bite, and Tang Wulin watched him do it, and that was the thing that finished him, because nobody in the world ate like that except one person. "Wulin."

"What."

"I've been in there for thirty days and I came out and you didn't know me." He chewed. "I want you to know that I have decided not to be hurt by it, and that it took me about four seconds to decide, and that I am telling you so that you will not spend a week being sorry about it in a way I have already forgiven."

Tang Wulin sat down on the step.

"You're sixteen."

"I'm eleven."

"You're not eleven."

"I had a birthday. It was small." Lin Hao sat down beside him, and the step was not big enough for what he was now, and he did not seem to notice. "I think I'm going to have to have a conversation about my age with a lot of people."

## Part 5: What Xie Xie Said

Xie Xie said it in the corridor, at length, and badly, and he was not wrong.

"Your *face*," he said. "Your face has — there's a — you had a face and now you have a *face*." He put his own hands on his own cheeks, as though demonstrating the loss. "I have known you for three years. I have a hawk. I have opinions about metal. I am a person with a personality, and you have just walked past me and I have had to *reintroduce myself to my own memory*."

"Good morning, Xie Xie."

"It is not a good morning, it is a *catastrophe*, Xu Xiaoyan is going to—" he stopped. "Where is Xu Xiaoyan."

"Breakfast."

"Then we have eleven minutes and I intend to use them." Xie Xie walked around him, the way a man walks around a house he is considering buying. "Your clothes."

"What about them."

"They're *small.* Look at your wrist. That's a child's sleeve on a —" he gestured at the whole of him. "Whose clothes are those."

"They were mine."

"They were yours *yesterday*."

"They were mine on Tuesday," Lin Hao said, "and I have been wearing them for thirty days, and they fit when I put them on, and I would like everybody to stop talking about my sleeves."

"Four rings," Xie Xie said.

"Yes."

"One of them is black."

"Yes."

Xie Xie stopped walking around him.

"I'm going to say something," he said, "and I want you to know that I have thought about it, and that I am going to say it anyway. Wu Zhangkong has six rings. Two black. He is a two-word battle armor master and he is forty years further along the same road, and he stood in this corridor six weeks ago and *you looked at him like he was weather*." He put his hand flat on Lin Hao's chest, which he had to reach slightly to do. "You're not weather. But you're not him either. You're *early*. And I don't know whether that's the best thing that's ever happened to this Academy or the worst, and I've decided I'm going to find out, and that I'm going to be standing next to you when it happens, and that you're going to make the metal."

"I know."

"Say it."

"I'm going to make the metal," Lin Hao said.

## Part 6: The Measurement

Wu Zhangkong was in the training yard, and he had clearly been in the training yard for some time, and he did not look up when Lin Hao came in.

"Rank," he said.

"Forty-five."

Wu Zhangkong's hand stopped on the whetstone.

"Say it properly."

"Rank forty-five." Lin Hao said it the way you say a number to a man who is going to write it down. "It moved while I was in the pool. I didn't do it. I was holding the jiao and it moved underneath me, five at once, and I have never had it move more than one at a time in my life."

"Soul Ancestor."

"Yes."

"At eleven."

"Yes."

Wu Zhangkong put down the sword he was holding. Lin Hao had never seen him put down a sword.

"Spiritual power."

"Five hundred and seventeen."

"Five hundred and seventeen." Wu Zhangkong said the number the way a man repeats a date he has been told is wrong. "That is Spirit Sea."

"Yes."

"You crossed the wall."

"I think I was pushed."

Wu Zhangkong laughed. It was not a large laugh and it did not last, and Lin Hao had never heard him make the sound before, and Wu Zhangkong seemed briefly uncertain about it himself.

"Four rings," he said. "Three purple and a black. At eleven." He walked a slow half-circle around him. "Do you know what a battle armor master is worth? Twenty ranks. That's the whole of it — a man in two words of armor is twenty soul ranks stronger than the same man without it, and every soul master on this continent knows the number and organises his life around it. You are sixteen ranks above your paper with nothing on." He stopped. "You have not used the full thing, either. Have you."

"No."

"Say what you have."

Lin Hao held out his hand.

The yard was wet. It had rained in the night and the flagstones had water in the seams, and there was a bucket by the wall with an inch of rain in it, and the air over the yard was the air over a yard after rain.

The water came up out of the seams.

It did not splash. That was the part Lin Hao had not expected and would think about for a long time. It came up out of the stone the way a person stands up out of a chair, and it stood there, and the inch of rain in the bucket was suddenly not in the bucket, and the moisture that had been in the air was suddenly not in the air, and all of it was in one place, above his open hand, holding still.

He had not asked it to. He had opened his hand and it had come, and it was waiting.

Wu Zhangkong did not speak for perhaps ten seconds.

"What is that," he said, at last, quietly.

"I don't know."

"It is not a ring skill."

"No. The ring gives me a stroke. This isn't a stroke." Lin Hao closed his hand, and the water fell, and the yard was a wet yard again. "It's been there since the pool. It isn't something I do. It's somewhere I *am*."

Wu Zhangkong picked up his sword.

"Don't do that where anybody can see it," he said.

## Part 7: The Ledger

He wrote at night, in the dormitory, because that was when he wrote.

He had not written in thirty days. He had the book open on his knees and the brush in his hand and he found that he did not know how to start, which had never happened, and that the not-knowing was not about words.

He wrote the numbers first, because the numbers were easy.

*Four hundred and five — no. Five hundred and seventeen. One thousand five hundred and twenty. Forty-five.*

*Four rings. The fourth is black. The sword is not the sword. It is called Frost Abyss and I named it, which is the first time I have named a thing I did not make.*

Then he stopped, and sat for a while.

*There is water in this room and it is mine now. I did not ask for that. I opened my hand and it came, and Wu Zhangkong told me not to do it where anybody can see it, and he was frightened, and I have never seen him frightened, and I want to write down that I saw it because I am afraid that I will decide later that I imagined it.*

*I am eleven and I look sixteen and Tang Wulin did not know me in a doorway. He has known me since I was three. He did not know me.*

*I have been thinking about that all day and I have decided two things. The first is that it is not his fault. The second is that it is going to happen again, to other people, for years, and that I should get used to it now rather than be surprised by it at thirty.*

*Thirty days. I have never left a gap. I left a gap.*

*Here is the line after the gap.*

## Part 8: The Month Without Him

Xu Xiaoyan told him at breakfast, because Xu Xiaoyan had decided that the person who should tell him was the person with the least to gain by it.

"There was a class battle," she said. "You weren't in it. Tang Wulin is the blacksmith representative and he didn't have to fight for it, because Shen Yi said nobody could compete with him, and a boy called Yang Nianxia asked why and she said the sentence I have just said."

"I got a seat," Lin Hao said.

"You got a seat."

"I can feel it. Five hundred points a month. Somebody's been putting it somewhere."

"Xie Xie has been buying metal with it." Xu Xiaoyan put her chin on her hand. "He has bought eleven ingots. He has them under his bed. He has not told you, because he wanted there to be a thing you didn't know, and I am telling you, because I don't care."

"What else."

"There's a boy called Yue Zhengyu. Second grade. He has got more points than this whole dormitory and he has spent a month being certain about something." She said it the way you say the name of a person you have already finished with. "He's looking for a girl. Red hair, working student. Somebody saw a Fallen Angel on campus and he has decided that Fallen Angels become evil soul masters, so he got his working-student application approved — to get into this dormitory and find her."

Lin Hao put his chopsticks down.

"That's not Gu Yue."

"No. Gu Yue has six elements and a bad attitude and no red hair." Xu Xiaoyan turned her bowl a quarter of the way around. "But he is looking for a strange girl with unusual elements in a working-student dormitory, and there are two strange girls in it, and he has decided which one he means. He says her name in corridors. Not loudly."

Lin Hao put his chopsticks down.

"The Academy has already ruled on it," Xu Xiaoyan said. "The enforcer said it to his face — *the Academy has already verified my identity and determined that I am not an evil soul master* — and Yuanen told him to find a way to prove it or shut up, in front of forty people, and he's still saying it. He's rich and he's stupid and he has time, which is the combination that costs you the most." She looked at him. "You've been in a pool for a month. This is the part where you find out that the month happened anyway."

"Yes."

"Gu Yue has not said anything about it."

"No."

"She's also not eaten anything since you sat down." Xu Xiaoyan got up, taking her bowl. "I'm not going to be in this conversation. I've done my part."

Lin Hao looked across the table.

Gu Yue was looking at him. She had been looking at him, he understood, for some time — not at the stranger in the doorway, not at the sixteen-year-old in the child's sleeves, but at him, in the particular way she had of looking at a thing until she had finished reading it.

She did not look away.

"You have a whetstone," she said.

"I have a whetstone."

"Two."

"I have one."

"I know how many you have." She pushed her bowl aside. "Your sword has a name now."

"Yes."

"Say it."

"Frost Abyss."

Gu Yue nodded once, and there was something in the nod that he could not read and would not have been able to describe to anybody, and she said the only thing she said about any of it.

"I told you I wanted the version where you say it."

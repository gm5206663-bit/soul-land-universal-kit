## Part 1: The Crate, and the Style's Second Half

The rental bench in the forge district cost one federal coin an hour and smelled like every honest morning of his life.

He opened the crate with the estate lot in it in front of the yard's owner, because sight-unseen lots are opened in front of witnesses. Heavy Silver, forty-one kilograms. Sky Dragon Iron, twenty-six. Two separated strata, wrapped apart, the way you wrap things when you know what they are — which meant the estate had known, and the auction had not, and the room had bid on the word *mixed* anyway. The owner looked at the strata, and then at the boy who had paid a third of his traveling money for them, and offered, on the spot, to buy the Iron back at triple. Lin Hao said no, and the man looked at him a second time, longer, the way people in this city were starting to.

Then the bench was his, and the morning was for the sword.

The six strokes had come out of the emergence twice as large as asked, and a month of yardwork had brought them back to their sizes — but not to their old shapes. That was the discovery of the Shrek mornings, and it was still arriving: each of the five question-strokes was growing a second register. The storm asked at the surface; the deep water could ask the same question underneath, colder, slower, from a different angle, so that the question arrived twice and the second one could not be dodged by answering the first. Still Water had been the style's first answer-stroke. It had not been an ending. It had been a preview. The style was growing its second half, stroke by stroke, and by the third morning the first stroke — the oldest, the one he had written in the book at ten — could ask twice.

He worked them at the air, at the pell, at a bar of the new Sky Dragon Iron propped in the vise, and the iron rang differently under the water-register than under the storm-register, and he listened to the difference the whole morning, because the sword's body was iron, his oldest acquaintance, and it was telling him which register the metal believed.

Sword Intent itself had changed register too. It used to be pressure. Now it had temperature. He could put cold into a look and a look into the cold, and the yard owner's dog, testing him at the fence line, sat down at the second look and decided permanently.

## Part 2: The Second Spirit

The dragon grew. That was the morning's other work, and it was work now, counted.

The jiao was a month old in him and ten thousand years old in itself, and both of those things were true at once, the way a marriage is young even when both people are grown. It had come out of the barrier at full weight and then done what every living thing does in a new country: it had begun to settle, and then, past settling, to grow. Not as the hawk had grown — the hawk had five years of mornings in him, and their joining was so finished it did not deserve the word *joining* anymore; it deserved *weather*. The dragon was younger than that. The dragon was developing.

He measured it the way Xiaoyan had taught him to measure everything, which was honestly.

The deep-water joining — the step inward where he stopped carrying the spirit and started being carried by it — had held forty seconds on the ship of the first week. That morning it held ninety. The difference was not small and not comfortable: at ninety seconds the cold had opinions about his breathing, and at ninety seconds something in him that was also something in it tried, gently, to speak with his mouth, and he stepped out and stood in the ordinary warm morning shaking slightly, with the whole yard's frost already gone, and did it again. The second attempt held seventy. He wrote both numbers down. A measurement you did not write down had not happened.

The domain's hem was a hand wider than the month before. Domineer's breath, which had been one breath at the emergence, had become two — he tested it over the pell and the pell, three years honest service, split to the grain. The body deepened with all of it, because there were not two things here to begin with: the spirit grew, the body grew, one existence — the regeneration quicker by the week (he took a forge nick opening the crate's second stratum and watched it close, eighty seconds, timed, which would have been half a morning in Eastsea), the Mysterious Heaven breath one count deeper, the cold sense reading the room's drafts like a page.

The hawk sat the fence through all of it, winter-tipped, unbothered, senior. Toward the end of the morning it flew one slow circle, low, over the bench — over the Iron, over the sword, over the whole arrangement of him — the way a foreman walks a floor that is going well.

There was a third thing he did not do, and its name was the perfect joining, and it stayed in its sheath. It had a door of its own, and the door was not today's. But it was also true, and he wrote this down too, in the book, in the plain way: *everything spent to the limit, both spirits included, the Emperor number is not the ceiling. There is a higher arithmetic. It is reserved.*

## Part 3: The Effect

He walked back through the city at noon with Wulin, and the city continued its campaign to be noticed noticing him.

The tea stall girl at the corner under the inn had started giving him the tall cup instead of the short one, unprompted, and would not take the difference. The tailor's apprentice two doors down — sent out to hand-deliver a repaired seam — had measured him with her eyes twice on the first day and now simply turned faintly pink at the counters, with the tape, professionally, every time. At the smelter's yard, the afternoon crowd had doubled; the smelter himself had taken to calling him *young master metalsense* in front of visitors, half mock and half salesmanship, and let him read two pours for customers, and both numbers had been right, and the yard's reputation was becoming his by association and vice versa. Two girls from a passing academy group walked into a lamp post between glances. The lamp post was fine. Wulin watched the second one happen, turned to his best friend with the slow dawning joy of a man collecting evidence, and said nothing, which between the two of them was the loudest available thing.

"She didn't see it," Lin Hao said. "The post."

"They never do," Wulin said, wheezing. "Xie Xie said it first, you know. A face that is a personality. He wants royalties. He's asking around about how royalties work."

"It's just a face."

"It's just a face," Wulin agreed, "that a woman at an auction house read your profession off in under a minute, and a smelter calls *master*, and a lamp post has met personally. Just one of those." He clapped his shoulder, and they bought the cake — the good place near the station, the order Xie Xie had placed with the authority of a small parliament — and carried it back flat between them like a relic, and the whole way, at crosswalks, in queues, at the bakery door, people moved around the two of them a half-beat sooner than the crowd's own rhythm, the way water moves around a stone it has an opinion about, and neither boy said any more about it, because there was nothing to do about being liked by a city except be back at the inn by six, as instructed.

## Part 4: The Sentence

The birthday committee took everyone out of the inn at dusk — Xie Xie, Xiaoyan with the map, Wulin under protest, on a mission for candles of a specific number — and for one hour the corridor upstairs was empty of everyone except the two people it was about to be empty of.

He knocked. She said, "It's open."

Gu Yue's room was the girls' room, one bed, two cabinets, a desk, a window with the station in it, and she was at the desk with the dusk behind her doing nothing in particular, which he suspected was cultivation, and suspected equally was waiting, and did not ask which. He came in, closed the door, and did not sit on the offered chair. He sat on the floor across from her, cross-legged, the way they had sat at ten, and looked at her silently for some time.

The looking was the new thing. Since the second spirit, his eyes had been a different instrument — the deep water read everything now, drafts and grain and people — and what it read when it read her had a shape to it, something old and layered and utterly quiet, sitting inside the girl at the desk the way the jiao sat inside him. He had been carrying that reading for a month. He had told no one, including her.

"Well," he said. "Many things changed after my second soul spirit. Honestly. You look more beautiful." She went very still, and he kept going, level, the way you cross a bridge you have decided on. "It isn't that you changed. I changed. I think you know why." A breath. "A person has a right to privacy. I'm not asking. I wanted to say something to you, and then I'll have said it."

For a moment he thought the room had gotten colder, and then understood that it hadn't, and that this was worse and better than cold: she was attending with everything she had, all of her, the way she stood inside a hem of frost without a mark on her.

He rose, went close to her, and looked into her eyes.

"I love you." He said it plainly, no wind before it, the sixth stroke of sentences. "The sentence isn't complete — that time, I didn't finish it. What I didn't say then was that I liked you. Now it's more. I'm telling you because I'm certain. I don't expect the same, and I'm not forcing anything. I wanted to say it because I wanted to tell you." One corner of his mouth moved, not quite a smile. "I know the others all know it. It's been too obvious the whole time, maybe. If you don't have anything — you don't need to say anything. We go as always. I won't say it again." And the last of it, the truth under the truth, given to her the way you hand over a weight with both hands: "If I tell you the truth — I'm not expecting a Yes."

The room held still. Somewhere below, the station announced a train to a city none of them had seen. The dusk moved one shade toward dark.

And Gu Yue said nothing.

She looked at him — a long, level look, the whole of her attending behind it, something in it he had no register for — and no word came. The elements in the room did not move. Nothing arrived in place of an answer, the way nothing ever arrived with her when she had decided a thing was not yet for saying.

He had set the terms himself, kindly, before he ever crossed the room: if you don't have anything, you don't need to say anything. We go as always.

So this was the answer, and it was the exact shape of the one he had promised to accept — nothing said, nothing done, no thing changed hands — as before, like before. He held the look as long as she lent it, and then he nodded, once, small, the way a man closes a ledger he has finished writing in, and said, "As always," and went out, and closed the door gently, the way someone had once closed a door in a girls' dormitory in another city, and stood in the empty corridor being quiet and finished and not sorry. The sentence was done; that was the whole of what the day had been for. Whether she kept it or put it down was hers, and he had told her so, and a promise made in advance is still a promise.

## Part 5: Line One Hundred and Nine

That night, in the room where Xie Xie slept like the honest and the fed, he opened the book.

Line one hundred and nine had sat at the bottom of its page for two years, unfinished, coat on, one button undone — a sentence begun at eleven in a yard and never completed, guarded like a door that was also a wound. He had rules about the book, and the rules had held a long time.

He wrote the sentence out, complete, all of it, the whole thing he had said and the whole thing he had carried: *I love her. Told her. Not expecting a Yes. She said nothing. That is the nothing we agreed on. Everything is as before.* And the date.

Then, because the book was honest or it was nothing, he added one more line below it, the first line of whatever came next: *I didn't tell her to get anything. I told her because I wanted to tell her. The page turns.*

He closed the book. Downstairs the committee argued about candles. Tomorrow his best friend turned fourteen, and behind that door was a thing that would change him, and behind every other door in the greatest city on the continent was an examination that did not test winning.

He slept with his hands open, and the room, if anyone had measured it, was one degree colder than the corridor, and the cold was the polite kind, the kind that was learning his breathing, night by night, exactly as fast as he was learning its.

## Part 1: The Ledger

Lin Hao kept a ledger. Not on paper — paper would have been found, and a ledger like this one was not a thing you left lying around a dormitory where Zhou Zhangxi snored. He kept it the way his father kept the temper of a blade: in the hand, where it could not be lost and did not need to be explained.

Every fight was a line in it.

He had begun counting after the tournament, because the tournament had taught him something his father never could: that a fight you survive is a fight you *keep.* The hawk did not forget the wind it had ridden. It asked the wind a question, watched which way the wind leaned, and then it knew — permanently, the way you know a stair — and it never had to ask that question again.

So the ledger had lines.

*Mo Si* — short sword, agility, quick hands, always elsewhere the moment you decided where he was. *Answer: don't decide where he is. Ask.*

*Qin Lang* — Iron-Back Ape, a wall with a feed-line running into its left shoulder. *Answer: you don't cut walls. You cut the door.*

*Jia Long* — bear-class assault, momentum in a straight line, all of it honest. *Answer: honest momentum is a gift. Turn it.*

*Lin Zunyuan* — staff support, amplification, the boy who made other boys better. *Answer: cut the line, not the man.*

*Gu Tianming* — wings, sky, a flyer who is only dangerous while you are on the ground. *Answer: then don't be on the ground.*

The four from class two. The three from class three. Class one's whole roster, fought across four days on the way up a ladder nobody had expected them to climb. *Guang Long* — rank twenty-seven, two lizard souls, Armored Body and Armored Dragon Body, a man who shrugged off hammers; the fight Lin Hao had *lost,* the line in the ledger written in a different ink, because you learn more from the lines you write in different ink. *Wang Jinxi* — Bone Dragon King, two yellow rings, and a three-week rehearsed decoy fold built specifically to lie to Lin Hao's eyes, which had worked, which had been the first successful lie ever told to him, and which he had not forgotten for one single day since.

And then the daily ones. Xie Xie, every morning, nine weeks and then months, until Lin Hao could tell from the set of the boy's shoulders which of eleven openings he was going to commit to. Wulin, every evening, the forge against the sword, the immovable against the unreadable. Wu Zhangkong's pointing stick, which did not care that you were ten.

Seventy-one lines. Seventy-one opponents, or versions of opponents, and behind every one of them a small permanent answer that had cost him a bruise to buy and would never cost him anything again.

That was the thing nobody in class zero had understood yet, and that Wu Zhangkong — who had been hitting seven children for five months and had hit Lin Hao precisely four times, and had begun, without saying so, to aim at him the way a man aims at something he is not entirely sure he can hit — understood perfectly.

🔴 *Lin Hao was the strongest in the room, and had been since the day he was measured.* Gu Yue's 153 was canon's crown for a world without him in it; his 178 was above it, and Wu Zhangkong had stopped saying otherwise two measurements ago.

Lin Hao was the one who had *already fought you.*

"You're all holding back," Wu Zhangkong said, on the evening in question, to seven children who were holding nothing back and could not, in fact, hold back any more than water holds back downhill. "Not on purpose. You're holding back because you're each of you waiting to find out what the others will do. Combat isn't waiting. Combat is deciding, before the other person decides, what you're going to be." He looked at the seven of them — the four and the three, who had stopped, without any of them saying so, being a four and a three — and said, "Tomorrow, we measure you. Then we find out what you're made of."

"I'm made of meat," Xie Xie said, from the ground, where the pointing stick had recently introduced him, "and the meat is *tired.*"

"Good," Wu Zhangkong said. "Tired meat doesn't lie." And he was gone, and the seven of them lay on the training-ground dirt like a crop that had been harvested, and Wulin said, "Hao, I can't feel my legs."

"You never could," Lin Hao said, without opening his eyes. "That's not new. That's just Tuesday."

"I can *genuinely* not feel them."

"Then tomorrow they'll measure your legs at zero," Lin Hao said, "and Wu Zhangkong will write down 'legs: absent,' and you'll be the only one in the federation with a certified missing-legs score, and you'll be *famous,* Wulin, isn't that what you always wanted." He cracked one eye open. "You're fine. Your legs are the finest legs in the federation. They're just tired. There's a difference, and tomorrow they'll measure the difference, and it'll be a number, and the number will be fine."

"You're very calm about being measured," Wulin said.

"I'm a blacksmith," Lin Hao said. "I measure things for a living. I'm going to enjoy watching them try to measure *me.*" And he said it lightly, the way he said everything, but there was something under it, and Wulin heard it, because Wulin always heard it, and neither of them said what it was. They both already knew: tomorrow they were going to put a number on Lin Hao, and the number was going to be a problem, and Lin Hao had known it was coming since the tournament, when a man with smoky green eyes had logged a number in total silence and never once said it out loud.

What Wulin did not know — what nobody knew, because Lin Hao had not told anyone, because you do not tell people the ledger exists — was that the number had been climbing the entire time. Not quietly. Not slowly. Every fight fed it, because his martial soul did not merely *have* an adaptation talent. His martial soul *was* adaptation. Pressure was not something that wore him down. Pressure was the fuel.

## Part 2

He was sweating, and the marks on his forearms stood out darker when he was hot, six of them in a row from wrist to elbow, and the boy from class three who had been watching from the rail had stopped watching the training and started watching his arms.
: The Test

The room they measured them in smelled of dust and old machines, and there were tables of strange apparatus arranged in it, and Wu Zhangkong stood with a form and a pen, and said, "We'll start with spiritual power. Xie Xie, you're first."

And the order, it turned out, was not random. Zhang Yangzi figured it out about halfway through, and said so, in the way of a boy who could not un-see a pattern: Wu Zhangkong had arranged them so the numbers would rise, one after another, the way you arrange a staircase. Xie Xie first (lowest), then up. Which meant the last one measured was the highest. And Zhang Yangzi, doing the arithmetic, looked at the order, and looked at who was last, and his eyebrows went up.

Xie Xie's spiritual power was normal. It had gone up two since the last time. "Normal," Wu Zhangkong said, and wrote it, and Xie Xie took "normal" the way he took everything, which was loudly.

Zhang Yangzi was next. **Forty-one.** "Not bad," Wu Zhangkong said, which from him was the first kind thing anyone in the room had heard all morning, and Zhang Yangzi — who was the arithmetic one, and who had been doing sums quietly since the second boy — went slightly pink and sat down as though he had been given something.

Wang Jinxi was next. His spiritual power was low — eighteen. Wu Zhangkong looked at the number, and then at Wang Jinxi, and said, in the flat voice he kept for things that mattered, "Wang Jinxi. You must spare no effort in improving your spiritual power, or you won't be able to fuse with any more spirit souls in the future." Wang Jinxi, who was normally very quiet, nodded, because Wu Zhangkong was right, and Wang Jinxi had learned — in the five months, in the room with no number — that this man was always right, and had started, without saying so, to listen.

Zhang Yangzi was next. Forty-one. "Not bad," Wu Zhangkong said. "If you do your best, you might break through from the Spirit Origin realm within a year." Zhang Yangzi, who had been the quiet one, the one who had signed the contract without a word and sat on the edge of things, straightened a little at "not bad," because it was the first kind thing anyone had said to him since the rematch, and he did not, entirely, know what to do with it.

And then there were two left. And Zhang Yangzi, who had figured out the staircase, looked at the order, and looked at Lin Hao, and then at Gu Yue, and understood — with the slow, cold understanding of a boy doing arithmetic he did not like — that the staircase went up to *both* of them, and that the numbers were about to stop being normal.

Lin Hao went before Gu Yue. Because the staircase rose, and his number was lower than hers, and Wu Zhangkong had arranged it that way, and Lin Hao — who had known this was coming, who had watched the man with the smoky green eyes log a number in silence at the tournament and never say it — stepped up to the machine the way he stepped up to a forge, and put his hand on it, and let it measure him.

The number climbed.

It went past the white line. It went into the yellow. It broke a hundred, the way it had broken a hundred at the tournament, and then it kept going, and this was the part that made Wu Zhangkong's pen stop moving, because at the tournament it had stopped at one hundred and eighty and he had written that down and closed the book and carried it in his head for five months waiting to see if it would hold.

It had not held. It had not even *stayed.*

One hundred and forty. One hundred and sixty. One hundred and eighty — and Zhang Yangzi's jaw dropped, and Wang Jinxi's jaw dropped, and Xie Xie, who had been about to say something loud, did not say anything at all, because the number was still climbing, and it did not stop until it reached a number that had no business belonging to a ten-year-old who had never held a spirit soul until he was eight and a half.

The machine said: **one hundred and seventy-eight.**

Spirit Connection realm. At ten years old. Thirty-four points higher than the number a man with smoky green eyes had written down, in silence, at the tournament.

Thirty-four. In five months.

Silence. And in that silence, Zhang Yangzi — who was the arithmetic one, who could not un-see a pattern, who had been doing sums quietly all morning — said, in a small voice, "That's over thirty."

"It's thirty-four," Lin Hao said helpfully.

"I can *count.*"

"I know you can. I just like hearing you say it."

Wu Zhangkong looked at the number, and his face did not change, because Wu Zhangkong's face did not change, and because he had written a number down once, in silence, at the tournament, and had carried it in his head ever since. He looked at the boy. He looked at the machine. He did the arithmetic that Zhang Yangzi had done, and then he did the arithmetic that Zhang Yangzi had not, which was to divide ninety-eight by five months and ask what in the world had been feeding a fire that hot.

And then he looked at the seven children, and at the bruises on six of them, and at the one without any, and he understood.

"Every fight," Wu Zhangkong said. It was not a question.

"Every fight," Lin Hao agreed.

"You have fought how many times since the tournament."

"I stopped counting at seventy," Lin Hao said, "because counting past seventy felt like showing off."

Xie Xie made a sound like a kettle.

Wu Zhangkong wrote the number down. He wrote it slowly. And then he said, "Well," and "that's not *normal*" was what Xie Xie said next, in a voice that had gone strangely small, and Wu Zhangkong agreed, and wrote it down, and said nothing else, because there was nothing else to say that would not raise a hundred questions he was not, yet, going to answer.

And Lin Hao — who had known, who had always known, who had watched this number coming since the sea took a girl and a man with smoky green eyes wrote it in a ledger and closed the book — looked at the number, and shrugged, and said, "I told you they'd measure me," in the voice of a boy who had told you, and had been right, and was not, particularly, enjoying it. "I told you it'd be a number. I didn't say it'd be a *normal* number." He looked at Wu Zhangkong. "You knew. You knew at the tournament. You wrote it down and you didn't say it."

"I wrote down a great many numbers at the tournament," Wu Zhangkong said, which was not a denial, and which Lin Hao took for what it was.

"And you didn't say this one."

"No," Wu Zhangkong agreed. "I didn't." And then, because he was Wu Zhangkong, and because the kindness he gave came sideways, in the flat voice, he said, "Some numbers are better carried quietly until they're heavy enough to set down." He looked at the machine, and at the thirty-four points that had grown on top of the quiet number like a debt accruing interest. "Yours got heavy faster than I expected. That is not a compliment. That is a warning. When a number moves that fast, people stop asking how and start asking *what.* I'll tell you what it means when I know. Not before." He capped the pen. "Understood."

"Understood," Lin Hao said, and meant it, and filed it, and did not say that the reason he meant it was not the warning.

## Part 3: The Monstrous One

Gu Yue went last, because the staircase went up, and because Wu Zhangkong had arranged it that way, and because Wu Zhangkong — unlike everyone else in the room — already knew what the top step was.

**One hundred and fifty-three.**

Wait.

Zhang Yangzi blinked. Xie Xie blinked. And Zhang Yangzi, who could not un-see a pattern and who had spent the last four minutes arranging the morning into a staircase with one boy at the top of it, said, in a small voice, "That's *higher.*"

"Yes," Wu Zhangkong said.

"But Lin Hao's was one hundred and seventy-eight, and he's been—"

"Climbing," Wu Zhangkong said. "Yes." And he wrote down Gu Yue's number with exactly the same expression he had written down every other number, which was the expression of a man filling in a form, and the entire room understood, in one lurching instant, two things at once: that one hundred and fifty-three on a ten-year-old elementalist was a number that did not belong to children at all. Had not belonged to them since before any of the rest of the class had been measured; and that the boy who had spent five months climbing had climbed *to second place,* which was not the same thing as failing and was also not the thing he had been climbing for.

Gu Yue looked at Lin Hao. It was the first time she had looked directly at him all morning. Her face did not do anything that could be called an expression, and yet Xie Xie, watching from the safety of the boy's own considerable cowardice, felt the temperature in the room make a decision.

And Lin Hao — because he was Lin Hao, and because the teasing was how he said things that were too heavy to say straight — grinned at her and said, "Don't worry. You're still the mysterious one. I'm just the *loud* one."

"I have not said a word all morning," Gu Yue said.

"Exactly," Lin Hao said. "Mysterious."

Something happened to Gu Yue's face then that was not a smile and was absolutely not nothing, and it lasted less than a second, and Zhang Yangzi saw it and would think about it for a year.

Wu Zhangkong said, "Next is the endurance test," in the casual voice of a man changing the subject on purpose.

The endurance test measured the power of a single blow, because for soul masters, only attack power had meaning. Gu Yue went first. And her left fist — the left fist of a ten-year-old elementalist — registered **one hundred and fifteen kilograms,** and Zhang Yangzi nearly fell over, because she was an elementalist, she was ten, and elementalist soul masters did not hit like that, and Xie Xie, who had been measured at a number he was not, yet, going to be teased about, trembled a little, and said nothing, because he had learned.

And then it was Lin Hao's turn. He stepped up and put his fist into the machine, and the machine said **four hundred and seventy kilograms** — higher than Gu Yue's, higher than Wang Jinxi's, higher than a number that had any business coming out of a ten-year-old. Zhang Yangzi's jaw dropped a third time, and Lin Hao looked at it and said, "I've been swinging a hammer since I was four," in the voice of a boy explaining something obvious, "so either the machine's broken or I've been doing it right," and Wu Zhangkong wrote the number down. Said, "Your form is wrong," because he was Wu Zhangkong, and because the kindness came sideways, "which means the number is going to be a great deal higher when you fix it," and Lin Hao, who had been carrying a number quietly since he was six, looked at the man, and grinned, because that was the first kind thing anyone had said to him all day, and it had come, as all of Wu Zhangkong's kindness did, dressed up as an insult.

Wu Zhangkong looked at his form. The numbers were monstrous. The form was, indeed, wrong — a smith's form, built for a hammer and an anvil and a fixed target that never moved, and it had never been corrected because there had never been time and nobody had thought to.

"one hundred and seventy-eight," Wu Zhangkong said, "and four hundred and seventy, and your stance is a blacksmith's stance and you telegraph your left shoulder a full breath before you commit." He put the pen down. "Which means that everything you have is *earned* and none of it is finished." He looked at the seven of them. "Get up. All of you. We're not done."

## Part 4: Six

"Here is what we are going to do," Wu Zhangkong said, and there was something in his flat voice that Xie Xie had learned, over five months, to recognize as the sound of a man who had decided something and was going to enjoy finding out whether he was right. "Lin Hao. Center of the ground."

"Yes."

"The rest of you." Wu Zhangkong looked at the six. "One at a time. In order. Full commitment — no pulling, no yielding early, no theatrical collapse, Xie Xie, I know what you do. You fight him until you are stopped. Then the next one goes." He picked up the pointing stick. "And Lin Hao does not use his wind, and he does not use his talon."

The ground went quiet.

"Sir," Xie Xie said, "that's his *kit.*"

"I know what it is."

"You're taking away the two things that—"

"I am taking away," Wu Zhangkong said, "the two things that let him not be a swordsman." He looked at Lin Hao. "Your sword. Your body. Your eyes. That is all. If that is not enough, then I have been wrong about you for five months, and I would like to find that out now rather than in three years when something is trying to kill you."

And Lin Hao, who had been carrying a number quietly since he was six years old and the sea took a girl, and who had been carrying seventy-one lines in a ledger nobody knew existed, and who had been waiting — though he would have died before admitting it, and did nearly die, several times, in the next eleven minutes — for exactly this, drew the Stormbringer.

"You're going to be *so*—" Wulin began.

"Don't," Lin Hao said. "You're going to say something kind and encouraging and I need you to say something insulting, because I fight better when I'm annoyed."

"You have terrible hair."

"Perfect," Lin Hao said. "Thank you. Go be the first one to lose."

**Wang Jinxi** went first, because Wu Zhangkong put him first, and Wu Zhangkong put him first because Wang Jinxi was the one who had built a decoy fold to lie to Lin Hao's eyes, and Wu Zhangkong wanted to know whether the boy who had been lied to had learned anything from it.

Wang Jinxi's Bone Dragon King rose around him in yellow light and his claw came in on the line that had broken three of class one's best.

Lin Hao did not dodge it. He *met* it — Stormbringer flat, angled forty degrees, taking the impact on the blade the way an anvil takes a hammer, and the shock went down his arms and into his feet and into the dirt, and he did not move, because four hundred and seventy kilograms is what a body looks like when it has swung a hammer since it was four years old. And then the sword spoke. Not a cut. A *question* — laid along the inside of Wang Jinxi's wrist, exactly where the claw's own momentum had committed the arm to a direction it could no longer leave.

Wang Jinxi's claw was still going. Wang Jinxi was not. He went down on one knee with the Stormbringer's edge resting against the tendons of his own wrist, and he stayed there, and he said, "You read that."

"I read it five months ago," Lin Hao said. "You told me how it worked when you built it. You were very thorough. I appreciated it."

Wang Jinxi, who was not a boy who smiled, smiled. It was a small, terrible, delighted smile, and he got up and walked off the ground, and Wu Zhangkong made a mark on his form.

---

**Zhang Yangzi** went second. Shadow Phantasm Eagle — speed, angles, the boy who could be somewhere else. He had learned a great deal in five months. He came in low and fast and feinted the left, and Lin Hao's sword was already at the place the feint was going to end, because Lin Hao had sparred with this exact feint eleven times in the last three weeks and had answered it correctly nine.

One exchange. Zhang Yangzi's blade was on the ground and Zhang Yangzi was holding his wrist and looking at his own hand with the expression of a boy who has just been introduced to a stranger who knew his name.

"You knew," Zhang Yangzi said.

"I know," Lin Hao said. "I've been keeping notes."

"On *me?*"

"On everyone. You're seventh. You're a very good seventh, Zhang Yangzi, your feint is genuinely excellent, it's just that I've seen it, and once I've seen something I don't have to see it again." He offered a hand up. "That's not an insult. That's the most expensive thing I own."

Zhang Yangzi took the hand. He had been, all morning, a boy who had been told "not bad" for the first time since the rematch. Now he was a boy who had been told he was worth keeping notes on, and he did not know — nobody in class zero knew yet — that this was the highest thing Lin Hao had to give.

---

**Wei Xiaofeng** went third, and Wei Xiaofeng went third loudly.

Green Shadow Snake. Green Shadow Clones. Rank twenty-two and a grudge, and Wei Xiaofeng had spent five months being the seventh in a class of seven and had not, one single time, been told "not bad." He came in with two clones flanking and the real one behind, which was a genuinely good tactic, which was the tactic that had beaten Lin Hao in the test spar, which was precisely why Lin Hao had spent three weeks learning it.

The Stormbringer took the left clone apart. The right clone went for the ribs. Lin Hao turned into it — not away, *into* it — because the thing about clones is that they are all committed to the same intent, and intent has a direction, and if you walk toward the intent instead of the blade, the blade has to come find you, and by then the sword is already where the intent lives.

Wei Xiaofeng went down. Both clones went with him, because clones are not people and do not survive their owner's surprise.

"That was the *same thing* I did last month," Wei Xiaofeng said, from the dirt, in a voice of pure outrage.

"Yes."

"You *stole* it."

"I *learned* it," Lin Hao said, and offered the hand, and Wei Xiaofeng did not take it, and Lin Hao left it there anyway for a full three seconds before withdrawing it, because Wei Xiaofeng would take it eventually, and because leaving it there was the kind of thing that made that eventually arrive sooner. "You're the only one in this class who's taught me something twice. You should be proud. Nobody else has managed it."

Wei Xiaofeng's face did something complicated. He got up without help. He did not, however, say anything else for the rest of the afternoon, which Xie Xie would later describe as the single most frightening event of the entire day.

---

**Xie Xie** went fourth, and Xie Xie lasted longest, and Xie Xie knew it was coming, and Xie Xie was still furious about it.

Two rings. Light Dragon Dagger. Nine weeks of mornings and then months more, and Xie Xie had, over that time, become the one person alive who could genuinely hurt Lin Hao in a spar — which is why Lin Hao had asked for him every single morning, and why Xie Xie, who was many things but not stupid, had eventually understood what he was being used for and had decided, characteristically, to enjoy it.

Eleven exchanges. Xie Xie landed two — one on the shoulder, one across the forearm, and the second one opened skin, and Lin Hao hissed, and Xie Xie's whole face lit up like a lantern.

"*I got you.*"

"You got me."

"I got you *twice.*"

"You did."

"And you still won."

"I still won," Lin Hao agreed, and the Stormbringer was at Xie Xie's throat, and Xie Xie was grinning the grin of a boy who had drawn blood off a monster, and Lin Hao was grinning back, because the shoulder was going to bruise and the bruise was worth more than the win, and this was the whole arrangement between them and had been since the first morning.

"That's your sixty-ninth," Xie Xie said, touching his own throat where the edge had been. "I'm in the ledger at sixty-nine."

"You're in the ledger at *one,*" Lin Hao said. "You're the first line in it. You get top billing. Be honored."

"Top billing for *losing?*"

"Top billing for teaching me the most." Lin Hao sheathed nothing, because the next one was already walking, but he let the sword drop to a rest. "Nobody's taught me more than you. Nobody's close."

And Xie Xie — who had spent five months being the loud one, the fast one, the one who talked through everything because talking was how he kept from feeling it — did not say anything at all for a moment. Then he said, "Right. Well. Good. Excellent. I'm going to go bleed somewhere dignified," and walked off the ground holding his arm up like a banner.

---

**Wulin** went fifth.

The Stormbringer and the hammer met, and the training ground — which had been quiet for four fights — made a noise, because this was the fight everyone had wanted to see since the day the roster was read and the two names came out first and second.

It was not a long fight. It was, however, the only one that made Wu Zhangkong step forward.

Wulin's body was not, entirely, the same stuff as the other six. He took the Stormbringer flat across the forearm and the forearm did not bruise and Wulin did not slow, and Lin Hao — who had fought this exact brother every evening for months, who knew the hammer's lines the way he knew his own — found himself in the one exchange in the ledger that had no answer in it, because Wulin did not adapt. Wulin *endured.* You could learn the angle and the angle would still break your arm.

So Lin Hao did the only thing that worked, which was to stop trying to win the exchange and win the *position* instead — one step, blade down, edge at the base of Wulin's hammer wrist where the grip lived, and Wulin's hammer stopped, because the hammer could survive anything except not being held.

"You cheated," Wulin said, delighted.

"I *adapted.*"

"You cheated *adaptively.*"

"That's the whole thing," Lin Hao said. "That's literally the entire thing. It's in the name."

Wulin let go of the hammer's head with one hand and put that hand on Lin Hao's shoulder, and left it there, and the training ground — the six, the man with the pointing stick — watched the two of them for a beat longer than was strictly about combat, and then Wu Zhangkong said, "Gu Yue," in the flat voice, and the beat ended.

---

**Gu Yue** went sixth.

She did not release a ring. She did not raise a hand. She walked to the center of the ground the way she did everything, which was as though the ground had agreed to be walked on, and she stopped one meter from the Stormbringer's point, and she looked at Lin Hao, and Lin Hao — who had seventy-one lines in a ledger, who had a sword that spoke first, who had four hundred and seventy kilograms and one hundred and seventy-eight points and five months of pressure behind him — understood, with total clarity, that he had no line for this one.

Because Gu Yue had never fought him. Not once. She had fought beside him, and near him, and she had poured a cup of water under four gripping toes, and she had looked at him exactly once that morning. But she had never, in five months, given him a single thing to write down.

"You can't," Gu Yue said, quietly.

"Can't what."

"You can't win this one. Not because I'm stronger. Because you have nothing." Her eyes were the color of a filed report. "You fight with everything you've already seen. I haven't shown you anything. So there is no fight. There's only you, and a person you don't have."

Lin Hao stood there for a long moment with the Stormbringer at the ready and the whole of his ledger — seventy-one lines, five months, every bruise he had ever been handed — completely, entirely useless.

And then he laughed. He laughed so hard he had to lower the sword, and Xie Xie, from the dirt, said, "Is he *broken?*"

"No," Lin Hao said, wiping his eyes. "No, I'm — she's right. She's completely right and it's the *best* thing anyone's ever said to me." He looked at Gu Yue with an expression that Zhang Yangzi, watching, would spend a year thinking about. "Everyone else in this class, I've got them. I've got their whole self written down. You've given me *nothing.* Do you know how long it's been since I had to fight somebody I couldn't read? Since the tournament? Since *Wang Jinxi's decoy?* You're the only mystery I've got left and you've been keeping it for five months and I didn't even *notice,* because I was so busy having the answer to everybody else."

Gu Yue looked at him.

"So don't fight me," Lin Hao said. "That's the answer. I'd lose. I'll lose every time until you show me something, and you're not going to, because you're *Gu Yue.* So instead I'm going to say the thing nobody's said to you all morning." He put the Stormbringer away. "Thank you. Genuinely. You're the only one who could have told me that, and you told me before I found out the expensive way."

Gu Yue did not fight him. Wu Zhangkong made a mark on his form that was, by his standards, a long one.

And the six were down — five of them beaten, one of them undefeated by declining to begin — and Lin Hao stood in the center of the training ground with two rings and a sword and no wind and no talon and a bruise forming on his shoulder where Xie Xie had opened him, and Wu Zhangkong looked at the form, and at the six, and at the boy, and said the thing that the whole of class zero would repeat, in one version or another, for the rest of their lives.

"Four times," Wu Zhangkong said.

"Sir?"

"I have hit you with this stick four times in five months." He turned it in his hand. "I hit Xie Xie more times before breakfast today. I have not hit you four times because you are fast. I have not hit you four times because you are strong. I have not hit you four times because after the first time, you had me — and I have six rings, and two of them are ten thousand years old, and I have fought people whose names you will hear before you die." It is, in my experience, the more dangerous thing. And it is the reason I am going to stop holding back on you, starting tomorrow, which I want you to understand is not a kindness."

"I understand," Lin Hao said.

"You don't," Wu Zhangkong said. "You will." And he was gone, and the six of them lay on the training-ground dirt like a crop that had been harvested — again — and Xie Xie said, "I have *never* been so tired and so proud at the same time, and I got beaten by a boy with a *stick*," and Wulin said, "Hao."

"Yes."

"You beat all of us."

"I beat five of you," Lin Hao said. "Gu Yue beat me and she didn't even touch me, which is the worst kind of losing, and I'm going to think about it for a year." He lay back on the dirt beside his brother and looked at the sky, which was doing the thing the Eastsea sky did in the late afternoon, which was to turn the color of cooling steel. "Wulin. Nobody's ever just *declined* to fight me before."

"She's strange."

"She's *magnificent,*" Lin Hao said. "There's a difference. You'd know the difference if you paid attention to anyone other than hammers."

"I pay attention to you."

"You pay attention to my *hair.*"

"That's attention," Wulin said, and the two of them lay in the dirt and argued about it for the length of a cooling forge, and neither of them said the other thing, which was that the boy who had kept a ledger of seventy-one fights had, today, been handed his first blank page in five months. That he had laughed about it, and then filed it, and then lain in the dirt next to his brother and felt, for reasons he would not have been able to explain to anyone, considerably lighter than one hundred and seventy-eight points.

## Part 5: What the Numbers Meant

That night, on the roof that was not theirs anymore, the two brothers sat with their backs against the warm chimney, and Wulin said, "one hundred and seventy-eight," the way you say a number you've been carrying.

"one hundred and seventy-eight," Lin Hao agreed. "Gu Yue's a hundred and fifty-three. I'm thirty-five behind her. I've had all day with it and I've stopped minding."

"She's stronger than you," Wulin said. It was not a challenge. It was Wulin being Wulin, which was to say the true thing straight, without the dressing that Lin Hao put on everything.

"She is," Lin Hao agreed, without a flicker of anything that needed managing. "She's stronger, she's faster than she has any right to be, she hits like a siege engine, and she has never once given me a single thing to write down. She'd win. Today, tomorrow, next year, she'd win." He nudged his brother's shoulder. "And I'd beat everybody else in the building twice, and lose to her every time, and I have made my *peace* with that, Wulin, which is more than I can say for you and your feelings about the boy from the beef shop."

"I have made my peace with the beef shop."

"You've made a *grudge* with the beef shop."

"That's a kind of peace."

Lin Hao laughed. And then, because the laughing was the door and the room was behind it, he said, "Do you know what Wu Zhangkong told me, after the numbers? Not the warning. Before it. He said 'some numbers are better carried quietly until they're heavy enough to set down' — at the tournament, he said that, and today he said mine got heavy faster than he expected." He looked at his brother. "It means he's carrying it too. I've been carrying it alone since I was six, since the sea took Na'er, since the day I decided I'd be strong enough that it wouldn't happen again — and today a man with smoky green eyes told me he's been carrying it too, quietly, and he'll tell me what it means when he knows. And I don't know why that's the thing that got me, Wulin. It should be the number. It's not the number. It's that I'm not carrying it alone."

Wulin's shoulder was against his, warm, and the harbor did its accounting, and neither of them said the rest, because the teasing was the whole of the saying, and the quiet was the room you got to through the teasing, and you could not get to the room any other way.

"Also," Lin Hao said, after a while, "she said I fight with everything I've already seen. And she's right. That's the whole talent. Every fight I've ever had is still in there — Mo Si's short sword, Qin Lang's feed-line, that bear's honest momentum, your brother's hammer, seventy-one lines of it. That's why the number moved. It's not that I'm working harder than everyone. It's that everyone else *forgets* their fights and I don't. Every bruise I've ever taken is still working for me." He turned his hand over, palm up, in the harbor light — the smith's hand, the sword hand, the one that had taken Xie Xie's dagger across the forearm that afternoon and would carry the line for a week. "Thirty-four points in five months. Do you know what that means?"

"That you're a monster."

"It means *the fighting is the cultivation,* Wulin. Everyone in that class sits and breathes and waits for the numbers to move. I get hit. Wu Zhangkong told me to expect him to stop holding back, and I said I understood, and I meant it, and I meant it because I understood the *other* half of it too." He closed his hand. "Every time he hits me properly, I get better. Faster than anyone in that building gets better. So the more dangerous it gets, the faster I move — and that's not a warning, that's a *schedule.*"

Wulin was quiet for a moment. "That's terrifying."

"That's Tuesday," Lin Hao said.

And under it all, on the far water, the hawk watched the boy who had been measured, and had been given a number, and had beaten six of them with a sword and a body and nothing else, and had been told — by the only person in the class who had nothing to teach him, and by the only person who had ever hit him — what he was. And the hawk, which had been asking a question for a very long time, did not ask it that night, because the boy was, for the first time since the sea took a girl, not entirely alone with it. And the question stayed quiet. And the dark water kept what it kept. And the number — one hundred and seventy-eight, and climbing, and heavy enough now to set down, though not yet set down — sat on the shelf with the rest of them, and the ledger next to it had seventy-one lines in it, and one blank page, and the blank page had a name on it that nobody had said out loud.


Rank twenty-nine arrived during the gauntlet, somewhere between the fourth opponent and the fifth, and he did not mention it, because he was busy. That was the whole mechanism, and he understood it better than anyone in the building: he did not cultivate his way up. He was *fought* his way up.

Somewhere between the fourth opponent and the fifth the hawk stirred, eight hundred and forty years of condensed wind turning over in him like a sleeper changing position.

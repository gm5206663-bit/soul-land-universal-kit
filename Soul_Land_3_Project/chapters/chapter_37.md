## Part 1: Six Saturdays

Xie Xie came on the first Saturday at seven in the morning, which was earlier than Lin Hao, and said, "I'm not here to talk," and then talked for forty minutes.

He came on the second Saturday with his sleeves already rolled up, which was the first time he had ever arrived anywhere prepared, and he asked which anvil was his as though he had been allotted one, and Lin Hao gave him the far one by the window because the light was better and did not say why for a week.

On the third Saturday he stopped talking about the letter entirely.

On the fourth he cut his thumb and swore in a way that made Mu Chen look up from the far forge, and Mu Chen said, "Good," and went back to work, and Xie Xie said, "He said *good,*" and Lin Hao said, "That means you did it properly. He only says good when you do it properly."

On the fifth Saturday he made a hinge.

It was not a good hinge. It was a hinge. It had two leaves and a pin and it opened and it closed and it did not wobble, and Xie Xie held it in his hand for about a minute without saying anything, and then he said, "I made this," in the voice of a person who has understood something they will not be able to un-understand.

"You made this."

"I *made* it." He turned it over. "My father has bought me everything I have ever had. Everything. The dagger, the boots, the bed, the school, the — everything. And I have never once made a thing." He put the hinge in his pocket, where the letter used to be. "That's a horrible thing to find out at ten."

"You found out at ten. That's the good version."

## Part 2: What the Bench Said

On the sixth Saturday, Mu Chen put a bar on the far anvil and said, "Refine it," and went away.

Xie Xie worked for three hours.

Lin Hao worked beside him and did not look over once, which was the hardest thing he had done all week, because the whole of him wanted to see whether the boy was doing it right, and he had decided a week ago — after a conversation with Wu Zhangkong that had lasted four sentences — that looking was the same as helping, and that helping was the thing that would ruin it.

At noon Xie Xie put the hammer down and sat on the floor with his back against the anvil, which was where Lin Hao sat, and said, "I don't want to build mechas."

Lin Hao did not say anything.

"I want to be clear about it, because I've been saying 'Mecha Master' since I was six and everybody has heard me say it about nine thousand times, and I need to hear myself say the other thing." Xie Xie looked at his hands. They were blistered in two places and there was a burn on his wrist that he had not mentioned. "I don't want to *build* them. I want to *fly* one. I want to be in it. I've never wanted to make a thing in my life until this week, and the only thing I've made is a hinge, and I like the hinge, but I don't want to make hinges." He stopped. "I want to fight in a machine I didn't build, and I want whoever built it to be very good, and I want to be very good at flying it, and those are two different people and I've been pretending for four years that they're the same person because it's one word."

"That's not nothing," Lin Hao said.

"It's nothing. 'Mecha Master' is a word that means both and I've been using it to not decide."

"That's not nothing either." Lin Hao sat down beside him. "Here's what's not nothing. You found out in six Saturdays, on a bench, with a hinge, and your father has spent a fortune and you are ten years old and you have just found out something that most people in this city find out at forty with a wife and a debt. You did it in six weeks and it cost you a thumb."

"It cost me a thumb and a hinge."

"Right. Best money you've ever spent."

## Part 3: The Letter

Xie Xie wrote it on the Sunday, at Lin Hao's kitchen table, with Lin Hao's mother's good pen, and it took him four attempts.

The first three were to his father.

The fourth was to the prep course at Glorybound, and it was one paragraph long, and it said that he was withdrawing his place, and that he was grateful, and that he had decided to remain at Eastsea, and that he would like to be considered instead for the pilot track when he was old enough, and that he understood if that was not possible.

"Short," Lin Hao said.

"Short is the only way I can do it without lying." Xie Xie folded it. "If I write it long I start explaining, and if I start explaining I start apologising, and if I apologise he'll think I'm frightened, and I'm not frightened, I'm just wrong, and those are different."

"He's going to be angry."

"He's going to be *hurt,*" Xie Xie said, "which is worse, and I've worked that out, and I've decided I'd rather he be hurt once than be lied to for six years." He put the letter in his coat. "Can you come."

"Yes."

"I didn't ask you to come."

"I know. I'm coming anyway." Lin Hao stood up. "You told me at two in the morning three weeks ago that the letter was still folded and six Saturdays wasn't an answer, and you were right, and this is the answer, and I've decided that I'm going to be in the room when you give it, because you're my brother and that's what that means, and I'd like you to know that I've made this decision without consulting you and I'm not taking it back."

Xie Xie looked at him for a second.

"You're extremely annoying," he said.

"Yes."

"Fine."

## Part 4: The House on Fenmere Street

Xie Xie's father was a thin man with a very good coat and a room full of things that had been bought, and he listened to the whole of it standing up, which Lin Hao understood was the part that was going to matter.

He did not interrupt once.

When Xie Xie finished, he said, "You made a hinge."

"Yes."

"Mu Chen wrote to me." He said it the way you say something you have been holding. "He does not write to me. He has never written to me. He wrote to me on Thursday and he told me that his journeyman said your hinge had an honest pin, and I have been sitting with that sentence for two days because I did not know what it meant, and I have worked out that it means you made something that was not bought." He sat down, finally. "I bought your dagger for a thousand years' worth of money when it was worth a hundred, and I have never once been able to explain to anybody why, and I have worked that out too. I bought it because I could not make you anything, and I have never in my life been able to make my son anything."

The room was quiet.

"So go and be a pilot," he said. "I don't understand it and I don't like it and I am going to be extremely difficult about the fees, and I would like you to know that I am proud of the hinge and I would like you to not tell anybody I said that."

Xie Xie said, "I've already told Lin Hao."

"Then he knows more than your mother."

## Part 5: What Wu Zhangkong Did With It

He was told on the Monday, in the yard, by Xie Xie, who told it badly and at length, and Wu Zhangkong listened to all of it with his hands behind his back.

"Pilot track," he said, when it was finished.

"Yes."

"You have two rings, a dagger that cost your family a fortune, and a body that is faster than anything else in this building, and you want to sit in a machine."

"I want to fly."

"I know what you want." Wu Zhangkong looked at him for a while. "You should know that a mecha pilot is worth less than a battle soul master of the same rank, and that everybody in this city knows it, and that you are choosing the smaller thing on purpose."

"I know."

"And you still want it."

"Yes." Xie Xie put his hands in his pockets. "Because I'm good at it and I'm not good at the other thing, and I've spent four years saying a word that meant both so I wouldn't have to find out."

Wu Zhangkong was quiet for a moment.

"Then you have done the hardest thing anyone in this academy has done this year," he said, "and you have done it at ten, and it has cost you a fortune and a thumb, and I am going to write to the pilot track myself because they will not take you seriously otherwise, and I want it understood that I am doing this because you were honest and not because you were right." He turned to go. "And Xie Xie."

"Sir."

"The hinge." Wu Zhangkong did not look back. "Bring it to me. I want to see it."

---

Gu Yue was at the gate when he went out, which she did not often do, and she said, "You've been at the forge six Saturdays."

"Yes."

"I know. I've been counting." She was looking at the street and not at him, which was how she did the serious things. "I have been counting a great many things lately. Your Saturdays. The days since the forest. The number of times you have looked at me when you thought I wasn't looking, which is nine, and I have decided to let you have those without comment." She turned her head a fraction. "You spoke to it."

Lin Hao went still.

"You said 'hello' to something in Fenmere Street on Tuesday," Gu Yue said. "I was on the other side of the wall. I didn't know what you were doing and I have thought about it for four days, and I have decided you were speaking to the hawk, and I would like you to know that I think that is either the strangest or the most sensible thing anyone in this academy has done, and I have not decided which."

"It's not a wall," Lin Hao said.

"No," Gu Yue agreed. "I don't think it is." And then, after a moment, in the voice she used when she had decided to give something away, "I have something in me that does not speak either, and I have never once said hello to it, and I have been thinking about that for four days as well."

She went inside. Lin Hao stood at the gate for a while and understood that he had just been given the third thing, and that she had counted it, and that she had given it to him anyway.

## Part 6: What the Hawk Was Doing

Lin Hao walked home alone, which he did not often do, and the hair at his temple was moving, and it had been moving for a week, and it had started moving before the wind did.

Nine hundred and fifty years.

He knew the number the way he knew his own hands, and he knew what was at a thousand, and he had known it since Mu Chen told him what a spirit soul was when he was eight and a half, and he had never once in his life been able to do anything about it. It was not a wall he could hit. It was not a seam he could find. It was a bird, and the bird had its own weather, and the only thing he had ever been able to do was feed it, and he had been feeding it for four years with everything he had.

Fifty years.

He stood on Fenmere Street with his hands in his pockets and thought about it, and understood something he had not understood before, which was that he had been treating it like a bottleneck, and a bottleneck is a thing you break, and this was not a bottleneck.

It was a *somebody.*

"You're not a wall," he said, out loud, to a bird that could not hear him and probably could. "You're a person who's taking their time. And I've been rude about it. I've been *waiting* at you for four years like you're a door."

The hair at his temple moved.

The wind had not started yet.

Lin Hao stood on Fenmere Street for a while with his hands in his pockets and the street going on around him, and thought that he had spent four years trying to get through something, and that the thing had been listening the whole time, and that he had never once in his life asked it anything, because he had been too busy wanting.

"Right," he said. "So. Hello."

Nothing happened.

But he walked home with the feeling that something had turned over, the way it turned over before it did something, and that this time it had turned over because he had spoken to it, and not because he had wanted something from it, and that those were different, and that the difference was probably the whole of what he had been missing since he was six years old.

## Part 7: Three Hundred

Wu Zhangkong told them on the Monday, in the flat voice he used for things that were going to change their lives.

"Twice a year, the spirit ascension platform rebels."

Xie Xie, who had been leaning back, sat up.

"It began as a fault," Wu Zhangkong said. "The energies the platform is built from were unstable, and twice a year they came loose. That was fixed. What was not removed was the *effect* — during a rebellion the soul beasts grow excited and become more visible, and the danger goes up. So they kept it. They made it a feature." He looked around the room. "**The Eighteen Pillars of Heaven limit entry to three hundred people per rebellion.** The academy spent an enormous amount of money to get five of those places. For you."

"Five," said Zhang Yangzi.

"You may enter as a team of up to seven, so five is no problem." Wu Zhangkong set down the paper he had not been reading from. "There is one more rule, and it is the one that will kill you if you forget it. **If you are ejected within the hundred seconds it takes to absorb a soul beast's spiritual energy, the remaining energy goes to the nearest Soul Master.**"

Nobody said anything.

"You will not be alone in there," Wu Zhangkong said. "Three-ring Soul Masters pay a great deal to enter a rebellion, because it is the fastest route to upgrading a spirit soul. They will be hunting. Some of them will be hunting each other." He capped the pen he had not been using. "The beasts are the lesser problem. They do not lie about what they want."

Afterwards, walking out, Xie Xie said, "So we can steal theirs too."

"You can," Wu Zhangkong said, from behind him, without turning around. "If you're confident. Try it and find out what a three-ring Soul Master is."

Lin Hao stopped in the doorway.

He had been on the platform eleven times. He had a ledger of it — ninety-eight lines, every one of them about what a beast would do. He had never once written a line about what a *person* would do, because until thirty seconds ago there had not been any people in there.

"Teacher."

Wu Zhangkong stopped.

"The hundred seconds," Lin Hao said. "Does the platform tell you when they start?"

"No."

"Then the only way to know is to be the one who killed it." Lin Hao said it slowly, because he was working it out as he said it. "Which means the safe position is to never be near somebody else's kill. Which means the team has to hunt in a shape where nobody is ever within a hundred seconds of a stranger." He looked up. "That's a formation. Not a tactic. A formation."

Wu Zhangkong regarded him for a moment.

"You have three days," he said. "Bring me one."

And he went, and left the five of them standing in a corridor with three hundred places in it and three days to work out how to come home.

## Part 8: Room Two-Oh-Five

He went back on the Sunday, because he had worked out that he had not been in room 205 in eleven weeks, and that this was a fact nobody had said out loud.

The corridor was the same. The door was the same. There were four bunks in it and two square desks and two cabinets and one roof lamp, and it was exactly as small as he remembered, which was the part that got him, because he had not thought about the size of it once since the day the academy had given the six of them rooms like teachers and he had carried his things down the stairs without looking back.

**Zhou Zhangxi** was on the lower left bunk. **Yun Xiao** was at the desk with his book, in the same place, at the same angle, under the same bad window.

"You're tall," Zhou Zhangxi said, without sitting up.

"I'm the same height."

"You're tall *in here.*" He rolled onto his side. "That's the thing nobody tells you. You lot got the big rooms and the food and the platform, and the actual cost is that this room got small, and now when you stand in it you can't pretend it was ever big."

Lin Hao sat down on the floor, because there was nowhere else, and because he had sat on that floor for a year.

"How's the roster going?" he said.

"We won three," Yun Xiao said, without looking up. "Lost two. I have the numbers if you want the numbers."

"I want the numbers."

"You never wanted the numbers before."

"I want them now."

Yun Xiao turned a page and read them out — four numbers and a ratio, in the flat voice he used for things he had already decided were true — and Lin Hao wrote them in the back of his ledger, in the column he kept for things that were not about himself, which was shorter than the other column and ought not to have been.

Zhou Zhangxi watched him write.

"You're still doing that," he said.

"Doing what."

"The paper." He propped himself on an elbow. "Wu Zhangkong made you start it, and you're still doing it, and I want you to know that I've told four people you're insane and none of them agreed with me, which I find insulting on your behalf."

"Thank you."

"I'm not being kind." Zhou Zhangxi lay back down. "I'm being accurate. Yun Xiao's been accurate at me for a year about you and he's never been wrong yet, so I've stopped arguing."

There was a silence, and it was not an uncomfortable one, which surprised Lin Hao, because he had rehearsed this visit nine times on the walk over and in none of the nine had it gone quietly.

"We've got three days before the rebellion," he said. "Five places. Up to seven in a team."

"I know."

"I can't take you."

"I know," Zhou Zhangxi said. "Three rings can go in. I've got one." He said it flatly, the way he said most things now. "That's not your fault and it isn't the academy's either, it's just where the arithmetic landed."

"Yun Xiao's got one too."

"And Yun Xiao's got a book," Zhou Zhangxi said, "and he'll have the numbers on all three hundred of you by the second day, which is worth more than either of our fists and we all know it." He looked at the ceiling. "So go. Bring back something we can use. That's the whole arrangement, and it always was — you go and get hit, and we're the ones who tell you what it meant."

Lin Hao looked at Yun Xiao, who had not looked up once, and who said, to the page:

"Noted."

He stayed another hour. He did not know why, and neither of them asked him to, and when he left the room was exactly as small as it had been when he arrived, and he went down the stairs of the twelve-floor dormitory slowly, which was not like him, and which he did not examine.

## Part 9: The Distance

He found Wu Zhangkong where Wu Zhangkong was always found at that hour, in the empty training ground with the wooden sword back on its rack and the wards humming at the edge of hearing.

"Teacher. The rebellion is three days. What comes after it?"

Wu Zhangkong did not answer at once, which with him meant the answer was a real one.

"After it comes the third year," he said. "And after that the exam."

"What exam?"

"**Shrek Academy.**" Wu Zhangkong looked at the rack, not at him. "They hold it **once every three years.** There is no second attempt, and there is no next year. If you are thirteen when it comes and you are not ready, you will be sixteen when it comes again, and by then you will be somebody else's generation."

Lin Hao did the arithmetic, because that was what the arithmetic was for. Three years. He was ten.

"They only take monsters," Wu Zhangkong said.

"I've heard that."

"You've heard the motto. You haven't heard the number." He turned, at last. "Class zero exists because Eastsea Academy cannot get its best students into Shrek Academy, and has not been able to for some years. That is what this class is *for.* The rest of it — the food, the rooms, the platform, the three hundred places, me — is all in service of four children standing in front of a gate in three years' time."

There was a silence with a great deal of weight in it.

"Why are you telling me this now?" Lin Hao asked.

"Because you asked what comes after," Wu Zhangkong said. "And because you are the one who is going to ask the difficult questions on the way, so you may as well start with the largest one."

He was quiet for a moment, and then he said something he had not said before, in the flat voice he kept for the things that mattered.

"There is a reason this class exists in *this* city, and it is not the food."

Lin Hao waited.

"Eastsea City's Spirit Pagoda has noticed the four of you." Wu Zhangkong looked at the rack. "They noticed Wulin first. Then they noticed the girl. Then they noticed you, because you are the one whose numbers do not behave. There is a man who carries a card with your name on it and who has not written your name on a form, and you already know that, so I will not insult you by pretending it is news."

Lin Hao did not say anything, because it was not news.

"When a spirit soul crosses a thousand years," Wu Zhangkong said, "it is not a private event. The Pagoda can see it. They have ways of seeing it that I am not going to explain to you tonight." He turned. "So the plan is not to hide. Hiding four children like these does not work and I have not tried. The plan is to be somewhere with **more** of you. Heaven Dou City has great clans and no clan that dominates, and a thousand geniuses, and in a room like that a talent shines for a moment and then it is somebody else's turn."

"That's not hiding," Lin Hao said slowly. "That's a crowd."

"That's a crowd," Wu Zhangkong agreed. "And you have fifty years, give or take, before you need one." He picked up the wooden sword and put it back, exactly where it had been. "Three days. Bring me a formation."

Lin Hao walked home through a city he had learned the shape of from a free pamphlet on a train — **eighteen first-class cities, five regions, a centre that held only Heaven Dou and Shrek**, an east with five coastal cities in an alliance and his own ranked second among them, a tournament every three years and a larger one every five, held in the city whose name had arrived in his life next to an advertisement for soul-lamp repairs.

He was ten years old, and he was thirty-seventh in a ledger of his own making, and in three years he was going to have to be a monster in front of a gate.

He thought about the hawk, which was fifty years from doing something it had not yet done.

He thought about a sword that had not yet decided what it wanted to say.

He thought that three years was not very long, and then — because he was Lin Hao, and because the teasing was how he said the things that were too heavy to say straight — he thought that it was probably about the right amount of time for somebody who had already learned to read the grain.

He went home. He started the formation that night.

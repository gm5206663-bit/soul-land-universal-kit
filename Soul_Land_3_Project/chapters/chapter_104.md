## Part 1: The Third

The vortex grew to five meters before Gu Yue let it land.

"Go," she said gently, and the whirlpool of fused wind and water went out onto Spirit Ice Plaza like a housewife's rag the size of weather.

The plaza hadn't even been dirty — dust, a night's leaf-fall, nothing disgraceful — which made the result more absurd, not less. Dust rose in a frenzy, whirled once around the hurricane, and was sucked inside; the flagstone came out from under it as though scrubbed by a brush that had also forgiven the stone. Lin Hao, standing the margin with two buckets he no longer had a reason to hold, watched it the way he watched a master pour: the woman moved at a tenth of combat speed, and the plaza surrendered a third at a time.

At the third she stopped, drained the whirlpool — black now, and heavy — down the plaza drains, and walked off to sit cross-legged where the stone was cleanest. Nobody suggested she do more.

"I'd be embarrassed," said Xie Xie, grinning the mischievous grin, "if I let Gu Yue do all of it."

"Then you can treat us to dinner tonight," Wulin said.

"Sure," said Xie Xie, "as long as *you're* not coming. The two girls eat like people. If we add you, I'd be concerned the restaurant even has stock. I swear your stomach is an endless hole."

"You're so insincere."

"Fine! I'll treat everyone. But you finish those black buns before we go out."

The rest of the morning and the early afternoon went to the room, and Lin Hao, with the pavilion's afternoon fire unclaimed, went to buy his first hours from Tie Jin — which was why he was not present for the furnishing, and why he heard about it afterward from Xie Xie in the tone of a man who had been used as a tool.

"It turns out," Xie Xie reported, in the courtyard, while Wulin was still at the wood yard, "that daggers are for cutting plank. Three souls, seven knives, and my captain used me as a saw. The beds are decent. The cloth divider Gu Yue hung herself — wind, obviously, she didn't even get on a stool."

The room had shaped up, and kept down. Beds where bunks had been; the broken panes closed; the curtain down the middle, boys to the warm wall, girls to the window. But nothing fine — Wulin had walked the slum flank first and counted the doors. Three or four rooms in the whole barracks showed recent life, and those were as plain on the outside as their own. Working students did not feather their nests here. It was tradition, and Wulin understood traditions the way he understood armor: you did not test the weakest plate first.

Only two things had been issued at registration besides the dark-green uniforms, and Lin Hao turned the second over in his hand while the others rested: the Shrek identification card. Metal, not wood, with soul circuits visible in it like veins under skin — needed to pass any gate on the grounds. A fine piece of work. He read it the way he read everything made, and what he read was this: a card that knew where you went was a card that could be asked where you had been. Every gate that accepted it was a pen, and the book it wrote in was not the cardholder's.

He put it in his pocket with the rest of his ledgers, and went to the pavilion to buy his first hours with work: pouring for Tie Jin while the scrap-master watched his wrists instead of the metal, and starting the second wrist guard out of another unclaimed off-cut, which folded at the sixth strike instead of the fourth. Failure moving later. On the failure wall, Tie Jin watched him hang the second cuff beside the first, and said only: 'It's learning to wait. So are you.'

The job paid at the administration window on the way back: one hundred contribution points, each, printed onto the metal cards in a small grey notation.

One hundred points. Lin Hao, standing at the window, watched the number and did the pricing the steward clearly expected nobody to do. Meals in the outer-court hall were paid in points. Five working students, three squares, and a captain whose bloodline ran a restaurant tab the fish themselves feared. One hundred points fed the five of them for a day. A day.

The points were not pay. The points were a leash with a dial on it, and the dial read: *work, or don't eat.*

## Part 2: Two Envelopes

There were two letters waiting on the boys' bunk when he got back to the room.

The first was coarse paper and a stamped seal, and it said what Tie Jin's wire had already told the city it would say: the Shrek branch of the Blacksmiths' Association summoned the fifth-rank Master Craftsman Lin Hao to bring his hands and his proof — rank registration on the continent's terms, no card needed, the card being the thing the Association existed to issue. The smiths' long wire, laid down before he had ever seen Shrek, had followed him across the map the way money follows a pour: slowly, and without asking permission.

The second was grey-edged paper, and it smelled, faintly and impossibly, like the inside of a filing cabinet.

He had seen that paper before. Eastsea. Three employees in one morning giving him the same look over the same counter, and a man in grey pocketing his name card like a man pockets a coin he means to spend.

He opened it. Wulin came in while he was reading the enclosure — came in quietly, because Wulin walked quietly when Lin Hao was doing arithmetic, the way you don't startle a man at a forge.

"What is it?"

Lin Hao handed him the letter. Wulin read the offer, and his captain's face got slowly more dangerous, the face he wore when a thing in front of him was broken and standing in his way.

Full sponsorship. Tuition dissolved like smoke in a banker's palm. Research placement in a Pagoda foundry. Spirit-soul access from the vaults that *made* spirit souls — ten-thousand-tier workshop stock, the kind men's grandmasters mortgaged estates to bid on. A stipend the point-dial would never have reached. Signed, with the precise courtesy of people who have never once needed to raise their voice: the Spirit Pagoda.

He looked up. "This is better than Shrek. All of this — the room, the points, the brooms — the Pagoda offered you the opposite of it."

"Dozens of times." Lin Hao sat on the bunk, and for once did not do the thing his voice was trained to do, the thing where the honest truth went through the margin and came out smaller. He said it flat, full size. "In my life, Wulin, no one has disappointed me the way this academy has. Seriously. I have had better offers dozens of times than anything this place has given us. I'm not joking. Mostly from the Spirit Pagoda."

Wulin didn't argue with the feeling. He sat down on the other bunk, and for a moment he looked like the boy who'd spent the same seven years over the same kind of anvil, because he had, in the city at the other end of the map.

"Blacksmiths too?"

"Blacksmiths especially." Lin Hao's thumb moved along the grey edge of the envelope — the old grain-read, done on paper now. "The smithing life is the honest one. The Association knows exactly what my hands are worth because their own hands are the same hands. Five years of letters from guild branches I've never visited, all opening the same way: *We are acquainted with your work.*" He almost smiled. "They don't want me as a student. They want the work a fifth rank makes. That's a clean offer. The Pagoda is the other kind — they don't want the work. They want the *boy the work came from.*"

"And the academy?" Wulin said, very quietly. The curtain on the girls' side had stopped moving entirely.

"The academy wanted a broom and a hundred points a day." He said it without heat, which made it worse the way ledger truth is worse for being calm. "Do you know what the fifth-rank Master Craftsman sitting in this room is worth to a metal market right now? I don't need to know it — Tie Jin knew it from one pour. That man would pay me to let me work. The academy pays me a leash. *That's* the disappointment, Wulin. Not the slum. The slum is honest too — nobody pretended the room wasn't a slum. The disappointment is that the greatest academy on the continent, the one that's supposed to be the whole continent's highest bidder, looked at my file and saw one working student with a bucket."

He tapped the enclosure's unregistered line.

"They didn't see this. The Pagoda *can't not* see it. Which of them do you think gets the better of me in the end?"

Wulin said nothing. The curtain rustled on the girls' side, and did not move otherwise — if Gu Yue was listening, she was listening like weather, invisibly and completely.

"Look at the enclosure," Lin Hao said.

Wulin turned the page.

It was a list. Not a threat — a *manifest.* His file, as the Pagoda kept it: the Eastsea awakening registry, year and mark and witness; every counter of the Spirit Ascension Platform his card had ever touched; every purchase the Pagoda had ever sold him, dated and priced; the assessors' notes, three clerks' names attached to the same morning, with the careful language of men who write what they have seen rather than what they understand. The man in grey's name card. Long Hengxu's awareness of it, recorded in a margin line in someone else's hand.

And at the bottom, in a newer ink, one line that did not resolve: *Second spirit-soul signature, four-ring reading, origin unregistered. Classification pending.*

"Too much," Lin Hao said. "They have too much of my information already. They have the whole book of the boy I was when I came through their counters. And this last line" — he tapped it, once — "is the one they actually wrote the letter for. They count everything. They count what they can't explain more than anything."

The room was quiet. Under it, very far inside him, the dark water of his country sat without a ripple, the coil at the bottom holding still the way it had held under Su Wen's mirror; and the storm-sky above had gone dark with clouds moving the wrong direction. The spirits did not speak. They never spoke. They were weather and water, and they had weathered worse than a clerk's envelope.

"So that's the offer," Wulin said slowly. He set the pages down on the bunk between them, flat, the way he set things he meant to answer in full. "Full vaults. No points. No slum. A room where the hot water is a *right* and not a hike across a plaza." He looked up, and his eyes were perfectly steady, because Wulin did not beg, even for this. "Are you going to take it?"

## Part 3: The Price of Staying

"No," said Lin Hao.

He said it the way he said a temperature read: immediate, uninteresting, final.

"Not because the room's fine. The room's bad. The points are a leash. One hundred points for a day of us — I priced it at the window; that's not tuition, that's a clock." He stacked the two envelopes against each other on his knee, the smiths' coarse paper and the Pagoda's grey. "I've had the better offer on paper since before I could grow a proper callus. Here's the thing about paper. Paper lists what they'll give you. It can't list what you'd be leaving, because the things you'd be leaving aren't inventory."

He nodded at the failed cuff line, which wasn't visible through any wall, and at the courtyard with the tree, which wasn't either, and at the curtain where Gu Yue was being weather.

"An elder of the Pavilion told me a man's request is an easement and a boy's request is a firework. Same arithmetic applies to a man's choice. A boy goes to the Pagoda for what's in the vault. A man looks at the Pagoda and asks the question their own file answers." He picked up the grey envelope. "What kind of institution writes a boy's whole life down and then *offers* it to him, Wulin? That's not recruitment. That's a merchant showing you his ledger to prove he can afford you. Nobody shows you his ledger unless he's afraid you'll walk."

He folded the enclosure once, along the unregistered-signature line, and put it away in the bag with his own book — not the one Shrek read, and not the one the Pagoda read. The third ledger.

"They've been pricing me for years," he said. "They just haven't worked out that I'm the one who reads prices. They keep that book because they're afraid of what the book doesn't contain yet — the signature they can't file, the ten-thousand-year reading nobody sold me. The day I ever knock on their door, I don't knock as a name in their registry. I knock as the man who already knows exactly what's in their ledger, because they sent it to me in an envelope and tried to call it a gift."

Wulin was quiet for a moment. Then he did the Wulin thing, the thing that made him captain at thirteen: he did not argue, he settled.

"Then we should be fed," he said. "Tonight's free. First day only — the steward said so, the steward's clock said so, and I intend to argue with it at every window in the hall." He stood, and the floorboards took his weight with the usual misplaced confidence. "You know, that envelope offered you vault stock. I'm about to do something in a dining hall that gets a discipline committee summoned."

"Of course you are," said Xie Xie, who had been listening from the other bunk for entirely some time. "Wait, dinner? I treat? I said that under duress. Also I take it back — and also whatever he's planning, I hereby pre-deny everything, three of me, unanimously, in advance."

## Part 4: Wicked

The dining hall held a thousand and looked built to feed that many twice.

Every seat in the outer court seemed to be in it — noise, steam, windows of food, the particular roar of an entire school deciding at once that the day was over. Working students ate on points; the first day's meals were free; the free line was the only thing about the evening that was ordinary.

Wulin found a large table.

Then he began.

He went from window to window with his metal card and his radiant, law-abiding smile, and at each window he asked for, and received, the amount of food a working student might reasonably accumulate if a working student were preparing for winter and also were two men. The table began to grow a landscape. He came back with more. The table developed layers. By the third level of food — trays stacked on plates stacked on the generosity of a man testing exactly how far a rule could be bent without being broken — the hall had begun, as a hall will, to watch.

Xiaoyan watched with the quiet awe of a surveyor watching a mountain happen. Gu Yue watched Wulin, which was its own layer of landscape. Xie Xie watched the doors for the management.

The management came.

A stern man around twenty, with a red armband and the walk of a person the building had told on someone to, crossed the hall to the table.

"I'm Xie Peichen," he said, "of the discipline committee." He looked at the table for ten — piled with enough food to feed thirty — and at the boy standing by it with sparkling white teeth. "Which class are you from? Why did you get so much food? Don't you know that according to the academy's rules, if you waste food, you pay a fine of ten times its weight?"

Wulin stopped mid-step. He turned, radiance unshaken.

"Hello, senior," he said. "I can finish all of this food."

"You can finish all of this. By yourself." The senior's voice went up an octave, because it had nowhere else to go.

"Yeah. I can finish it all myself. I've still got four friends joining me though," Wulin said, sincerely, and for exactly one heartbeat the discipline committee's eyes met Lin Hao's across the table, and Lin Hao's face was the face of a man whose ledger column had just come up empty.

"Four people can finish all of this?"

Wulin nodded earnestly.

"All right," said Xie Peichen, with the dark optimism of an official standing on solid rule for the first time all evening. "Let's see you eat it all. If you can't finish, don't blame me for being rude." He took up a position to the side, folded his arms, and watched.

Wulin smiled.

"Senior," he said, "how about we make a bet? If I finish all of this food, you'll help me get another round. If I can't, you can punish me as you wish."

Xie Peichen agreed immediately.

Lin Hao sat down at the table, because there was no longer anything to do but witness, and because witness was the one thing he was unfailingly willing to provide for this captain. He took his portion of a black bun to blunt his own edge, and in the noise of the hall, with Wulin rolling up his sleeves across a three-level mountain of food, he allowed himself one last thought about the grey envelope, small enough to fit in a ledger margin:

*They named a price for me tonight. So did this hall. A hundred points for a day, and a mountain of free food for the man who can clear it. Fine. I'll take the day rate. I'll do the hours. And I'll keep the third book — the one nobody gets to read — until I know exactly what I'm spending it on.*

The captain took his first bite.

Somewhere across the outer court, a blacksmith's summons waited for morning. Somewhere in a filing cabinet that smelled like paper and future, a page in a grey-edged folder sat with its newest line still unclassified. And at a table for ten in a hall of one thousand, Tang Wulin began to eat, and the discipline committee of Shrek Academy settled in, smiling the smile of a man who had not yet read the last column of the ledger.

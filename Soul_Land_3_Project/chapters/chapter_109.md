## Part 1: The Map of the Guilds

The associations found them before lunch.

Invitations began arriving with the morning post the way rain finds a leak — one at first, then a seam, then a steady drizzle folded into the wooden cubby each new student had been assigned outside the lecture theatre. By the end of the first week there were cards and ribbons and stamped leaves of paper from the Mecha Designers' Guild, the Mecha Makers' Lodge, the Cultivation Resources Exchange, the Outer-Court Combat Club, the Spirit Ascension Society, and a dozen smaller outfits whose names Lin Hao wrote down once and then forgot because they were, almost to a one, the same offer in different ink: join, rent your share of a senior's knowledge, and stop being the smallest fish in the largest pond on the continent.

"All of them," Shen Yi had said on the second morning, anticipating the question, "are founded and run by students. The academy leases them the land and leaves them alone. They exist because a second profession cannot be learned from a lecture — it is learned from the person two years ahead of you who has already wasted the metal you are about to waste. You will not progress far without one, and you may join as many as you have the hours for. Choose like you are spending time, because you are."

Lin Hao read the whole sheaf that evening with the particular interest he gave to anything that explained how a place fed itself. The associations were not clubs. They were the academy's economy wearing a friendly face — senior knowledge rented to juniors for contribution points and loyalty, the best of them feeding their strongest up toward the inner court and the rest quietly sorting out who could not keep pace. He filed the architecture the way Gu Yue filed weather systems, and he set aside exactly one invitation.

The Blacksmiths' Association of Shrek Academy.

He had carried its summons since before the term began — a sheet of paper that had reached him through Tie Jin's wire, the pavilion scrap-master's one connection to the continent-wide guild, offering to register a fifth-rank smith on Shrek's own terms rather than Eastsea's. He had let it wait through the trials and the headmaster and the frost receipt because there had always been something louder in the day. There was nothing louder now. Class ran only in the mornings, and the afternoons stretched ahead of him like unworked stock.

The next day, after the bell, he walked to the foundry quarter alone.

## Part 2: The Mark of the Fifth

The Blacksmiths' Association was a long, loud, sweltering hall built against the outer wall where the vent-fires could breathe. It smelled of scale and coke and the particular hot-metal smell that Lin Hao had loved before he knew the word for loving anything. A bored student clerk behind a desk looked up at the dark-haired boy in working-student grey, and opened her mouth with the sentence she gave every new fish, and then her eyes fell on the summons he laid on the counter, and the sentence changed into a different one entirely.

"Fifth rank," she said. "You. At — how old are you?"

"Thirteen," Lin Hao said. "It's been verified. Eastsea City guild hall, and the paperwork came through your own wire. I'm not here to argue the rank. I'm here to use it."

She fetched someone older. The someone older fetched someone older than that, and twenty minutes later Lin Hao stood at a forge table in the back while a one-armed smith named He Qiao — Association master, sixth rank, missing the arm to a spirit beast he declined to name — watched him draw the two demonstration heats the guild required of any rank it registered on its own authority.

It was not a test he could fail. He had spent the summer of his thirteenth year telling metal what it was instead of asking it. He did the first heat slowly enough that the old smith could follow the method — the listening, the place where his hammer did not strike so much as answer a question the metal had already asked — and the second heat at something closer to a working pace, and when he set the hammers down the finished bar rang once, very sweetly, and stayed ringing a little longer than a bar had any right to.

He Qiao turned it over in his single hand for a long moment. Then he said, without looking up, "You don't just thousand-refine. You ask first."

"Metal answers better if you do."

"That's the fifth-rank trick everyone lies about having." He set the bar down. "You're registered. Full workshop rights, member rate on tables, access to the guild stock. We don't get many of you — most of the young geniuses go into design or making because the early ranks come faster there. Smithing's slower. It hurts more. And every rank past mine needs a thing I can't teach." He held up his stump with a grim sort of humor. "I'm the proof it asks a price. But a fifth-rank at thirteen will be a sixth-rank by nineteen if he just keeps hitting the table. There's no wall past five for a smith. Just work."

That matched everything Lin Hao had already priced. He thanked the man, paid the member token's small fee, and stood a moment in the heat while the foundry roared around him.

Wulin would have been beside himself in this room. The rare-metal shelves he could glimpse through the inner doorway, the five-charge forging tables, the sheer scale of the stock — his captain would have taken one breath of this air and started calculating.

As if the thought had summoned it, a roar split the afternoon over the whole of the outer court.

"WHO IS TANG WULIN? COME HERE!"

Lin Hao went to the foundry door with the rest of them. Down the slope, across the court, a middle-aged man had erupted into the dining-hall entrance — disheveled flame-red hair, a scorching aura, broad and low as a furnace, arms thicker than most boys' whole bodies, the general silhouette of a lion that had learned to stand. His shout flattened the noise of a thousand students the way a hand flattens a candle. A heartbeat later he came back out with something under one arm — Wulin, held by the shoulder, utterly without the option of being anywhere else — and went back the way he had come in a blur of hot air, with a flustered second figure (ordinary, fast, swearing, Yuanen) sprinting after.

The foundry smiths laughed and went back to their work.

"Taken by a smith, that one," He Qiao said, following Lin Hao's eyes. "Old Man Feng of the battle-armor forges. He only drags off the ones he can't stand to watch waste themselves. Your friend just got adopted by the most violent teacher in the outer court." He squinted. "You knew the boy?"

"He's my captain." Lin Hao watched the blur vanish toward the forging quarter. He did not move to follow. The hand on Wulin's shoulder had not been a threat; he had seen the way the man's strength wrapped around the boy rather than closed on him, and underneath the roar there was the same heat that lived in this hall — the heat of a smith who had just felt metal worth forging. Wulin was not being abducted. Wulin was being *collected*. There was nothing to rescue. "He's in good hands," Lin Hao said. "Better than mine for what that man wants."

He turned back to the foundry. He had his rank, his token, his tables. His second profession was a thing he now owned outright in Shrek's own books.

So it was not the thing on his mind as he walked down toward the plaza to find the others. The thing on his mind was much older than the guild.

## Part 3: The Itch

He found Wulin's other half — Xie Xie, Xiaoyan, and Gu Yue — at the plaza fountain, Wulin himself being conspicuously absent in a way that required no explanation. Xie Xie was mid-account of the lion-man's abduction, in increasing detail and decreasing accuracy.

Lin Hao sat down on the fountain's rim with no preamble at all, because the thing in him had been building across the forging room and the roar and the whole careful, watchful, instrument-maxing year behind him, and it wanted saying while it was hot.

"Battle armor," he said, into the gap in Xie Xie's story. "All of you listen, because Shen Yi gave you the worry of it and I want the worry off the ledger early. Compatibility. Sixty percent minimum, nine pieces, grandmaster this, grandmaster that — it's a mountain for everyone else. It is not a mountain for me. When my turn comes to fuse a suit, I will not be lying awake over whether the metal takes to me. I have never once in my life met a piece of metal that didn't, by the end of the conversation. Compatibility is the one number in that whole wall I can stop worrying about today."

He said it the way he said the grain-read aloud sometimes — flatly, as a fact about the work, and not one of them needed to know the deeper reason, the adaptation that had made his whole body a thing that learned the shape of whatever it touched and became it. They heard a fifth-rank smith's confidence. That was all the truth the room needed.

Xie Xie blinked. "Well. Good for you. Some of us have to learn a whole second profession."

"Then learn it," Lin Hao said, and said the second thing, the one that had been sitting under his ribs like a held breath. "But that's not what I want to spend the afternoon talking about. I want to talk about money that doesn't come off a forging table."

That got their attention. Even Gu Yue, half-turned away toward the spray, shifted a fraction.

"I want to find work that pays for fighting," Lin Hao said. "I want to know if any of you know how a soul master in this place actually earns points by hitting something. And I want it badly enough that I'm asking out loud, which I don't usually do."

The honesty of it seemed to surprise them as much as the subject. He pushed on, because if he did not say the whole of it now he would not say it.

"It has been a very long time since I had a good fight. Not a spar that's over because I decided where it ended. Not a contest I won in the margin before the first exchange. A real one — something that walks out onto the same ground I'm on and makes me *reach*. Every fight since I came through that gate I've won holding something back, and I don't say that like it's a virtue, because it isn't. It's a man eating half a meal every day and calling himself fed. I have been careful for a year. I have been the quiet thing at the edge of every room, and it was the right thing to be, and I am not complaining about any of it. But I want to find the one place I can go where there's nothing to hold back for and nothing to tip off, where the thing across from me is allowed to be as strong as it wants and I am allowed the same, and it pays me for the privilege. Does that exist here, or do I have to build it?"

The fountain ran. For a moment none of them spoke, because the speech had come out of the margin — and the margin did not, as a rule, give speeches.

It was Xiaoyan who found her voice first. "You mean like the spirit ascension platform."

"Start there."

"The academy runs ascension slots out of the platform hall," she said, getting into it the way she got into anything that involved a pattern. "It costs points to enter, and you go in against spirit beasts at the floor you pay for, and the platform pays you back out of what you bring down — spirit souls, materials, the kill itself counts. It's not a spar with a person. It's a hunt. Nobody in there is your classmate. Nobody in there is watching to decide what you are." She looked at him. "You could go up to a floor where the beasts are actually dangerous. People do it for resources. Some do it because it's the only place on campus they can cut loose."

"Or the culling tickets," Xie Xie offered, recovering. "There's a job board at the administration that isn't cleaning. Spirit-beast suppression — forest work, the real wilderness, contracts the academy lets out when something near the outer range gets strong enough to need thinning. Those pay in points and in parts, and half the board nobody takes because the beasts are mean. Strong alone out there. No crowd."

"No crowd," Lin Hao repeated. The words landed somewhere deep and exactly right. "That's the shape of it." He thought it through out loud, the way he did with metal. "I can't cut loose on a person. Not here. A peer I beat at full strength is either a secret I have to keep with my hands, or a body, and I am in the market for neither. But a beast on a high ascension floor, or a cull out in the range where it's me and something with teeth and no one from the dean's office holding a clipboard — there's nothing to hide there. It can't be offended. It can't report me. It can't faint and need carrying. It can only hit back, which is the entire point. And it pays. That's the work I want. Something that hits back and settles up in points."

Gu Yue, who had said nothing through all of it, turned her head at last and looked at him. Her face had no expression he could read — he had catalogued that fact across two years and given up cataloguing it — but there was the smallest of pauses, the length of a held breath, before she spoke.

"The high ascension floors," she said, "are where people die."

"People," Lin Hao said. "Not me." He said it without heat, the way you read a number off a scale. "I'm not going up there to gamble. I'm going up there to find out what the margin looks like when I stop choosing it. I know the size of my own reach down to the inch. I have simply never been allowed to spend it all at once, and I would like, before I'm much older, to know what it feels like."

Wulin would have argued him out of it out of love. Wulin was not there. Gu Yue looked at him for one beat longer, and then gave a small, single nod — not approval, exactly, but acknowledgment of a thing she had correctly identified as already decided — and turned back to the fountain.

## Part 4: The Ticket

The administration job board was exactly where Xie Xie said it was, tucked into a side hall off the same building that paid them for the plaza, and it was the antidote to every sweeping job on the morning roster.

Lin Hao read it the way he read a menu he had been waiting a year to be handed.

The spirit ascension platform slots were there, floor by floor, each with its entry price and its posted reward band — the low floors cheap and crowded and useless to him, the high floors expensive and sparsely subscribed and, in a couple of cases, marked with the little symbol that meant the academy advised against them for outer-court students. The culling contracts were pinned below that: range work, spirit-beast suppression, payments in points plus the value of whatever was brought back, each ticket carrying a beast, a location, and a posted danger mark.

He did not take the very highest. That would not have been care; that would have been a lie about who he was, because he had no wish to die and no romance with the idea. He did not take the safe ones, because the safe ones were the half-meal again. He ran his finger down the board and chose the way he chose metal at the shelf — by listening for the note.

He stopped on a culling ticket in the middle of the dangerous band: a high-tier spirit beast ranging the wild country beyond the outer training ranges, old and smart and twice struck off by teams that had not been strong enough to finish it. Good points. Full salvage rights. No partner required. Posted for a third time because the first two contracts had come back empty.

A beast that had already beaten two groups and lived. Something that hit back.

He took the ticket down, carried it to the counter, and registered it against his new Blacksmiths' Association token and his working-student card. The clerk looked at the danger mark, looked at him, and did the arithmetic of a thirteen-year-old in grey uniform versus a beast that had turned back a team, and opened his mouth.

"I've read the mark," Lin Hao said. "I'm not asking for an escort and I won't be needing a rescue. Log the points to this token when I bring proof."

The clerk shut his mouth, and stamped the ticket.

Lin Hao walked out into the Shrek afternoon with the job folded into his inner pocket, and stood a moment on the steps of the administration building with the sun warm on his face and the weight of a real fight — the first one in a year he would not have to hold back through to its end — sitting in his chest like the first breath of cold air in the foundry.

He had his craft registered. He had his work chosen. And tomorrow, or the day after, in the wild country beyond the ranges where there were no clipboards and no classmates and no headmaster's frost, he would finally let the margin go and find out exactly how much of him there was.

He started walking toward the foundry to tell Wulin — if Wulin was still in one piece under the lion-man's table — and to borrow a better hammer for the road.

## Part 1: Three of Everything

The morning after the worst night ran on breakfast, the way mornings do.

"I'll stop mocking you in the future" had needed explaining, and the explaining had gone the way of all Gu Yue's explanations, which was that Xie Xie ended up worse off and no wiser.

"What do you mean?" he eyed her, with suspicion.

"I guess you can be considered a male."

"Are you saying you treated me like I was a girl before?"

"You can take it however you want."

Xu Xiaoyan latched on to his arm, beaming. "So we're actually a group of sisters! I wouldn't have known if big sis Gu Yue didn't tell me."

"You girls are always bullying me!" He wrested his arm free, lips pinched, a man making a strategic withdrawal from a war he had lost years ago. "I bet you don't have what it takes to mess with Wulin. Oh, whatever. I'm hungry now. You two guard him while I get some breakfast. Do you want me to bring anything back?"

Gu Yue nodded. "I want meat, steamed buns, juice, and yogurt. Oh right, some vegetables too."

Xu Xiaoyan counted on her fingers. "I want some bread, jam, yogurt, and a fried egg. Actually, make that two. Yeah, I'm still growing after all."

"And the captain's," Lin Hao said, before Xie Xie reached the door. "Three of everything. He came off the first seal hungry once; this one ran three days. The number's a number. Bring it."

Xie Xie looked at him, then at the washroom door, then did the arithmetic himself and went pale. "Three of *everything*," he repeated, solemn, a man accepting a logistics assignment, and was gone before Xiaoyan could erupt — "Yeah, growing as round as a fried egg!" rattling down the stairwell after him, timed to miss her.

The pulses had run sixteen seconds all night. Sixteen meant *today*, and the room was arranged for it — and the knock came before Xie Xie did.

"He's back so quickly?" Xu Xiaoyan mumbled, going to the door — and opened it on the white-haired lady from yesterday.

"Oh, it's you. Auntie, where's Teacher Wu?"

"Your teacher is fine. He's just taking care of some matters for you all at the academy right now. Has your captain awakened yet?"

"Not yet."

A sigh escaped Shen Yi's lips. "Then all we can do is wait. When he's conscious, I'll bring you all over."

"But it's already too late!" Xiaoyan said, confused — and Shen Yi smiled faintly and tousled her hair, exactly the way a white-robed man had tousled hers in an ancient street the day before, a gesture handed down like a coin.

"Don't worry about it. Your teacher has already settled things. Your tests might be a bit harder, but you still have one last chance. We'll depart once your captain is awake."

"Really? That's awesome! Teacher Wu is the best!" — and Xiaoyan bounced, and the bounce was honest, and under it the room reorganized itself around a fact nobody had dared to plan for: both. They had given up the exam for their captain, and the exam was coming anyway, harder.

Gu Yue stared at Shen Yi in surprise, and a heavy mountain came off her face, and Lin Hao looked away from the lifting, because some weights were private coming off, too.

## Part 2: The Hall

In the washroom, the water had gone lukewarm, and the gold had thickened, and inside the sleeping boy a door he had been three days in opening finally stood open.

"Have I succeeded?"

The mystical hall was as it always was, and the old man in it was not. Old Tang stood near him in a flash of light, clearer than before — more human, somehow, as if something spent had been refunded.

"That's right, you've succeeded," he said, chock-full of satisfaction. "How do you feel?"

"Good. It wasn't as painful as the first time."

"That's because you're more prepared this time. Both your body and soul power have made remarkable progress in the last three years, especially since your soul power is compressed by your new cultivation method. It has refined your meridians and increased the load that they can bear. You also found high-quality spirit items this time. All of these factors eased the process."

The second had been easier than the first — boiled alive, and then accustomed, and then nothing but a vast filling. Then the shattering, jaws closing — and through it, this time, no brink of bursting at all. A hollow image of a pain he had already survived once.

"Old Tang, what changed with my body this time? I remember back then you said there would be a nice surprise for me!"

"That's right, there's a surprise. But you need to go experience it for yourself. I suggest you don't worry about it for now and instead focus on breaking the third seal in the near future."

"The third seal." Tang Wulin laughed, doing the arithmetic out loud. "Isn't the deadline when I'm twenty? I still have six years — five plus fifteen is twenty years old!"

Old Tang arched a brow at him. "Who said the deadline is twenty years old for the third seal? No, that's not how it's calculated. Your next seal needs to be broken by the time you turn sixteen. In other words, you have three years left. The countdown for the next seal starts immediately after you've broken the previous one."

"Are you telling me the time between seals is going to continue shrinking?"

"That's right. It shortens each time. Your preparation time will shorten by three months each time. After the ninth seal, you will only have one year to prepare for each. You'll be under a lot of pressure from now on."

Eighteen seals. Windows shrinking, seal over seal, down to a single year — and a tribulation for the unready, the old man said, and left the sentence exactly as unfinished as it needed to be. The list came, and Tang Wulin checked the prefixes of the spirit items that rose in his mind against it, and was relieved past all decency to find them thousand-year things. If they had been ten-thousand-year items, he would have just given up right then and there.

"You can go now. Continue working hard."

The world melted.

## Part 3: Seventeen Seconds

Cold.

His eyes shot open on cooled bathwater and daylight, and the first thing his body reported was not power, not gold — it was hunger, visceral, clawing, from the pits of a stomach that had been three days on fire. *How many days has it been?*

He climbed out, dried, dressed, in a hurry to stave it off — and opened the washroom door and nearly jumped out of his skin. Instead of Teacher Wu, the room held five people, one of them an unfamiliar white-haired woman.

"You're finally awake!" Xie Xie leapt at him.

"So I woke up in time?" His hopes rose on the sight of them — there should still be time, otherwise his teammates wouldn't be here, otherwise —

"What do you mean 'in time'?" Xie Xie grumbled. "We're already late."

*"Huh?"* — so shocked he forgot the hunger.

"Let's go." Shen Yi stood, giving him a meaningful look, and walked out; and Xiaoyan beamed at him, and Gu Yue got up with an apathetic face whose eyes were doing more than the face, concern standing in them openly, and Wulin turned to Xie Xie. "Who is that?"

"She's a friend of Teacher Wu's from Shrek. We're already three hours late; Teacher Wu is helping us out at the academy right now, so we need to hurry over, otherwise we might miss our chance."

Three hours. Tang Wulin slumped, and his head went down, and the dominoes fell in front of him one by one, all in a row, all with his fingerprints on them. His stinginess. The cheap room, far from the academy. The meditation he had let run. His teammates, holding the line at a landing and then at a doorway for him. A once-in-a-lifetime opportunity, leaking out of the hourglass. And all this, just for the sake of saving money.

"I'm sorry. This is all my fault."

Xie Xie chuckled — actually chuckled. "What's the point in talking about it now? When you protected us at the forefront all those times, have we ever said thanks? How's your body? How did your deep meditation go?"

"I don't know either, but I feel great." And he did — strength surging under the hunger, the body a third stronger than the immense thing it had already been, soul power up around twenty-seven or twenty-eight, and something else waiting in him, untested, the surprise an old man had promised. No time to test it. No time even to eat. He asked Xie Xie if there was anything to eat; Xie Xie shook his head; and a bag appeared at his elbow, held out in silence.

Steamed buns, and meat. He knew by the weight before he looked up, and he knew by the hand, and he did not thank her, because there are bonds — life-and-death, forge-and-fire, hand-stack — where thanks is a unit too small to transact in. Three buns and a portion of meat — and then the rest of the bag, because somebody had ordered three of everything, and the somebody was not sorry.

He finished it at a speed that would have made the continent's competitive eaters file a grievance.

And it was there, with the captain upright and fed and the shame hanging off him like a wet coat, that Lin Hao said the thing he had been carrying since a room two days ago, where a teacher with a file had asked him what the academy was worth and he had told her to ask the cards.

"I don't care about Shrek Academy."

The room went quiet. Even Xie Xie.

"Shrek Academy only helps me achieve things I should anyway — a few years earlier. That's all it is to me. A shortcut." He said it flat, the way he said numbers. "So don't think we just lost everything. We're good enough. We were good enough in a yard in Eastsea with nobody's permission, and we're good enough now — we walked a five-hand shape at midnight and it held." A breath. "Academies change their rules."

He tipped his head toward the stairs, and the street, and the sleek dark-green car standing in it — an academy's car, an academy's teacher, sent across a city to wait on five children who had missed the exam by three hours.

"And the academy came himself."

Nobody answered that, because there wasn't an answer that improved it. Wulin looked at him for a second, and something in the slump came back upright, and Xie Xie muttered "he's not wrong" in the tone of a man conceding a point to a ledger. Shen Yi, at the door, said nothing at all — but she had asked the question first, two days ago, and she had just been given the longer answer, and she filed it where she filed things.

The count, for the record, had ended at seventeen seconds — the last interval Lin Hao ever timed through that gap, unfinished, mid-number, taken with his hands full, because he was stacking the third portion's wrappings when the washroom lock clicked and the captain came out under his own steam.

## Part 4: Five Papers

The car was everything a soul car should be, and Shen Yi drove it the way Wu Zhangkong taught class: fast but smooth, reactions precise, no unnecessary movement, full speed displayed like a fact being stated.

"Get in." Xie Xie tried the back and was promptly dragged into the middle of it by Gu Yue, who took the front seat beside Lin Hao herself — Xiaoyan at one window laughing into her hand, Wulin at the other, Xie Xie between them, aggrieved. *Too controlling,* he cursed silently. *Hogging the front seat all to herself.* Nobody in the car was deceived by anybody's face.

A juice box appeared against Lin Hao's arm at the second traffic bridge. He looked at it, then at her — she was already watching the road as if the hand were not hers — and drank it, and happiness flashed through Gu Yue's eyes before she closed them to rest; her shoulder settled against his arm, cool against warm, and the back seat had the manners not to report it.

Then the woman driving the academy's car used an hour of enclosed road the way teachers do.

"The letters never said how the fourth ring went black," Shen Yi said, eyes on the traffic, voice level. "A ten-thousand-year ring at your age. The hall will want to know. I'm asking first."

"It was offered," Lin Hao said. "The metal was real. I didn't take it lightly and I didn't take it alone."

"And the footwork they keep writing to each other about."

"Is footwork."

She let three blocks go by. "Show me the sword after the exam."

"That," he said, "is the first thing anyone from Shrek has asked for that I actually want to give."

Shen Yi's eyes ticked once to her mirror — the boy in the front seat read as sixteen until he opened his mouth, and she had stopped correcting her own count of him two days ago — and went back to the road.

The city ran past the windows and did not end. The tin rode where it had always ridden, and he caught himself doing the thing three years had built into him — reading crowds for a height, a braid, a way of standing — and put it away, and it did not stay put for long. It never did, in new cities. This one was just the biggest thing it had ever not found her in.

Within the hour, the walls. They towered beyond vision, plain and undecorated, without even a signboard — an enormous dragon coiled around the academy, sleeping with its eyes open. Outside the front gate, a sea of people, the continent's cream pressing at the continent's standard. Shen Yi did not even slow down: off to the side, seven or eight turns, a small gate that opened at a button she pressed without lifting her wrist and closed behind them, and the outside world closed with it. Peace. Tranquility. An era long gone, kept.

"Are we in Shrek Academy now?" Xie Xie asked.

"This is Shrek Academy's inner city, built for the sake of the academy, so this is the core of the city as well as where students study. Only those recognized by the academy are permitted to enter Shrek Inner City." The car did not slow one bit as she explained. And then, evenly, driving fast through a world ten thousand years deep, she gave them the price:

"Because you were late, your exam will be harder than normal. You'll be tested on the same things, but will have less time to finish and might not even have a chance to rest throughout the entire process. Furthermore, if you do pass, you'll only be working students."

"Working students? What's that?" Tang Wulin asked.

"It means you'll have to pay for the costs to attend Shrek by yourself."

"Huh?" Dread gripped Tang Wulin at the mere mention of spending money — full-body, ancestral, immediate. And then the guilt did its own arithmetic: "The quotas were four," he said, mostly to his knees. "There are five of us. That's my fault too —"

"No." Shen Yi's voice did not raise and did not soften. "The quota arithmetic died when your teacher walked through that gate on his knees. Five of you are sitting in this car; the halls have five papers. Harder papers, drawn last night, for names the examiners were already holding. *That* is what the apology bought. Understand it before you carry it."

Nobody said anything to that either. It was one thing to give up an academy for each other; it was another, quieter thing to learn what had been paid for the papers in your hands.

"You're not paying the school money," she went on. "You're working for the school to pay for your tuition. Do you think Shrek Academy is short on money? Normal students all attend for free."

*Work.*

It landed on Tang Wulin like a door opening — he had never once backed down from hardship in his life; his mood lifted the moment the word arrived. It landed on Lin Hao differently, in a different part of the car, and stayed there, glowing quietly: a smith ranked Master Craftsman at twelve did not hear *work for the school* and feel dread; he heard a forge somebody else paid the charcoal for, in a city ten thousand years deep in metal. Whatever the school was short of, he was long in it. He looked out the window and did not say any of it — yet.

Xie Xie's face had gone sour, and the sourness tumbled straight out of his mouth:

"What work?"

Nobody answered. The inner city flowed past, ancient, green, and somewhere in its core a white-robed man was waiting who had knelt for thirteen of those years to buy this ride, and five children were being driven toward five harder papers, and the last thing Lin Hao wrote that day, at a stop that lasted eleven seconds, was the line the morning had left him.

*He woke. We're good enough. The academy came himself.*

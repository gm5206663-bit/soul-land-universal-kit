## Part 1: What the Mirror Said

He noticed it on the Tuesday morning, washing his face, the way you notice a thing you have stopped seeing.

The lock at his left temple had gone **storm-gray.** Not gray the way hair goes gray. Gray the way the underside of a cloud is gray, with a sheen to it, and when he dried his face the towel came away faintly damp even though his hair was dry, because there was a breeze in it. There had been a breeze in it for weeks. He had simply stopped feeling it against his own skin.

He turned his forearm over.

The marks were there, as they had been since the winter — faint, the colour of a healed scratch, running from the wrist to the elbow in the pattern a feather makes when it is pressed flat. Permanent now. He had forgotten that they used to fade.

And when he put his hand on the iron basin, the iron tasted of rain.

Lin Hao stood in front of a cracked mirror in a dormitory and understood two things at once. The first was that he had changed a great deal in four years and had not once looked. The second, which was worse, was that **everybody else had stopped looking too.** They had watched him turn into this, one degree at a time, the way you watch a tide come in — and now he was a boy with storm-gray hair and feather-marks on his arms and a breeze in his head, and nobody said anything about it any more, because it had simply become what he looked like.

"Well," he said, to the mirror. "That's not nothing."

The mirror did not argue.

## Part 2: Wulin

Wulin was on the dormitory steps with two bowls of congee, and had been sitting there long enough that his had gone cold, which was how Lin Hao knew he had been waiting rather than eating.

"You've been out," Wulin said.

"I've been in the forge."

"You've been in the forge for four hours and you came out and walked past me." Wulin held out the second bowl. "I said good morning."

Lin Hao took the bowl. He sat down. He did not immediately say anything, because there was a version of this conversation where he apologised and a version where he made a joke, and he had been doing the second one for about three weeks without noticing.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I walked past you."

"I know." Wulin was looking at the courtyard and not at him, which was how he did the serious things. "It's not one time. It's been a while. And I'm not saying it to make you feel bad, I'm saying it because you're my brother and I have noticed that I have stopped being in your day, and I don't know whether that's me or you or the forge."

"It's me."

"I thought it might be." Wulin finally looked at him. "So tell me something."

"Something."

"Something true. You've been carrying something since the examination and you've been carrying it alone, and I can tell because you've started being pleasant to people, and you are never pleasant when you're worried." He stirred his cold congee. "So. Something true."

Lin Hao looked at his brother.

There had been a night, four months ago, when Wulin had told him everything — the gold, the scales, the thing under the skin that neither of them had a word for, all of it, sitting on a floor at two in the morning with his arm bare and his voice flat. He had given all of it away, and Lin Hao had taken it, and neither of them had mentioned it since, and Lin Hao understood now that he had been treating that night as a favour Wulin did once instead of a thing that was supposed to keep happening.

"There's a file about me," Lin Hao said. "Not from the Association. Somebody sent the Association a copy as a courtesy, which the man from the head office told me is not a thing that happens, and he read it twice, and he came down on a Saturday to watch me hit metal because he wanted to know whether it was describing a person or a rumour." He ate a mouthful of congee. "He said it was describing a person."

Wulin was quiet for a while.

"Is it accurate."

"Yes."

"Then what's the problem."

"The problem is that I don't know what's in it." Lin Hao put the bowl down. "He wouldn't tell me. He said the moment I know exactly what people believe about me I'll start managing it, and the moment I start managing it I'll stop being the thing they were interested in, and he was right, and I have thought about nothing else for two days."

Wulin nodded slowly.

"You want to know what I think," he said.

"Yes."

"I think you're doing the thing you always do, which is treating yourself as information." Wulin said it without any heat at all, which made it land. "There's a file. Fine. There's going to be a file about you for the rest of your life, because you're you, and there's going to be a file about me for the rest of mine, because of what's under my skin, and neither of us is going to be allowed to be a person who just walks down a street. So you can spend your life wondering what's in it, or you can decide that you know what's in it, because you're the one who's in it."

"That's a very Wulin thing to say."

"I've been paying attention to you for four years," Wulin said. "It's the only thing I'm better at than you."

## Part 3: What It Is Not

Wu Zhangkong was in the yard, doing nothing, which was the only way he ever did nothing: standing very still, with his hands behind his back, looking at a wall.

"It did it again," Lin Hao said.

Wu Zhangkong did not turn around. "Where."

"The forge. Alone. On a piece of scrap." Lin Hao put his hands in his pockets, because he had noticed that he was opening and closing them. "Tuesday. No wolves. No examination. No audience of any kind, which I thought was going to be relevant, and turns out not to be. It just happened, and I've had two days to work out why, and I can't, and you told me to come and find you when I couldn't."

Now he turned around. And his eyes went to the temple, the way everybody's eyes did and then didn't.

"Say it properly," Wu Zhangkong said.

"I cannot account for it."

"How many times."

"Three. The forest. The examination. Tuesday." Lin Hao took his hands out of his pockets. "And I want to be precise, because you've been precise with me and I'd like to return the favour. I did not ask for it. I have never once decided to use it. The first time I thought it was the pressure and the second time I thought it was the audience and the third time there was no pressure and no audience and it happened anyway, so both of my explanations are wrong and I have run out of explanations, and I have been carrying a thing inside me for four months that does things when it wants to, and I would like to know whether I should be frightened."

Wu Zhangkong looked at him for a long time.

"No," he said. "But first sit down, and I want you to look at your arm while I talk, because you have stopped looking at yourself and I have noticed."

## Part 4: Neither

"It is not the soul," Wu Zhangkong said, once they were sitting. "I want to start there, because it's where everybody starts and it's wrong, and if you leave here believing your spirit soul is doing this you will spend the next ten years trying to negotiate with a bird."

"It's not the hawk."

"The hawk is nine hundred and thirty-odd years old and it is, whatever else it is, an *animal.* It does not plan. It does not wait. It does not decide to help you on a Tuesday." He said it flatly, the way he said the weather. "Animals do not have intentions, and what you are describing has the shape of an intention, which is why you are frightened, and which is why you are wrong."

"Then what has the shape of an intention and isn't one."

"You do." Wu Zhangkong looked at him. "Not the part of you that decides. The other part. The part that has been running your eyes for four years without consulting you about it — the part that reads a man's shoulder a half-beat before his hand moves and does not tell you it's doing it, because telling you would take longer than the half-beat." He let that sit. "You have been calling it a talent. It isn't a talent. A talent is something you have. This is something that *runs,* and it has been running since before you were born, and the lightning is simply the newest thing it has learned to operate."

Lin Hao thought about that for a while.

"That's worse," he said, at last.

"Why."

"Because if it's the hawk I could feed it or starve it or make it a deal with. If it's *me* — the part of me underneath the part that decides — then I can't put it down, and I can't negotiate with it, and I can't even find out what it wants, because it doesn't want anything, it's just *doing,* and I'm going to spend my whole life being the person it happens to."

"Yes," Wu Zhangkong said. "That is exactly right, and it is the correct thing to be frightened of, and you are the first person I've met who got there in four months instead of forty years." He stood up. "Now. Gift or debt. I said I'd decide, and I've decided."

"It's neither," Wu Zhangkong said.

"You said you'd decide between them."

"I said I'd decide whether we were dealing with one or the other, and I've found out we're not." He began to walk, which meant the next part was the part he had thought about longest. "A gift is something given to you by someone, which implies a giver, and there isn't one — nobody handed you this, it grew, the way a hand grows. A debt is something you owe for something you took, and you didn't take anything, you were *made.* So there's no giver and no creditor, and the reason I wanted to know which it was is that both of them have a way of being paid, and I wanted to tell you what to do."

"And now."

"Now I have to tell you something less useful, which is that there's nothing to do." Wu Zhangkong stopped. "It isn't a gift you can be grateful for and it isn't a debt you can settle, and the reason that's less useful is that human beings are extremely good at carrying gifts and extremely good at carrying debts and completely useless at carrying *weather.* You can't pay it off. You can't give it back. You can only learn what it does and stand where it's useful."

"That's a very unsatisfying answer."

"It's the true one, and I've noticed you prefer those even when they cost you." Wu Zhangkong turned to face him. "So here is the part that is actually useful, and it's the only thing I'm going to tell you to do. You've been treating it like a thing that happens *to* you. Stop. It isn't weather in that sense — it's *yours,* the way your eyes are yours, and you don't say your eyes happened to you. So the work isn't controlling it. The work is finding out what it's been trying to do, and getting out of the way."

Lin Hao opened his mouth, and stopped, because something had arrived while Wu Zhangkong was talking, and it had arrived from underneath, which was, he noticed, exactly where the answer had come from every single time this month.

"It's been reaching," he said.

"What?"

"The three times. I've been trying to work out what they have in common and I've been looking at the conditions — pressure, audience, no audience — and they don't have anything in common, so I was looking in the wrong place." He was talking faster than he meant to. "The forest: I couldn't see the wolf, I was looking at Xie Xie, and my arm did something at a thing I wasn't looking at. The examination: I was making an edge and the lightning went into the *hammer* and out the other side, into the steel, and it made the edge keener than I drew it. And Tuesday: a bead, on scrap, on the far side of the piece, where I was *about* to go." He looked up. "It isn't protecting me. It's never once been about protecting me. It's **reaching** — it goes at the thing I can't get to yet. The wolf I couldn't see. The edge I couldn't draw keen enough. The far side of a bar."

Wu Zhangkong was quiet for a moment.

"That," he said, "is a considerably better answer than the one I was going to give you, and I'd like it understood that I gave you the question." He looked at the marks on Lin Hao's forearm, and said, after a pause, "And for what it's worth — you have nine hundred and forty-odd years in each of two rings, and your body has been taking what that gives for four years, and you have never once in your life gone all out against anything that could measure it. You are not stuck at thirty. You are *coiled.* The rank is a number on a certificate. Everything under it is still climbing, and it has been climbing this whole time, and you have not been looking."

## Part 5: Reaching

He tried it that night, and it did not work, and then — because he had learned something this month about trying — he stopped trying in the way he had been trying.

He went down to the harbour wall at about ten, where there was nobody, and he put a nail on a post thirty feet away, and he stood with the Stormbringer in his hand and he did not decide to do anything at all. He looked at the nail. Not at the sword. Not at his arm. At the nail, which was thirty feet away and could not be cut from where he stood by anything he knew how to do.

Nothing happened.

He stood there for perhaps a quarter of an hour, and the harbour did what the harbour does, and at some point he stopped standing *at* the nail and simply wanted — not decided, wanted, the way you want a thing you have already given up on getting — to be able to reach it.

The blue went out of the sword.

He did not see it go. That was the part he would keep turning over for years: there was no line, no flash, no sound, no arc. There was the sword in his hand and there was the nail on the post, and then the nail was in two pieces on the ground, and the sword was a sword again, and the whole of it had taken no time at all and had used no method he could describe.

And on his forearms, in the dark, the feather-marks went faintly **bright** — the same blue, for the same instant — and went out again.

Lin Hao stood on the harbour wall for a long moment.

Then he walked over and picked up both halves of the nail, and looked at the cut, and the cut was **clean** — cleaner than anything he had ever made with a Gale Talon, cleaner than the examination bar, clean the way a thing is clean when it was never touched at all.

"Right," he said, to the water.

And then he laughed, because he had been frightened for four months, and frightened properly, in the way he had not been frightened by wolves or bears or a man with a card in his pocket, and the thing he had been frightened of had been *his own arm reaching for things,* and it turned out that what it wanted was not to hurt anybody. It wanted to *get there.* It had been trying to get places for four months and he had spent the whole time asking whether it was going to kill him.

## Part 6: Told

He told Xie Xie the next morning within about ninety seconds, because that was the arrangement between them and it had never once needed discussing.

They were in the yard with the rest of them, which was the other thing he had stopped doing, and Xie Xie was leaning on a post with his arms folded and one boot up on the bottom rail, and Wang Jinxi was standing very still six feet away the way he stood everywhere now, and Zhang Yangzi was sitting on the wall with his wrist still wrapped, and Wei Xiaofeng was pretending not to be listening from the far side of the yard with an efficiency that fooled nobody.

"It goes at things," Lin Hao said. "That's all it does. It doesn't protect me, it *reaches.* Thirty feet. A nail. Cut clean and I didn't do it."

"You didn't do it," Xie Xie repeated.

"I didn't do it."

"And you're telling me this in front of everyone."

"I'm telling you this in front of everyone because I've spent three weeks telling nobody anything and it turned me into a person I don't like." Lin Hao shrugged. "So. Everyone. It reaches. I can't summon it. It happens when I want something I can't get and stop deciding to get it."

There was a pause.

"That's the stupidest thing I've ever heard," Xie Xie said, with enormous satisfaction, "and it explains *everything* about you, and I have been saying for four years that you are not a normal person and everybody kept telling me I was being dramatic."

"You have been saying that."

"I have been *saying* it." Xie Xie pushed off the post. "Right. So. It reaches. Can it reach a person."

"I don't know."

"Have you tried."

"No."

"Good," said Xie Xie. "Don't."

From six feet away, Wang Jinxi spoke, which he had not done in a while, and his voice had the flat quality it had acquired since the platform.

"It reaches because you cannot," he said.

Everybody looked at him.

"You cannot reach," Wang Jinxi said. "You can read anything in a room in half a second and you can see a strike before the man decides to throw it and you cannot cross thirty feet of empty air, and it does it for you." He turned his right hand over, slowly, and the fingers did not quite straighten, and he did not seem to notice, and everybody else noticed. "I know what it is to have something do things for you that you did not ask for. Mine does it when I am asleep. It is not a gift and it is not a debt and it is not yours either. It is just the thing that is happening to you, and the sooner you stop asking it what it is, the sooner it will start being useful."

Lin Hao looked at him for a long moment.

"That's the second best answer I've had this week," he said.

"Who gave you the first."

"My brother." Lin Hao said it easily, and was surprised by how true it felt to say it out loud. "He told me to stop treating myself as information."

Wang Jinxi considered that.

"He's right," he said. "He's usually right. It's extremely annoying."

On the wall, Zhang Yangzi said, without looking up from his wrapped wrist, "For what it's worth, I'd like to be told things too. I have been sitting on this wall for three weeks and nobody has told me anything and I have been extremely curious about all of you."

"I've been telling you things," Wei Xiaofeng said, from the far side of the yard.

"You've been talking *at* me."

"That's telling."

"That's the opposite of telling," said Zhang Yangzi, and Gu Yue — who had come out into the yard at some point and was standing in the doorway with her arms folded, and whom nobody had seen arrive — said, without moving, "He's right. You talk at people. It's your worst quality."

Wei Xiaofeng opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it.

"I'm going to go and be offended somewhere else," he said, and did not go anywhere, and stayed for another hour.

## Part 7: The One Who Did Not Arrive

Gu Yue stayed after the others went in.

She did not sit down, because she did not sit down, and she looked at the lock of storm-gray hair at his temple for long enough that he became aware of it, which was a new experience for him, because he had spent the morning deciding that nobody looked at it any more.

"That's new," she said.

"It's not."

"No," Gu Yue agreed. "It isn't. That's the interesting part. I've watched it happen for two years and I never once said so, and I don't know why I'm saying so now." She was still looking at it. "It's the colour of the sky before it decides."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's ever said about my hair."

"It wasn't nice. It was accurate." She unfolded her arms. "I was injured in the forest. I was carried out by somebody else's decision, and I woke up in a room, and nobody told me what had happened, and you were standing at the end of the corridor for about ten minutes and then you left, and you have not said one word to me about it in nine days."

Lin Hao opened his mouth.

"Don't," Gu Yue said. "I'm not angry. I want you to understand that. I'm telling you because I have spent nine days working out whether you left because there was nothing to say or because you were frightened, and I couldn't decide, and not being able to decide is the only thing I don't like."

"I was frightened," Lin Hao said.

"I thought it might be that."

"I was frightened because you put your arms under a thousand kilograms and you didn't stop to work out whether it would hurt, and I stood at the end of a corridor and I could not think of a single thing to say that wasn't either a lie or a speech, and I have never in my life been able to say a small true thing to you."

Gu Yue looked at him.

"Say a small true thing," she said.

"You're the only page in my ledger with nothing on it."

"I know."

"And I've known for weeks that that isn't because there's nothing there. It's because you're the only person I've met who reads me faster than I read them, and I have spent four years being the person who reads first, and I don't know how to be the other one." He stopped. "That's small and true and I'd like to stop now."

"All right," Gu Yue said. And then, after a moment, "It's the colour of the sky before it decides," she said again, about the hair, and went inside, and Lin Hao stood in the yard for a while and thought that he had said one small true thing and had not died, which was a genuinely surprising result.

## Part 8: What He Did Not Say

There was one thing he did not say to Wu Zhangkong, and he thought about it on the walk back, and he thought about it again for a week, and he never did say it.

The hawk had a question. It had had one for four years — not in words, because it was not a thing that used words, but in the particular way it went quiet sometimes and turned its head and waited, the way an animal waits at a door it has learned to open. He had never been able to work out what it was asking, and he had stopped asking himself, because there had been a great deal going on.

But on the harbour wall, when the blue went out of the sword and the nail came apart at thirty feet, he had felt — for perhaps a second, and he would never be able to prove it, and he was not going to claim it to anybody — that the waiting had stopped.

Not that the question had been answered. That the *waiting* had stopped, the way it stops when somebody finally gets up and goes and looks.

*Is that what you were asking,* he thought, standing on the wall with two halves of a nail in his pocket. *Not whether I can see. Whether I can **reach.***

He did not say it out loud. He had learned this month that the true things were worth saying and that the *almost* true things were worth keeping, and this was an almost true thing, and it was his, and he was going to carry it until he could prove it or drop it.

The hawk, nine hundred and forty-two years old, did not comment.

But it was not waiting.

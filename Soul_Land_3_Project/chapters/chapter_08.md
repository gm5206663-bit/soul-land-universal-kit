## Part 1: The Girl in White

She came through the gate from the direction of the academy buildings, in the middle of the afternoon class, which was the first wrong thing about her.

The second was that she was **not wearing a uniform.**

She was dressed in **white** — plain white, travel white, the white of somebody who had come a long way and had not stopped to change — and she was the same age as every first-grader on the field, and she was not, Lin Hao noticed before he decided to notice it, particularly beautiful. She was delicate. She was pretty. She had long black hair and black eyes and an average build and bright eyes that were full of something. And she walked with a rhythm to it, a particular spacing between her steps, and her whole manner had a feeling to it that Lin Hao's hawk read as *foreign* before his brain had any word for it.

Wu Zhangkong had seen her too, because Wu Zhangkong saw everything, and he had stopped the roster.

"What class are you in?"

"I'm here to attend school," the girl said. "However, I was told the registration period has already passed."

"You're not an Eastsea Academy student?"

"I'm not a student at the moment." She said it the way you'd read out a fact about the weather. "But I would still like to be one. Is that possible, teacher?"

Any other teacher on that field would have sent her to the administration building. Lin Hao watched Wu Zhangkong decide, and could not afterwards have said what the deciding looked like.

"This is a Soul Master's academy," Wu Zhangkong said. "If you want to attend here, you must have the ability."

"I can take a test."

"Your name."

"**Gu Yue.**"

Wu Zhangkong nodded once. Then he turned around and walked back across the field to Xie Xie, who had been listening with the expression of a boy watching something excellent happen to somebody else.

"Give your metal shirt to Tang Wulin. Come here."

Xie Xie gave the shirt. Wulin put it on without being asked, which told Lin Hao more about the last five weeks than anything anybody had said.

"Your physical strength is horrible," Wu Zhangkong told Xie Xie.

"We Agility System Battle Soul Masters only need a burst of power and some nimbleness," Xie Xie said. "We don't need—"

"**How shallow.**" Wu Zhangkong said it without heat, which was the worst way to say it. "Fine. I'll give you a chance today. **Defeat her and you won't have to run this afternoon.**" He looked at the sky. "Twenty minutes' rest. Then we begin."

---

The field had gone completely quiet, which was the only register in which class five ever expressed awe.

Wu Zhangkong gave Xie Xie twenty minutes' rest, and then he let him find out.

---

She was given a uniform, a key, and the back-left desk under the bad window, behind Tang Wulin.

She sat there from the first day and never once explained why.

## Part 2: The Field

Tournament training had turned the first-grade sports field into a place with weather of its own.

Class five owned the far end — not by assignment, but by the quiet arithmetic of the last month, in which nobody else wanted the ground where the worst class did its worst. Wu Zhangkong stood at the center of it with his hands behind his back, running the roster in pairs, and today the pairs meant what everyone had been waiting for: Lin Hao and Wulin, the two furnaces, sparring properly for the first time since the fence incident.

It did not look like sparring. It looked like two different disasters negotiating.

Wulin came in the way he came at everything — directly, with the whole forged weight of him, Bluesilver Grass already uncoiling in golden-threaded ropes to own the ground underfoot. Lin Hao did not meet the charge. He *wasn't there* for it, hollow-boned and hawk-read, his body leaving the collision line a half-breath before the collision decided to happen, the Stormbringer answering from an angle the grass had not yet grown into.

The hawk inside him was working harder than he was. That had been true for weeks now — seven hundred and fifty years of sharpened instinct marking the fight *ahead* of him: there, Wulin's weight committing; there, the grass's third rope feinting left to root right. Lin Hao stopped fighting the head start and started trusting it, the way you trust a hand on your shoulder in the dark, and the match climbed into a register neither of them had reached before — until Wulin's fourth rope finally caught his ankle and his wind-blade finally skidded off Wulin's crossed forearms at the same moment, and they called it even by mutual breathlessness.

"Again," said Xie Xie, from the sideline, where he had been drilling his light-step patterns with the industry of a convert. "Again, and this time Wulin, use the low ropes first, he hates the low ropes—"

"You've been counting my ropes," Wulin said, pleased.

"I've been counting *everyone's* everything," Xie Xie said, with dignity. "It's my whole personality now."

Yun Xiao, passing with two waterskins, murmured, "It's been his whole personality for a week," and was awarded the rare Xie Xie speechless-face.

Zhou Zhangxi laughed like a rockslide. Wu Zhangkong's mouth did not move, which the class had learned to log as amusement. And from beyond the field fence, where the road curved up from the academy gate — a place no student ever stood, because there was nothing there to stand for — a girl was watching.

She had been watching for perhaps ten minutes. Small, unremarkable at fence-glance distance: dark hair pulled back, plain clothes, the particular stillness of someone waiting. Two second-graders walking past gave her a wide berth without seeming to notice they were doing it.

Lin Hao noticed. Not her — that was the strange part. He noticed that his hawk had gone *quiet*, the way it went quiet before weather, and when he followed the silence to its source, there was only a girl at a fence, watching class five train, with her hands folded and her face at rest.

*Probably nothing,* he thought, and reset his stance, and the moment passed the way such moments do.

## Part 3

There were a dozen storm-light tips in his hair by then, and the eyes were gold-flecked in strong light, and the transfer students had noticed both within a day, because Eastsea Academy had not had anybody who looked like that before.
: The File

At the break, she came onto the field.

She crossed the dirt the way some people cross rooms — as if the space had agreed to it beforehand — and stopped at the exact distance from Wu Zhangkong that protocol wanted, and said, in a voice that carried without lifting:

"Teacher. I'm here to enroll."

The field had gone quiet in the rippling way fields do. Wu Zhangkong regarded her for a long moment. "Enrollment closed six weeks ago."

"I know."

"The Academy isn't accepting—"

"The Academy accepted my file," she said. "It was sent ahead. Sealed." A pause, level as still water. "I was told to present myself to you."

Something moved behind Wu Zhangkong's ice — not surprise; the recognition of a man who had, in fact, received a sealed file six weeks ago, and had read it once, and had not slept particularly well afterward. He studied the girl: the plain clothes, the folded hands, the dark ponytail, the violet eyes that met his with the patience of deep water.

"So I have," he said at last. "The file says you may enroll. It does not say into which class." He let the silence stand one second, then two. "I'm choosing. Class five." He tilted his head a fraction toward the back knot of students — the worst class, the ground-floor-end-of-the-building class, whose members had stopped looking surprised about anything roughly three weeks ago. "But you'll test first. This Academy doesn't take sealed files on faith. It takes them on demonstration." He turned his head, and his pale green eyes found exactly one person. "Xie Xie."

Xie Xie straightened like a drawn blade. "Me?"

"You've wanted a real opponent since the day you arrived. Here's one." Wu Zhangkong's voice stayed flat as a work order. "Standard test. No rules as to method. Yield, fall, or disarmament ends it. Begin when she's ready."

The class formed its ring before anyone asked it to. Xie Xie rolled his shoulders, set his light-step stance, and — because three losses had burned the arrogance out of his openings and left something better in its place — he did not taunt. He watched. He waited. He let her make the first decision.

She made it by not moving at all.

## Part 4: Six Things at Once

Xie Xie went in at three-quarter speed — a probing line of light, the Light Dragon Dagger tracing an arc meant to herd her toward the soft dirt.

**Fire** answered. Not a wall — a *ribbon*, thin as a whip and exact as a scalpel, laying itself across his arc at the precise height where the dagger's light would have to pass through it or stop. He stopped. The ribbon did not flicker. It hung in the air, patient, the way a rule hangs.

"That's a soul skill," someone whispered. "That's a *fire* soul skill—"

**Water** took his feet. Not a wave — a *film*, beading out of nothing across the packed dirt under his back step, frictionless as oiled glass. Xie Xie's light-step, which needed grip the way birds need air, skated. He saved it — barely — into a sliding half-turn, and the ribbon of fire was already gone, and he was already deciding, fast, the way Wu Zhangkong had drilled him to decide.

*She ranged me. Range is hers. Close it.*

He closed it. Full speed, the yard-blur, the afterimage string that had finally stopped losing to Wulin's hands—

And Lin Hao, who was not in the match, said one thing out loud, quietly, to nobody: "She's not choosing. She's **answering.**"

Xie Xie did not hear him. But the yard did, and the yard repeated it, and by the time the match was over it had gone around the field twice, and it was the only sentence anybody had that described what was actually happening — because Xie Xie was fighting a person and she was fighting a **decision tree**, and the only boy in the yard who thought in decision trees was the one not in the ring.

That is the butterfly. In a yard without him, Xie Xie loses and everybody learns that a girl with six elements is frightening. In a yard with him in it, Xie Xie loses and everybody learns **what she is doing**, which is a different lesson and a much more useful one, and Xie Xie spends the next nine weeks fighting differently because of a sentence said by somebody who wasn't in the match.

**Earth** met him. A pillar the width of a plate rose out of the dirt exactly where his third afterimage was going to land — not where he was, not where he'd been: where he was *going*. Xie Xie's whole body reorganized in a tenth of a second, light flaring, and he vaulted the pillar and came down dagger-first—

—into **wind**.

On the sideline, Lin Hao went still.

He knew wind. Wind was the language his hawk spoke, the weather in his bones, the thing his soul had been folded around since before he was born — and the wind that rose under Xie Xie's descending strike was *wrong.* Not violent. Not wild. It was **obedient** — packed, shaped, held to a purpose the way a blade is held, pressing up against the dagger's light with a control that had nothing of weather in it whatsoever. Wind, to Lin Hao's entire being, was a thing you read and rode and asked. This wind did not ask. It *instructed.*

And in his chest, seven hundred and fifty years of hawk went silent. Not the silence of rest. The silence of a bird that has seen a shape in the sky it has no name for, and has decided, with every feather it has, to watch.

Lin Hao filed the feeling exactly where he filed the golden scale's wreck-water: deep, private, marked. *Later,* he told himself. *Not now. Watch the fight.*

The fight ended the way endings do when one side has been reading a book the other side hasn't opened. **Light** went up — Xie Xie's own element turned against him, a cold flare that blinded without burning, that dazzled the light-step right out of his feet — and then there was no flare, no fire, no water, no earth, no wind at all, and Xie Xie was on his knees in the middle of the ring with his dagger stopped an inch from finishing an arc that had nowhere left to go, and the girl was standing behind him.

Standing behind him. He had never turned. Nobody had seen her move, because — and the class would argue about this until the tournament, in increasingly imaginative theories — at some point in the last exchange, the *distance* between them had simply changed its mind.

**Space.** Six things. One girl. Not a hair of her out of place.

The field was so quiet you could hear the second-graders' footsteps stopping on the road.

Xie Xie stayed on one knee for three breaths. Then he did something none of the three people who had beaten him had ever seen him do — he laughed. Not bitterly. The cracked, delighted laugh of a boy who has just been shown that the world is bigger than his losses.

"What *are* you?" he asked, still kneeling.

"Gu Yue," she said.

"That's not—" Xie Xie got to his feet, dusted himself with both hands, and extended one of them, because three losses had taught him what four apparently taught permanently. "Fine. Fine. Gu Yue. I'm Xie Xie. You're going to have to teach me that last one."

She looked at the hand. For a moment Lin Hao thought she wouldn't take it — she stood in it the way her stillness stood in everything, complete and untouchable — and then she took it, briefly, the way one acknowledges a fact.

"No," she said. Not unkindly. Factually. "You wouldn't survive it."

Wu Zhangkong's voice cut the ring's murmur like a struck anvil. "Test concluded. Enrolled." His pale green eyes rested on her one second longer than protocol needed. "Class five. Their classroom is the ground floor, innermost door. You'll find the company… instructive."

## Part 5: The Desk Behind

The classroom received Gu Yue the way the sea receives a stone: total surface rearrangement, immediate and silent.

There was one empty desk. There had been one empty desk for six weeks, because class five's roster was twenty-two strong and the room held thirty, and nobody sat in the back-left corner under the window where the light came in wrong. Gu Yue crossed to it without being directed — the way she had crossed the field, as if the space had agreed beforehand — and set down a plain satchel, and sat.

The desk behind Tang Wulin.

Xie Xie opened his mouth. Gu Yue looked at him. Xie Xie sat down.

The lesson proceeded. Wu Zhangkong lectured soul-theory at his usual altitude — three sentences' worth of content compressed into one, delivered flat, for the class to unpack later like crates — and somewhere in the middle of it, Wulin found his attention slipping backward.

Not turning. *Slipping.* The particular sideways slide of a mind passing something it half-knows. There was a girl sitting behind him. That was all. A transfer student with violet eyes and six impossible soul skills and a handshake like a stamped document. And yet twice in ten minutes his shoulders had gone careful the way they went careful carrying something fragile and unnamed through the dormitory dark, and both times he'd lost the thought trying to hold it, like a fish in cupped hands.

*Silver,* said nothing at all, somewhere under the thought, and was gone.

He frowned at his notes. Lin Hao, two desks over, caught the frown across the room and filed it, and said nothing, because the frown was carrying the same texture as the golden scale and the fence-quiet: something deep, something private, something marked *later.*

At the bell, the class emptied in its usual detonation. Gu Yue remained seated — one breath, two — and then rose with the desk's whole population around her, and left at the exact middle of the stream, and the stream, without any visible negotiation, made room.

On the walk to the dining hall, Wulin was quiet in a way Lin Hao had learned to sort: this was not grief-quiet. This was *static*-quiet. A receiver picking up something it couldn't tune.

"You're doing the face," Lin Hao said.

"What face."

"The one where your brain's chewing on something it can't swallow." Wulin didn't answer, so Lin Hao offered the honest currency of their friendship: his own. "My wind doesn't like her."

Wulin glanced at him. "Your wind."

"It goes quiet around her." Lin Hao kept his voice in the register of complaint, which was the register truths traveled in between them. "The whole time on the field, it just — laid down. Like before a storm, but there's no storm. Hawks don't do that. Hawks do everything except that."

Wulin was quiet for four more steps. "Sounds like your hawk's scared of a girl."

"Sounds like it," Lin Hao agreed. And then, because the friendship ran on exact weights: "I don't think it's scared *of* her, though. I think it's scared *for* something. I don't know what." He bumped Wulin's shoulder with his own, light as a landing bird. "Come on. I'm hungry enough to eat Zhou Zhangxi's share, and he's already offered it twice, which means he's plotting."

Wulin smiled — half a one, but real — and the static did not clear, but it quieted, the way receivers do when you turn them toward the window.

## Part 6: Weather

That night the roof of dormitory three held its usual parliament: two boys, a city, and the harbor wind coming in off the water with the tide's long argument.

Wulin had his tournament sheet out — the class roster, five names deep in his neat forge-temperature hand: *Tang Wulin. Xie Xie. Zhou Zhangxi. Yun Xiao.* And, added that evening in ink that had paused once before the fourth set of strokes: *Gu Yue.*

"Class five is going to walk into that tournament with a girl who does six elements and a boy who does weather," he said. "Class one's going to have feelings."

"Class one's going to have a bracket problem," Lin Hao said. He was lying back on the tiles, hands behind his head — and if anyone had watched him closely, which nobody was, they would have noticed that he was not, in fact, watching the stars.

He was watching, out of the corner of everything, the far window of the girls' dormitory across the yard, where the new student's lamp had burned for two hours without moving — without a shadow crossing it, without the ordinary coming-and-going of a person inside a room.

And in his chest, the hawk sat. Not slept — *sat*, alert, composed, facing that window with the still and absolute attention of a bird facing weather on the horizon. It had been facing it since the field. It had not, in two hours, once suggested they go to sleep.

*All right,* Lin Hao thought at it, the way he thought at his own hands. *You've made your feelings clear.*

The hawk, being seven hundred and fifty years old and increasingly of the opinion that it was *someone*, did not answer.

It kept watching the weather.

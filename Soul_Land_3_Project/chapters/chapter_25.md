## Part 1: Zhang Yangzi's Bad Luck

He landed on his feet, which surprised him, and then he was sick, which surprised him less.

The forest was enormous. That was the first thing, and it stayed the first thing for some time, because Zhang Yangzi had grown up in a city and had seen trees the way you see furniture, and this was not trees. This was a *world* that had been left running. Insects argued in the undergrowth. Something with a good voice sang three layers up. The air was so full of life that it had a taste, and the taste was wrong for him — he was a dark-attribute soul in a green cathedral, and every breath felt like walking into a room where everyone had stopped talking.

And then he noticed the other thing.

His soul power was moving faster. Not a lot. A little — a warmth at the base of his spine that kept arriving half a beat before he called for it, the way a dog comes when it hears the bowl. The forest was feeding him. The forest that was wrong for him was feeding him, and Zhang Yangzi stood there in the green light and felt, for the first time in four months, that something in the world had been arranged in his favour.

He released his martial soul. Black wings came out of his back and unfolded, and the Shadow Phantasm Eagle settled onto his shoulder, and he put his hands into claws and went up the tallest tree in the area in eleven seconds, because that was the clever thing to do, and he had decided, on the way in, that he was going to be clever this time.

Ten metres up, the view opened, and Zhang Yangzi stopped breathing for a moment.

Green. Green to the horizon in every direction, an ocean of it, treetops rolling like swell under a clear sky. There was no edge. There was no city, no wall, no sea, no smoke. There was just the world with the people taken out of it, and it went on forever, and it was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen, and he understood, standing in it, exactly why Wu Zhangkong had looked at them the way he had.

He would find Wang Jinxi. That was the plan. Their fusion was incomplete — the Shadow Eagle Dragon had never once fully formed, and it was the reason the tournament had gone the way it had gone, and if they could complete it here, where nothing was watching and nobody was keeping score—

A bird came over the treetops.

It was big. Five metres of wingspan, green as new leaves, soaring on nothing at all, and it turned its head — it *turned its head* — and looked directly at the tallest tree in the area, and Zhang Yangzi felt something in his chest that he had been carrying since the tournament, something hot and old and entirely unexamined, stand up and take the wheel.

He had **conceded** three bouts at the tournament — a boy with a hammer, a girl with a cup of water, a swordsman who had read his feint before he finished it — and **not one of them had ever touched him.** He had stepped out of all three, because he had been coordinating, because he had been a team player, because he had spent the entire tournament being careful about what he showed. **Nobody had beaten him. He had simply declined to win, three times, in front of four hundred people, and nobody in the stands had been able to tell the difference — which was the part that had bothered him afterwards, not the losing.**

Not here.

Two soul rings came up under his feet. The Dark Devil Eagle rose off his shoulder and folded into him, and his wings went wide, and his **second soul skill — Eagle Soars the Sky** — took more than half of everything he had in a single breath, and his speed tripled, and he went off the branch like a black arrow straight at the bird.

The bird shrieked. It flapped once, hard, and three green wind blades came off its wings and met him in the air.

He smashed them. That was the good part, and it lasted about a second — black against green, the blades bursting — and then he understood, in the middle of his own triumph, that he had stopped moving.

Eagle Soars the Sky gave him speed. It did not give him *flight.* His wings could glide and they could catch, and they could not hold a boy of ten in the air on their own, and he had traded all his momentum for the pleasure of breaking something, and now he was falling, and the tree canopy was below him, and the canopy was the clever place to be, the bird could not follow him into the trees, he had worked this out, he was *clever*—

The treetops opened.

Not one bird. Not two. Green figures came up out of the leaves in every direction at once, and the sky above him filled with wings, and the cries came from all sides and answered each other, and more came, arriving from further off, joining, and Zhang Yangzi hung in the air at the top of his skill with nothing left in him and watched a hundred birds decide about him at the same time.

He pressed the button on his left wrist.

---

The box slid out and the light came in and Long Hengxu said, "Get up," without a single trace of happiness in it.

Zhang Yangzi sat up. He was in the room with the screens. He had been inside for four minutes and eleven seconds, and he was the first one out, and the screens showed six dots still moving and one that had stopped being a dot, and his face went hot all the way to his ears and he crawled out of the box on his hands and knees.

Wu Zhangkong said nothing at all. He pointed at the chair beside him.

Zhang Yangzi sat down. He was still dizzy — the platform left you that way, he understood, the body catching up with the mind — but everything was perfectly clear, and he looked up at the screens out of simple embarrassment, wanting somewhere to put his eyes.

And then he found the flock.

They were still there. A hundred green birds wheeling over the tallest tree in that sector, crying, searching, and more arriving while he watched, and Zhang Yangzi looked at the place where he had been hanging in the air and did a sum he did not want to do, and got an answer he did not want.

If it had been real, he would have been dead. Not hurt. Not taught a lesson. Dead — and there would not have been enough left of him for ten of him to make a difference.

He looked at Wu Zhangkong, waiting for it. Waiting for the flat voice to say the thing that was obviously true and that he had already said to himself.

Wu Zhangkong looked at the screen, and did not say anything at all.

And Zhang Yangzi understood — slowly, and then all at once — that the man was not going to say it, and was not going to say it *on purpose,* because a thing you are told you forget, and a thing you watch happen to yourself from the inside of your own skin you keep forever, and this was the entire method, and it had been the entire method the whole time.

*True combat experience was best gained when experienced for themselves.*

Zhang Yangzi sat in the chair with his splinted wrist on his knee and his face still burning, and felt something in him — the hot old thing, the thing that had been carrying him since the tournament — go quiet. Not gone. Quiet. And underneath the quiet, to his complete surprise, he found that he was *excited.*

Next time, he thought. Next time I go up the tree and I do not attack the bird. Next time I find Wang Jinxi first. Next time I last an hour.

## Part 2: Xie Xie

Xie Xie had been in the forest for nine minutes and had not made a sound.

He moved from one tree's shadow to the next and did not use the open ground, because he had known since he was four years old that this forest was full of things that would kill him, and because his brother had come home at eleven and talked about a mouse the size of a house for a week, and because Xie Xie had listened to every word of it and had gone to bed every night for seven days with his dagger under the pillow.

He also knew the other thing, and the other thing was why he had asked to go in first.

Everything you killed in here fed the spirit soul you had in the real world.

His spirit soul was a metal soul the Spirit Pagoda had manufactured specially — a hundred-year soul his family had paid a thousand-year price for, because it was the only one of its kind available, and because the money had bought something nobody outside his family knew about. When it fused with his dagger, it had *mutated.* Not much. Not the way Wulin's Goldsong had mutated, which everyone had seen and nobody had explained. A small mutation, a quiet one, and what it did was this: it let him take two spirit souls and make them one, if he had the spiritual power to hold both.

He had two daggers. He had always had two daggers. The Light one was the one everyone had seen, at the tournament, on the display, in every ring he had ever shown, and the Shadow one had never once left his sleeve in front of another person, because his father had sat him down when he was six and explained that a thing nobody knows about is the only thing that is truly yours.

The problem was the bottleneck. Two martial souls meant two rings at every wall, and his spiritual power was — Xie Xie had heard the number, and Xie Xie had made his peace with the number, and Xie Xie had then spent four months being loud about everything else so that nobody would look at the number again. But the platform was a way *around* the number. If the metal soul reached the purple thousand-year level, it would give him a ring by itself. At the black ten-thousand-year level, another.

So Xie Xie moved through the green dark without a sound, and looked for something to kill, and was happier than he had been in his entire life.

The feeling that arrived next was not a sound.

He dived. He did not decide to dive. Some part of him that his father had paid a great deal of money to train simply put him on the ground, and several dozen lights went through the air where his head had been and punched into the tree behind him, and Xie Xie rolled and came up running.

He looked back, and his blood went cold.

Eyes. Dozens of pairs of them, scarlet, opening in the bark of the trees all around him, at every height, patient as fruit.

*Scarlet Demon Tree.* Plant-type. He knew it — plant soul beasts came in groups, they did not chase, they waited, and the ground he was standing on was inside six of them at once.

He did not fight. He rolled sideways and ran, and the trees fired the instant he moved, and the lights stitched the air behind his heels, and Xie Xie went out of that grove at a speed he had never once produced in front of Wu Zhangkong and did not stop until the scarlet eyes were behind him and his lungs were on fire.

"That," he said aloud, to nobody, because talking was how he stayed himself, "was *extremely* close."

Then something yellow came at his face.

He stopped and slashed — a Light Dragon Blade, clean and full-strength, the strike he had practised eleven thousand times — and the yellow thing *twisted* in the air and his blade cut nothing, and the ground burst open two metres in front of him and a mouse came out of it.

It was taller than he was. Two metres of grey fur and muscle, standing on its hind legs, with a tail like a whip that had already flicked once and nearly taken his head off, and a mouth full of teeth that Xie Xie knew, from a book, could chew through uncommon metal.

**A hundred-year Longtail Mouse.**

And Xie Xie, standing in the dappled green light with his heart going like a hammer, felt exactly one emotion, and it was not fear.

"Oh," he said. "*Oh, you're perfect.*"

He kicked off the trunk and went up. The mouse's tail came for his chest like a thrown javelin and he put a Light Dragon Blade in front of it — and a ray of light flashed at the tip of the tail and his blade **split in half** and swept past harmlessly, and the mouse leapt after him with a speed that had no business belonging to something that shape.

Then the mouse shrieked.

A bloody line burst open along its flank and it went down sideways, splattering, and Xie Xie did not waste a single instant of the gift — he came off the trunk spinning, dagger-first, and dropped onto it with everything he had.

## Part 3: The Screen Room

"There were four," the staff member said. "Now there are three."

Wu Zhangkong did not answer. He was looking at the screens, and there were three dots on them, and one of the three dots was moving in a way he had not expected.

The Wang Jinxi dot had stopped.

Not slowed. Stopped — and then it went off the board entirely, without the graceful fade of a pressed button, and the light above box four went from blue to red, and the staff member was already moving.

"Prepare for emergency treatment."

The box slid out and Wang Jinxi was shaking. The electrodes came off him and it did not help; the shaking was underneath the skin, and his face was the colour of the paper on the clipboard, and a dozen people in white arrived at a run and one of them put a needle into his neck without asking anything first.

"Is it that Man-Faced Demon Spider again," somebody said, in the tone of a man who was tired.

"Yes! That thing is so violent. It didn't even give this child a chance to press the button. Ah, this child's luck is truly terrible."

Zhang Yangzi had come to his feet at some point without noticing. He was standing at the edge of the room with his splinted wrist against his chest, and he watched the people in white work on Wang Jinxi for ten full minutes, and then he said, in a voice that did not sound like his, "Teacher Wu. Director Long. Is Jinxi okay? Isn't the spirit ascension platform a virtual place? How did he end up like this…"

His words trailed off. He was looking at Wang Jinxi's face.

The staff member — the young one, the one who had explained the boxes and smiled while doing it — turned around, and the smile was gone, and what was left was a person whose job included this.

"I told you," he said. "The platform is virtual. But it contains a degree of reality. Everything you sense inside there, you sense as if you were feeling it in reality. So if you are harmed inside, you feel all of the pain." He said it evenly, the way you read a manual. "If you had been sliced into a corpse by the wind blades of those big green birds, you would have felt the exact same sensations as a corpse. Exceedingly real. And they attack the mind.

"Simply: if someone watches their own wrist being slit, they feel the blood going. They hear it dripping. And when the wound closes, and the bleeding stops — they still sense the dripping. So the brain decides it is already dead. And after that thought appears in the brain, the person truly begins to die."

The room was very quiet.

"The platform has this problem, especially for first-timers like you. Fortunately, the effects gradually decrease as your spiritual power increases. By the time you can ignore them, you won't be able to enter the elementary platform anymore — you'll have to enter the intermediate one." He shrugged. "And the sensations in the intermediate platform are even more different. Hehe. The intermediate platform…"

Zhang Yangzi sat down, because his legs had made a decision without him.

*This could actually kill us.*

Wu Zhangkong said nothing. He had walked over to the screens, and he was looking at box six, and at the dot on board six, which had not stopped, and had not slowed, and had not — the staff would mention this later, twice — pressed its button once.

## Part 4: Gu Yue

She had been walking for twenty minutes and had almost forgotten what she was.

The forest was doing something to her. Not the circulation — she had felt that, the little warmth at the base of the spine, and filed it — but the *green.* The clean air. The light coming down through the leaves in the particular broken way that light comes down through leaves, and she had walked under light like this before, and the before was not a memory, because memories belonged to a life, and this was older than that.

*So beautiful,* she thought. *I really like this feeling.*

She killed three soul beasts in the first ten minutes, and they were weak, and each time one went down a faint light came off her body and went somewhere she could not follow, and each time it happened her eyes got a little wider, because she had not been told about that part, and she and Tang Wulin had known less about this place than anyone.

She was still thinking about the light when the bush rustled.

It came out on two legs and it was over three metres tall and covered in light brown fur, with claws the length of her forearm and yellow eyes that caught the green light and gave it back wrong. It roared — a sound that came up through the ground and into her teeth — and charged.

---

In the screen room, the staff member half-rose.

"Thousand year," he said. "A thousand-year soul beast appeared. Do you want to warn her?"

"Just watch for now," Wu Zhangkong said. His eyes did not leave the screen.

It was a Crystal Bear — a mutation off the same species as the Fear Claw Bear, and weaker for it, but it had kept one thing and the one thing was enough: every strike it landed left a little of the opponent *crystallised.* A fraction. Then another fraction. The fractions stacked, and the crystallised parts did not bend, and eventually a body that could not bend shattered under the next blow. You beat it with strong soul power and fast hands. You survived it by removing the crystal before the next hit arrived.

It had no ranged attacks at all. That was its weakness.

Wu Zhangkong knew all of this, and he knew the other thing too, which was the only thing he had ever really believed about training, and which he had believed since before he had been asked to leave the place he did not talk about:

**The greater the pressure, the greater the growth of a Soul Master.**

He wanted to see what his only female student would do with a thousand years standing in front of her.

---

Gu Yue was not flustered at all.

A single yellow soul ring rose from beneath her feet and her aura strengthened, and she said, quietly, "Elemental Tide."

Her body went light. A green glow flashed out of her, and then there was a flash of silver, and she was not where she had been.

The Crystal Bear's charge went through empty air. It could not stop. It ran headfirst into a large tree and the tree lost the argument.

Gu Yue was already running, with her back to it, and she did not look behind her, and she waved her hands as she went — and a tornado came up out of the undergrowth and went into the canopy and tore a hundred leaves loose and filled the air between them with green confetti.

It was not an attack. Its defences were far too stout for wind, and she knew that before she cast it. The wind was for the *eyes.*

Behind the leaves, she flashed silver again, changed direction, and bolted.

"Beautiful," Long Hengxu said. "Her response was clever, fast as well."

Wu Zhangkong nodded, once. He was pleased, and the reason he was pleased was not the teleport — the teleport was a tool, and tools were common. It was the *decision.* She had looked at a thousand years of bear, measured it against herself, and chosen to run, and she had done it in an instant, and running was the easy part. Any coward could run. The hard part was knowing, in the first half-second, that running was the correct answer and not a failure — and then doing it, cleanly, without a single wasted movement, while a thing that could shatter her was four metres away.

He turned to the board with the three dots on it, and stopped.

The Gu Yue dot was moving fast. And it was moving in a straight line. And the line it was moving along was—

"Yi," Wu Zhangkong said, very lightly.

## Part 5: Xie Xie, Again

Two booms came through the forest, close, and getting closer.

Xie Xie had been skinning the Longtail Mouse — spiritually, not literally, the light doing what the light did — and the booms put him on his feet instantly. Somebody was making that much noise in a soul beast forest, and whoever it was either very confident or very stupid, and in this forest those were the same thing wearing different hats.

He went up a tree with lightning speed and put his back against the trunk and made himself small and made himself *quiet,* and stayed there, because staying still was the better option. The mouse had cost him. He had less than half of what he had walked in with.

The thicket exploded.

A shape came out of it running — on two legs, over four metres tall, its whole body plated in metallic scales that threw the green light back in hard flat flashes, and one horn growing straight out of its skull.

It was a **Horned Dragon.** It was extremely ferocious. And it was coming directly at Xie Xie's tree.

## Part 6: What the Forest Teaches

Lin Hao landed in a crouch, and the first thing he did was take his hand off the button.

That was not a decision he made. It was a decision his body had already made, because the staff member had leaned over the box and said *first-timers reach with the right,* and because Lin Hao had spent six years of his life converting information into behaviour at a speed that had never once failed him, and his left hand was already resting on the strap while his right hand was empty and free.

The forest came up around him. Green, enormous, loud with insects, and — he noticed it the way he noticed everything, as a fact to be used rather than a thing to be felt — his soul power was circulating faster than it had any right to.

*So that's what it does,* he thought. *It feeds you. That's the trap. It makes you feel good so you'll stay.*

He went up a tree, because he had watched Zhang Yangzi go up a tree in his own head for four minutes while they waited, and because a vantage point was worth more than a plan. Eleven metres up, the forest went to the horizon, and Lin Hao looked at it, and thought about the harbour, and thought that the sea had been the first forest.

Then he heard the pack.

Not saw. Heard — three separate sets of footfalls, out of phase, closing from three directions, and the fourth thing he heard was the absence of a fourth set, which was the most informative sound he had heard all day.

They came into the clearing together, which was the first thing that told him he was in trouble, because wolves did not come into clearings together.

Four of them. Grey, low, ribbed, with the particular lean look of things that ate regularly and not often. They fanned out without being told. They did not charge. Three of them stopped at the treeline and one — the biggest, with a scar running from its left eye down into its shoulder — walked into the open and stood there, and looked at him.

"Right," Lin Hao said. He drew the Stormbringer. "You're the clever one."

The scarred one did not move. And Lin Hao, who had seventy-nine lines in his head and who had never in his life met an opponent he could not read in four seconds, understood with total clarity that he was not being read.

He was being *studied.*

---

He killed the first one in six seconds, and it cost him nothing.

Gale Talon along the steel, the wind-ribbon laid down the length of the blade, and the wolf on his left came in low and fast and met an edge that had been given teeth, and it did not get up. That was the baseline. That was the fight he was supposed to have, and it lasted six seconds, and the other three did not react at all, and the scarred one did not look at the dead one.

It looked at the *blade.*

The second one came in from the right, and Lin Hao laid the Gale Talon again — same angle, same timing, because the timing had worked — and the wolf on the right **stopped.** Not dodged. Stopped, mid-commit, four feet short of the arc, and backed out of it, and waited.

Lin Hao stood in the clearing with the sword in his hand and felt, for the first time in four months, the specific cold sensation of a page in his ledger turning over and being blank on the other side.

*They saw that,* he thought. *Not the one that died. The others. They watched me do it and they learned it.*

The third one came in while he was thinking, which was a lesson of its own, and he took it on the blade and turned it and it went down — and as it went down the fourth came from behind, silent, and its teeth closed on the outside of his left thigh, and the pain was—

The pain was **enormous.** It was not like the stick. It was not like the bruised ribs or the split skin or the training ground. It went into him and *stayed,* and something in the back of his skull said, very calmly, in a voice that sounded like the young man at the Spirit Pagoda, *you feel the blood going, you hear it dripping, and when it closes you will still hear it dripping,* and his left hand moved toward the strap.

It got as far as the strap. And then it stopped.

Because one hundred and ninety-one.

The pain was there. It was completely, entirely there, and it was not *working* — it was not doing the thing it was supposed to do, which was to become a fact about his death. It stayed a fact about his leg. He could feel the teeth and he could feel the blood and he could hear the dripping, and underneath all of it, steady as a gauge, his spiritual power was holding the whole screaming structure at arm's length, the way his father held a hot bar with a pair of tongs: it was very hot, and it was not going to be allowed to touch him.

*That's what the number is for,* Lin Hao thought, and the thought was almost funny, and he used the almost-funny to get his feet back — and then he did the thing.

He reached behind himself, without looking, and put the Stormbringer's edge through the wolf on his thigh, and it let go, and he turned, and the four of them were three, and the scarred one was watching him do it.

Watching *how.*

His left hand went to his back and came up with the other one — the blade from the side forge, one hundred and ninety-one strikes, still smelling faintly of oilcloth. It was a small thing and it was not a soul device and it had no name, and it was the only weapon he owned that had **no history in this clearing at all.** The Stormbringer the wolves had already read. This one they had not.

So he held the Stormbringer low and the new blade high, and the scarred wolf looked at the second sword and did the thing wolves do, which was to recalculate — and recalculation, Lin Hao understood, takes time, and time was the only thing in the forest that he had more of than they did.

---

What happened next was the longest four minutes of Lin Hao's life, and he would think about them more than the tournament, more than the gauntlet, more than anything that had happened to him since the sea took a girl.

The pack learned.

He used Wind-Step to cross inside the second one's guard and it went down — and the third one, which had been about to come in on his recovery, *spread out* instead, giving him room, refusing to be inside the step. He laid the Gale Talon and the survivors broke formation and came at him from outside the arc, one at a time, so that the wind-edge had nothing to cut. He took the sword flat against a charge and stood, and the next charge came at his legs instead of his chest, because they had watched where he was strong.

They were not smart. That was the horror of it, and the wonder. They were not clever the way a person is clever. They were doing exactly what *he* did — every exchange bought them a fact, and the fact was permanent, and they never had to buy it twice.

He was fighting his own talent, in a forest, on four legs, and it was better at it than he was, because it did not have to think.

*So that's the answer,* Lin Hao thought, and the thought arrived completely calm, in the middle of the third minute, with his leg opened and his sword arm numb and the scarred wolf standing ten feet away with its head down. *You can't out-adapt something that adapts. It has nothing to lose. It's never done anything else.*

He lowered the sword.

Not in surrender. In *reconsideration.* And the scarred wolf, which had been reading him for four minutes and had built, out of four minutes of watching, a complete and accurate model of a boy with a sword, saw the sword go down and came in to finish it.

Lin Hao did not use the Gale Talon. He did not use Wind-Step. He did not use the stance he had spent eleven days fixing, or the read he had spent five months building, or any of the seventy-nine lines.

He used the sword.

One stroke. No preparation, no angle he had used before, no shape that had ever been in him previously — he *invented* it in the moment the wolf committed, the way you invent a sentence, and the wolf's model of him had no entry for it, because the model was made of things he had done, and he had never done this.

He would call it, afterwards, the **Unwritten Stroke** — because it was not a form and could not become one; the instant he fixed it into a shape it would go into the ledger, and the instant it went into the ledger it could be read, and the instant it could be read it could be beaten. It was the only technique he would ever have that worked *because* it did not exist. And it was, he understood, standing in that clearing with three wolves down and his leg going numb, the first true thing his sword had ever said that was entirely its own.

**Sword intent.** Not the whole of it. A corner of it — the first time the intent had gone *ahead* of the blade instead of behind it, the first time the sword had expressed something rather than delivered something. Wu Zhangkong had told him a true sword speaks first, before the world finishes its sentence, and he had spent four months believing that was about *speed.* It was not about speed. It was about the sword having something to say.

The wolf went down.

The other two came at once, and he invented two more, and they went down, and the clearing was quiet except for the insects, and Lin Hao stood in it with his leg bleeding and his arm numb and the Stormbringer hanging loose in his hand, and understood what had just happened to him.

*The ledger is a trap,* he thought. *If I fight out of the ledger, then anything that can read the ledger can fight me. And there's always something that can read the ledger.*

He looked at the dead wolves. He looked at the sword.

*So the sword has to be able to say something new. Every time. That's what it is. That's what Wu Zhangkong meant. Not "the sword speaks first." The sword speaks* first *because a new sentence is the only thing nobody's written down yet.*

He sat down on a root, because the leg had opinions, and he tore the sleeve off his training jacket and bound it above the bite with the competent, unbothered hands of a blacksmith's son who had grown up around hot metal, and then he got up.

He should have pressed the button. He understood that he should have pressed the button, and that the sensible thing — the thing Wang Jinxi had not been able to do, the thing Zhang Yangzi had done after four minutes — was to go out, and sit in the chair, and let the people in white look at his leg.

The pack had taught him something that had cost him four minutes and a leg, and there was more of it in the forest, and every exchange was a fact, and the facts were *permanent,* and he could feel his soul power working the way it had worked on the ninth night against Wu Zhangkong — quietly, in the background, converting.

one hundred and ninety-one was becoming something else. He could feel it happening. It was the most interesting sensation he had ever had.

And it settled, about a hundred paces deeper in, the way a bar settles when it comes off the fire and stops moving — not with a sound, with an *arrival.* one hundred and ninety-one closed over. **one hundred and ninety-one** opened underneath it. Five points from four minutes of being taken apart by four animals that could not think, and Lin Hao stopped walking for a moment to stand in the green light and do the arithmetic, because he was his father's son.

Wu Zhangkong had hit him eleven times and given him eight points. A falling beam and a girl who was not what he thought had given him fifteen. Four wolves had given him twenty-six.

And underneath the number he reached for the rank, the way he had reached for it every time — and his hand came back empty.

**Thirty.** Still thirty. The soul had taken twenty-six points of growth in four minutes and had nowhere at all to put the rank, because the second-ring band ended at thirty and there was no third ring, and there would be no third ring until a hawk that was eight hundred and sixty-two years old decided to become a thousand, and the hawk had its own schedule and had never once consulted him about it.

It was the first time in four years that his growth had arrived and found the door locked.

"Right," Lin Hao said, to the green dark, and he was surprised to find that he was not annoyed. He was *interested.* Because a wall you cannot climb is information, and information was the only thing he had ever actually wanted. "So the number keeps going and the rank waits. Which means everything I get from here until the bird moves goes into the *sword* instead." He looked at the blade in his hand — the one from the side forge, one hundred and ninety-one strikes, no history in this clearing. "That's not a bad trade. That might be the best trade I've ever been offered."

*The harder it is, the more it's worth,* he thought. *And nothing in this forest knows my name.*

Lin Hao put his hand on the button, and did not press it, and walked deeper into the trees.

## Part 7: The Dot That Did Not Stop

"There's one left," the staff member said.

Wu Zhangkong looked at the board.

Six of the seven lights were blue. Zhang Yangzi had come out first and sat in the chair and watched. Xie Xie had come out under his own power eleven seconds after a Horned Dragon decided he was not worth the trouble, and had said "I have *never* been so frightened and so disappointed at the same time," and had been given water. Wang Jinxi was asleep in the corner with a blanket over him and a person in white sitting nearby. Gu Yue had pressed her button at the exact moment she arrived within sight of Tang Wulin, which was a choice Wu Zhangkong made a note of and did not examine. Wulin had come out four minutes before that, muddy to the eyebrows and grinning.

The seventh light was still red.

"He's been in fifty-one minutes," the staff member said. "Sir, the elementary session is meant to run to thirty. Should we bring him out?"

On the screen, the board six dot was moving. Not fast. Steadily. Deeper into the sector than any of them had gone, and then further, and then off the edge of the mapped area entirely, into the part of the board where the grid ran out and the dot was the only thing on it.

"He's wounded," the staff member said. "The telemetry shows a bite, left thigh. Sir."

"I know," Wu Zhangkong said.

"He's had the button for fifty-one minutes and he hasn't—"

"I know."

The staff member looked at him. Wu Zhangkong looked at the screen, at the single dot moving steadily into the green, and his face did the thing it did, which was nothing, and there was something behind the nothing that the staff member was not senior enough to identify and would describe, later, to a colleague, as the expression of a man watching something he had been waiting a long time to see.

"Just watch for now," Wu Zhangkong said.

And on the board, the dot kept going.

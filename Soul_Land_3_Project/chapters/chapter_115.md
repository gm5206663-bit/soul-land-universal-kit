## Part 1: The Dawn Spar

Ye Xinglan was on the practice field at dawn with her Stargod Sword already drawn, which was where she was every dawn, and she only bothered waiting for him because she had decided, in her level way, that waiting was the politest thing left to try.

"Again," she said, by way of greeting. "The way we did it last time. Only this time I want to understand what you're doing with the third form, because I've worked it nine ways and none of them do what yours did."

That was Ye Xinglan exactly. She did not gawk at the gap between them and she did not flatter it; she measured it and then she climbed. She was one of the very few first-years in the academy whose sword sat on the same tier as Lin Hao's own — Sword Consciousness, man-and-sword-as-one, the place where the weapon moved on the reflex before the thought finished — and she had reached it younger than most grown masters ever did. She was not below him. She was a few years' worth of dawns behind him, and she had correctly identified that he was the only thing on campus from which she could steal distance.

He took up a length of plain foundry scrap, as he always did, and settled into the first Still Water form. "Your third form fails the same way everyone's fails the first year," he said. "You decide to cut, and then the sword cuts. The gap between deciding and cutting is where all the wasted motion lives. At our tier you don't close that gap by being faster. You close it by stopping the decision. The cut has to already be true before you know you've made it — the way your hand is already moving to catch a falling thing before you've seen it fall."

"That's what I can't do," she said, and it was not a complaint; it was the flat report of a craftswoman taking a measurement.

"No one can do it the first forty days. Watch the scrap, not me." He ran the form slowly, and at the third form he let her see the thing she was looking for — the moment where intent and stroke ceased to be two things, the scrap edge travelling the line before his shoulder visibly committed. It was not a higher realm. It was the depth of the realm they both already stood in, the place Wu Zhangkong had spent years trying to drill into him and that Xinglan was now drilling herself after, one cold dawn at a time.

They sparred until the sun was up. He did not let her win and he did not shut her out; he held the forms at exactly the height she could almost reach, and she reached, and when she put the practice point past his guard for the first time she made a small, satisfied sound deep in her throat, the sound a smith makes when a struck seam finally opens the way it is supposed to.

"Forty days," she said afterwards, wiping her blade down, already counting. "I'll have the third form honest in forty days. Come back then and I'll put a second touch on you." She said it without arrogance and without false modesty, the way she said everything, and Lin Hao laughed, because she was exactly the kind of person the five of them needed in the yard.

## Part 2: The Eastsea Master

The train from the coast got in before noon. Wulin was on the platform, which surprised no one, and Lin Hao was a half-step behind him, which surprised no one either.

Mu Chen came through the gate with one solid case in his hand — a case that held two hammers and a full master's weight of expectation — and he looked exactly the same: square, patient, built like an anvil himself, the kind of man who listened to metal the way other men listened to gossip. He saw Wulin first and laughed and clapped him hard enough to stagger him, and said the things a teacher said to the student who'd dragged his name clear across the continent.

Then his eyes found Lin Hao.

For a moment Mu Chen just stood there, holding his case, and on his face was the exact expression Lin Hao had been carrying in his own memory for years — the face of a man who had done the arithmetic in Eastsea and never been able to make it close, now confronted with the answer standing alive in front of him in the open air.

"You," Mu Chen said.

"Master Mu." Lin Hao bowed the foundry bow, fist to palm. "You brought the hammers."

"I brought the hammers because the metal's in this city now." Mu Chen set the case down between his feet the way a smith set down a tool he meant to keep, and he looked Lin Hao over openly for the first time in a year — the height, the shoulders a forge had built, the hawk-gold eyes, the forearms with the faint blue-black marks gone quiet at the edges. "I gave a boy of eleven a test meant for a first-rank apprentice, to pass an afternoon. He handed it back finished like a man rinsing his hands, and when I asked his rank he said fifth, and I spent six months telling myself I'd misheard. Then he got on the train to Shrek and I gave up on the arithmetic entirely. Are you going to stand there being polite, or are you going to tell me true?"

"Fifth rank," Lin Hao said, the same word, and for the first time he did not have to say it into disbelief. "Registered here at the Shrek association. Workshop, key, a job quota I keep. Wulin and I, we're both working the trade in this city, same as you taught us. That's the true part, Master Mu. I'd like to work the same fire as you again. I've missed having a man in the room who can tell me when my quench is lazy."

Mu Chen looked at him a long moment, and then he did a thing he had never once done in Eastsea, because in Eastsea there had always been a careful teacher's distance to maintain. He put his hand on Lin Hao's shoulder, where he had just clapped Wulin.

"Two of you," he said, half to the sky, and shook his head with something caught between exasperation and wonder. "A golden dragon in an apron and this one who still won't tell me half what he is. I resigned a perfectly good post to come here. I should have resigned the day your train left." He picked up his case. "Take me to your forge. I don't give advice on train platforms. I give it at the anvil."

## Part 3: Four Flights Up

He Qiao had been waiting for this with the particular grim satisfaction of a one-armed man who had been right for weeks. He saw Lin Hao come through the Blacksmiths' Association door with Mu Chen's case in tow and pushed himself off his stool.

"He's in," He Qiao said. "President's on the top floor today, and he'll have heard there's a Saint-tier visitor and a fifth-rank child and a relocating master all in the same building. Saint Blacksmiths don't come down four flights for rumours, boy. We go up, and we bring the metal, and we don't waste the visit."

Lin Hao had the plates with him — the three best hides of the ten-thousand-year ridge-armour he'd brought down off the mountain, the two-word-grade foundation stock He Qiao had priced weeks before and told him wanted a sixth-rank hand to open. Mu Chen's eyes went to the bundle the moment it came off Lin Hao's shoulder the way a farrier's eyes go to a horse's leg; he didn't say anything, but he stopped walking for half a step, which was, from Mu Chen, the equivalent of a gasp.

Four flights, past the soundproofed workshops where Wulin kept room eighty-eight, up to the top floor where the doors stopped carrying numbers and started carrying names on small brass plates. The door at the end stood open.

Inside, working a bar of iron with the slow, almost meditative strokes of a man for whom forging was breathing, was the lion-like man with the red hair and the lion's mane of beard and the black-hammer badge of a rank so far above the ordinary ladder that it stopped being a number and became a title. Nine rings. Feng Wuyu, president of the Shrek Blacksmiths' Association, Saint Blacksmith, the youngest the trade had ever crowned. He set his hammer down and looked at the four of them.

"So," Feng Wuyu said, "you're the two I've been hearing about. The golden dragon who spirit-refined at thirteen — don't blush, Wulin, the whole trade knows — and the other one. The fifth-rank who brought down a black-class suppression solo and walked the salvage into his own rented workshop like he was carrying groceries." He held a hand out, palm up, not for a greeting. "The plates. He Qiao says you've got two-word-grade foundation metal. Let me see it."

## Part 4: The Hand That the Metal Wanted

Feng Wuyu did not read the ridge-plate like a merchant. He laid it flat and put his palm on it the way a smith reads a finished edge by the flat of his thumb, and his eyebrows came up a fraction of an inch, which in a Saint Blacksmith was the same as another man falling out of his chair.

"This is good stock," he said. "Cleanly cored, not overworked, the seams respected. Who taught you to respect a ten-thousand hide?"

"Mu Chen," Lin Hao said, and beside him Mu Chen's mouth moved in a line that was, under the control, pride fighting a man who did not lose fights. "And the job taught me the rest. The beast had a weak seam where the shoulder met the spine, poor quench-line in the horn plate. I read it on the mountain. I did the same thing to the metal I'd have done in a fight — found where it wanted to come apart and put the edge there."

Feng Wuyu looked at him for a moment, and then he did the thing that he did not do for anyone below the trade's highest ranks. He picked up a second hammer and held it out handle-first.

"Left side of the seam. I'll take the right. He Qiao tells me your sixth gate drags and you showed the exam-hall craftsmen how to true it. I want to feel whether that was bravado."

Lin Hao took the hammer. He didn't light anything off and he didn't perform; there was nothing to perform, up here, that a hammer wouldn't show or hide on its own. He simply struck — the way he struck at his own bench in the early mornings when the workshop was empty, the Disorder Splitting Wind rhythm climbing its own even mountain, forty-nine breaths of strike, each one landing where the plate was already willing to open. He was a fifth-rank smith with the wrist and eye of one, and under his hands the two-word metal did the thing He Qiao had promised it would only do for a sixth-rank and better: it opened. Not because of anything that wasn't in his hands, but because the hands were, by themselves, that far past where his rank badge sat.

Feng Wuyu's hammer kept pace with him without hurry and without ever needing to correct the seam, and that was the loudest thing in the room. A Saint Blacksmith could tell in two strokes whether he had to cover a weaker man's side. He did not have to cover Lin Hao's side at all. He matched it, the way a man matched the second voice in a song he already knew.

When they set the hammers down the plate had opened along its readiness line, clean and even, foundation stock for a two-word suit of battle armour, begun.

Mu Chen was quiet. He Qiao let out a long breath through his teeth. Wulin looked like a boy watching the two people he most admired in the world share an anvil and finding that the world had room for that.

"You're not going to make the whole suit at your rank," Feng Wuyu said to Lin Hao, plainly, without any of the false modesty a lesser man might have wrapped around the obvious. "A two-word suit is years of work and it needs hands in every profession. But the foundation's begun now and you'll be at every piece of it, because the metal's yours and the seam read was yours, and I'll put my own hand on the parts that want a Saint's. You're welcome at this anvil whenever I'm on the floor." He said it like a man announcing the weather; but to a smith, Lin Hao understood, that was the Saint coming all the way down four flights. He bowed, fist to palm, and didn't bury the moment under words.

Mu Chen exhaled. "I retired a month too late," he said to the floor. "No. A year."

## Part 5: The Pagoda

While the smiths were reading one another four flights up, Gu Yue was standing at the counter of the Shrek branch of the Spirit Pagoda, giving the institution the thing every student on the inner track had to give them once a term: her report.

The clerk behind the counter was not really a clerk. The Pagoda did not station a branch at Shrek Academy to process paperwork; it stationed one to keep an ear on the one school on the continent that manufactured monsters, and on the back of the quiet girl with six elements it held a file as thick as any first-year's in the building.

"Gu Yue," the officer said, turning a page she was not reading. "Second-rank mecha designer. Reported elements — all six, which the intake evaluators still argue about. And you came in with the blacksmith's-table five. We made that boy an offer once already, before the exam. Tang Wulin's friend. The fifth seat." It was barely a question. "If there came a day the Pagoda had something he needed, you'd tell him the offer was still open."

"He'd make up his own mind," Gu Yue said. Her hand rested on the counter, easy, unhurried; the energy-tile beside it lit steady and even with the warm, unmistakeable signature of six elements in a girl who was supposed to have at most two. "And I already know how it falls. He stays with his own. All of you keep asking the question as if the answer might change. It won't."

The officer noted the tile's reading in her file without looking at it directly — the six-element signature was the kind of thing the Pagoda was specifically on this campus to watch. "And yourself? The Pagoda doesn't keep an office here to file reports. A girl who handles six elements has doors open to her that none of her classmates will ever see."

"I'm here for the report," Gu Yue said, with the small, flat courtesy that was as close as she ever came to rudeness. "Have you got the stamp, or did you bring me in here to waste a morning?"

The officer stamped the report. Gu Yue walked out into the Shrek sun with the file on the counter a page thicker than it had been — a page that said, in essence, that the quiet girl's elements were still extraordinary and her loyalty was still, completely, somewhere the Pagoda could not reach. She didn't go up to the association floor where the smiths were. She didn't need to. She could feel, the way she always could, that Lin Hao was somewhere being seen and valued by people whose trade he'd chosen for his own, and for one morning the weight he always carried was being held by the right shoulders.

By the time she reached the courtyards the four smiths were coming down the four flights — Mu Chen carrying his case into his new city for the first time, Feng Wuyu watching from the landing with the long patience of a man who'd found what he'd come down for, and Lin Hao in the middle of them with the opened plate wrapped again under his arm, no longer a hunk of a dead beast but a begun thing: the first true piece of a two-word suit, with a Saint's hand promised on the work ahead.

---

The days after the awakening ceremony were the worst days of Tang Wulin's life, and he was six, so he had no way of knowing that they would also turn out to be the useful ones.

Red Mountain Academy sorted the new children the way it sorted everything: by what they had. Powerful martial souls and high soul power went up. Weak martial souls, or no soul power at all, went down. There was no ceremony to it and no cruelty in it either, which somehow made it worse — it was simply how the academy was built, the same way the roof was red and the walls were white.

Wulin, with Bluesilver Grass and innate soul power rank 3, went to the bottom.

Their teacher was a middle-aged man with the permanently bored expression of somebody who has explained the same thing four hundred times and stopped expecting anyone to hear it. He gave the class the meditation instructions. He gave Wulin the meditation instructions. He did not give Wulin anything else.

"Bluesilver Grass," he said, in the tone of a man reading a weather report. "Just sit and see if anything happens."

At lunch Wulin ate more than any other child at the table, and then more than any other child in the room. His appetite had gone strange since the awakening, as though his body had been handed a bill and was trying to pay it in advance. By the end of the first week the class had named him **Rice Bucket**, and he discovered that he did not mind, because a nickname meant they had noticed him, and being noticed for eating was better than being noticed for the grass.

What he minded was the rest of it.

"That one with the pretty eyes. Bluesilver Grass. Rank three."

"His friend got a sword. Rank five. Must be nice."

"He'll never be a Soul Master."

Wulin sat with his knuckles going white under the table and thought, with the flat grim practicality that was going to turn out to be the most important thing about him: *Dad says success is ninety-nine parts effort and one part talent. So if the talent is no good, I'll just have to be very good at the other ninety-nine.*

He finished lunch. He went back to class. He did not tell his father.

---

Lin Hao was waiting for him outside the gate, leaning against a soul lamp with his arms folded, looking like a man who had been leaning against soul lamps his whole life.

"How was it?"

"Fine."

"Wulin."

"It was bad," said Wulin.

"Right." Lin Hao pushed off the lamp and fell into step beside him. "Who was worst?"

"What?"

"Who was the worst one. I want a name. I'm not going to do anything about it, I just want to know, because I'm keeping a list and I'd like it to be accurate."

Wulin, despite everything, almost laughed. "There isn't a worst one. It's all of them."

"That's worse for the list, actually, that's a lot of writing." Lin Hao considered this. "Did the teacher say anything?"

"He said sit and see if anything happens."

"Ah." Lin Hao nodded slowly, in the manner of a man receiving confirmation of a theory he had held for some time. "That's the tell. When a teacher gives you the short version, it means he's already decided what you are and he's saving his breath. You should be flattered. He thinks you're a settled matter."

"He thinks I'm trash."

"He thinks you're *known*," said Lin Hao. "Those aren't the same and he's wrong about both, but he's more wrong about the second one, so let's start there."

They walked. The soul lamps were coming up along the street, one after another, and Glorybound City smelled of salt and hot metal the way it always did.

"I'll train with you," said Lin Hao. "Every day after school. I'll push you and you'll push me."

"You don't have to—"

"I absolutely have to, I've got a sword and rank five and no idea what either of them is for, and you're the only person I know who's stubborn enough to stand there while I work it out." He punched Wulin lightly on the shoulder. "Also you owe me. You made me watch you get sorted into the bottom class and I had to stand there and be polite about it."

---

They found Na'er on the fourth day, in the alley behind the fish market, and neither of them ever agreed afterwards about who had seen her first.

She was five and a half. She had short silver hair and eyes the colour of amethyst, and she was wearing clothes that had been somebody else's three times over, and she was standing very still in the way that small children stand still when they have learned that moving attracts attention.

There were three older boys with her, and they were not being kind, and what they were saying made Lin Hao stop walking.

He understood it, in the abstract, because Glorybound City was a port and ports have edges. There were people who bought children. There was a market. It was not a thing anybody said out loud at dinner.

"Right," said Lin Hao.

"Hao—" Wulin had hold of his sleeve.

"Wulin." Lin Hao did not look at him. "I want you to go and get a policeman. There's a tower on the square, I've counted, it's four minutes if you run. Can you do that?"

"What are you going to do?"

"I'm going to stand here and be extremely visible."

Wulin went. He went fast.

Lin Hao walked into the mouth of the alley and stopped, and put his hands in his pockets, and looked at the three boys with an expression of friendly interest.

"Hello," he said.

The largest of them turned. "Get lost, kid."

"I will," said Lin Hao, "in about four minutes, which is how long it takes to run to the Administrative Law Enforcement tower from here. I've done it. I was curious."

Something changed in the alley.

"You're bluffing."

"I'm six," said Lin Hao. "I don't have the range for a proper bluff, I've tried." He took his right hand out of his pocket. "But I would like to tell you a fact, because I think it's relevant and I don't think you know it. The government keeps records. The Spirit Pagoda keeps records too, separately, and they compare. Every child who wakes with soul power gets written down — name, martial soul, rank, address. It's how they find the good ones."

He let that sit.

"So if you take her, and anybody ever looks for her, there's a piece of paper somewhere with her name on it and a man in an orange robe who read it out loud. And if she *doesn't* have soul power, then she isn't worth what you think she is, and you'll have committed a very serious crime for nothing."

The largest boy said something rude.

Lin Hao drew the sword.

He had been practising. It came out of the air the way it had come out in the chamber — silver-blue, light, restless along the edge — and the wind in the alley turned over and picked up a fish wrapper and carried it past everybody's ankles, and there was one thin bright line of lightning running the length of the steel.

"I'm rank five," he said. "You're what, twelve? You'd win. But you'd have to explain the sword, and the wind, and the fact that a six-year-old in this city has a martial soul like this, which means the records, which means the orange robe." He smiled, pleasantly. "I'm not threatening you. I'm doing your arithmetic for you."

They left.

They did not leave because of the sword, and Lin Hao knew it, and he thought about that for a long time afterwards. They left because of the paperwork.

---

Na'er came home with them because there was nowhere else, and Lang Yue — Wulin's mother, who had been dead in the way the neighbourhood understood and alive in the way that mattered — no. Lang Yue was alive. Wulin's mother was alive and a good cook and had opinions about how thin the girl was.

They put a divider up in the boys' room.

Na'er ate like someone who had been told food might stop. She and Wulin competed. Within a fortnight the Tang household's food bill had become a subject of quiet conversation between Tang Ziran and his wife, conducted in the kitchen, at volume, on the assumption that the children could not hear.

She did not say where she had come from. She said she did not remember. She was, everyone agreed, very good at not saying things.

---

Mang Tian's workshop was at the end of a lane that smelled of scale and quench-water, and it was the only building on the lane with no sign, on the principle that anybody who needed to find it already knew.

He was a tall, dark-skinned man with a full beard and hands the width of shovels, and there was nothing remarkable about him at all, which was the first thing people got wrong — because his martial soul was a hammer.

Not a beast. Not a plant. **The Earth Hammer.** A tool soul, brown-veined, heavier than it looked.

Wulin found this out the hard way, on the second week, when a piece slipped and Mang Tian caught it — not with his hand, but with a hammer that had simply *appeared*, and then a second one, and then, rising from under his feet, four rings of light: **one white, two yellow, one purple.** A ten-year white rabbit. A hundred-year yellow pair of hammers. A thousand-year purple brown bear.

The first of them, he said later, without any particular pride, was **Tenacity**.

Fortieth rank. **A Soul Ancestor.** The man who had been shouting at them about their shoulders for a fortnight was a Soul Ancestor with four rings, and he spent his days in a workshop at the end of an unsigned lane in a town nobody important visited.

"My first ring was a white ten-year," he told them, once, when Wulin asked. "Weakest thing there is. I got the hundred-year because I saved. I got the thousand-year because I'm a blacksmith and blacksmiths make money." He shrugged, which with shoulders like his moved the whole doorway. "That's the whole secret. It isn't a secret. It's a price."

Lin Hao had gone because his father had gone. Lin Wei knew Mang Tian the way blacksmiths know each other, which was mostly by reputation and partly by argument, and he had said: *take the boy, he'll either hate it or he won't, and either way he'll be useful.*

Mang Tian looked at Lin Hao for eleven seconds, which Lin Hao counted, and then looked at Wulin, who had come along because he had come along to everything for three years.

He looked at Wulin longer.

"You," he said. "Pick it up."

It was a lump of metal on the floor, the size of a man's two fists, and it did not look heavy, and Wulin bent down and nearly fell over.

"That's your test," said Mang Tian. "Two hammers. Five kilos each. You hit that one thousand times. When you're done, we talk."

"One thousand?" said Wulin.

"You can stop whenever you like," said Mang Tian. "That's the whole test."

He set them up in the back, where the light was good, and went away, because that was the kind of teacher he was.

Lin Hao stayed. He had not been invited and he did not leave, and Mang Tian, coming back an hour later with tea, found him sitting cross-legged on the floor three metres away with a stick, drawing lines in the dust.

"What are you doing?"

"Counting."

"He can count."

"He counts wrong," said Lin Hao. "He speeds up when he's tired and then he hits flat and the sound goes dull and he doesn't notice. So I'm counting the flat ones." He looked up. "There's been forty-one."

Mang Tian, who had been a blacksmith for thirty-one years and who had a specific opinion about children, looked at the lines in the dust, and then at the boy, and then at Tang Wulin — who was red-faced and shaking and had done, by then, six hundred and four.

"Say something when it goes flat," said Mang Tian.

So Lin Hao did.

*Thump. Thump. Thud.*

"Flat."

Wulin adjusted. He did not know what he adjusted. He only knew that a voice behind him said *flat* every so often, and that when it did, something about his shoulders was wrong, and that if he fixed it the sound came back.

He finished in half an hour.

Mang Tian, who had expected him to stop at two hundred, and who had prepared a speech for two hundred, stood in the doorway with his tea going cold and did not use the speech.

---

"You understand," said Mang Tian afterwards, to both of them, because Lin Hao had not left and Mang Tian had stopped expecting him to, "that most men never learn to hear it."

"Hear what?" said Wulin.

"The metal." Mang Tian held up the lump. Under a thousand strikes it had not changed shape very much. It had changed *colour*, slightly, and it had got denser, and if you tapped it now it rang instead of thudding. "It talks. It talks the whole time. Every man in this trade thinks the work is the hitting. The work is the listening. The hitting is just how you ask the question."

He put it down.

"Three months of this. Nothing else. No components, no shapes, no pretty work. You hit metal and you listen and you get it wrong and you hit it again. After three months I'll teach you extraction. After that, simple components." He looked at Wulin. "It'll be two and a half years before you make anything I'd let a stranger touch."

"That's fine," said Wulin.

"You say that now."

"It's still fine."

Mang Tian grunted, which in his idiom was applause.

Then he looked at Lin Hao, who had been very quiet, in the way that Mang Tian was beginning to recognise as the boy *working something out*.

"And you?"

"Can I do it too?"

"You've got a sword."

"I've got a sword *and* I've got hands," said Lin Hao, "and I've been watching, and I'd like to say for the record that Wulin's problem is his shoulders and mine won't be, because I'm already doing it differently, but I'd like to find that out properly."

Mang Tian looked at him for a long moment.

"Five-kilo hammers," he said. "Same test."

---

Lin Hao took eleven hundred strikes, because he did not stop when he reached a thousand, and Mang Tian — watching from the doorway, tea going cold for the second time that day — did not tell him to stop.

The boy was not stronger than the other one. That was the interesting part. He was considerably less strong. But somewhere around strike four hundred he had stopped hitting the metal and started hitting *with* the metal, and his shoulders had never once gone flat, and he had never needed anybody to tell him.

Mang Tian had a theory about this and he kept it to himself, because he had learned that theories about children were mostly about the theorist.

What he said, later, to Lin Wei, over a cup of something stronger than tea, was: "Your boy hears it. He heard it before I taught him anything. I've had apprentices older than me who never got there."

And then, after a while: "The other one is better."

"Wulin?"

"The other one is *better*," said Mang Tian. "Your boy hears it like he was born hearing it. Tang Ziran's boy hears it like it's a language he's learning and refuses to stop practising. First one gets you to journeyman. Second one gets you somewhere I can't see from here." He drank. "This child's talent in forging far surpasses my own. I'd like to be annoyed about it. I find I'm not."

---

The Spirit Pagoda branch in Glorybound City was three layers, which meant it was the lowest rank of branch, which everybody in the city knew and nobody said.

Inside, behind glass, were the spirit souls.

The woman at the counter was polite in the flat way of somebody who has given this exact tour to every child in the city for eleven years.

"Seventy-three ten-year white spirit souls," she said. "**Seventy thousand Federation Coins** apiece."

Wulin did the arithmetic on his fingers and stopped.

"Eleven hundred-year yellow," she went on. "**One million.**" She allowed a small pause, because she was not unkind, and because the pause was the honest part. "Or the draw. **Thirty thousand.** Random. You might get something good. You might get something that isn't a spirit soul in any meaningful sense. We don't refund."

"How much does a Soul Master earn?" asked Lin Hao.

"Official stipend is **a thousand Federation Coins a month**," she said. "More at Soul Grandmaster. Not a lot more."

They walked home in silence for two blocks.

Then Wulin said, "So a white one is seventy months."

"Nearly six years," said Lin Hao.

"And a yellow one is a thousand months."

"Eighty-three years. You'd be ninety."

"I'd be dead."

"You'd be dead at the counter," said Lin Hao. "They'd find you leaning on the glass."

Wulin was quiet for a while. "Thirty thousand for the draw."

"Wulin."

"It's the only number we can reach."

"It's the only number anybody can reach," said Lin Hao, "and that's the whole point of it, that's not an accident, that's a wall with a slot in it." He kicked a stone along the gutter. "Three years of saving. Every coin. For a *chance*."

"It's still a chance."

Lin Hao looked at his friend — at the boy with the trash martial soul and rank three and an appetite that frightened his mother, who had just done arithmetic in his head and arrived at the same answer and refused to be frightened by it — and felt something shift in his chest that he did not have a word for at six and would not have for a long time.

"Yes," he said. "It is."

---

That was the shape of that year.

Wulin pounded metal and learned to listen, and ate, and meditated at night with a stubbornness that his teacher would have found alarming if his teacher had ever watched him. Lin Hao pounded metal and heard it immediately, which was less admirable and more annoying, and spent his remaining hours working out what a sword was *for* — because a sword, he had discovered, was not a thing you swung; it was a thing that had opinions, and the opinions changed depending on how you held it, and nobody had told him this and he was furious that nobody had told him this.

Na'er lived with the Tangs and did not say where she had come from.

And in the garden behind the house, on a night when the moon was up and Wulin was asleep, a small girl with silver hair sat bolt upright in bed because a ring of light had come out of her body — yellow, green, red, blue, purple, gold and silver, seven colours turning one by one — and it went in between her eyes, and she did not scream, and she did not tell anybody, and she did not sleep much for the rest of that week.

Nobody in Glorybound City knew.

The records said nothing about her at all.

he will simply be excellent at the other ninety-nine parts. He and Lin Hao find **Na'er** in the alley behind

## Part 1: The Rung Above Her

She was waiting for him at dawn with her sword out, the way she had said she would be, the way she had in fact been waiting since the reunion day, and the only thing different was that this time she was not arriving to test him — she was arriving to ask him what the air had done.

"Something moved last week," Ye Xinglan said, by way of greeting, her Stargod Sword held low and steady in the grey light. "I don't know the word for it. I was asleep and the sword in the corner woke me up. It rang. My whole sword rang, the way your branch made it hold its breath the day we met, only this wasn't a thing in the room. It came from somewhere far off, and it was a step I am trying to climb to, and it was the next step." She looked at him levelly, the girl who decided things. "You felt it too. Don't tell me you didn't. I'm not asking what it was. I'm asking you to show me where the rung is, because I have spent every dawn since trying to find it by myself and I keep reaching one rung short."

Lin Hao had brought no sword of his own. He stood in the practice yard with a length of plain iron he had pulled from the foundry scrap, the same habit as the last spar, and he considered her for a moment — the Stargod Sword bright and eager, the Consciousness-tier swordswoman who had crossed the gate of man-and-sword-as-one and could now feel, without understanding, the note of the gate above it. He could not give her the Sword Soul. A realm was not a thing handed over like a hammer. But the last time he had taught her, he had let her feel the next rung as a rung on the ladder she was already climbing, and that had been the true lesson.

"I won't light anything," he said. "Watch the iron, not me. The rung above man-and-sword-as-one isn't that the sword moves when you think. It's that you stop having to *think the sword at all*. Consciousness is the weapon on your reflex. The place above that is when there's no reflex left between you — when the water your mind sits in and the iron the sword is made of are the same water, the same frequency. Don't try to feel that. You can't reach a frequency by reaching. You can only stop standing where the note isn't."

He raised the scrap iron, and he did the one thing he had done a single time in his life, in a king's ground, and had spent every day since carrying folded down in himself — and he did not light it, did not spend it, did nothing that would register on any instrument in the academy; he simply *let a fraction of the tuning show*, the way a master smith lets a beginner feel the weight of a hammer without putting the whole stroke into it. The scrap in his hand did nothing visible. But the Stargod Sword in Xinglan's hand, three feet away, RANG — a clear low note, the steel vibrating of its own accord, answering a frequency it had been made to carry and had never once been held beside.

She went very still. Her eyes went wide, and then narrow, and then — because she was the girl who decided things and because the thing she did better than anyone her age was learn — she closed her eyes and stopped trying and simply *stood there*, with her sword ringing in her hand, listening.

It lasted perhaps ten breaths. The note in the Stargod Sword faded when he folded the fraction back down. Xinglan opened her eyes.

"I'm not there," she said. It was not frustration. It was the flat, accurate report of a craftswoman taking her own measurement. "I felt where *there* is. I'm a year's worth of dawns away, maybe more. But it's a place, not a legend. I thought it was a legend." She looked at him with a long, even look that held no resentment at all, only the recognition of a climb she had chosen. "You crossed it. That's fine. I don't want the rung handed to me. I want to earn it, and now I know which direction it's in. That's enough. That's more than I had a month ago." She settled into her stance. "Now spar me, and this time don't be gentle about holding forward. I want to feel the whole height of the ladder, even if I fall off every time."

They sparred until the sun came up. She did not land a touch on him. She landed twice as many touches on him as she had last time, which he told her, because that was the measurement that mattered.

## Part 2: The Eastsea Master

The train from the coast arrived two days later, and Lin Hao was not at the station, because he had not been told and because Wulin, bouncing on the platform, had every right to the welcome. But the welcome was not only Wulin's, and he knew it before the ticket-gate finished opening.

Mu Chen came through the gate with a single case in his hand and a full man's life in it — the case held two hammers, and Lin Hao would have known the silhouette of that case across a foundry. He was the same square, solid, patient man he had always been, the master who had taught Wulin first and then, late, the other boy, the quiet one who had walked in one day and been tested with a piece of metal that was supposed to take a fortnight and had come back finished and when asked his rank had said one word. His eyes found Wulin first, naturally, and he laughed and clapped the boy on the shoulder and said the things a teacher said to the student who had justified him to the whole continent.

Then his eyes moved over Wulin's shoulder, and found the second tall boy standing a respectful half-step back, and Mu Chen's face did something carefully controlled, the face of a man who had made his peace with a thing being impossible and had now to confront it standing in the open air.

"You," Mu Chen said.

"Master Mu." Lin Hao bowed the bow of the foundry floor, fist to palm, the Eastsea way, and did not stand on ceremony and did not grin. "You came to Shrek."

"I came to Shrek because the metal's moving here." Mu Chen set his case down between his feet, the way a smith set down a tool he did not intend to part from, and looked at him openly for the first time since Eastsea, this man who was not his teacher and was not not his teacher, the man who had heard "Fifth" from a child and kept it. "And because I've spent a year on a train platform in my head, waiting for the day I'd stand in front of you again and the math still wouldn't close. You walked into my shop. You were eleven. I gave you a test for a first-rank apprentice because I did not yet understand what I was looking at, and you handed it back done like a man washing his hands, and I asked you what rank you were and you said fifth and I spent six months deciding I'd misheard. Then you got on a train to the hardest academy on the continent and I stopped trying to make the math close. Are you going to stand there and let me be polite, or are you going to tell me what you've become, Lin Hao?"

"Smith, same as you," Lin Hao said. "Fifth rank, registered in the association here, same as Wulin. I've a workshop and a key and a job quota I'm keeping up with. That's the true part, Master Mu, and it's the part that matters for the work." He paused. "There's other parts. They're not for a train platform. They're for a forge. But I'll tell you this for nothing, because you're owed it: everything you handed me in that shop is in my hands the way it's supposed to be. The metal was never wasted on me. I'd like to work at the same fire as you again, if you'll have me."

Mu Chen looked at him a long moment, and then he did a thing he had never done in Eastsea, because in Eastsea it had not been true the same way: he put his hand on Lin Hao's shoulder, the way he had just put it on Wulin's.

"Two of you," he said, half to himself, shaking his head, with something close to wonder under the exasperation. "The one's a golden dragon in a blacksmith's apron and the other won't even tell me half of what he is. I resigned a perfectly good association to come here. I should have resigned years ago." He picked up his case. "Take me to your forge, boys. I'm not a man who gives advice at train stations. I give it at the anvil. Let's see what your Shrek fire's worth."

## Part 3: Four Flights Up

He Qiao had been watching the stair for a week, the way a man with one arm and no patience for the academy's paperwork watched for the one conversation he could not have down on the ground floor. When Lin Hao came through the foundry door with a second master smith in tow — a square Eastsea man with a hammer case who moved like he had lived at an anvil since before half the Shrek smiths were born — He Qiao pushed himself up and, with the grim pleasure of a man whose instinct had been right, said, "He's upstairs. He'll have heard there's a Saint visiting and a new master in the building and a fifth-rank child with ten-thousand-year metal in a cupboard. He'll come down, or we'll go up; my advice is we go up, because the Saint coming down happens once in a decade and I'd rather it happen on a floor where we can close the door."

Lin Hao had the plates with him. He had fetched them dry and wrapped, the three best hides of the Thousand-Plate, the two-word-grade foundation metal He Qiao had priced and said wanted a hand He Qiao did not have. He carried them up the four flights of the association's stairs behind Mu Chen, past the coiling corridor of soundproofed workshops where Wulin had been given room eighty-eight, to the top floor, where the air was hotter and quieter and the doors stopped having numbers and started having names on small brass plates he did not stop to read.

The door at the end was open, and inside, working a piece of iron so slowly that it looked almost like meditation, was the lion-like man with the red hair and the black-hammer badge of rank so high Lin Hao did not know its number. Nine rings. He had stood in a king's ground and felt a hundred-thousand years of patience, and there was a piece of that same gravity in this room, though it wore the shape of an old blacksmith.

"President Feng Wuyu," He Qiao said, one-armed and unafraid. "You said you wanted to see the metal when it came up the stairs. It's come up. And I've brought you something you told me to bring — the other freak."

Feng Wuyu set down his hammer. He looked at Wulin, who was beside Lin Hao and glowing faintly with the effort of not being overwhelmed, and he looked at Mu Chen, and gave the Eastsea master a nod that was the equal nod of two men who had followed the same trade to its top. Then he looked at Lin Hao.

"So," the Saint Blacksmith said, "you're the other fifth-rank. The one who doesn't need to spirit-refine in front of an audience to prove anything, and who killed a ten-thousand-year beast alone and carried home its armour and never once reported how long it took him. He Qiao here says you forge like a man holding a door shut with his whole body." He held out his hand, not for a handshake, but for the plates. "Let me see the metal."

## Part 4: The Hand That Matches the Metal

Feng Wuyu did not examine the Thousand-Plate hides the way a merchant did. He put his hand flat on the unshingled iron the way Lin Hao had read the gorge, and he closed his eyes, and for a long moment the room was simply four smiths — a Saint, a relocated master, a one-armed rank-six, and the boy — and a piece of dead beast-metal learning that it was not done being anything yet.

"Two-word grade," Feng said at last, opening his eyes. "He Qiao wasn't being hopeful. It's foundation metal for two-word battle armour, clean. A ten-thousand-year beast that lived its whole life with plates like this was sitting on the single hardest ingredient a young armour-master can ever get, and it walked to its own death because it got contracted on a job board." He looked at Lin Hao with the red eyes of a man who had been a working student himself, the youngest Saint in history, and who had spent the walk up four flights deciding what he was about to offer. "You know what this needs. He Qiao told you. It wants a sixth-rank hand or better to open. You're fifth. Your golden-dragon friend over there is fifth, and he's a blood-essence spirit-refiner who's going to be famous, but his hand isn't *this* either, not yet." He tapped the plate. "My hand is. And I don't make a habit of coming down four flights, but I'm already down them and you're already in my building, so we're going to talk like smiths and not like a hierarchy."

He picked up a fresh hammer, held it out toward Lin Hao not to hit anything with, but handle-first, an invitation.

"Take the left side of this seam. I'll take the right. You've never worked beside a Saint. I want to feel what the one-armed man has been telling me to feel."

Lin Hao took the hammer. He did not light a ring, did not spend a thing, did not do anything that would show on a face or a chart. He simply struck, the way he struck at his own bench in the early mornings when no one was in the room — and somewhere under the ordinary stroke, the metal felt it, the way the iron gorge had felt it, the way the king's plate had rung: the frequency he had carried folded down in himself since the king's ground, the note of a sword soul, which lived in him not only through his father's iron but through every piece of metal he had ever touched because his whole inner country was tuned to iron now, the vein of it running through the sea.

Feng Wuyu's hammer was already moving. It stopped for half a beat — a Saint Blacksmith's hand, stopped on a stroke, a thing that did not happen — and came down a finger's width differently than it had been coming, meeting the note instead of fighting it.

For two minutes the four men struck in the room, and the Thousand-Plate hide under their hammers did the first true piece of its becoming: it opened, along the seam Lin Hao and Feng struck together, to the readiness he had been told it would take a sixth-rank hand to reach. He Qiao, watching with his one good arm, made a low sound. Mu Chen, watching his student strike in a Saint's rhythm he should not have been able to hear, went completely expressionless, which was how a master took a thing he could not put into words.

When they stopped, Feng Wuyu set the hammer down very carefully.

"The armour," he said, "the two-word suit this metal is for — you're not going to make it in a month and you're not going to make it alone, and you don't get to make the whole thing at your rank. But the foundation is good and you'll be there when the parts get made, every one. And I'll put my hand on it when it needs a Saint's." He looked Lin Hao full in the face, and the Saint's voice was flat and sure, the voice of a man who had bet on working-student monsters his whole life and lost not one of those bets. "I don't know what your metal is doing when you hold a hammer, boy, and you're not going to tell me on the first day. But I've heard that note once before, in my own country, at a different anvil, and I've waited a long while to hear it in someone else's. You've a workshop in this building. Use mine, too, whenever I'm in it. The door stops having a name on it for you."

That was, Lin Hao understood, as close as a Title Douluo Saint Blacksmith could come to saying *welcome, brother*, and he bowed, fist to palm, and did not disgrace the offer with too many words.

Mu Chen, beside him, let out the breath he had been holding since the hammers started.

"I retired a month early to come here," he said, mostly to the floor. "I can see it was a month too late."

## Part 5: The Pagoda and the File

While the smiths took the measure of one another four flights up, Gu Yue was standing at the counter of the Shrek branch of the Spirit Pagoda, giving the institution the thing every inner-court-track student was required to give them: her report.

The clerk behind the counter was not a clerk of the ordinary kind. The report was the excuse; the Pagoda did not run a branch at Shrek to file student paperwork. It ran it to keep an ear on the one academy in the world that produced monsters. And on the back of the young woman with the six elements and the quiet manner, the Pagoda already held a thick and valuable file — the girl they had once offered full sponsorship, the girl whose element-compatibility readings had never once come back ordinary.

"Gu Yue," the Pagoda officer said, turning a page she was not really reading. "Second-rank mecha designer, first-year. And your — association. You're with the boy who brought you, the one whose entry readings our intake evaluators are still arguing about. The smith's-table fifth seat. You'll have noticed we made him an offer once already." It was not quite a question.

Gu Yue set her hand on the counter, the way she set everything down — without any rush at all — and the small glass tile the Pagoda kept for residual energy readings took the temperature of her fingertips and then, for a reason the officer did not understand, frosted over at one corner, a thread of cold that was not hers, that she had not put there, that came off her like a shadow she could not shake, the same shadow that had made the courtyard wind go still the night a sword soul woke a hundred kilometers away.

"I'm here for the report," she said. "That's all I came for."

"Of course." The officer noted the frosted corner of the tile in her own file without being obvious about it — a weather-event around an element-user whose element was not ice, which was the kind of thing the Pagoda was specifically on this campus to catch. "Tell me something straight, since you're here. The boys and girls who came in with you — the blacksmith Tang Wulin, the dagger boy, the surveyor girl. And the fifth of your class. We keep the offer open for the one you all orbit. If there were a day he needed what the Pagoda holds, you'd — "

"He'd make up his own mind," Gu Yue said, "and I would already know what he'd decide, because he has always decided the same thing. He stays with his own." She took her hand off the tile. "You can write that down if it's useful to you. Now have you got a stamp for my report, or did you bring me in here to waste a morning?"

The officer stamped her report. Gu Yue walked out of the Pagoda branch into the Shrek sun with the frost already gone from the tile behind her and the file on the counter, she knew, a page thicker than it had been when she walked in. She did not go toward the association building, where she could feel the particular quiet weight of Lin Hao at the top of the stairs, meeting the old red-haired man she had seen take Wulin away weeks before. She did not need to. She could feel, the way she could feel a change in the wind, that he was in a room with people who would not waste him, and that for one morning the weight he carried was being shared up the ladder of his own trade instead of carried alone.

She went back toward the courtyards at the same moment the four smiths came down the four flights, Mu Chen carrying his case to his new city for the first time, Feng Wuyu at the top of the stairs watching them go with a Saint's long patience, and Lin Hao in the middle of them with the plates now more than plates — a suit of armour begun, two masters' hands on it, and a door at the top of the building that no longer had a name on it for him.

The morning was ordinary Shrek weather, clear and loud and full of other people's ambitions. Nothing in the sky marked the fact that three of the most powerful smiths on the continent were now on the same ground as the boy whose hammer rung like a sword's soul, and that every one of the things he had been hoarding in workshops and dormitories and sealed rooms had begun, at last, to be taken out and *used*.

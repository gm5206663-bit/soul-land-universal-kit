## Part 1: The Morning After the Gorge

He came back through the gate at mid-morning of the third day looking like a man who had walked somewhere a long way off and then walked all the way home, and the courtyard read him the way a courtyard read a man who belonged to it.

The note he had left on Wulin's pillow was folded into a square and tucked back under his own, which meant the captain had read it and, in the way of brothers, had said nothing and waited. Wulin was by the fire, turning a piece of scrap iron over his knuckles, and he did not stop turning it when Lin Hao came through the gate. "Four days, I'd have gone looking," he said. "It's the third. So whatever you priced, it priced back, and you walked out."

"It priced back," Lin Hao agreed. He set the Stormbringer in its corner, the father's iron quiet now, the note in it folded down into the steel the way a finished piece held its temper. "The board was wrong. The ticket said one tier and the ground had another. I don't recommend anyone follow it down. I told the registry to close the zone."

Wulin's hands stilled for half a beat at the wrongness of a ticket, and then he filed it the way he filed everything — under the smith's headings. "You closed a ticket yourself, rather than collect on it."

"There was nothing to collect. You don't carry off pieces of the place that nearly killed you unless you want to carry them home in your hands." He sat down by the fire and let the heat take two days of iron-country cold out of his clothes. Across the courtyard, Gu Yue was sitting with a book open on her knees that she had not turned a page of since before he left, and she lifted her eyes to him and held them there for the length of a held breath, and then she looked back at the book and turned a page she had already read, and said nothing at all. She had asked him nothing for two days. She would not ask him now, in front of the fire and the others. She would wait, the way the weather waited, until there was no one else in the room and she could put the question the way she put everything — without ever quite saying the large thing underneath it.

Xie Xie came over, sniffed at him for novelty, and pronounced him "smell[ing] like a struck anvil," which was closer to true than he could possibly have known, and Xiaoyan took his face between her hands for an assessing second in the way of the surveyor of the group and declared him present, thin, and not in need of a medic, which was her version of relief. They did not ask him what was different. They did not have the instruments to read that the boy who had walked out of the gate three mornings before was the same number of rings and a different reach of sword. The family fussed at the surface; the crossing stayed where it had been earned, quiet, under the ribs.

He let them fuss. It was good, after the king's ground, to be small and warm and argued-over, to be one of five in a courtyard where the largest problems were the next meal and the morning class. The note of the sword soul in him was not burning to be spent; it had done its one true thing in the gorge, and now it lived in him the way a master-level technique lived in a craftsman who had made it once — present, certain, and entirely the kind of thing you did not bring into a dormitory argument.

## Part 2: The Sword Masters

What he did not know, and what no one in the courtyard could tell him, was that the note had not stayed in the gorge.

On a Shrek roof two evenings before, at the dusk he had knelt in the iron gravel and rung the king's own plate, Wu Zhangkong had stopped mid-stroke with the Skyfrost in his hand and turned his head toward the deep country as a man turned toward a sound he could not name. A Consciousness-tier swordsman carried the note of his own blade the way a singer carried his own pitch, and what he had heard for that one clean heartbeat was a note struck in the register *above* him — a sword soul, waking far off in the wild and then gone, the way a bell in another valley rang once and fell silent. He had not finished the stroke. He had stood a long time in the dusk with the cold creeping up the Skyfrost's blade, and then he had gone down off the roof and done the only thing a sword master at Shrek could do with a thing that large.

He had reported it up.

Not as a conclusion — he had no conclusion; he did not name a name, because he would not have believed the name, and because he did not have it. He reported the *fact*, the way the academy taught its teachers to report facts and leave the naming to the institution: that at a certain dusk, from a certain direction out beyond the outer training ranges where the academy no longer sent students, a sword-soul-class resonance had been present in the world for approximately one heartbeat, and had not been present before and was not present after. The report went the way such reports went, from a teacher to the office that kept the Sea God's Pavilion's ear.

Lin Hao did not see any of that. He heard, in the next two days, only the very distant shape of it — a certain quiet in the way the senior staff passed one another in the halls, a note of something in the air that was not aimed at him because he was a first-year working student and the report did not name anyone, but that was part of the institution the way a change in weather was part of the sky before you could point to the cloud. Two inner-court teachers went out toward the outer ranges, he was told by a groundskeeper who talked to everyone, to take readings in the deep cut. They would find, if they got as far as the gorge, an old closed suppression zone and the quiet of a hundred-thousand-year king gone back into its mountain, and a resonance reading that did not point at a thirteen-year-old because no instrument on Shrek's books held a bracket for a sword soul at rank forty-five. The institution would hear the bell. It would not yet know which window it had come through.

That gap — between the fact of the note and the name of the one who had rung it — was the space Lin Hao was going to live in for a while. He had spent the sealed doors in the one place in the world with no human witnesses, and even so the truth of it had moved faster than his feet could carry him home, through the medium that carried such truths whether anyone watched or not: the note itself. He could not have hidden the Sword Soul any more than he could have hidden thunder by clapping his hands over his ears. What he *could* do, and did, and would go on doing, was be nothing like the thing the institution would be looking for — quiet, four-ringed, polite, the smith's boy who closed mis-marked tickets and did not collect on them.

It would hold. He read it the way he read a seam: the note pointed to a direction and a tier, not a person. He had time. Time, in fact, to become the rank and the rings that could carry the sword he already was, before the world finished the sum and looked at the sum and looked at him.

## Part 3: Mu Chen

The note the courtyard did hear about, openly and loudly, came over Wulin's soul communicator in the middle of the following day.

"Teacher, I made it into Shrek." Wulin was in the corner of the boys' room with the device, and he did not lower his voice because the news was the good kind and a man did not hide the good kind from his room. Mu Chen's answering laugh came through the little speaker, full and proud, the laugh of a teacher who had backed the wrong-looking horse and been proven right. Lin Hao went on polishing a hinge at his bench and listened with half an ear, the other half of him attending to the temperature of his own heart, because the name on the other end of that call was a name out of Eastsea, out of the same foundry years that had made his own wrists — Mu Chen, who had seen "Fifth" come out of him in a single word and never, in all the time since, had asked the wrong question about it.

Then the call changed note. Wulin hesitated, the way a boy hesitated before a thing he did not yet know how large it was, and said, "Teacher, there's something I need to talk to you about. I've broken through."

There was the expected shock, and the expected delight, and then the correction: not rank, Wulin said, *forging*. He had tried spirit refining, and he had succeeded.

The line went the exact kind of quiet a forge went when a piece you thought you knew the temper of rang at a note you had never heard in it. Mu Chen came out of it slowly. He said the careful thing, the teacher's thing: good preparation was the key, but it was far too dangerous, and Wulin was not under any circumstance to attempt spirit refining again before he reached rank thirty. And then, almost as if the dangerous news had settled the question of his own life rather than his student's, he said he was coming to Shrek. He would visit in a few days. "I've been in Eastsea too long," he said, through the little speaker, with the tone of a man naming a thing that had been true for years and only now permitted. "It's about time for a change of scenery, and Shrek is a pretty nice place." Wulin started to ask something, astonished, and Mu Chen said he had heard enough about the Blacksmiths' Association here and about a certain president to know the conversation was one he wanted to have on the ground rather than down a soul line. He held, Lin Hao noticed, every one of his opinions about Feng Wuyu in silence — gave nothing the device could carry.

Wulin rang off, half-bewildered and half-glowing, and found Lin Hao looking at him from the bench.

"He's resigning," Lin Hao said. It was not a question. He had heard the note in it the way he heard note in metal. A man who said *time for a change of scenery* and booked the visit before he finished the call and refused to commit to an opinion down a wire was a man who had already written the letter.

"Resigning? From Eastsea? He's — that's his association, he's the — " Wulin stopped. He had thought of Mu Chen as fixed, the way a first-year thought of home as fixed, the thing you left and came back to. It had not occurred to him that the teacher could be the one who came.

"A man like that doesn't visit his student's academy for a few days. He's coming to look at the ground." Lin Hao set the hinge down. "He taught the boy who broke to spirit refining at thirteen, and there's a Saint Blacksmith running the association in this city who tried to poach you off him within your first week. He's not stupid, Wulin. He's the least stupid man in Eastsea, and the least stupid men don't sit still when the centre of their trade moves somewhere else. He didn't ask you to come home. He told you he was coming here. That's a man bringing his hammer to where the metal is going to be."

Wulin thought about it, turning the communicator over in his hand the way he had turned the iron. "He's leaving because of us. Because of *me*."

"Because of what's going to be made on this ground, and he wants to be standing on it when it's made." Lin Hao said it gently. "Be happy for him, not guilty. He's not following his student out of weakness. He's a master craftsman with thirty good years and a whole new city's worth of fire, and the Saint's just shown him the door's open. Resigning from the place you've been too long isn't failure, captain. It's a relocation strike."

## Part 4: Five Working Students

Wulin took the free dinner with Yuanen that evening in the corner of the dining hall — his strange alternative cultivation, the meal that wasn't a meal to anyone who didn't know him — and he dragged his table along and introduced the working student of the old batch to the working students of the new one. Xie Xie, whose head had been full of working-student legends for days, could not hold the question in past the first course.

"How many are there?" he asked. "Working students, I mean. Right now. In the whole outer court."

Yuanen answered around a mouthful, plain as a parts count, because to her the working-student stream was not a legend, it was where she lived. "Six of us last year. Two graduated, two made the inner court, and one withdrew. So of the previous batch it's only me left." She tipped her chin at the table. "Counting you lot, there are five of us now."

Xie Xie's eyes went round at the funnel of it — six to one in a year, and then five all at once. Lin Hao watched Wulin file the number, and saw the exact instant the filing snagged. Because Wulin had been at the drink shop, and he had seen the working-student badge on the red-haired waitress with his own eyes, and even the sky-enforcer had verified it — and Yuanen had just told him there was no red-haired working student but herself and the four of them. Lin Hao had been at the drink shop too, and had seen what Wulin had seen, and had carried the quiet deduction home and said nothing. He said nothing now. He watched his captain work it out in real time — the badge, the enforcer, the one working student left standing — and watched Wulin, who had the bloodline's perception and the instincts of a boy raised in a forge, arrive at the edge of a conclusion and pull back from it out of loyalty, because you did not lay a thing that dangerous out on a dining table for anyone to step on. *Not our secret to hand him*, Lin Hao thought, with the exact phrase he had used to keep Xie Xie out of the collision by the forest. Wulin had the good sense to hold it too. Brothers, in the end, held the same silences.

The silence got company immediately, because Yue Zhengyu appeared at their table with the perseverance of a man who had mistaken obsession for duty, and turned a chair around and sat in it like a man settling in for a siege.

"You can't really mean you're the only working student left," he said to Yuanen, or at the space just beside Yuanen where the red-haired girl lived in his head. "What about her? The red-haired one. I saw her badge, same as you've got, and the enforcer confirmed it."

"You're really a leech that won't let go," Yuanen said, with the exhausted precision of a person who had decided long ago not to dignify any part of this with surprise.

"To eradicate an evil soul master is the duty of every Holy Angel soul master," Yue said, and said it loftily, the way a boy said a creed he had learned at his grandfather's knee and never once tested against an actual person. "I won't pretend I don't see what's in front of me. You're hiding her. The moment I catch her out, the matter's settled. In the meantime — " he reached for the meal's account with a flash of generosity meant to buy his welcome " — dinner's on me, for the lot of you. You working students can't all turn down a free table."

"No thank you," Wulin said.

Yue's eyes flicked to him, and there was the Angel-clan boy's whole self in the flicker, the pride and the puzzle of being refused a kindness that had always been accepted before. "You've a lot of backbone for someone who ought to be buying his own bread," he said, lightly, and did not quite leave, and did not quite belong there, the hunter sitting at the wrong table in the wrong shape of the thing he was hunting. Lin Hao said nothing to any of it. He watched Yue across the table with the read he had already taken by the forest — the Angel soul of light and judgement that struck like a verdict and carried no weight, against the thing at the table that struck like a falling wall — and let the dramatic irony stand, thick and ordinary, while the stew went round.

After dinner, walking back through the dark courtyard, Gu Yue dropped back beside him until they were a step behind the rest, and she asked it at last, the only thing she had been holding for two days.

"Where did you go," she said. It was not spoken like a demand. It was spoken like the weather being the weather. She did not ask if he was well; she had seen he was. She asked the geographical question and left it open, and the wind between the dormitory buildings did a small, wrong thing for no reason, the kind of thing that had no weather in it, turning a breath of cold up off the flagstones that did not come from anywhere cold.

"Out past the ranges," he said. He did not lie to her; he had learned, a year before in a forest, that lying to Gu Yue cost more than it bought, and that telling her the whole truth cost something different and larger that he had no permission from his own secret to spend. "I took the expensive ticket. It was more expensive than the board said. I walked out, and I walked back. Nothing you need to worry about came home with me."

She walked half a step in silence, the cold thread of the wrong wind thinning out the way frost thinned when the sun reached it. She had heard in his voice what she had not heard in two years of him — a note in it that had not been there before, the small settling difference of a man who had stood somewhere larger than himself and been changed by it without being broken. She did not know what it was. She knew metal, she knew six kinds of wind, she knew the elements the way a girl knew her own fingers, and she could not have named it. She only knew the wind had been too still in the courtyard the dusk he had been gone, and that he had come back with a new sound in him, and that the new sound did not frighten her, which she found, in the dark, was the stranger thing.

"You closed a ticket," she said, "and you didn't collect."

"There was nothing to collect," he said. "Only a note to be careful of."

She let it stand there, between them in the cold, the large unspoken thing underneath the small spoken ones, and she did not name it, and neither did he; and they walked on after the others into the lamplight, a boy with a sword soul he could not explain and a girl who could feel in the air that something in him now rang the way it ought to, carrying their silences the way they always had, hers and his, separate and close.

## Part 5: Two Masters on the Road

Three days later, which was the exact span a master blacksmith would leave a hot piece to settle before he struck it again, two men began moving toward Shrek city from two directions at once.

In Eastsea, Mu Chen finished the last of his affairs. The train tickets were two — his own and his apprentice's, because a master who relocated brought his hands with him — and the resignation went up the chain in the same hour as the luggage. The Eastsea Blacksmiths' Association would lose the man who had taught its two most impossible children, and it would not be told where the fire was going, only that the fire was going; and Mu Chen sat in the back of the railway carriage with his hammers in their case and did not look back, because the man who had said *time for a change of scenery* did not spend the ride regretting the scenery. He was coming to look at a Saint Blacksmith, and at a city that had gathered under it both his spirit-refining thirteen-year-old and — though he had never quite been given the name on the other side of that one word — the other boy, the one who had walked into his foundry a small, silent thing and said "Fifth" and left the whole shop holding its breath. He had always meant to keep an eye on that one. Shrek was about to make that easy.

And up the long stair of Shrek's inner courts, in the same week, the report about the sword note came back from the teachers who had taken readings in the deep cut. It did not name a person. It named a phenomenon, and a direction, and — the thing that kept Wu Zhangkong awake for two nights running — a *tier*. A sword soul. Somewhere out past the ranges, for one heartbeat, there had been a sword soul in the world, and there was no sword soul on Shrek's register that was not a Title-class master in the Sea God's Pavilion, and none of them had been in the deep cut that dusk, and a sword soul did not just *happen*, it was carried by a person, and a person who carried one and did not announce it and could not be found by going to the obvious masters was either an unknown old monster walking the wild country, or — and here the reasoning stopped, because the alternative was impossible and Wu Zhangkong did not waste nights on impossible things.

He ruled it impossible. He was a reasonable man, and a boy did not cross a realm that took mortal swordsmen thirty years of war to reach, and not one of his first-years, and not the quiet fifth seat, the careful one who struck as though he had all the time in the world. He ruled it impossible and went down to the morning class, and he looked at his students the way he always did, exactly, without favour or suspicion, and it would be a long time before the note he had ruled out followed the evidence home and turned, at last, into the name.

Lin Hao walked into the morning class among his year, four rings and a black one and no more outward signs than he had ever carried, and took his fifth seat, and set the father's iron — the named sword, plain and quiet — against the leg of his chair, and he did not know either that his Eastsea master was on a train or that his Shrek teacher had ruled him impossible. He only knew, the way a man in the quiet at the top of a very long climb knows, that the summit he had found alone in a king's ground had rung a bell the whole institution was now listening for, and that his job was the oldest job of a person who carried too much: keep being ordinary, keep the note folded down, and climb — rank by rank, ring by ring — toward the day the world finally heard it without looking anywhere else for the source.

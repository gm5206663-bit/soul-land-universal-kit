## Part 1: The New Metal Has to Be Worked

The morning after the Saint came down four flights and opened his door, Lin Hao sat in his own workshop before dawn with the Stormbringer across his knees and understood the thing he had been doing wrong for a week.

He had carried the sword soul the way a man carries a found bar of strange metal home from the gorge — carefully, folded down, never struck, never tested, proud of the having of it and afraid of the spending of it. But metal that was never worked was not treasure; it was just a heavier kind of stone. The realm he had crossed in the king's ground was the same. It had opened in him at the death-edge and he had spent the days since not touching it, as though leaving it alone would keep it bright. A smith knew better. Raw metal had to be folded, drawn, annealed, struck ten thousand times, or it held no edge and no temper. He had a new tier in his hands and he had not once put it on the anvil.

So he began.

He set the Stormbringer across his knees and took up the Mysterious Heaven Method the way he had every dawn for years, the slow breathing that pulled the world's spirit energy through the body in a long circuit — and when the circuit came around to the place where the new note lived, the place behind his breastbone where the iron vein of his Inner Country now rang in the same frequency as the storm-sky and the dark water, he did not flinch from it. He led the slow, steady current of the Method *through* the note, the way he led fire under a cold ingot, gently, evenly, giving it something to flow along instead of leaving it to rattle loose in him.

It was like putting a wild current into a cut channel. The note, which had woken a swordswoman three hundred feet away through sheer accident the week before, settled. It did not go quiet; a sword soul was never quiet, any more than the sea was. But it stopped being a thing that leaked whenever he stopped holding it, and became a thing that ran along the banks he gave it. He breathed the Method through it for an hour while the sky went grey and then pale gold, and when he opened his eyes he was aware of the workshop in a way he had not been — not through sight, but through the note, the way a blind smith feels the grain of iron under his thumb. Every nail in the wall, every tong on its rack, the hidden seam of his father's iron in the blade across his knees: all of it hummed, faintly, in the same key.

He had not gained a ring. He had not gained a rank. He had gained something a hundred times harder to win than either: the new metal was no longer loose in him. He could carry it the way he carried his own hands, without thinking, and that was the difference between a man who had crossed a realm once under duress and a man who *lived* there.

## Part 2: Every Old Tool Back on the Bench

He did not spend the morning only on the new thing. That was the other half of the smith's truth: when you brought home a strange new metal, you did not throw out the hammers that had won you the right to touch it. His whole kit had been sitting in the cupboard while he stood still in front of his big achievement, and a tool left unused was a tool that forgot its weight. So over the next days he put every one of them back to work, one at a time, and refined each of them against the new frequency he now carried.

He ran the six strokes of the Still Water sword on the practice field at dawn, the foundational forms Wuzhangkong had first taught him at Eastsea, and found that the new note changed them. The first stroke, the one that gathered stillness, gathered more now — he could pull the air of the yard into a quiet pool around his blade the way he had only managed for a breath in the king's ground. He ran the Disorder Splitting Wind Hammer at the anvil in the afternoons, the forty-nine-strike rhythm that climbed a mountain of its own momentum, and found that where the hammer had once struck *at* the metal, it now struck *with* it, the iron ringing before the blow landed, the note in his hands meeting the note in the work. He refined a customer's ingot in forty-nine strikes that afternoon, a thousand-refinement that came out clean and even, and logged it without a word, and his ledger of points ticked up the way it had not since he stopped taking ordinary work to stare at his sealed doors.

He worked the Frost Abyss Domain in the cold evenings, alone on the range. In the king's ground it had come out *at war*, a domain that sealed a tunnel and froze a king's breath and burned him to use it. He walked it out now a breath at a time and taught it the difference between a battlefield and a practice yard — he let the deep cold spread out from the Frost Abyss sword until the grass under his boots went white and the air went still, and then he *held* it there, ten breaths, twenty, a full minute, a domain at peace instead of at war. The frost no longer bit his own hands when he spread it. That was mastery: a power that had once only knew how to be an emergency now knew how to be a tool.

He ran the Purple Demon Eyes against the dawn until the light stopped hurting, the third stage he had reached at his own AU pace and never drilled past; he read the wind off the distant ranges with Storm Reading, the perception skill that had been half-drowned in the fight, and found that on a clear morning he could taste a storm building a day's march away; he called up Domineer, the black-edged Talon, the deep-water body that could heal overnight and walk cold water unburned, and he put each of them through its forms the way a smith oiled every tool in the box, not only the newest. By the fourth morning he was not a boy with one huge new power and a cupboard full of neglected old ones. He was a craftsman with a full bench, every tool in practice, and the new tool hung on the wall in its proper place among the rest.

## Part 3: The Hawk and the Second Body

The hawk had been waiting with the patience of a creature that lived in his bones, and it gave him no peace until he turned the grindstone onto it too.

The Hawk-Soul Union — the perfect fusion he had opened in the king's ground, the second body of winter-tipped feather and storm — had come together under duress the same way the sword soul had, and it had the same flaw: it was a fusion he could *reach* in extremity but not *hold* at will, and the dragon fusion that had opened imperfectly in the fight had burned him every second he sustained it. Mutations never stopped; that was the law he had been given, the dragon-bloodline in the hawk still walking its own long road. He had left it resting since the cull. Resting was not refining.

He took it out to the high ridge above Shrek on the fifth dawn, where no instrument watched and the wind was the only witness, and he opened the Union slowly, not as an emergency but as a practice: he reached for the hawk's soul as he reached for his own hands and let the second body rise over him, feather and storm, the winter-dark wings that had rung at the note of the sword soul in the gorge. It came faster than it ever had — the fusion was closer to the surface now, a door with worn hinges — and when the dragon-fusion opened in its wake he was ready for the burn this time, and he did the thing he had not been able to do in the king's ground: he let the new sword-soul note ring through it all at once, the iron frequency he had spent the week learning to carry.

The effect was immediate and startling. The dragon-fire that had scorched him in the cull, that had made the imperfect fusion a thing he could only hold for a few desperate seconds, settled when the note touched it — the way a torn seam goes flat under a steady hammer. The burn eased to warmth. The second body stopped fighting the dragon in it, and for the first time he held the full Union, dragon and hawk and winter-tipped storm all in one, *without it costing him*, for a long, calm minute over the ridge, his shadow a great dark bird on the rock below him. When he let it fold back down he was not even winded.

The hawk's power had come up with him in the fusion, the winter-dark feather a season deeper and the bloodline mutation running a little further toward whatever it was becoming — he felt it in the ease of it, the same way he felt the new ring in a piece of worked metal. He had not crossed the hundred-thousand-year gate; the hawk's true crossing was still far off and sealed to its own time. But a mutation that was never practiced went stale, and he had spent a week letting a great deal go stale. He spent the rest of that dawn flying the Union in short, controlled climbs, taking it apart and putting it back together, and by sunrise the second body answered him the way his hands answered him.

## Part 4: The Others Are Not Frozen

He was not the only one at the grindstone, and the workshop and the yards were loud with it.

Wulin was in the foundry every afternoon without fail, the two hours he had carved out of his schedule as his own law, and he had the look of a boy climbing toward something he could name: rank thirty, the third ring, the day he was allowed to spirit-refine again. Mu Chen, set up now in Shrek with his two hammers and his hard eye, worked the boy's strokes like he had at Eastsea, and when Wulin's forty-nine strikes ended in a clean thousand-refinement there was a flicker of something on the new master's face that Lin Hao recognized — the same wonder Mu Chen could not quite hide when a child handed back the impossible. Wulin was twenty-eight and climbing, every spare second cultivating, his soul power filling toward the bottleneck the way a kettle fills toward the boil. He did not break through that week. He put on the kind of honest, invisible progress that *preceded* a breakthrough and got mistaken, by anyone not watching, for standing still.

Xie Xie, ordered into the mecha-maker's association, spent his evenings swearing at his own clumsy hands and coming back the next evening; Xu Xiaoyan and Gu Yue, the mecha designers, bent over plans at the library tables, Gu Yue already a rank ahead and pulling the other two toward her. They were all grinding their own edges, because Shrek did not let you rest and because Wulin, for all his warmth, led by the simple method of being the hardest-working boy in any room he entered. Lin Hao worked beside them, refining their practice blades at cost, showing the smiths in the association the little trick of the sixth gate's drag he had offered the exam hall, because a tool that was not used for the people around you was a tool kept in a drawer.

It was into the middle of this ordinary, grinding, refining week that Shen Yi walked at the start of class with Elder Cai absent as usual and Wu Zhangkong a silent cold presence at the wall, and made the announcement that turned every head in the room.

There were seven positions in the class. One president, two vice-presidents, four profession-council seats, all of them decided in a single battle on the practice field — survive to the end, or touch the ground with four limbs or torso and be eliminated. The points were real, the inner-court advantage was real, and Shen Yi, with the class president's stipend of a thousand contribution points a month hanging in the air, looked at her gathered monsters and smiled. "First," she said, "I'll declare Tang Wulin the blacksmith representative. No one competes for that seat." When the enormous slit-eyed boy in the back row rose to object, she gave him the flat answer that silenced the room: no one could compete with a fourth-rank blacksmith under fifteen.

Lin Hao, sitting in his fifth seat, said nothing. He was a fifth-rank smith himself, and the seat could as easily have been his; but a declared representative stood in the light with all the class's eyes on him, and he had spent the last week carrying a sword soul out of a sealed zone. He let the seat belong to Wulin, where it was both earned and loud, and bent his attention toward the part of Shen Yi's announcement that mattered.

A battle. Survive to the end. Seven seats and a field full of geniuses, fifteen minutes to choose your allies. He had spent the week putting every tool he owned back into practice and carrying the new one like his own hands. It occurred to him, sitting in the fifth seat while the lecture hall erupted around him into the scramble for teammates, that the grindstone had just been given a purpose. He had spent the week refining everything he had. In two days he was going to have to use all of it, in a ring full of the best thirteen-year-olds on the continent, and he was going to have to do it without letting the note he carried ring any louder than it ought.

## Part 5: The Measure of a Week

That night he sat his last quiet dawn before the battle and took stock the way a smith measured a finished piece — not by its shine but by its grain.

A week before, he had come down from the king's ground a boy who had crossed the greatest threshold of his life and then frozen in front of it, afraid to spend what he had won, leaving every old tool in the drawer and the new tool unworked. He had measured himself wrong: he had thought that not touching the sword soul was prudence, the restraint the law demanded of a crossing so large. But the law had never said *stop*. Crossings, not trickles — that meant growth had to be *earned* to be kept, and the earning was the work. He had crossed the realm in the gorge; he had *kept* it in the workshop, one dawn Method-circuit at a time, one folded hammer-stroke, one held frost-domain, one calmed dragon-fusion.

The numbers of him told the truth of the week to any instrument that could read them. His spiritual power had climbed under the steady discipline of carrying the note — still well inside the Spirit Sea, far below the sealed Abyss gate, but up where a week of serious cultivation ought to put a working boy, no longer pinned to the floor of his own potential. The hawk had come up with him through the stabilized Union. His ledger had grown, a point at a time, from the honest refining he had gone back to doing instead of staring at his sealed doors. And the things no instrument measured — the frost that no longer bit him, the dragon-fusion that no longer burned, the sword soul that ran on a channel instead of rattling loose — those were the week's real product, a wide, calm body of mastery built under the threshold of any rank-up, the way a foundation was laid below ground where nobody cheered.

He set the Stormbringer across his knees one last time in the dark workshop, and the iron in the blade hummed, gently, in the same key as his own Inner Country, no longer a sound he had to clamp down on. Outside, the academy slept. In two days the field opened, and the whole class would be watching. He had everything he owned back in his hands now, and every last piece of it had been put through the fire and taken out sharper.

He was ready to use it.

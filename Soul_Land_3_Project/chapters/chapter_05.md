## Canon Reference: canon ch 41–44 (Ranking Test, Eight Star Saint Craftsman, Second Rank), 49-50 (Xie Xie's challenge, The First Class)
## Timeline: Age 10, first week at Eastsea Academy

## Part 1: The Letter

The Blacksmith's Association of Eastsea City did not sit near the harbor with the other money. It occupied a black stone block in the old quarter — no sign, no soul-lamps on its face, only a gate of folded iron and, from somewhere inside, a rhythm.

Wulin stood in front of that gate for a long moment, listening. Beside him, Lin Hao listened too — and heard it the way he heard everything now, in layers. Forty hammers at least, spread through the building, none of them in step with the others. But underneath the noise, every few seconds, one strike would land so clean it left no echo at all. A silent hit. His father had done that maybe six times a year.

"That's Thousand Refinement," Wulin said quietly. "The metal doesn't ring because there's nothing wasted in the blow."

"Then this building is full of people better than my father," Lin Hao said. "And your master's letter had better be good."

It was. The doorman read Mang Tian's blocky handwriting, looked at Wulin's calloused hands, and let them into a front hall that smelled of hot scale and oil. A woman with a surveyor's badge took the letter, read it twice, and told them to wait.

They did not wait long. A middle-aged man came out of an inner door with his sleeves already rolled — broad, unhurried, with the calm eyes of someone who had decided the worth of ten thousand pieces of metal and was rarely wrong. On his chest, eight stars were worked into the fabric around a golden hammer.

Eight stars. Lin Hao's breath went still without his permission. In Glorybound City, a fourth-rank blacksmith was a city celebrity. His mind did the arithmetic his father had taught him — fourth rank could purify iron a hundred times over; fifth rank, a thousand; each rank past that was not a step but a cliff — and the arithmetic arrived, trembling, at a word his father only said about legends. *Saint Craftsman.*

"You're Mang Tian's boy," Mu Chen said. It was not a question. His eyes went to Wulin's hands, then — briefly, the way a smith glances at the fire he isn't using — to Lin Hao's. "And you have a blacksmith's hands too. Glorybound?"

"My father's forge. Lin Wei."

"Lin Wei." Mu Chen tasted the name, found something in it. "He made a folding blade for a merchant captain eleven years ago. Wind-pattern welding along the spine. I remember it because it should not have worked, and it did." A pause. "The apple, then. We'll see." He turned back to Wulin. "Mang Tian says you understand Thousand Refinement. At ten years old, that claim is either a lie or a marvel, and I've grown too old to enjoy guessing. The testing room is through that door." He nodded at a side chamber where a surveyor was already unlocking it. "Heavy Silver. Purify it. If you can."

Wulin's jaw set. "How much?"

"Enough," Mu Chen said, and for the first time, something like amusement crossed his face. "The hammers are on the rack. You'll want both."

## Part 2: Second Rank (Canon Scene)

The Blacksmith's Association did not need a sign. It was grey, it was **thirty floors** tall, and at the very top of it was the shape of a hammer, which meant that nobody in Eastsea City had ever had to read its name.

Inside, past the glass doors, was a wall ten metres high with an eight-metre golden hammer sculpture set into it.

**Yun Xiaoling** at the front desk knew exactly who they were before they reached her, because Mang Tian's letter had arrived three days ahead of them and the Association had, in the manner of institutions that enjoy being efficient, already opened a testing room.

"Grandmaster Cen is on the fifteenth floor," she said, and pressed a button, and the doors of the soul elevator closed on the largest interior either boy had ever stood in.

**Cen Yue** turned out to be nothing like what Lin Hao had assembled from the word *Grandmaster*. He was a man in his forties, not particularly tall, extremely well built, with shoulders that seemed to have been put on separately, and a black jacket resting on his deltoids rather than being worn. He was looking at a design plan when they came in.

"You're Mang Tian's disciple?" he said, without looking up.

"Yes. Hello, Grandmaster Cen. My teacher asked me to greet you in his place."

Cen Yue's eyes crinkled. "Mang Tian. That man has spent his whole life trying to reach the peak, and he takes a disciple this small." He rolled up the plan. "Come on, then. I'll take you down and we'll find out whether he's gone soft."

---

The testing room was a vaulted stone cell built to swallow sound, and on the stand along one wall were **fifteen chunks of metal, each one third of a metre squared**, in every colour and quality Lin Hao had ever seen and several he had not.

"**Pick one and purify it,**" said the surveyor, a middle-aged woman who plainly knew Cen Yue well. "I grade you on the metal you choose *and* the degree of purification. **Pass sixty and you're a rank-one blacksmith.**" She glanced at the clock. "**One hour. Calcining counts.**"

She did not say what any of the metals were. That, Lin Hao realised, was the actual first test — you had to know what you were looking at before you were allowed to decide what you could do with it.

Wulin walked the line once, slowly, and stopped in the middle.

He picked up a chunk of metal the size of a man's two fists and carried it to the anvil without making a sound, and the surveyor's eyebrows went up, because it was **Heavy Silver** and it weighed **over three hundred kilograms**, and purifying it was, in the ordinary course of things, a **second-rank** blacksmith's standard.

On the rack behind the anvil hung the Thousand Refined Tungsten Hammers.

Lin Hao knew what they were before the surveyor said it, because he had lifted the forty-kilogram practice hammer in his father's forge and nearly lost a foot. These were a matched *pair*, forty kilograms each, tungsten-folded, balanced so perfectly they hung on the rack like sleeping birds.

"Most second-rank blacksmiths work Heavy Silver with one hammer and both arms," the surveyor said, watching Wulin. "Some can't lift one. You may withdraw and take the first-rank test instead. No one will think less—"

Wulin took a hammer in each hand.

He lifted them the way another boy lifts schoolbooks. The surveyor's mouth opened, closed. Beside the door, a girl who had been waiting for the other testing room leaned forward without noticing she was doing it — **tall for thirteen, exceptionally pretty, large bright eyes, golden hair combed back into a ponytail**, dressed in close-fitting clothes that made her look nimble rather than dressed, with the particular stillness of someone who had spent nine years being told she was remarkable and had decided, some time ago, to stop agreeing.

Wulin closed his eyes for one breath. Lin Hao watched the shift the way he watched everything: the small settling of his friend's weight, the breath that went down instead of up, the whole forge-honed architecture of him locking into place. Four years of Mang Tian's Thousand Refinements, of a body built strike by strike, of a first seal's furnace banked low behind the sternum.

The first hammer fell.

There was no ring. That was the first miracle — forty kilograms of tungsten meeting the densest common metal on the continent, and the sound that came back was a *thud* like a heartbeat, every scrap of the blow's energy going into the silver and none escaping as noise. Lin Hao's hawk-eyes caught the shockwave die inside the metal instead of out. *Nothing wasted. Father's silent strike — every single time.*

And Lin Hao — because he was his father's son, because he had lifted the forty-kilogram practice hammer in the Glorybound forge and nearly lost a foot, because the hawk-eyes read metal the way they read wind — did not only watch. He *read* the work, strike by strike, the way his father had taught him to read a bar: *first strike, true but a fraction high — the bar wants to go left, Wulin's holding it right. Second — there, he found it. Third — now the grain's opening; he's asking the iron and the iron's answering.* Lin Hao's own hands, at his sides, moved without his noticing — the ghost of the strike, the ghost of the turn, his body reading Wulin's forge the way it read everything, learning the silent strike by watching his brother find it. By the thirtieth strike, when the surveyor stopped writing to simply watch, Lin Hao had stopped being an audience. He was a smith watching a smith, and his hands knew the work.

The second hammer fell a half-breath later, and the third, and the room filled with a rhythm that stopped being noise and became weather. Strike. Turn. Strike. The heavy silver's sullen gray began, slowly, to lighten. Impurities were being *persuaded* out of metal so dense that persuading it of anything was considered a second-rank blacksmith's defining trial — and Wulin was not persuading it. He was informing it.

Thirty strikes in, the surveyor stopped writing and simply watched.

Fifty strikes in, the girl in the testing robe had come two steps closer to the anvil, her posture forgotten.

At the sixty-third strike, Wulin's hammers landed together — one sound, perfectly folded — and the Heavy Silver released a **note**.

It was beautiful. A single clean tone that filled the vaulted room like a struck bell, because the metal had become, at last, pure enough to sing. Lin Hao felt the note in his hollowed bones like a wind through reeds, and somewhere behind his ribs his own soul power stirred in sympathy, and he understood with total clarity that he was watching his brother become real.

Wulin set the hammers down. Only then did his arms tremble.

The surveyor found her voice. "That note… that was the first-rank standard passed. The purify-to-song is the *second-rank* standard, and you—" she looked at Mu Chen, who had arrived in the doorway at some point without anyone hearing him, "—President, he's ten."

"I can count," Mu Chen said. He walked to the anvil and put a bare hand flat on the singing silver, the way a doctor touches a pulse. His face did not change. It was, Lin Hao thought, the face of a man doing subtraction — this child, minus everything that should have made this impossible — and not liking the remainder.

"Tang Wulin," Mu Chen said. "As of this moment you are a second-rank blacksmith of this Association — the youngest in its records, which I will verify tonight and then never enjoy hearing about again." He reached into his robe and produced a bronze badge, plain as a washer. "The Association pays second-rank smiths per completed refinement, and it will have work for you every day your academy lets you breathe. Your master trained your arms. I'll train the rest." His eyes moved, once more, to Lin Hao. "And when *your* arms are old enough for the tungsten, Lin Wei's son, the door knows your father's name."

"Purification required a medium component as well," the surveyor said, when the hour was up and the silver sat on the anvil **seven percent smaller** and grey as old weather. "**Hundred Refine a rare metal and forge a medium-sized component** — those are the two requirements for second rank. You've done the first."

Wulin, who had been expecting to sit down, picked the hammers back up instead.

He made a **semi-circular joint-protection component for a mecha knee** — the piece that wears out fastest, and therefore the piece most worth putting a soul guide array into, and therefore the piece that wanted Heavy Silver's five-to-ten-percent soul-power amplification more than any other. He had worked that out before he started, and neither Cen Yue nor the surveyor said anything at all when they realised he had.

What they said, afterwards, to each other, in the corridor, was that the boy had forged **in harmonisation** — the state where a smith builds on a resonance with the metal until the two of them fuse, and where every work carries the smith's own feelings and ideals whether he wants it to or not.

---

Outside the testing room, the girl with the golden ponytail was still standing where the note had found her.

She had passed her own second-rank test **two days earlier**, in room six, and it had been the best day of her life, and then somebody at the front desk had mentioned — casually, the way people mention weather — that a boy had taken the same test in room three.

"*How* old?"

"Ten."

She had not said anything. She had gone home and thought about it for two days, and what she had arrived at was not jealousy, exactly, because she had made a decision about jealousy a long time ago.

Her father had told her, when she was small enough to have to stand on a box to see the anvil, that forging was a rough and heavy business and **not suitable for girls**. He had said it kindly. He had said it the way you say something that is simply true.

She had started anyway, at **five**, making small hammers out of offcuts. He had thought it was a joke. It had taken **two years** of her mimicking his every movement before Mu Chen — President of the Eastsea City branch, eight-star Saint Craftsman, a man with four hundred smiths under him — had come out one morning and watched her for eleven minutes and then, without any announcement at all, begun teaching her.

She had inherited his martial soul. Her figure had gone stocky from the strength work, and he had gone quietly and expensively looking for rare herbs to fix that, and she had let him, because fighting him about it cost more time than the herbs did.

**First-rank at eleven. Second rank a month after her thirteenth birthday.**

Her father had told her she had progressed faster than anyone her age. That she was worthy of being called a **child prodigy in the world of blacksmithing.**

And she had discovered, over about a year, that she hated the word.

Not because it was untrue. Because it was a word other people used to explain her to themselves, so that they would not have to think about the nine years. *Prodigy* was a thing that happened to you. What had actually happened to her was five-year-old hands and a box to stand on and two years of being thought funny.

**Her goal was to surpass her father and become a ninth-rank Divine Craftsman.** There were **three** of those on the entire Douluo Continent.

So she stood outside room three with her arms folded and watched a ten-year-old come out looking like he had been through a furnace, and she did not feel generous.

"That's him, then," she said.

"That's him," said the surveyor.

"Does he know what he did?"

"He knows he passed."

Mu Xi made a small sound that was not quite a laugh. "Then he'll find out." She pushed off the wall. "So will I."

And she went, because there was a second-rank task board on the third floor and she had every intention of being better at it than a boy who had been doing this for four years — which was, she reflected on the way down, exactly the sort of thing her father would have called *not suitable for girls*, and which she had therefore been doing since she was five.

Lin Hao, watching her go, said to nobody in particular, "She's not annoyed that he beat her."

"No," said Wulin, wiping his face with a sleeve.

"She's annoyed that he did it in an hour."

"What's the difference?"

"The difference," said Lin Hao, "is that the first one you can't fix and the second one you can, and she's already working out how."

## Part 3: Rice, Secrets, and a Fight

The academy dining hall had three windows, and the first window — the one with the spirit-nutrient meals that cost what a commoner family spent in a week — had a line of silk jackets. Wulin, holding a tray from the third window piled into a small mountain, walked past all of it with the peace of a man who had eaten.

Lin Hao, whose own mountain rivaled his friend's, had done the arithmetic differently. "The first window's food shortens cultivation time," he said. "That's what the sign says. Spirit-nutrient density."

"I know what it says."

"Then you know that for the two of us, food isn't a meal, it's a resource." Lin Hao looked at his tray, then at the bronze badge in Wulin's fist, then at the first window. "And as of this morning, you're employed."

Wulin opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

They were negotiating the economics of appetite when a voice arrived behind them like weather.

"Tang Wulin."

Xie Xie stood in the aisle with his tray abandoned somewhere, pale hair still damp from morning training, wearing the expression of a person who had rehearsed this. "Fight me. Again. I wasn't at my best yesterday."

"We didn't fight yesterday," Wulin said, which was true — Xie Xie had *tried* to start a fight yesterday, in the dormitory corridor, and gotten exactly as far as one shove before Lin Hao had appeared in the doorway doing nothing at all, and the corridor had suddenly felt very crowded.

"You know what I mean." Xie Xie's eyes flicked — briefly, unwillingly — to Lin Hao, then snapped back to Wulin, who was the actual grievance. "Two strikes. You ended it in two strikes and then you *walked away* like I was furniture. If you don't fight me, I'll tell the whole class your secrets."

Wulin, who had no secrets he knew of, blinked. "What secrets?"

"I'll make them up!" Xie Xie said with enormous dignity. "They'll be *believable.*"

Lin Hao was having the specific internal experience of watching a fire argue with a forge. "Xie Xie," he said. "He'll fight you after we eat."

"Now," Xie Xie said.

"After," Wulin said, surprising himself — and then, because Lin Hao had set the tray down with the terrible calm of a boy whose father had taught him that a promise about food is still a promise, "…fine. Now."

The training yard behind the dining hall was dirt and old straw, and by the time they reached its center a ring of first-graders had assembled with the speed of people who smell consequences. Xie Xie shed his outer robe, and his martial soul surfaced into his grip — a short dagger of pale, liquid light, the **Light Dragon Dagger**, faster than eyes and twice as pretty. Everyone in class five had already learned to watch for it.

"Two strikes," Xie Xie said. "That's all you got. My family has trained me since I could walk. I'm rank twenty. Do you even know what my soul can—"

Wulin hit him.

Not a technique. Not a soul skill. A *punch* — thrown with the whole stacked architecture of a body forged four years on Thousand Refinement hammers and fed by a sealed furnace behind the sternum, delivered with the plain, devastating honesty of a hammer meeting work. Xie Xie's speed was real; it simply arrived second. The first blow took his guard apart. The second — a controlled one, Lin Hao saw, controlled the way his friend controlled everything that mattered — put Xie Xie flat on his back in the straw with his dagger's light scattered around him like spilled quicksilver and his eyes on the sky.

Two strikes. The yard was silent enough to hear the dining hall's first window calling numbers.

Wulin extended a hand. Xie Xie looked at it, at the sky, at the hand again — and took it, mostly to get upright, and was on his feet before the pull finished.

"Tomorrow," Xie Xie said, breathing hard, straightening his collar with both hands. "Tomorrow I'll be at my best."

"You were at your best," Wulin said, not unkindly, and went back to his rice.

From the doorway of the yard, unnoticed by everyone except a boy with hawk-eyes, Wu Zhangkong watched the whole of it without expression, and left before the crowd did.

## Part 4: The First Class (Canon Scene)

"Today officially marks the start of your classes." Wu Zhangkong stood at the front of the ground-level classroom at the innermost end of the building, and twenty-odd first-graders sat in it like offerings. "Most of your lessons will be taught by me. Stand up."

They stood. Wulin and Zhou Zhangxi, the biggest boys in the class, formed one file; Yun Xiao and Xie Xie fell in beside them; Lin Hao stood at the file's edge, and the wind in the room — there was never wind indoors, but there was around him, a thumb's width of moving air — settled as he stilled it.

"For the first lesson today, you will show me your abilities," Wu Zhangkong said. "Thereafter, you will proceed into actual combat. I will be the judge. Elimination occurs after a single loss." His pale green eyes crossed the room. "Show me your true colors."

A girl's hand went up. "Teacher Wu, I'm a Tool Soul Master. Do I need to join the combat?"

"In the battlefield," Wu Zhangkong said, ice-flat, "would your enemy spare you if you told them you were a Tool Soul Master?" He pointed at the big file. "First group. From this end."

Zhou Zhangxi turned toward Wulin immediately, and his whole massive frame said what his mouth was too proud to. He had been punched out of a dormitory window in their first week — he would not say *by whom*, and everyone knew — and the wound in his pride had been sitting in a warm place ever since. "Let's compete with our strength," he said to Wulin. "I don't believe yours is greater than mine."

Wulin looked at Lin Hao. Lin Hao gave the smallest nod — *this one's yours, and it always was* — and stepped back.

"No rules as to how you defeat your opponent," Wu Zhangkong announced to the circle. "Begin."

Zhou Zhangxi was very large and very strong and had plainly spent the week deciding how this would go. It did not go how he decided. Wulin met the charge the way an anvil meets a decision — his feet slid two inches and no further — and the exchange lasted four collisions before Zhou Zhangxi went down on one knee with Wulin's forearm across his shoulders, not crushed, simply *out-argued*, and the yard-sized classroom made a sound like a held breath escaping.

"Winner, Tang Wulin," Wu Zhangkong said, without inflection. His eyes moved one place down the file. "Lin Hao. The center."

Lin Hao walked into the circle, and twenty-odd first-graders watched the smallest thing about him arrive first: the dust. It moved away from his steps in slow little tides, as if the floor had opinions.

"Your abilities," Wu Zhangkong said.

"My martial soul is the Stormbringer Sword." Lin Hao lifted his hand, and the silver-blue blade surfaced into it with its etched wind-patterns and its restless light, and the classroom's temperature seemed to lean toward him. "My spirit soul is the Gale Hawk." The hawk came — not large, not yet; a shape of wind and pale light that left his shoulder and circled the ceiling once, and every student with working eyes followed it like a compass. "Seven hundred and twenty years of cultivation. Two soul rings." He let the numbers stand in the silence a moment, because Wu Zhangkong had said *show*, and numbers were part of the showing. "My first soul skill is called Gale Talon."

He demonstrated it on nothing — a single compressed arc of wind from his sword's edge that crossed the circle and opened a clean, shallow line in the far wall's plaster, three meters of distance folded like paper. Several students sat down without deciding to.

"And your second?" Wu Zhangkong asked. His voice had not changed. His *attention* had — Lin Hao could feel the difference the way the hawk felt thermals.

"My second soul skill is Hawk-Soul Union." Lin Hao hesitated — genuinely — for the first time. "It… takes everything I have, Teacher. It's not a demonstration. It's a decision."

For one heartbeat Wu Zhangkong looked, beneath the ice, *interested*. "Noted," he said. "Combat. Elimination. Your opponent—" his gaze found the palest face in the room, "—will be Xie Xie."

"I accept!" Xie Xie said, with the speed of a boy who had been handed the exact thing he'd been screaming for all week, and the Light Dragon Dagger surfaced into his grip like a held breath.

What followed, class five would talk about until the snow. Xie Xie was fast — genuinely, beautifully fast, light pouring along his limbs, afterimages arriving before his body did. He crossed the circle in the time a blink takes to start. And Lin Hao did not try to be faster. He simply did not *be* where the blow arrived — his bones had the hollow lightness of a bird's, his hawk-eyes had read the air's pressure change before the light moved — and his flat blade turned Xie Xie's own momentum aside the way wind turns a thrown leaf. Twice. Three times. Each redirection gentler than the last, until Xie Xie was panting in the center of the circle and Lin Hao had not yet attacked once.

"That's not fighting!" Xie Xie exploded. "Fight me!"

"You asked for a fight," Lin Hao said. "I'm waiting for one." And then — softly, so only the front row and one ice-cold teacher heard it — "…sorry. I've been waiting all week to say that to someone."

Xie Xie came at him with everything — light, speed, family training, fury — and Lin Hao met the charge with a single Gale Talon fired not at the boy but at the *floor behind* the boy, and the compressed wind's crack lifted Xie Xie's heels and stole his rhythm for exactly one stride, and in that stride the flat of the Stormbringer was resting, gentle as a rumor, against his ribs.

"Yield?" Lin Hao asked.

The classroom was silent. Xie Xie looked down at the blade, up at the amber eyes, and grinned — suddenly, wildly, like a person who has been lost for a week and just found the road.

"Tomorrow," he said, "I'm going to fight you BOTH."

"Winner, Lin Hao," Wu Zhangkong said. But as the class dissolved into noise and the hawk returned to Lin Hao's shoulder as settling air, the teacher's eyes stayed on him a half-second longer than judging required — the look of a man recalculating a syllabus.

## Part 5: The Roof, Again

That night, above dormitory three, Wulin counted the day's coin out twice — Association wages, real federal coins, more than his father made in a week — and put it away with the careful violence of a boy who intended every coin to become a direction to look for a silver-haired girl.

Lin Hao lay back against the tiles and watched the wind over Eastsea City. His hawk-eyes could read the air out there — the long harbor thermals, the pressure-shadow of the incoming weather — and somewhere under all of it, beside him, the familiar furnace-hum of his friend's second seal, pulling at its chains, a finger's width higher than last month.

*Soon,* he thought, not unhappily. *And I'd better keep climbing, or he'll make my promise look slow.*

Below them, in room eleven, the door stood open its careful inch.

The Blacksmith's Association badge sat on Wulin's windowsill, catching the soul-lamp light, and tomorrow there would be a fight — Xie Xie had been extremely clear — and after tomorrow, class, and after class, the Association, and hammer-work, and the long climb all of it fed.

It was, both of them thought without consulting each other, a good shape for a life to have.

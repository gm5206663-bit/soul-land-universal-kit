## Part 1: The Verdict and the Door

Morning came to Sea God's Island the way morning comes to old money: without hurrying, and certain the room would wait.

Shen Yi walked them up the wooden path before the sun had finished clearing the lake. Five students in yesterday's clothes, four of them nervous in the ordinary way and one of them nervous in the way a ledger is nervous before an audit — quietly, and with all the columns in order.

Elder Cai received them in a room that had too few chairs for the number of people who usually wanted to sit in it. She did not stand when they entered. She had a page in front of her, and a brush, and she was writing as the door opened, the way a woman writes who has decided that the next minute of her life belongs to the sentence under her hand.

Wulin went first, because Wulin always went first, and he bowed the full bow.

"Elder Cai. We came to apologize. Yesterday — Gu Yue acted for my sake. You were testing my strength, and I wasn't hurt at all, and she should not have raised her hand to an elder of the Pavilion. She knows it. We wanted to say it before the day had its breakfast."

Gu Yue bowed beside him — lower, and held longer. "I was impulsive. I'm sorry."

Elder Cai set down the brush. She looked at the girl for a long moment, the way a jeweler looks at a stone that has refused the first setting.

"You know what I offered you," she said. It was not a question.

Gu Yue straightened. The room was very still; outside, the lake made its small, rich noise, water against wood that had heard six decades of apologies and six decades of verdicts.

"I do," said Gu Yue. "And I know what it's worth. Doors like that don't knock twice — Teacher Shen said so, and she's right." She folded her hands, and the next words came slower, each one placed like a foot on a wet stone. "I came in with a class, Elder. Five of us crossed the exam floor together. I'm not strong enough yet to be the only person through a door — not strong enough in the way that matters. Let me be a working student first. Let me walk the road they walk. If the door is still open when I've walked it… then I'll know I was the one who knocked, and not the one who was pulled through."

Xie Xie, behind her, made a small sound that he converted into a cough with moderate success.

Elder Cai's face did not change. But she picked the brush back up, and Lin Hao, watching from the second row the way he watched everything — from the margin, where the readings were truest — saw the old woman's hand move once across the page, slowly, the way you write something you intend to keep.

"Do you mistake me for a fool, child?" said Elder Cai, without heat. "I have tested two generations of monsters. A girl says *I am not strong enough to leave my people* — that is not weakness speaking. That is a decision wearing humility's clothes." She set the brush down again. "The door stays open. Decisions made to walk beside your people are not defects in a disciple. They are the better kind of backbone. Remember that you decided it — when the road gets cold and you wonder whether you were foolish, remember that you chose the long road on purpose."

Gu Yue bowed again. This one did not come back up quickly.

"Now." Elder Cai's eyes moved — past Wulin, past the girl, and stopped on the margin of the room where Lin Hao stood, having taken the smallest, most unclaimable position available, the position his father called *where the wall can see you and the customer can't.* "The other one. Step here. I didn't send for an apology from you. I sent for a verdict, and verdicts are delivered to faces, not files."

He stepped forward.

"Your exam," she said, "has no honest column. The eighth trial was scored for a floor of four. You were the fifth set of feet on that floor. The scoring structure of Shrek Academy is older than most of the people in it, and it is not going to be rebuilt in a week to hold one boy. So your official transcript will say what everyone's says: passed, placed, working student. Ordinary words. The Academy distrusts records that need a new column — a record no column can hold is a record everyone wants to touch, and touched records stop being true."

She turned the page toward her, away from him, and wrote on it while he watched. He could not read the line. He could read the hand that wrote it: unhurried, certain, and the first stroke went down in the place on the page reserved for the thing that had bothered her most.

"The cold you leave on chairs," she said, still writing. "That went first. I thought you'd like to know what an elder notices." The brush stopped. "You leave this island a working student. No door opens for you this morning. Do you know why?"

He thought about it the way he thought about metal — not for the answer that sounded best, but for the one with no cracks.

"Because a door opened for me would be in the record," he said. "And a marked boy is a watched boy. The Academy learns more from a boy it isn't watching."

Something moved at the corner of Elder Cai's mouth, and was put down.

"Also," she said, "the Pavilion does not forget a debt. You hold a request you have not named — full marks across a trial floor buy one thing in this Academy, and you left it on the table like a man leaving a coin he intends to come back for. Good. When you know its weight, the Pavilion will hear it. A request spent by a boy is a firework. A request spent by a man is an easement." She slid the page into a drawer, closed it, and folded her hands. "There are books being kept about you in this Academy, boy. I keep one. You keep one. Keep yours honest. I will keep mine."

Outside, a boat bell rang once across the water, gentle as a deadline.

"Breakfast," said Elder Cai, and that was the verdict.

## Part 2: The Black Buns

Xie Xie had spent the walk to the table spending money he did not have.

"This island," he said, helping himself to a seat and, by the looks of it, to the entire concept of the island, "is extraordinary. The energy in the air — I slept and I might as well have cultivated all night. Wulin, you should ask Grandteacher if we can live here. Permanently. I'll take the boathouse."

"Keep dreaming," said Wulin, who had missed his morning's Purple Demon Eyes for the first time in weeks and was taking it out on the air. "Shrek's rules are very strict. And I'm about to starve to death — I had one meal yesterday and it was trial buns."

Zhuo Shi was already at the wooden table, and beside him sat Wu Zhangkong, who had not gone to the verdict and did not look like a man who regretted it. The old elder pointed Wulin to the seat beside him, and then pointed at a bamboo basket.

"Zhangkong told me you have a large stomach," said Zhuo Shi. "Let's see what's left of this basket when you're done."

Inside were twenty black objects, fist-sized, odorless, the color of iron left in a forge overnight. Steamed buns, in the same way that a sword is a strip of metal with opinions.

Lin Hao took one last, and he did not bite first. He read it the way he read everything new — with the pads of his fingers, the way he read a bar for grain. Tender. Dense. Faint, faint trace of something fishy and far away, like a word shouted across too much water. The surface gave under his thumb the way cold-worked iron gives, reluctantly and with a memory of pressure.

Wulin had already taken four, two per hand, a habit of a boy whose appetite made politeness a luxury. He bit. He chewed. He was halfway through being unimpressed — *not delicious, fishy, strange texture* — and then he swallowed, and his whole body changed key.

Warmth went down his throat and his blood went to meet it like a river meeting the sea. Lin Hao watched the captain's shoulders settle, watched the hollow-eyed exhaustion of yesterday walk out of him the way a bad guest leaves when the food arrives.

Wulin ate. Four. Eight. Twelve. Zhuo Shi's eyes, narrowed to begin with, widened by degrees the way a shutter opens on something it did not expect to see — the boy's blood and aura strengthening with every bun, pulse by pulse, a change that had nothing at all to do with soul power.

Lin Hao bit into his own.

The bun was cold at the center. Not the cold of a kitchen — the cold of *depth.* The fish it was made from had lived three kilometers under water, in the black country where light is a rumor and pressure is the only weather; and the body it had grown in that country had known, the whole of its life, what three kilometers of water weigh. When Lin Hao swallowed, that knowledge went down with it.

And below him — inside him — the dark water moved.

His attention sank the way a stone sinks, past the morning and the table and the sound of Wulin eating: the still, vast sea of his own inner country, smoked-glass surface without a ripple, the permanent storm-sky overhead with its clouds going the wrong way and its lightning fishing far up, fishing, never striking. At the bottom, in the sightless dark where the jiao lay coiled — the coil turned over. Once. The way a continent turns in its sleep and does not wake. No crossing, no gate, no threshold breached; a maturing, a turning, the deep-water part of him filing away what the fish's body had known — *this is the weight of three kilometers; this is how pressure behaves; remember it against the day you need depth.* Between sea and sky, the adaptation moved through both, taking the reading, redrawing some small line of the map he would not see until the next time he looked. Overhead, the storm clouds went the wrong way, patient as weather.

He came back to the table. His bun was half-eaten. He set the other half down, and did not reach for a second.

Across the table, Wu Zhangkong's eyes were on him. Beside him, Zhuo Shi had stopped watching Wulin.

The old elder said nothing for a moment. Then, to the teacher, not the student: "The lake boy. He had one."

"One is his way of counting," said Wu Zhangkong.

"No," said Zhuo Shi. "He didn't count the buns." The old man's eyes came back to Lin Hao, and in them was the particular respect of a deep-water practitioner who has just watched a puddle recognize the ocean. "He counted the depth. Tell me, boy — what did it say to you, the fish?"

Lin Hao considered the half bun. "It didn't say anything, Elder. It was a fish. But it knew what three kilometers of water weighs, and now so does the part of me that's interested in that sort of fact." He paused. "One is a reading, Elder. Sixteen would be a claim. I'm not entitled to sixteen of anything here."

Zhuo Shi looked at him for another long moment, and then looked at Wulin, who was at that very moment patting his stomach after the sixteenth bun, beaming.

"Grandteacher," said Wulin, shining-eyed, "may I eat these every day?"

Zhuo Shi's face did a thing Lin Hao had never seen an elder's face do, and filed under *useful.*

"You can have the rest," said the old man, and stood, and left.

Wulin turned, puzzled, to Wu Zhangkong. "Teacher. Did I say something rude?"

It was Shen Yi — who had come in at the tail of the meal with the boat hour in her face — who answered, smiling the warm smile of a woman watching a family tradition happen to someone else.

"Those are Teacher Zhuo's monthly rations. He allows himself one a day." She nodded at the basket. "They're made from a deep-sea fish — lives three kilometers underwater, and it isn't a soul beast, so you can imagine the strength it takes to live at that depth at all. Only elders of the Sea God's Pavilion are granted the privilege. You just ate more than half of your grandteacher's month. And then asked for more, every day. The only way that happens is if every elder on the island yields his share to you."

"Eh…" Wulin scratched his head, abashed. "Then I'll leave the rest for him."

"He told you to take them, so take them," said Shen Yi. "An old man like that doesn't grieve a basket of buns. He grieves or he doesn't, and he's already decided." She smiled. "When you have a free day next week, Wu Zhangkong will bring you over to visit. He's fond of you. You're similar."

On the boat, the lake open before them in the daytime sun — a sapphire set into the earth, light breaking across it in handfuls — Wulin sat with the wrapped basket on his knees like a dowry, and finally said the thing he'd been holding.

"That warmth," he said quietly, to Lin Hao, the only other person at the rail who'd understand. "Down the throat and into the blood. It went straight to the seals. I could feel it — whatever those fish are, they feed the thing the seals are holding back."

"A fish that lived three kilometers down," said Lin Hao, watching the water. "That's not food for a body, Wulin. That's pressure. A body that grows up under that weight carries it like a deposit. You didn't eat sixteen meals. You made sixteen deposits."

"Deposits into what?"

Lin Hao thought about the dark sea he carried, and the continent that had turned in its sleep, and said the true thing in the boy's own currency:

"Into the bank you can't spend from yet."

Wulin looked at his basket, and at his chest, where fourteen seals waited on their own horizon, and nodded slowly, the way a captain nods when the navigator says the harbor exists but is not today's.

## Part 3: The Honest Instrument

The outer-court administration building was grandeur with a clerk's face on it.

A scholarly administrator in his fifties, with the careful boredom of a man who processes miracles before lunch, enrolled them each in turn and issued each of them a dark-green school uniform, folded with the precise indifference of paper goods. He turned to the last page of his intake memo and frowned.

"Working students from the Sea God examinee pool," he said. "Four names." He looked up at five children. He looked down. He turned the page over, as though the fifth name might be a printing error on the back. It was not. "Five of you."

"Five," said Shen Yi, pleasantly.

The administrator looked at her. She smiled and shook her head the particular smile that meant *I could explain this and I have chosen, for your peace and mine, not to.* He had, to give him credit, survived two decades of Shrek paperwork; he wrote the fifth line into the ledger in the same ink, in the same hand, and made the small annotation men of his profession make when a fact refuses a category.

"Don't bring shame to the working students," he said, handing over the last uniform, to no one and therefore to all of them.

Lin Hao turned the phrase over once, the way he turned over any metal before he struck it. Shen Yi was already herding them onward, and when he sent her the questioning look, she only smiled again, and shook her head.

*Medical hall first,* the hall clerk said. *All intake students. The working-student line is the last line; take it.*

The medical hall smelled of dried herbs and old, careful doubt. Its examiner was a woman of perhaps sixty with ink on her cuff and a face that had settled, over a long career, into the expression of a scale that intends to tell the truth regardless of who is standing on it. Her plate read **Su Wen**, and she had the tiredness particular to people who have measured two generations of monsters and would very much like one ordinary afternoon.

The ordinary students had gone through in the morning. She was plainly hoping for four unremarkable working children and an early close.

She got Wulin first, as it happened, and for three minutes she thought she had her anomaly for the year. Her diagnostic stones ran warm against his chest; the blood-essence reading came back like a small furnace reported in a residential district; she wrote it down with the faintest lift of one eyebrow, filed it under *remarkable but classifiable*, and waved the captain through with the private gratitude of a woman who has used only one page.

The next boy sat down on the examination stool.

She started with the wrists, because she always started with the wrists — the hands told the truth first. The conditioning array lit under his palms and she read the density off the stone the way she'd read it ten thousand times, and then she read it again, because the stone was reporting the forearms of a master smith — the callus architecture of years at a forge, bone set under repeated load the way riverbed stones are set — attached to a child who could not have seen fourteen summers.

She made a note. She did not yet know she would be making notes for the rest of the afternoon.

Reflexes next: the standard release test, a falling bead, the array catching the catch-time in thousandths. The bead dropped. The boy caught it — or rather, the boy's hand was where the bead was going to be, having been there, it appeared, before the bead had fully committed to falling. The array returned a figure in the band the stone only used for instrument faults. Su Wen looked at it. She reset the test. She ran it again with her own hand on the mechanism. The same figure came back, polite and implacable, and somewhere above the boy's head, for no reason the hall accounted for, the air moved as though high up and far away a weather system had turned.

She made another note, slower.

Cold tolerance: the standard tolerance crystal, pale blue, meant to be held while it drew a fixed chill and measured what the body did with it. He took it. It frosted over from *his* side — the rime climbing inward from his palm the way frost climbs a window from the outside, only this was inside and the boy was the outside. Su Wen stared at it. She reached for a probe-prick to test the circulation response, pricked the side of his finger, and turned to write the result; when she turned back, the prick had closed. She was looking at the fingertip when it finished closing, like a curtain drawn shut by a servant who has done it a thousand times.

She set the crystal down very gently.

The diagnostic stone came last — the general soul-force read, the one instrument that always had an opinion. She placed it against his chest.

It behaved the way a blade behaves when a blade master holds it.

Su Wen had felt that once before, twenty years ago, running the intake on a young man who later became a three-word battle armor master: the stone did not read the body, the body *read the stone back* — the held, waiting quality of a thing in the presence of an intent that could pick it up, wear it, or put it through the wall, all with equal courtesy. Intent, at intake. Weapon intent, riding the child's nerves like a second heartbeat.

And underneath all of it — underneath the smith's wrists and the storm-reflexes and the frost that went the wrong direction and the intent that looked out through his eyes like a second tenant — underneath, the array kept re-calibrating. Every reading settled slightly different from the reading before it, not error but *learning*: the body taking the test while the test took the body, a background field that made the other four signatures able to live on one frame at all.

Five signatures. None of them wrong. None of them belonging to the same boy.

"Step onto the soul array," she said, and her voice had the careful flatness of a woman removing a cherished instrument from its case.

The soul-soul array was the medical hall's deep instrument: a standing mirror set in bronze, ringed with tone-stones, built to read the spirit souls of new intakes for the Academy's records. He stood within the ring. The stones woke, climbed their scale — and hit the end of it and kept climbing, the needle pressed flat against the stop the way a man's shoulder presses a door that was built to stop him.

Su Wen read the result.

She reached out, turned the instrument's tone-stones down to silence, and let them wake again. She gestured him off the platform, checked the calibration with the two-tone strike she trusted more than certificates, and had him step back on. The array woke, climbed, and pinned itself against the far stop with the same patient, impossible report.

A second soul, on a four-ring intake student, reading past the ten-thousand-year gate. A threshold the instrument could not price — not a number too large, but a *gate* standing where no gate had standing room. The array's scale went to ten thousand the way a road goes to a cliff; this child's reading was the air past the cliff.

Then she looked at the mirror.

For one frame — one frame only — the bronze mirror did not show the boy. It showed dark water under a storm sky: a still, black sea reaching to every edge of the glass, clouds above it moving in the wrong direction, and far, far down, a shape at the bottom that was not landscape and was not beast, turning over with the patience of geology.

She looked again. The mirror showed a boy standing in a bronze ring, breathing fog, waiting politely for his results.

Su Wen closed her eyes for approximately one second, which is the amount of time a professional allows herself for the universe to apologize. It did not.

She opened a drawer she did not open often — the drawer at the bottom, the one her predecessor had labeled *Unclassifiable* and mostly used, in his day, for paperwork she strongly suspected he had simply hated filing. She took out a fresh folder. On the tab she wrote nothing, because nothing in the Academy's taxonomy was true on the tab yet. Inside, in the careful handwriting of a woman who had long since accepted that *impossible* is a filing category like any other, she wrote:

*Intake, working-student line. Five distinct body-signatures on one frame — smith-grade conditioning; storm-class reflex (instrument band, twice confirmed); deep-water cold signature with ambient-direction reversal; regeneration, observed closing; weapon intent present at intake. Background field: readings adapt under repeated test. No academy bracket applies.*

And below that, her hand steadier than her opinion:

*Second-soul array: result beyond instrument scale; ten-thousand-tier threshold confirmed twice on recalibration. Classification withheld pending instrument that does not exist. Annual review. — S.W.*

At the very bottom, small enough that she nearly didn't write it: *Mirror unclear.*

"Right," she said. "You're healthy. Annoyingly so. You can go."

The boy paused at the edge of the ring. "Is there… anything the Academy will need from me?"

"Come back in a year," said Su Wen. "And do try not to become more interesting before then."

"I'll try," he said.

"No," she said, already writing, "you won't."

He left. She sat for a while with the pen in her hand, and then she opened the folder again and added, beneath *Mirror unclear*, three more words, because honesty was her profession and she had never once let a profession down:

*Recheck annually.*

## Part 4: The Warm Wall

The job assignment came at the administration window, carried by a steward who clearly considered dusk a personal appointment: the five working students' first labor — Spirit Ice Plaza, the great flagstoned court before the main hall. Sweep, dispose, mop. Before dusk *on the morrow*. Not a single speck of dust, on pain of points.

"Tomorrow, then," said Xie Xie, when the steward had gone. "Plenty of dark between now and then. What do we do with the dark?"

The answer waited at the end of a key ring.

A porter with the cheerful fatalism of a man delivering orphans to a doorstep led them west across the darkening plaza and into the trees, to the quarter where the Academy housed its working students. The building showed itself by degrees: a long, single-story barracks, run-down and tucked into the forest as if the Academy hoped the trees would do the apologizing, some twenty rooms along its flank, and the far rooms with planks nailed where windows had given up entirely.

"Mostly empty this early in the year," the porter said, walking them past. "You lot are the end block." He stopped at a low gate in the flank wall, and handed over the ring of keys. "Best wall in the outer court. Nobody ever bids on the room — forge noise all night. You'll sleep through it; you're the working students." He squinted at them. "Five? The paper said four."

"The paper," said Shen Yi, who had walked them down, "is having a week."

Lin Hao stepped through the gate.

It was a courtyard the Academy had stopped trying to want — but it was a courtyard, which the barracks flank was not. A pavilion furnace-flue ran along its north side, and the stone wall there held the day's heat and would hold the winter's too, warm the year round. Beyond the wall came the breathing sound of the forge itself — bellows, the low talk of fire — and riding the sound to its source with his eyes, he found the scrap gate, low and unimportant, with a hand-painted sign nailed to it in soot-colored paint: **SCRAP IS NOT FREE. NEITHER IS READING IT.** A soot-armed figure moved behind the furnace door and did not look up.

"Jobs related to a secondary profession pay the best," Shen Yi said from behind him, following his gaze the way she followed everything — correctly. "The administration assigns basics, but a student with a trade bids for the trade work. Tuition here is 'very expensive,' I'm afraid." She said it with the odd, weighty gentleness she'd used on the island. "The price is real. That is not a cruelty — it's the one way the Academy has of saying the thing is worth real money."

But it was the tree that stopped him.

The old courtyard pine — the kind Shrek had been growing since before the current outer court was a proposal — had a branch. On the branch, in the last clean light of a September evening, spread a hand-span of white rime. Frost. At the close of a warm day. Under it stood a wiry old man with a broom and a little notebook bound in string, hands on his hips, addressing the branch.

"September," he told it. "It's September. I've got forty years of you in here, tree, and you've never frosted in September. This branch. This one branch." He prodded it gently with the broom. The rime did not melt. It sat there, white and patient, glittering against the sunset.

Lin Hao stopped. The groundskeeper looked up, and his eyes — the eyes of a man who had spent forty years believing the trees told him things and being, by and large, right — moved from the branch to the boy.

The boy looked at the branch. The branch glittered. The boy gave the groundskeeper the smallest, most noncommittal nod in the outer court, the nod of one professional to another across a difference of departments.

The groundskeeper squinted at him for a long moment. Then he opened his little book and wrote in it, and said to the tree, not the boy: "September."

He did not sound angry. He sounded like a man whose mail had finally arrived.

"Rooms aren't opened after dark this term — new rule, after the rat business," the porter said, locking the gate back and handing over the key. "Admin's got visitor beds for the new lot tonight. Bring your things in with the light; the place'll look worse before it looks better."

Which was how, while the others went ahead with the porter, Lin Hao came to borrow the groundskeeper's spare broom and spend the last gold of the evening sweeping out *their own* courtyard — the flags under the pine, the threshold of the warm wall, the little court that was theirs as of tomorrow and not one breath sooner. He swept the way his father swept a forge floor at close of day: with the grain, and the margin held.

Mid-stroke, something rose in his wrist that was not the broom.

The intent — woken at the lake, promoted by the coldest man on the continent from student to colleague — moved through his arms and looked out of his hands at the broom handle, and for one breath the broom was *nothing*, no body at all, empty air and a held purpose, and the Unwritten Stroke, the stroke that had worked all along because it did not exist, the stroke that needed no blade to live in, rose to deliver itself into empty hands in a courtyard full of sunset—

—and he stopped it.

Not here. Not yet. A thing that good spends its first coin on ground it chooses, with the correct witnesses, and a borrowed broom in an empty courtyard on moving-in eve was neither. He let the breath go. The broom was a broom again. But the master's last correction came with the breath and would not be dismissed: *you left the sword behind tonight. Keep the first body fluent.* Two days. He had not taken a single sword form since the branch on the lake, because there had been no sword to take one with — and that, he understood now, standing in a courtyard with a broom in his hands, was exactly the laziness the correction had been built to kill.

So when the light went gold and the courtyard flags were clean, he took the broom through Still Water, the first form, slowly — the block laid flat, the line taken away, the stroke meeting the birth of a thing rather than its arrival — and the broom wore it completely, for as long as it was needed, and not one breath longer. The sword's grammar, kept fluent in the body it had started in. Over the warm wall, on a branch nobody could explain, a hand-span of frost outlasted the sunset. Under the tree, a groundskeeper who believed the trees watched a boy do a slow sword-dance with a broom in an empty court, and he wrote that down too, and he did not show it to anyone, because nobody believed the tree book and that had always been the finest part of owning it.

That night, in the visitor beds the admin kept for the new lot, Lin Hao opened his own ledger at the last page and wrote the day in his honest hand: one request still unspent and now acknowledged by the Pavilion; one elder's page being kept he could not read; one file in a drawer with nothing written on its tab; one fish's depth, filed; one salt line moved, on the map only he could read; one courtyard with a warm wall and a frost branch and a gate sign that read like a price list, waiting for morning; one stroke, waited for.

Three books in Shrek Academy were being kept about the same boy now — his own, and Elder Cai's, and the honest instrument's in the bottom drawer — and none of the three knew the others' contents, and that, Lin Hao thought, settling the ledger into the bag beside the folded dark-green uniforms, was a great deal of arithmetic for one first day, and exactly the amount a working student could afford.

Outside, in the dark of the trees, the September branch held its frost through the first night, and the forge breathed slow against the warm wall, and morning — morning was going to show them the room.
